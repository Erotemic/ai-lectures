# When Harvests Failed: Land, Climate, Famine, and the Politics of Survival

## A failed harvest is not yet a complete history

Imagine two households after a crop failure. One has land, some savings, and relatives who may send food. The other grows food on rented ground, owes money to a creditor, and depends on wages that disappear when an employer stops hiring. If both live through the same dry season, they may experience very different degrees of hunger. These are invented illustrative households, not people from a discovered archive. Their difference introduces the central question: how does an environmental shock become a human catastrophe, and why are some people far more exposed than others?

The economist Amartya Sen argued in Poverty and Famines, first published in 1981, that hunger must be explained through people’s ability to command food, not just through the total quantity available in an entire market. His entitlement approach follows what people own, earn, receive, or can legally exchange. It has been tested and debated especially for twentieth-century crises. We may use its questions to study nineteenth-century famines, but we should not pretend that Sen personally established the causes of every event we discuss here. Later research has also shown that physical shortage can be devastating and that food access and supply must be studied together.

This distinction is a reason for more empirical work, not a reason to ignore weather. Drought can eliminate crops and pasture. Flooding can spoil food or undermine transport. Disease can kill people weakened by hunger. War can move armies through fields and remove labor from farming. Governments can help or obstruct relief. Markets can transmit grain to distressed regions, but they can also price poor buyers out of food that still exists somewhere else. No single item on this list is sufficient by itself. To understand what happened in one region at one time, we need rainfall records, food prices, property relations, administrative documents and human testimony.

## Ireland: the crop, the law, and the relief papers

Ireland’s Great Famine, commonly dated from 1845 through 1852, began with devastating failures of the potato crop and became a disaster of mortality, disease, migration, and social transformation. The potato was vital to the diets of many rural families, yet Ireland was not economically isolated from the United Kingdom. Land tenure, rural poverty, food markets, and government policies shaped what people could obtain. The Irish National Archives’ guide to Distress Papers and the Relief Commission gives a first point of entry into contemporary documentation. It confirms the period and lists the petitions, official reports and attempts at relief that survive.

An archive of petitions is an unusually close view of disaster, but it is not a transparent recording of everyone who suffered. A letter asking for relief has an author, an intended recipient, a place, and a strategic reason for its wording. Officials may record names and numbers while leaving out those who died before they could petition. The Irish National Archives identifies separate collections of Distress Papers and records from the Relief Commission. It also specifies finding aids and series identifiers. These are genuinely useful research directions: they tell a reader how to follow claims down to documents rather than relying on a story built from anonymous “contemporary sources.”

Relief was also organized through the poor-law system, which placed workhouses at the center of many local responses. The National Archives explains that the Irish Poor Law Act of 1838 initially divided Ireland into more than a hundred unions, with workhouses and local boards of guardians. By March 1851 the workhouse system was sheltering a far larger share of the population than its original planners envisaged. These institutions can be examined through registers of admission, discharge, and local correspondence. But the existence of a workhouse does not tell us whether entering it was safe, voluntary in any meaningful sense, or adequate to preserve a family’s dignity.

Historian Peter Gray’s chapter Famine and Land emphasizes disagreement over the British state’s responsibility and the long aftermath of property change. A famine's aftermath lasts beyond the first recovered harvest or the closing of a relief committee. Surviving tenants may face eviction, land consolidation, and migration. Families can experience changes in language, inheritance, settlement and political loyalties that last decades. Gray’s publisher abstract describes these controversies; I am citing it as evidence that historians argue over responsibility and consequences, not as if I had read every page of his chapter.

It is therefore misleading to offer either “the blight did it” or “the market did it” as an adequate standalone explanation. The crop disease was an immediate destructive force. The conditions that made households dependent on a crop, and the institutions that decided what help was given, changed the consequences. Even the best historical calculation of deaths cannot by itself tell us what a tenant believed would happen the next month. This is a case where biological history, political economy and archival evidence need to be read together. None cancels the others.

## A drought that crossed borders

In the middle and late 1870s, large areas of South and East Asia suffered prolonged drought and severe famines; effects of abnormal rainfall patterns reached parts of Africa and Brazil as well. A 2018 study in the Journal of Climate reconstructs this multiyear episode using rain gauges, tree-ring-based drought atlases, and simulations. It associates the extreme conditions with a sequence of ocean temperatures, including the intense El Niño of 1877–78, but stresses that drought in some places began before that El Niño fully developed. That detail matters: it is wrong to say simply that one named climate event arrived and switched on all the famines at once.

What can such a climate reconstruction establish? It can estimate which regions were unusually dry and when, compare reconstructions with measurements, and test plausible relationships among ocean circulation and rainfall. It cannot show directly that a particular landlord evicted a tenant or a district official withheld a relief shipment. Nor does a modeled rainfall deficit tell us the sex, wealth, or occupation of the people who died. The study itself separates the physical drivers of drought from political and economic processes translating agricultural failure into mortality. That separation is a model for how our citations should work too.

Some global estimates of nineteenth-century famine mortality are enormously high, but the counts are reconstructed from partial records and should not be presented as exact measurements. The global-climate paper reports a very large mortality figure drawn in part from earlier historical scholarship. For this lecture, we do not need to adopt a single dramatic number that appears more certain than its evidence. The geographic overlap is consequential even without a precise global total. It reminds us to compare different systems of governance and relief under partially connected climatic pressure, while resisting the temptation to assume the same policies operated everywhere.

## Madras: drought, policy, starvation, and disease

In British-ruled parts of India, the famine of 1876–78 occurred amid severe failures of monsoon rainfall and contested government decisions about relief. The Journal of Climate reconstruction documents the geographic and seasonal drought pattern. Rune Møller Stahl’s historical chapter, visible through its university abstract, examines the influence of laissez-faire economic ideas on colonial officials’ choices about relief. Those two sources answer different questions. One measures the weather and its timing. The other analyzes the political arguments through which administrators weighed intervention and market freedom. Neither alone can explain the experience of every affected community.

Mortality raises a further question. Did people die directly from prolonged hunger, or from infections spread among malnourished and displaced populations? The scholar Leela Sami revisited medical and mortality records for the Madras famine in Social History of Medicine. Her publisher abstract rejects an explanation that neglects starvation as a major direct cause. Other research examines how rain returning after famine could produce disease conditions. These are not mutually exclusive in every case, and the evidence must be dated. It would be irresponsible to turn a complex set of causes into a neat chart with percentages invented for a lecture.

Colonial rule mattered partly because its fiscal and legal structures shaped who could draw on resources when conditions worsened. Local communities and officials did not all behave alike, and contemporary debate included criticism of inadequate relief. Stahl’s work highlights the ideological constraints policymakers placed on themselves. But one must still distinguish an argument found in colonial administrative writing from a verified outcome in a particular district. To move from national policy to household mortality, a historian needs local prices, movements of grain, work requirements, disease registers, and evidence of who received assistance. A good lecture identifies that unfinished bridge rather than making every kind of evidence say the same thing.

Nor can the history end with the fact that drought passed. Farmers may have sold animals, seeds, tools, or land to survive. Workers who moved may not have returned. Disease and debt can continue to harm households after rainfall recovers. In some districts government responses changed, while in others the political legacies of famine persisted through later crises. These are possible consequences to investigate, not a fabricated uniform biography of an Indian family. The most ethical use of a representative scene is to reveal a question that real records can answer, and then to leave the imagined family behind when making factual claims.

## North China: how a disaster is recorded

North China experienced a catastrophic drought-famine from 1876 through 1879, with especially severe conditions in parts of Shanxi and Henan. Historian Kathryn Edgerton-Tarpley’s Tears from Iron explores cultural and moral responses to the disaster, while a later Journal of Chinese History article examines records from Shaanxi and surrounding regions. These scholarly accounts emphasize that the surviving evidence is not only an official death ledger. It includes local writing, testimony, religious interpretation, and judgments about what rulers and neighbors ought to have done. The story of the famine was also an argument over social obligation.

The Shaanxi study demonstrates why even an exceptionally large famine should not be described as one spatially uniform episode. It traces drought moving across northern regions and explains that warfare, local ecological stress and weakened relief institutions could amplify suffering. The same historian notes that sources sometimes remembered earlier rebellions more extensively than the hunger experienced around them. If an archive says little about starvation, that may be a property of the recording institution or genre, not evidence that the people of a district were unharmed. Silence is a source problem requiring investigation, not a number we can casually fill in.

Columbia University’s teaching unit includes an autobiographical account narrated by Ning Lao Tai Tai about hardship during a later North China famine, in 1887–88. We must not shift that testimony backward ten years merely because both crises occurred under Qing rule. It is evidence about a different time, a particular life, and the gendered restrictions she described. The distinction illustrates a surprisingly common citation failure: a moving first-person account is used to make an earlier historical scene vivid, but the time and place no longer match. It is better to tell the listener that the two crises were separate, then ask what could be compared responsibly.

The 1870s famine is also a case for combining climate research with locally situated administration. A quantitative study in Weather, Climate, and Society analyzes thousands of Qing-era famine records and finds strong associations between drought and famine patterns, while highlighting longer pressures on the land and the ability of regions to buffer shocks. Its statistical pattern is not proof that every local outcome was determined by rainfall. The contemporary cultural accounts and archival work add questions about why relief worked in one setting and failed in another. Climate and institutions interact; the task is to specify where, how, and according to which evidence.

## What counts as a responsible comparison?

We have crossed Ireland, India, and North China. They share disasters involving crops and survival, but they do not share a single land system, imperial relationship, climate, or political culture. They should not be used as three examples proving one ideological slogan. The Irish archive contains administrative petitions and poor-law records; Indian scholarship debates policy and direct mechanisms of death; Chinese histories investigate regional ecology, local texts and moral interpretation; climate scientists reconstruct a multiyear physical pattern. Each records a different part of human experience. Before drawing a comparative conclusion, we must first identify which parts can genuinely be compared.

Try a practical source audit. Choose one factual sentence: “The famine followed drought.” Ask which time series and place support it. Choose another: “Relief was inadequate.” Ask which policy, district and period are being judged, and by whom. Choose a third: “Many people died from disease.” Ask whether death registers identify causes and whether medical vocabulary has changed. Finally choose: “The government was responsible.” That is not only a statistic; it is a causal and ethical claim requiring explicit standards. A source that establishes drought timing may tell us little about the adequacy of relief. The same catastrophe can demand different kinds of evidence depending on whether a claim concerns weather, medicine, relief policy, or responsibility.

Return to the two imagined households. The drought was shared. Their protection against it was not. The history of a famine therefore requires both the field and the household door: rain, soil, seed, transport, ownership, bargaining power, relief, illness, and memory. By naming where the evidence comes from, we allow a reader to disagree in an informed way. Attribution is not an appendix to the explanation. It is part of how the explanation becomes trustworthy, particularly when the dead cannot speak and the records were shaped by institutions that survived them.
