# Who Got to Make the Peace? Versailles, Mandates, and Unanswered Claims

## Who was allowed to make peace?

A peace conference sounds like a place where peoples harmed by war come together to decide how to prevent the next one. The Paris Peace Conference of 1919 was more constrained. Its central decisions were made by the victorious powers, especially their leading representatives. Defeated states did not negotiate on equal terms, and the new Bolshevik government of Russia was excluded. People living under colonial rule often had no formal place at the negotiating table. Nonetheless, organizations and petitioners around the world read promises about political rights and tried to turn them into enforceable claims. The central tension was between language about a new international order and control over who could define it.

The settlement was not one document resolving everything in June 1919. The Treaty of Versailles dealt with Germany. Other treaties addressed Austria, Bulgaria, and Hungary; the Ottoman settlement remained contested and was reworked in the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923. Alan Sharp's historical synthesis stresses the sequence and the effort to impose order on a shattered set of empires. New borders, territorial transfers, minority questions, economic losses, security fears, and claims to national self-government were entangled. The conference's decisions were not a final verdict on the meaning of a nation, but a series of political agreements negotiated under unequal power.

At Paris, proposals for international cooperation and rules meant to restrain future aggression developed into the League of Nations. Yet the League's founding covenant also permitted continuing colonial administration. One should resist imagining a simple contrast between idealistic speeches and cynical secret arrangements. The same powers could believe that international procedures would improve security while maintaining imperial hierarchies they considered legitimate. The contradiction is visible in the text itself. To understand it, we need to read the clauses, not merely repeat the name of the institution.

## Versailles: responsibility, damage and the burden of a phrase

Article 231 of the Versailles Treaty stated that Germany and its allies accepted responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war imposed through their aggression. Article 232 acknowledged limitations on Germany's resources while requiring compensation for specified civilian damage. These are legal and diplomatic provisions in a reparations framework. Later political debate turned the first clause into a symbol of humiliation, often labeled the war-guilt clause. It is historically important not to present that label as though the treaty were a modern scholarly investigation proving that one state alone caused every aspect of the war. The provision concerned the settlement's allocation of legal responsibility and claims for compensation.

Reparations, boundaries, and disarmament became arguments within the new German republic. Political movements could cite the treaty to mobilize resentment, while governments faced actual obligations and economic disruption. It is equally misleading to treat those later consequences as the inevitable product of one sentence. Historical explanation requires the interaction of diplomatic terms, domestic conflict, economic circumstances, and decisions made after 1919. Sharp's account of the settlements puts responsibility and self-determination within that longer, contested history rather than making a single clause carry the entire explanation.

The defeated powers were not the only people dissatisfied. Some newly created states faced unresolved questions about minority populations or access to resources. Others hoped for independence and found that their territories would be administered by governments already powerful in the imperial order. The categories victory and defeat therefore do not map neatly onto everyone who gained or lost politically. Some communities had supplied soldiers and labor to the victors and still received no sovereignty. Others obtained new states, but not always the borders or political guarantees sought by the people living within them.

## Article 22: an international order that kept colonies

Article 22 of the League Covenant described the populations of former German and Ottoman territories as not yet able to govern themselves under what it called the modern world's conditions. It declared their welfare a trust of civilization and placed administration with mandatory powers under League supervision. The language claimed international responsibility, but it was not the language of equal popular consent. The text assumed that other governments could judge peoples' readiness to rule themselves. Its precise wording, rather than a later commentator's description, supplies the strongest evidence for the paternalistic premise built into the new system.

Mandates were organized in different classes. Former Ottoman territories in the Middle East were treated differently from former German possessions in Africa and the Pacific. Britain, France, Belgium, Japan, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa received administrative responsibilities in different places. Archival files and later League reports document those arrangements, but assigning a mandate did not resolve the political claims of people living there. The important historical distinction is between recognition of an international duty and recognition of local sovereignty. The League institutionalized the former without consistently delivering the latter.

In Africa, many people had experienced imperial conquest before the war, then wartime recruitment, seizure of resources, and disruption. The redrawing of colonial boundaries did not suddenly permit them to choose independent governments. Michael Crowder's history of the war's African consequences describes how European conflicts shifted control of former German colonies. From the point of view of the settlement, a mandate could appear to be a new, regulated form of administration. From the viewpoint of people demanding political autonomy, it could look like continued foreign rule under a new legal label. Both statements describe aspects of the same institutional arrangement.

The records demand precision. A British Library catalogue for mandate files establishes that officials debated the arrangement of Palestine, Mesopotamia, Syria, Lebanon, and the other classes of territory. A catalogue entry does not itself tell us exactly what every inhabitant said or what all the enclosed memoranda argued. The League Covenant tells us what the formal system authorized, not whether a mandatory power administered it fairly. For those questions we would need petitions, local newspapers, administrative reports, and independent studies of particular communities. Treating a catalogue description as a full reading of the file would disguise a gap in evidence.

## China: Shandong and the refusal to sign

In China, the diplomatic decision over Shandong triggered a political crisis. The Treaty of Versailles, Article 156, transferred Germany's rights and privileges relating to the Jiaozhou Bay territory and connected infrastructure to Japan. These were specific legal rights, not an assertion that all of Shandong's land was newly annexed. Chinese delegates had sought the return of German privileges to Chinese authority. The transfer exposed the limits of their negotiating position. British archival materials assembled by the United Kingdom's National Archives trace the response of Chinese students and activists who considered the decision a betrayal of China's sovereignty.

On 4 May 1919 students protested in Beijing. The movement widened through demonstrations, boycotts, and strikes, linking foreign policy with arguments about education, intellectual change, government legitimacy, and China's place in the world. The Chinese delegation refused to sign the Versailles treaty. It would be too simple to say that the students merely copied European ideas of national self-determination. They organized in a society with its own revolutionary and intellectual history. The diplomatic outcome provided an immediate focus for grievances already concerned with foreign power and internal political weakness.

This episode also recalls the Chinese laborers who had served Allied needs in France. Their contribution formed part of the argument that China deserved a consequential voice in the peace. Yet participating in a war coalition did not guarantee control over the treaties negotiated in its aftermath. The contrast is concrete: the treaty text identifies the privileges Germany relinquished in favor of Japan, while contemporary protesters and delegates registered refusal. International relations cannot be understood only by listing states that signed agreements. A refusal to sign, a strike, or a boycott can also be an act of political history.

## Korea and Vietnam: declarations addressed to the world

Korea's March First Movement began in 1919 under Japanese colonial rule. A Declaration of Independence announced Korea's right to self-government; protests followed across the country. An English translation published by Columbia University's Asia for Educators preserves a text that appeals to the equality of nations and Korea's historical existence. The declaration was an assertion by political organizers, not a document written on behalf of every inhabitant after a universal vote. Protestors faced repression, and the movement did not immediately achieve independence. Its existence shows that demands for national rights reached beyond territories directly addressed by the main European peace treaties.

It would also be inaccurate to describe Korean activism as an uncomplicated effect of speeches from Washington or Paris. Korean resistance to Japanese colonial rule had its own political networks, organizational traditions, and history. The international moment offered a language in which campaigners could make claims to foreign audiences. That language did not substitute for the strategies and sacrifices of the movement itself. The contrast between an independence declaration and an imperial state's coercive response is an example of why self-determination was a contested political demand rather than a principle uniformly applied by the powers.

In June 1919 a group of Vietnamese patriots, represented by Nguyen Ai Quoc, submitted demands at the peace conference. A letter addressed to U.S. Secretary of State Robert Lansing survives in the records of the American Commission to Negotiate Peace. Rather than simply declaring the formation of an independent state, the petition called for amnesty, equal legal safeguards, freedom of the press and assembly, education, movement, and elected Vietnamese representation. Reading the numbered demands is more informative than reducing the petition to one slogan. It reveals an immediate agenda for curbing arbitrary colonial authority, even as the petitioners argued that the new international moment should bring justice.

Put the Korean declaration beside the Vietnamese petition. Both asked powerful outsiders to recognize political rights, but they took different forms and worked from different strategic positions. One asserted national independence under Japanese rule; the other listed legal and representative demands to the powers that might influence France. Neither document alone establishes the opinion of every Korean or Vietnamese person. Together they show activists translating the language of international order into claims grounded in the circumstances of their own societies. The victors' promises were only part of the story; others struggled to decide what those promises would mean.

## Egypt and Pan-African claims

Egyptian politics erupted in 1919 when British authorities arrested and exiled Saad Zaghlul and other Wafd leaders. Protests extended beyond the capital and involved students, workers, officials, rural participants, women, Muslims, and Copts. A scholarly history of independence and constitutionalism shows that the movement combined demands for independence with disagreements about the future shape of political authority. Protest was not simply a petition from elite diplomats to European officials. It became a mass social event. The fact that many people joined it does not mean they shared one precise constitutional program or that all social divisions vanished.

At Paris, W. E. B. Du Bois and other participants in the 1919 Pan-African Congress pressed for protections and political rights for peoples of African descent and for inhabitants of African territories. The Congress's resolutions survive in an edited documentary publication hosted by UCLA's African Studies Center. They called for safeguards against exploitation of land and labor and for international oversight. The document should be read as a set of demands made by participants, not as a decision of the peace conference or as the opinion of every African community. The record of who could attend is part of its historical meaning.

A useful comparison now comes into view. Article 22's authors presented imperial trusteeship as a responsibility exercised by advanced states. The Pan-African Congress asked for protections and representation but also used some of the era's hierarchical terminology. The similarity of vocabulary should not conceal the difference in political position between people controlling the mandate system and people seeking influence over it. The resolutions are evidence of organizers arguing about who could legitimately govern, own land, and benefit from resources. They are not proof that those claims were adopted.

## A settlement and a continuing argument

The postwar order achieved some of what its supporters wanted. It created new machinery for diplomacy and international cooperation, established some new states, and imposed formal constraints on the defeated powers. It also preserved and reorganized imperial government. In the same year that victorious diplomats tried to settle the war, colonized peoples and political movements asserted claims that the settlement did not satisfy. It would be inaccurate to call Paris either a complete failure or a universal realization of self-government. The best description is a set of institutions and compromises whose uneven legitimacy remained contested.

The documents teach us how to test a claim of self-determination. First, identify who wrote it and for whom. Second, ask what they demanded: independence, legal protections, land rights, voting, or a change in administrators. Third, compare the demand with the actual treaty article, decree, or governmental response. Fourth, follow what happened after the document was published. On that test, China, Korea, Vietnam, and Pan-African activists present different stories but share an important condition: their arguments confronted a system in which political recognition was neither equally distributed nor reliably enforceable.

A second international organization was born from the same peace moment. Labor activists and governments had argued that lasting peace would require improved conditions for workers, not only new borders. The International Labour Organization became part of the Versailles framework and proposed international rules concerning hours and conditions of work. Its creation illustrates that postwar politics were not solely about national sovereignty. People also debated the authority of employers, the obligations of governments, and the international standing of workers. We will turn next to these struggles over mass participation, public welfare, and the meaning of democracy after 1918.

## Attribution and unresolved questions (display only)

- Historical examples are documentary or explicitly analytical; no fabricated testimony or invented dialogue has been attributed to historical participants.

- Primary government records show what governments wrote or did, not automatically the consent or full experience of affected communities.

- Statistical estimates, especially of influenza mortality and wartime deaths, carry model and source uncertainty and should not be presented as exact individual histories.

- Original researchers and source institutions are identified in the bibliography. Search summaries, abstracts, and archival catalogues are labeled as limited access.
