# When Credit Stopped Moving: The Global Financial Crisis, 2007–2009

## A house and a promise

A mortgage begins with an apparently simple exchange. A household borrows money to buy a home and promises to repay it over time. The lender expects interest and takes the house as collateral if payments fail. By the early two thousands in the United States, many mortgages no longer stayed with the institution that originated them. Loans were sold and pooled, and claims on their payments were turned into securities that investors could buy. This made it easier to move funds into housing markets. It also made the risks harder to see. A family negotiating a monthly payment could be several contractual steps away from the people ultimately exposed to its failure to pay. The distance mattered because decisions at every step shaped the quality of loans and the information passed along.

House prices had risen for years in many American markets, helped by credit availability, expectations of further appreciation, and local conditions. Borrowers and lenders made plans on the assumption that the future might resemble the recent past. Some borrowers received mortgages with payments that could increase, weak documentation of income, or terms they did not fully understand. Lending practices varied; many households with conventional mortgages were also exposed when local housing markets weakened. Regulators, banks, brokers, and securities buyers each had responsibilities. It would be inaccurate to portray every borrower as reckless or every lender as knowing fraudster. The important mechanism was systemic: when house prices stopped rising, defaults could increase, collateral values could fall, and losses could appear across institutions that had treated their mortgage exposures as manageable.

## How a mortgage became a security

Securitization converted claims on mortgage payments into tradable instruments. A pool might contain thousands of loans. Its cash flows could be divided into portions, often called tranches, which were promised payments in a particular order. The first losses might fall on one portion before reaching another. In principle, dividing risk could let investors choose different levels of exposure. In practice, the safety of each portion still depended on the loans, the assumptions used to model defaults, and what happened when housing markets across many places declined together. The process did not make losses disappear. It rearranged who was supposed to absorb them. And when one security became part of another structured product, tracing the original loans could become even more difficult.

Investors often relied on ratings and mathematical models that assigned low probabilities to severe correlated losses. A model based on a period of rising house prices could produce reassuring estimates that did not survive a nationwide reversal. Complex structured products and credit-default swaps could add further exposures. A credit-default swap is a contract in which one party pays for protection against a defined credit event; its economic effects depend on the contract and the ability of the protection seller to perform. It is not itself a loan to the homeowner. The distinction matters because a security might be held by investors far from the underlying houses, while derivatives created financial obligations among firms that had never met the borrowers. Risk became both dispersed and interconnected.

Financial firms also borrowed to enlarge their investments. Leverage can increase returns when asset prices rise, but it magnifies losses when prices fall. Some institutions funded long-lived mortgage securities with short-term borrowing. If lenders refused to roll over that funding, a firm might need to sell assets quickly even if not all of its holdings had yet defaulted. Selling into a falling market could depress prices further and damage other institutions holding similar securities. A bank run, familiar as customers queuing to withdraw deposits, had a parallel in wholesale markets when institutional lenders and investors withdrew short-term funds. The machinery was technical, but the underlying fear was recognizable: each lender wanted cash before others demanded theirs.

## The first cracks

By two thousand and seven, difficulties in the United States subprime mortgage market were becoming visible. Some mortgage lenders failed, and investors began questioning the prices and safety of mortgage-backed securities. Institutions faced losses not just because individual households missed payments but because the market for complex assets became less reliable. If a bank could not confidently value what it owned or what another bank might be hiding, lending between institutions became harder. The reduction in trust tightened credit for firms and households that had nothing to do with high-risk mortgages. The financial crisis was moving outward through funding relationships, and it was doing so before the collapse of the best-known Wall Street firms.

In Britain, Northern Rock became a public example of the new vulnerability. The mortgage lender depended heavily on wholesale funding rather than relying only on ordinary retail deposits. As those funding markets tightened, it sought emergency support from the Bank of England. News of the assistance in September two thousand and seven led depositors to queue outside branches. The British government intervened and later took the institution into public ownership. The episode showed that trouble originating in United States housing finance could affect a British bank through the funding system. It also demonstrated that the old image of a queue at the bank had not disappeared. Modern short-term finance and retail confidence could fail together.

In March two thousand and eight, Bear Stearns, a major American investment bank, faced acute funding problems and was acquired by JPMorgan Chase with government assistance. During the summer, stress deepened around mortgage markets and institutions holding or guaranteeing housing-related debt. In September, the United States government placed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, two large housing-finance enterprises, into conservatorship. The authorities were trying to prevent further disruption to mortgage finance, but doing so did not settle the question of how other financial companies would be treated. Market participants were not observing a fixed rule for rescue versus bankruptcy. They were watching officials decide under severe uncertainty, sometimes with very little time to negotiate alternatives.

## September

On September fifteenth, two thousand and eight, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. The event became a marker of crisis because it disrupted expectations about the survival of large financial firms and the safety of their obligations. But the panic did not begin that morning, and Lehman was not the only institution in distress. Within days, the Federal Reserve provided extraordinary support to the insurer AIG, while a large money-market mutual fund announced that its share value had fallen below the customary one-dollar level. Investors who had considered money-market funds near substitutes for cash confronted the possibility of losses. Funding stress reached businesses that relied on short-term commercial paper to pay routine expenses. Events in specialized markets now threatened the ability of ordinary companies to finance payrolls and inventories.

The crisis exposed a problem sometimes called interconnectedness. One firm's assets could be another firm's collateral; one firm's insurance-like contract could be another firm's expected protection; one bank's funding could be another investor's apparently safe cash investment. When confidence broke, attempts to reduce risk individually could make the system less stable. Selling assets lowered prices. Withdrawing funding forced more sales. Hoarding cash made it harder for firms to meet obligations. The process helps explain why authorities feared failures could spread beyond the institutions that had made the original mortgage bets. It also complicates moral judgment. Preventing a cascade might require aid to institutions whose managers had taken excessive risks, even while families harmed by those risks received slower and more limited support.

## The state steps into the market

In October two thousand and eight, the United States Congress enacted the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, authorizing the Troubled Asset Relief Program, commonly called TARP, with authority initially set at seven hundred billion dollars. Public discussion often imagined a fund mainly buying impaired mortgage securities. In practice, an early major use was to inject capital into financial institutions. The authorization was a ceiling for the program, not a statement that the entire amount was immediately spent or permanently lost. That distinction mattered for the politics of rescue, but it did not remove the legitimate questions about who qualified for aid, what conditions were attached, and how public authorities would recover funds or limit future risk.

Central banks acted on the shortage of dollars outside the United States. The Federal Reserve expanded arrangements that allowed certain foreign central banks to obtain dollars and provide liquidity to banks in their jurisdictions. In October, announcements covered European and Japanese partners and later Brazil, Mexico, South Korea, and Singapore. This was not simply financial diplomacy among governments. It addressed a practical problem: firms around the world had borrowed or conducted trade in dollars and needed access to them when ordinary funding markets ceased functioning. A shortage of dollar liquidity could affect a business operating far from the place where the mortgage crisis began. The response demonstrated that the global financial system had a central currency and that its breakdown required cooperation among monetary authorities.

Governments made different choices about banks. Some guaranteed deposits or debts. Some arranged acquisitions. Some nationalized troubled institutions or injected public capital. Each approach attempted to contain a fast-moving problem while raising questions about public cost and private responsibility. Iceland's banking troubles, for example, differed from the mortgage-centered origins of the American crisis, while European and British institutions faced their own exposure to wholesale funding and falling asset prices. It is misleading to imagine that every national banking crisis was the same event repeated. What connected them was a dense web of credit, asset prices, currency funding, and confidence. National authorities remained responsible to their own citizens even as the system required action across borders.

## The recession arrives at work

For households the crisis did not arrive as a diagram of securities. It arrived as a foreclosure notice, a canceled construction contract, a job loss, a reduced pension balance, or a rejected small-business loan. In the United States the recession officially ran from December two thousand and seven to June two thousand and nine, although recovery was slow for many families. Unemployment rose sharply and reached ten percent in October two thousand and nine. The date that economists call the end of a recession means output has stopped contracting by a particular measure. It does not mean workers are rehired, debts have been paid, or neighborhoods have returned to their previous conditions. Different households encountered the same recession with different savings, legal protection, and access to family support.

Mortgage distress also redistributed the costs of falling house prices. A borrower who had accumulated little equity could owe more than the house could be sold for. A family living next door to foreclosed properties might suffer declining neighborhood values or public services even without missing a payment. Renters could be displaced if a landlord lost a building. Local governments relying on property taxes faced revenue pressures at the same time that residents needed help. The crisis was therefore not a private matter confined to mortgage contracts. Its consequences flowed through streets, schools, municipal budgets, and the labor market. That spread helps explain why statistics on bank solvency, while essential, cannot stand in for a social history of the downturn.

The downturn reached other regions through trade, investment, credit, and changing commodity prices. An export factory might lose orders because consumers overseas were spending less. A bank that relied on international funds might tighten lending even when local borrowers had no exposure to United States housing. Governments with more fiscal and monetary space could respond differently from governments facing severe debts or foreign-currency constraints. The crisis was global, but its paths were not uniform. Not every country had a housing bubble or the same banking model. What was shared was the way production and finance depended on confidence and demand elsewhere. A shock originating in one set of markets could be transmitted along chains of trade and finance assembled over decades.

## Who decides what caused the crisis?

In the United States, the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission investigated the disaster and published a report in two thousand and eleven. The majority concluded that the crisis was avoidable, emphasizing regulatory failures, poor corporate governance, excessive borrowing and risk, and failures of accountability. Commissioners also published dissents with different emphases. That disagreement is important evidence about the arguments being made after the event; the majority report is not a substitute for reading every participant's evidence. Historians and economists have continued to debate the relative roles of financial innovation, housing policy, global savings, credit-rating agencies, regulatory choices, and market expectations. But disagreement over proportions should not obscure mechanisms that can be documented: fragile funding, falling mortgage values, and contracts that connected institutions across borders.

Another debate concerns rescue and fairness. If the state provides emergency finance to a bank, it may protect deposits and jobs while also appearing to shield investors and managers from their choices. If the state refuses, a failure may impose costs on innocent people who had no say in the firm's risk taking. Rescue programs were therefore decisions under conflict, not solutions with no losers. Some measures stabilized markets, while household recovery and assistance to borrowers proved more difficult. The speed with which governments could mobilize liquidity for large institutions became part of a lasting public argument over whose claims were urgent. A good account must explain both the danger policymakers were trying to prevent and the uneven distribution of the protection they offered.

## A system, not an accident

By the middle of two thousand and nine, some immediate financial panic had subsided, although the consequences of the recession continued for years. Governments introduced or debated new rules concerning bank capital, consumer protection, derivatives, supervision, and resolution of large institutions. Those responses were informed by competing interpretations of how the crisis had arisen. They would be judged not only by how quickly banks regained access to markets, but by whether they reduced the chances of another systemwide collapse. Prevention means asking whether institutions can withstand falling asset prices without needing every lender to remain confident at once. It also means asking how losses are recognized and who is allowed to shift them to others.

The crisis of two thousand and seven through two thousand and nine reveals a paradox of modern finance. Credit makes distant cooperation possible. Household savings can help fund construction; an investor can support a business across an ocean; a bank can transfer risk rather than carry every exposure alone. Yet the same connections can hide responsibility and transmit distress. The mortgages, securities, short-term loans, and swap lines were not merely technical details. They were the mechanism by which decisions about houses reached pension funds, foreign banks, factories, and public budgets. To understand why a family lost a job during a financial panic, we have to follow the promises made far from that family's workplace—and ask what happened when too many of those promises could no longer be kept.
