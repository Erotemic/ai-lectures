# The Square, the Peninsula, and the Front Line: Ukraine, 2013–2015

## A decision made in November

In November two thousand and thirteen, Ukraine's government suspended preparations to sign an association agreement with the European Union. The decision followed years of argument about Ukraine's economic relations, constitutional direction, and place between powerful neighbors. For some Ukrainians, the agreement promised legal reforms and closer ties to European institutions. For others it raised worries about industry, trade with Russia, or whether distant politicians understood local livelihoods. President Viktor Yanukovych's government turned instead toward closer economic discussions with Russia. Demonstrators gathered in Kyiv's Independence Square, commonly called the Maidan, to protest the reversal. They did not arrive with one perfectly shared program for the country's future.

Ukraine had been independent since the Soviet Union dissolved in nineteen ninety-one. Its politics included regional and linguistic differences, but those categories were not neat maps of allegiance. Speaking Russian did not automatically mean wanting rule from Moscow, just as speaking Ukrainian did not define every position on European integration. Households moved between languages; workplaces and families crossed regional identities. Ukrainian citizens argued over corruption, oligarchic influence, police conduct, elections, and relations with foreign governments. The country was not an empty board on which Russia and the European Union could move pieces. Ukrainians were political participants who disagreed among themselves about their own state.

What began as a protest about an agreement grew into a broader challenge after police violence against demonstrators. Students, civic groups, opposition parties, clergy, professionals, and people from many regions participated in different ways. Some wanted elections, some demanded that security officials be held accountable, and some sought sweeping reform of a political system they considered corrupt. Organized nationalist groups were present, as were protesters whose outlook was liberal, conservative, or simply anti-corruption. Their presence must be neither erased nor inflated into a claim that every person on the square shared their politics. The Maidan was a contested coalition held together partly by opposition to state violence.

## February and the collapse of authority

Confrontations intensified during January and February two thousand and fourteen. Protesters built barricades; police and other security forces used force; violence also came from armed participants in the unrest. During the most deadly days of February, large numbers of people were killed in Kyiv. Human-rights investigators later worked to document deaths, identify perpetrators, and distinguish verified cases from rumor and political accusation. The killings were a central turning point because they shattered confidence that the confrontation could be managed as an ordinary dispute between demonstrators and the government. But counting those killed does not by itself tell us who ordered each act or settle every unresolved investigation.

On February twenty-first, Yanukovych and opposition leaders signed a European-mediated agreement proposing steps toward a political settlement and earlier elections. It quickly broke down. Yanukovych left Kyiv and then Ukraine. On February twenty-second, parliament voted to remove him from office and called an early presidential election. The constitutional legitimacy of that procedure became a matter of fierce political argument. It was not the completion of the normal impeachment process, and saying so is necessary for an accurate description. But the disputed procedure also cannot be understood without the president's departure, the deaths in the streets, and the failure of the negotiated arrangement. Two institutions were being tested at once: the authority of an elected president and the capacity of a parliament to respond when executive power collapsed.

Supporters of the new authorities described a popular revolution against repression. Opponents called it an unlawful seizure of power. Russia's leadership amplified the latter argument, while many other governments recognized the interim authorities and supported new elections. Neither a slogan about revolution nor a slogan about a coup tells the whole story of the events in Kyiv. There were genuine mass protests, deadly state violence, a failed negotiated agreement, and a parliamentary transfer of authority with contested constitutional foundations. Historians should describe those events before arguing about how they fit a broader label.

## Crimea under armed control

While Kyiv was reorganizing its government, armed men without clear insignia took control of key sites in Crimea in late February. These forces were subsequently identified as Russian military personnel. Under their control, Crimea's political authorities moved toward separation from Ukraine and union with Russia. A referendum was held in March. Russia cited the vote as legitimizing accession; Ukraine and most of the international community rejected the change as having been imposed under coercive conditions and in violation of Ukraine's territorial integrity. The Russian government formally incorporated Crimea in March two thousand and fourteen. This was an annexation, not merely a disagreement about how to interpret a survey of public opinion.

On March twenty-seventh, the United Nations General Assembly affirmed Ukraine's sovereignty and internationally recognized borders and stated that the Crimean referendum could not provide the basis for changing the peninsula's status. The resolution was not a military intervention and did not reverse Russia's control. It established a clear public position on recognition. There is a difference between effective control of a territory and lawful title to it. States can possess and administer land while their claims of sovereignty remain unrecognized by other governments. That distinction shaped diplomacy, sanctions, and the everyday legal situation of people living in Crimea.

Crimea itself was not one voice. Its residents included ethnic Russians, ethnic Ukrainians, Crimean Tatars, and people with other backgrounds and political views. Crimean Tatars carried a history of mass Soviet deportation and later return to the peninsula. Some strongly opposed annexation and faced pressure on political and civic activities afterward. Treating the referendum as a complete record of local consent would ignore the circumstances of armed control, the absence of a normal internationally recognized process, and the voices of those who dissented. The political rights of residents mattered independently of which government claimed to represent them.

## The war begins in the east

Events in eastern Ukraine developed differently. In Donetsk and Luhansk, groups opposed to the new authorities seized government buildings and demanded changes ranging from federalization to separation. Some residents supported these demands; others rejected them or wanted reform without secession. In April the Ukrainian government launched an operation to regain control of territory and facilities. Armed conflict escalated. Russian support for separatist forces, including weapons and personnel, became a central part of the war, while Ukrainian government forces and volunteer formations fought on the other side. The forces and allegiances involved changed over time, so the word local cannot explain away outside involvement and the word foreign cannot erase local political choices.

An OSCE monitoring team in April recorded occupied buildings, checkpoints, conflicting local claims, and fear in cities that had not yet become familiar images of war. Such reporting is valuable precisely because it does not reduce every scene to a retrospective explanation. An observer at a checkpoint can report flags, weapons, statements, and people encountered; an observer cannot infer from one encounter what an entire region wants. Local politics were not simply a binary choice between two flags. People worried about policing, employment, language policy, and whether their town would become a battlefield. Those concerns could be exploited by competing armed actors.

The conflict destroyed ordinary certainties. A road used for commuting could become an armed checkpoint; a school might close because of shelling; relatives on opposite sides of a contact line could lose the ability to visit. Fighting damaged electricity, water systems, housing, and hospitals. People fled to other parts of Ukraine or across borders. Some remained because they could not travel, needed to care for relatives, or feared leaving their homes unattended. Civilian harm was not confined to the moment a shell landed. It continued through lost pensions, interrupted treatment, disrupted transport, and the difficulty of proving identity or residence to an institution on the other side.

## Voting while fighting

Ukraine held an early presidential election on May twenty-fifth, two thousand and fourteen. International observers found that it offered a genuine opportunity for voters to choose, despite significant problems and violence in parts of the east. An election does not become meaningless because some areas are affected by armed intimidation; nor does a generally positive observation report imply that every citizen could vote freely. Election monitoring requires attention to both the national procedure and the places where participation was obstructed. The resulting presidency had an electoral mandate, but it inherited a state facing annexation, armed rebellion, and urgent questions about security and constitutional reform.

Other events compounded the rupture. In May, deadly clashes and a fire in Odesa took dozens of lives, exposing the danger of political violence far from the main front line. In July, Malaysia Airlines Flight seventeen was destroyed over eastern Ukraine, killing all two hundred and ninety-eight people aboard. International investigations followed. Those deaths made the conflict visible to families far beyond Ukraine. The existence of a crash and its human cost are established facts; responsibility for the weapon and chain of command required separate forensic and legal investigation. A history of the year should not replace that process with rumor or conceal how deeply the tragedy affected those who had no role in the fighting.

## Ceasefires and the problem of enforcement

In September two thousand and fourteen, representatives of Ukraine, Russia, and the OSCE, together with representatives of separatist-held areas, agreed to a Minsk protocol seeking to stop the fighting and begin political and security measures. Combat continued and lines of control shifted. In February two thousand and fifteen, a further package of measures was negotiated in Minsk. It addressed a ceasefire, withdrawal of heavy weapons, monitoring, elections, border arrangements, and constitutional questions. The provisions were interdependent, but the parties did not agree about the sequence or interpretation of several obligations. A written ceasefire is a political commitment; on the ground it must be made real by armed people who have reasons, orders, and capabilities to keep fighting.

The OSCE Special Monitoring Mission worked to observe and report on security conditions, access restrictions, and civilian consequences. Monitors were unarmed and could not compel the parties to comply. Their reports documented incidents under the limits of what they could reach and verify. That limitation should not make their reporting irrelevant; it should make readers careful. Governments and armed groups routinely used accusations against one another, but a careful description distinguishes claims by a party from observations made by an outside mission. The task is not to split the difference between incompatible assertions. It is to seek the strongest evidence for each particular event and say where the record remains incomplete.

By late two thousand and fifteen, the United Nations had recorded thousands of deaths in the eastern conflict, among civilians, Ukrainian government forces, and members of armed groups. Human-rights monitors documented unlawful detention, torture allegations, movement restrictions, and challenges to access to water, medical care, and social benefits. Their findings were not a license to erase the difference between the invasion and annexation of Crimea and abuses committed by different actors in the eastern war. Several wrongs can be documented without making their causes or legal status identical. The experience of people living near the contact line deserves its own history, not merely a place in diplomatic timelines.

## Who is represented by a map?

One story of this period emphasizes Russian aggression and Ukraine's right to decide its international alignments. Another focuses on the political failures of Ukraine's elites, competing protest constituencies, and regional anxieties about changes in Kyiv. These lines of inquiry can both contain evidence, but they do not cancel the central facts of Russian military seizure of Crimea and support for armed separatists in the Donbas. Understanding the anxieties voiced by eastern residents does not establish that outside military intervention was lawful. Understanding aggression does not require pretending every Ukrainian held the same views. Responsible explanation can retain national sovereignty and plural local politics in the same account.

For people who lived through the winter demonstrations, the question was whether their state could become more accountable. For those forced from Crimea or Donbas, it also became whether they could safely go home. For families divided by language, geography, or political loyalty, the war entered ordinary relationships. The years two thousand and thirteen to two thousand and fifteen cannot be understood only as the prelude to later events. They were already a period of revolution, annexation, war, displacement, and contested efforts to restore a political order. The maps drawn at international conferences mattered, but so did the paths between a household, its workplace, and the people it could no longer reach.
