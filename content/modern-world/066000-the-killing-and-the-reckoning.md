# The Killing and the Reckoning: Yugoslavia and Rwanda in the 1990s

## Two histories, not one explanation

The end of the Cold War encouraged the idea that the world had entered a period when great-power rivalry would no longer dominate politics. Within a few years, families were fleeing ruined towns in the former Yugoslavia, and in Rwanda a government-directed campaign of genocide was killing people in their homes, churches, and places of refuge. These catastrophes are sometimes set beside one another as evidence of a sudden return of ancient ethnic hatred. That phrase explains little. It makes political decisions sound like an uncontrollable force inherited from the distant past. Yugoslavia's breakup and the genocide against the Tutsi in Rwanda had different institutions, timelines, wars, and perpetrators. To understand them together is to ask how leaders organized violence, how institutions failed to protect civilians, and what people later did to establish facts and responsibility.

The Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia was a state with six republics: Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, and Macedonia. Within Serbia, Kosovo and Vojvodina had autonomous provincial status. The federation held together different languages, religions, national identities, and regional histories. It had also built workplaces, cities, military institutions, and families in which people lived across those lines. By the late nineteen eighties, economic crisis and disputes over the distribution of authority made the federation increasingly fragile. Political leaders claimed to speak for national communities, sometimes representing legitimate fears and sometimes deliberately heightening those fears to build power. No map of ethnic groups can by itself explain why one neighbor came to fear another. One must ask who controlled the police, army, media, property, and political offices.

Slobodan Milošević rose through Serbian communist politics and used nationalist mobilization as one source of authority. Other republican leaders advanced their own competing claims about sovereignty and the federation's future. Some citizens wanted a looser association; others wanted independence; still others feared that an independent republic might leave them a vulnerable minority. The language of national rights became tied to control of territory. If officials insisted that a community could only be safe where it held exclusive political power, homes inhabited by people of different identities became obstacles. That was a political proposition, not an unavoidable conclusion drawn from a person's name or religion. The transition from rivalry to violence required decisions, weapons, and institutions willing to enforce exclusion.

## The wars of Yugoslav dissolution

Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in June nineteen ninety-one. The Yugoslav People's Army intervened in Slovenia, but the fighting there was brief. In Croatia, disputes over the status and security of ethnic Serb communities expanded into a longer war. Armed formations, supported in varying ways by state and military networks, drove people from homes and seized territory. Croat and other non-Serb civilians faced persecution and expulsion in Serb-held areas. In nineteen ninety-five Croatian offensives restored much territory to Zagreb's control, and large numbers of Serb civilians fled. Violence and expulsion were not unique to one community, but acknowledging that fact does not make every political leadership equally responsible for each campaign. Identifying the forces, decisions, locations, and victims matters more than balancing atrocities in an abstract moral ledger.

Bosnia and Herzegovina was especially vulnerable to a territorial logic of ethnic exclusivity because its communities were closely interwoven. An independence referendum in nineteen ninety-two was boycotted by many Bosnian Serbs; the republic declared independence, and war followed. Bosnian Serb forces, backed by forces and resources linked to Serbia and the former Yugoslav army, seized large areas and undertook systematic persecution of non-Serbs. Bosnian Croat forces and the Bosnian government also fought, and crimes were committed by members of more than one side. But the civilian experience was not a symmetrical contest in which everyone possessed the same means of violence. In many places, the ability to control roads, heavy weapons, detention sites, and local government determined who could flee and who could be forced out.

The siege of Sarajevo became a symbol of the war, but it was also a daily routine of danger for the city's inhabitants. Civilians attempted to obtain food, water, fuel, and medical treatment amid shelling and sniper fire. Schools, hospitals, and apartment buildings were not distant from the fighting; they were part of its geography. In other parts of Bosnia, prisoners were held and mistreated in detention centers, and sexual violence was used against civilians. The language of ethnic cleansing described policies aimed at making territory homogeneous by intimidation, killing, rape, detention, and expulsion. It should not be used as a euphemism that makes those acts sound like a change in population statistics. It meant people being forced from their neighborhoods and sometimes permanently separated from their families.

For someone displaced from a village, the war was not only the loss of a house. It could mean losing the documents needed to claim property, the neighbors who knew a family's history, work routines, access to graves, and the confidence that return would be safe. Refugee camps and temporary accommodation offered shelter but could also prolong uncertainty. Later peace agreements could define new borders without making the former inhabitants able to go back. Survivor and family testimony held by the Srebrenica Memorial Center helps historians ask about the lives behind military maps. The existence of an archive does not mean that we have listened to every interview. Its significance here is methodological: people who endured these events are essential witnesses, not merely anonymous evidence for the claims of armies.

## Srebrenica and a promise that failed

In nineteen ninety-three, the United Nations declared Srebrenica in eastern Bosnia a safe area. Thousands of Bosniak civilians had sought refuge there. The designation was supposed to offer protection, but it did not provide the capacity or political will necessary to defend the enclave against a determined assault. In July nineteen ninety-five, Bosnian Serb forces under Ratko Mladić captured Srebrenica. Men and boys were separated from women, children, and older residents. Thousands of Bosniak men and boys were systematically murdered; many bodies were later moved into secondary graves in attempts to conceal the crime. Women and children were forcibly transferred. International courts subsequently determined that the killings at Srebrenica constituted genocide. That is a legal finding grounded in evidence about a specific campaign and intent, not a generic label for all wartime killing.

Families of the missing faced a second history after the violence: the search for bodies, identification of remains, recovery of names, and effort to establish what happened at each location. Evidence came from survivors, intercepted or preserved records, excavations, forensic analysis, and judicial investigations. Some relatives received confirmation years later; others remained uncertain about missing family members. Tracing the process of destruction counters a form of denial that tries to turn murdered people into a dispute over whether an event occurred at all. A memorial and a court do different work. A memorial keeps human lives and loss visible; a court examines responsibility under legal standards. Both depend on records, and neither can reverse a death.

By late nineteen ninety-five, changes on the battlefield, NATO air strikes, and intense diplomacy contributed to negotiations. The Dayton peace agreement, negotiated in the United States and signed in Paris in December, ended the Bosnian war. It preserved Bosnia and Herzegovina as a state while establishing a complicated constitutional arrangement involving two entities and shared institutions. The settlement reduced large-scale fighting, a vital achievement for people in danger. It also reflected the territorial and political realities produced by the war, leaving difficult problems of return, accountability, and political cooperation. A peace treaty can stop armies without giving everyone back the society they had before the fighting.

## Rwanda before April nineteen ninety-four

Now consider Rwanda, where the immediate mechanism of mass killing was different. Rwanda was not a Yugoslav-style federation breaking apart. It was a small country with a history shaped by monarchy, colonial administration, post-independence discrimination, population displacement, and political struggle over the state. German and then Belgian colonial authorities governed through and sharpened classifications of Hutu and Tutsi, including identification documents. Those categories had social histories before colonial rule, but colonial policy transformed them into rigid administrative distinctions. After independence in nineteen sixty-two, governments led by Hutu politicians discriminated against Tutsi people, and episodes of violence forced many into exile. The labels did not make genocide inevitable. They created vulnerabilities that later extremists could exploit.

In nineteen ninety, the Rwandan Patriotic Front, an armed movement with many members from Tutsi exile communities, entered Rwanda from Uganda. A civil war began. President Juvénal Habyarimana's government confronted military pressure and internal opposition, while extremist politicians and militias portrayed Tutsi civilians as enemies allied with the rebels. Propaganda helped make an entire category of neighbors appear dangerous. Local administrative networks, political parties, and armed groups developed plans and means for violence. The Arusha agreement of August nineteen ninety-three attempted to create a power-sharing transition. Some people saw it as a possible route away from war; extremists treated it as a threat to their hold on office. Peace talks and preparations for atrocity could therefore proceed at the same time.

There were warnings before the mass killing. In January nineteen ninety-four, the commander of the United Nations Assistance Mission for Rwanda, Roméo Dallaire, sent a cable reporting information about militia training and an anti-Tutsi extermination plan. Subsequent analysis has made that warning an emblem of missed prevention. The existence of such a report is not proof that every foreign diplomat understood the full scale or timetable of what would follow. It is evidence that potentially grave information reached international institutions. What authorities chose to investigate, how they assessed the risk, and what they authorized peacekeepers to do were political decisions with consequences for people on the ground.

On April sixth, nineteen ninety-four, an aircraft carrying Habyarimana and the president of Burundi was shot down near Kigali. Responsibility for firing the missile remains disputed. The assassination provided the immediate trigger for violence, but it did not create the apparatus of genocide overnight. Extremist leaders and their supporters moved rapidly to kill political opponents, including Hutu politicians who might resist them, and to target Tutsi civilians. Roadblocks, local officials, militia groups, soldiers, and broadcasts were used to identify, isolate, and attack people. The genocide against the Tutsi was a project of destruction directed at that population, accompanied by political killings of others. Describing all the deaths as undifferentiated ethnic fighting would erase its organization and intent.

## How a society was made into a killing ground

During roughly one hundred days, hundreds of thousands of people were murdered. Estimates differ according to methods and records, so one apparently exact number is not more authoritative simply because it has been repeated often. Much of the killing was carried out in places where people knew one another. Churches and schools, which some civilians had hoped would provide refuge, became massacre sites. Many attackers were organized, armed, or directed by officials and militias; others were pressured or recruited locally. Some people sheltered those at risk, refused orders, or helped them escape, often at enormous danger to themselves. Explaining the machinery of killing must not collapse every Hutu person into a perpetrator, nor obscure the targeting of Tutsi people as a group.

Alison Des Forges and the investigators who produced the Human Rights Watch study Leave None to Tell the Story documented how authorities, militia leaders, and local administrative structures were used to direct the genocide. The report matters not because it gives history one final voice, but because it connects large claims about intention to identifiable decisions, records, and testimonies. Killings could be encouraged through radio and meetings, but threats had to be enacted by people with weapons, authority, or control over roads. That makes the responsibility of organizers and participants historically intelligible. Mass murder does not require a population to share one timeless hatred. It can be generated by institutions and individuals exploiting fear, loyalty, obedience, and the threat of punishment.

The international response failed at crucial moments. United Nations peacekeepers had been deployed to support implementation of the Arusha agreement, not equipped to defeat a campaign of genocide by force. After the killing began and Belgian peacekeepers were murdered, Belgium withdrew its contingent. In April the Security Council reduced the mission rather than immediately reinforcing it. Individual peacekeepers nonetheless protected thousands of people at some sites. In May the Council authorized an expansion, but promised troops were slow to arrive. These details undermine both easy claims that the United Nations did nothing and equally easy claims that a protective international system was functioning adequately. Some people were saved by particular actions; many others were abandoned by decisions made far away.

The Rwandan Patriotic Front continued its military offensive and captured Kigali, with its victory ending the genocidal government's power by July. Enormous numbers of people fled, including civilians afraid of retaliation and leaders or participants in the genocide who escaped across borders. Refugee camps in neighboring states became sites of humanitarian suffering and political struggle. The RPF's defeat of the genocidal authorities should be stated plainly; it does not mean the new rulers were beyond scrutiny for their own abuses. Human Rights Watch and other investigators have documented violations committed during and after the war. Nor did the genocide stop affecting the region at Rwanda's border. Armed groups, refugee crises, and interventions contributed to further wars in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

## Courts, evidence, and the limits of a verdict

While the wars were still being fought, the United Nations Security Council established the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia in nineteen ninety-three. It established a separate tribunal for Rwanda in nineteen ninety-four. These courts were not general commissions empowered to rewrite every country's history. They prosecuted individuals accused of defined international crimes, using rules of evidence and legal standards. That difference is essential. Crimes against humanity, war crimes, and genocide are related but distinct categories. Genocide requires proof of a specific intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a protected group. Atrocities that do not meet that legal definition can still be grave crimes. Precision is not a retreat from moral judgment; it is what permits legal responsibility to attach to actual conduct.

One landmark came in nineteen ninety-eight when the Rwanda tribunal convicted Jean-Paul Akayesu, a former local official, of genocide and crimes against humanity. The judgment established that sexual violence could constitute an act of genocide when carried out with the required destructive intent. This finding mattered because rape had too often been treated as an inevitable, incidental feature of war rather than as a prosecutable means of persecution and destruction. The court's reasoning did not claim every rape was genocide; it linked acts to context, intent, and the accused's responsibility. Survivors' testimony, and the willingness of investigators and judges to treat it as evidence, changed how international criminal law could recognize harms that earlier legal histories had marginalized.

The Yugoslavia tribunal developed an extensive record of the crimes committed in Bosnia and elsewhere, including the Srebrenica genocide. Later judgments and the International Court of Justice's findings established important legal conclusions about the killings. Yet verdicts have limits. They do not automatically create political agreement among communities, compensate survivors, restore destroyed institutions, or compel acceptance by those invested in denial. A trial focuses on accused individuals and charges that can be proved; a society also has to confront propaganda, property loss, disrupted education, and the persistence of wartime identities in politics. Courts can secure crucial truths without being a substitute for historical inquiry or public reconciliation.

## The survivors' archive

The Srebrenica Memorial Center and the Genocide Archive of Rwanda preserve the voices and records of survivors, relatives, rescuers, and witnesses. Such collections make a demand on historians. A witness may remember the smell of a room, a particular road, the person who offered help, or the last ordinary morning before the violence. These are not decorative details to place between accounts of military strategy. They reveal what political violence destroys and how people survive it. But an archival catalogue is not an interview one has listened to, and a survivor's experience of a place should not be generalized automatically into the experience of a whole country. Historical care requires both respect for testimony and clarity about what has actually been examined.

The two histories also resist a single geopolitical lesson. The Yugoslav wars unfolded amid the collapse of a multinational federation, nationalist state-making, military competition, and the struggle over sovereignty. The genocide in Rwanda was organized through a state and allied militias determined to destroy the Tutsi population amid a civil war and a threatened power-sharing settlement. International actors made serious failures in both, but not through precisely the same chain of decisions. In Bosnia, peace required agreements among armed factions and outside powers, while legal institutions later prosecuted specific crimes. In Rwanda, military defeat ended the genocidal regime, after an international protection failure, and courts developed another record of individual criminal responsibility. The differences are part of the explanation.

What, then, can be learned from the first great wars of the post-Cold War decade? That peace does not follow automatically when a superpower confrontation ends. That people who have lived together can be made into political targets, not by an ancient force awakening, but through institutions, propaganda, armed coercion, and choices by leaders and neighbors. That international promises require capacity and a willingness to protect those at risk. And that ending the fighting does not end the work: identifying the missing, documenting crimes, returning property, prosecuting individuals, and making coexistence possible. A responsible history does not offer survivor suffering as proof of a slogan. It tries to name what happened, distinguish causes, and preserve the humanity of the people whom those causes placed in danger.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Official state, court, UN and medical agency materials establish specific decisions and records, not the entire lived history or intent behind them. Archival collection descriptions and article abstracts are labeled and not passed off as complete interviews or full-text scholarship. Where casualties or responsibility remain disputed, the lecture distinguishes verified events from uncertainty. Expand with Baltic and Polish oral histories, Uganda and Brazil community records, South African local organizing files, and preserved Bosnian and Rwandan survivor testimony before claiming comprehensive regional coverage. Lecture length is dictated by the material, not a word or time limit.
