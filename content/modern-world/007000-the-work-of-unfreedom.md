# The Work of Unfreedom: Captivity, Debt, Drafts, and Slavery in an Interconnected World

Here is a historical puzzle. In one place a person can be compelled to leave home and work for an assigned period, yet remain legally attached to a community and entitled, at least in principle, to payment. In another, a captive may be purchased and sold, may have children whose status is inherited, and may be denied any recognized right to depart. Elsewhere a person works to repay a debt that cannot easily be discharged, while the creditor claims that the arrangement is voluntary. In a fourth setting, a household describes its servants as dependents or members of a family, though it also claims the power to command their labor.

These are not four descriptions of the same legal institution. But neither are they four unrelated stories. They can exist along the same trade routes, within the same empire, even within the same working landscape. The early modern world was connected by ships carrying silver, cloth, sugar, spices, and grain. It was connected just as consequentially by systems for taking, buying, compelling, inheriting, and controlling labor.

It is tempting to replace that complexity with a single word: slavery. The word has genuine explanatory power. It can name the extreme domination under which a human being is treated as transferable property. But when applied carelessly to every coercive relationship it conceals distinctions that mattered to the people living under those systems. Conversely, differences in law must not be used to make violence disappear. A labor obligation could be technically temporary and still be ruinous. A person could be called a servant and be unable to leave.

To understand the period, I want to ask five concrete questions every time we encounter a labor regime. Who has the power to compel the work? What can they legitimately claim under local law or custom? Can the worker refuse, leave, marry, keep earnings, or protect children? Through what violence, debt, or political machinery is the arrangement maintained? And who benefits when its products reach a market far away?

## The problem of an apparently simple category

Historians disagree about exactly how to define slavery across different societies. Some emphasize ownership and sale. Others emphasize the violent removal of a person's recognized social belonging, the lasting control of their body and labor, or the power to transfer their status. Orlando Patterson's influential concept of social death highlights forms of natal alienation and dishonor, but it is an analytical argument, not a statement that enslaved people lacked family, solidarity, memory, or agency. They continually made social lives under conditions designed to restrict them.

We should therefore resist a false choice. Either every forced worker was exactly like a person on an eighteenth-century Caribbean sugar plantation, or every local system was so different that comparison becomes impossible. Both conclusions prevent inquiry. Legal categories matter. So do a coercer's practical powers. So do the options available to a person being coerced, including options that exist only because a community helps someone evade or resist the rules.

Look at the surviving records. A government writes a labor ordinance. A merchant enters a person into a cargo ledger. A judge records a petition. A priest reports abuse. A family preserves a story of an ancestor who escaped. These documents are not interchangeable views of the same reality. The account book may say what was paid for a person but almost nothing about who that person was. A law may describe rights that officials routinely violated. A petition may reveal a desperate negotiation but not an entire life. A useful history follows the difference between what institutions claimed and what human beings experienced.

## Captivity in African political economies

Begin in African societies before the expansion of Atlantic plantation demand. People were already subjected to enslavement through warfare, raiding, punishment, and political conflict. In some settings enslaved people worked in households; in others they were agricultural laborers, soldiers, administrators, or workers in commercial production. Some systems allowed forms of incorporation into households or paths toward emancipation; others maintained lasting divisions of status. Neither the place nor the century gives us one uniform rule.

Paul Lovejoy's history of African slavery offers a crucial corrective to two opposing caricatures. It is wrong to imagine that slavery was invented in Africa by Europeans who arrived in ships. It is equally wrong to imagine that an existing, unchanging African institution can explain the subsequent Atlantic trade. The scale of external demand and the organization of violent capture and marketing transformed political economies over time. African rulers, merchants, soldiers, communities, and captives did not share one interest. Some profited, some opposed participation, and some struggled to survive within arrangements they could not control.

This is not a story of an entire continent choosing slavery. Africa contained many states, polities, and communities with different institutions and different exposure to slave trading. In West Central Africa, the expansion of Atlantic exports cannot be understood without warfare and political changes within and around African kingdoms, and without the European and American buyers who sustained commercial demand. Historians reconstruct those connections from shipping records, local political traditions, missionary accounts, archaeology, and African-language and European-language sources, each with its own silences.

The violence of the Atlantic trade began long before a slave ship departed the coast. Capture disrupted families and communities. Marches to markets and ports could be deadly. Coastal trading involved brokers, credit, diplomacy, and coercion. The fact that some sellers and intermediaries were African does not shift responsibility away from the transatlantic commercial system or eliminate the agency of those who fought against enslavement. An account restricted to the ship itself misses much of the history.

## Counting the Atlantic, without reducing people to counts

The SlaveVoyages database estimates that more than twelve million enslaved Africans were embarked on transatlantic voyages over the whole period it studies, from 1501 through 1866. Its table estimates roughly twelve and a half million embarkations. That is not a census made at the time. It is a reconstruction built from documented voyages and modeled gaps, and its totals can be revised as the evidence changes. It is also not a count of all people enslaved in Africa or of all deaths in capture, transport to the coast, or after arrival.

The difference between people embarked and people disembarked matters. The Middle Passage killed enormous numbers of people. Yet mortality at sea must not become the only measure of violence: sale, forced labor, separation, sexual abuse, and the coercive creation of a hereditary condition continued on land. The statistics show scale; they cannot tell us by themselves how an individual negotiated a language they did not understand, cared for a child, organized resistance, or remembered home.

The trade changed over centuries. Portuguese and Brazilian routes to the South Atlantic were central, especially links to West Central Africa; Dutch, British, French, and other networks gained prominence at different times and in different regions. Demand from sugar, tobacco, mining, and other plantation and urban economies contributed to these movements. It is inaccurate to picture a single, stationary triangular trade with every ship following one identical path. The Atlantic was a system of overlapping routes, contracts, state claims, and local decisions.

## A labor draft in the Andes

Now change the scene to the Andes. There were established traditions of collective obligations and labor service under Inka rule, although their organization changed across time and place. Spanish colonial authorities exploited and transformed these arrangements. Under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo's reforms in the 1570s, the colonial mining mita required specified Indigenous communities to supply workers for Potosí and other enterprises.

The word mita is not simply another spelling of plantation slavery. People subjected to the colonial draft were not thereby all defined as saleable property. They were drawn through an administrative machinery of assessed communities, rotational service, and wages fixed under unequal conditions. Yet the formal features of the institution do not erase the heavy physical demands, population dislocation, or danger of mining work. An obligation that arrives as a legal order is coercion even if a wage appears beside it in the record.

The history is more complex than a mine operating entirely through unpaid forced labor. Potosí also employed workers hired through markets, as well as people tied to varied kinds of dependency. Their conditions differed. A laborer might seek wages outside an assigned draft to obtain better payment; that possibility did not make the draft equitable. Some Indigenous authorities negotiated, petitioned, or attempted to redistribute burdens within their communities. Others were drawn into extracting them. Spanish demands for silver changed the meaning and reach of earlier institutions.

Research on free and unfree labor in colonial Andean society shows how misleading it would be to draw a hard boundary between a supposedly free market and an otherwise coercive system. The two operated together. Wage incentives, tribute obligations, local authority, compulsion, and the pressure of colonial taxation interacted. Remember this when a neat economic narrative says that early modern growth occurred because labor was becoming more freely mobile.

## Debt, service, and bondage around the Indian Ocean

Across the Indian Ocean world, networks of enslavement and other dependent labor existed long before Portuguese expansion. European merchants and states inserted themselves into arrangements that were already diverse, while also introducing new forms of extraction and trafficking. Recent research on the western coast of India traces connections among poverty, trafficking, caste relations, debt bondage, and the demand for service. The terms used by Portuguese officials did not always describe an entirely new institution; colonial government could appropriate preexisting obligations while changing who controlled them.

Consider a debtor who is required to work for a creditor. Debt can appear to supply an agreed contract, but how was the debt incurred? Can the person challenge the account? Do interest, recruitment fees, or advances make repayment practically impossible? Can a family member be held in someone else's place? Answers to those questions tell us more than the phrase 'debt labor.' A system may combine transactions that look contractual on paper with a practical inability to exit.

It is also important not to treat the Indian Ocean as a secondary Atlantic. Forms of domestic servitude, household enslavement, military service, commercial trafficking, and plantation labor had distinct histories across East Africa, South Asia, islands, and Southeast Asia. Richard B. Allen's research on the Mascarene Islands, particularly Mauritius and Réunion, demonstrates how late eighteenth-century regional slave trading linked Madagascar, Mozambique, and other places to French colonial demand. The region also connected to Atlantic commerce; the two oceans were not sealed historical worlds.

Here comparison requires restraint. The legal status of a person in an Indian Ocean household cannot be inferred from the language of an Atlantic plantation inventory. Nor should a historian presume that being inside a household meant safety, freedom, or the absence of sale. The practical powers of owners, creditors, officials, and kin networks must be reconstructed from the available evidence.

## Military and domestic power

Some early modern polities employed enslaved or dependent military servants and administrators whose careers could include authority and wealth. The Ottoman Empire's history of military recruitment, including the devshirme and the Janissary institution, reminds us that a person's legal dependence did not always correspond to low rank. But a privileged position granted within a coercive system did not make that system voluntary. We must distinguish the biographies of people who achieved influence from the mechanisms that enabled rulers to claim children, service, or loyalty.

Likewise, domestic service could place a dependent person within intimate family spaces without eliminating the domination embedded in ownership or servitude. Household intimacy and exploitation are not opposites. Some people negotiated manumission, property, or family relationships; others were exposed to sexual violence and severe limits on autonomy. General claims about an entire civilization's supposedly 'benign' or 'harsh' slavery rarely survive scrutiny of actual rights and practices.

## What resistance looks like when the law has already failed you

Resistance need not mean that a community launches a successful uprising. It includes running away, seeking asylum, negotiating manumission, evading recruitment, filing claims, preserving kinship ties, refusing particular forms of work, and passing knowledge between people whose speech authorities tried to control. None of these actions should be romanticized as automatically successful. They were responses to constraints that often remained in place.

The evidence is often stronger for attempts to suppress resistance than for the reasoning of those resisting. Administrators left orders about fugitives. Traders left complaints. Courts preserved disputes that reached them, not the countless acts that did not. Oral histories and community memory can recover perspectives absent from official paperwork, although they too require contextual interpretation rather than automatic acceptance of every detail.

We can now answer the opening puzzle. It is not possible to rank all forms of early modern labor on a simple ladder from free to unfree and read their consequences straight from the labels. What matters is the combination of legal status, social belonging, coercion, opportunity to exit, and the scale at which an institution operates. Some kinds of enslavement made people inheritable property and engineered lifelong subjection. Some labor drafts claimed a time limit but exposed communities to recurrent demands. Debt bondage could make a nominal promise of eventual freedom difficult to realize. These differences matter precisely because the violence mattered.

The modern world was not made solely by mobile goods and brilliant innovations. It was made by people whose movement was forced and whose work was claimed by others. And it was made by their families, allies, and descendants who contested those claims. If we want to understand where the wealth went, we must also reconstruct who paid its human cost.
