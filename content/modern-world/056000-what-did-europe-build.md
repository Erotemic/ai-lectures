# What Did Europe Build? Markets, Welfare, and the Postwar Settlement

## Two buildings, two promises

Imagine two doors in postwar Europe. Behind one is a public hospital. A person enters because they need medical treatment, not because they have saved enough money for the bill. Behind the other is a meeting room where governments negotiate the treatment of coal, steel and trade across national borders. The hospital concerns what a society owes people within its territory. The negotiating room concerns how states can limit conflict and organize production together. Both belong to the remaking of Europe after the Second World War. They are sometimes bundled into the phrase European social model, but they were not a single institution or even a single political program.

The distinction matters because prosperity, social protection and European integration did not advance at exactly the same speed. Britain's National Health Service opened in nineteen forty-eight, before the European Economic Community existed. Other countries had their own insurance traditions, municipal services, cooperative movements and labor politics. The European Coal and Steel Community became operational in the early nineteen fifties, and the Treaty of Rome established the European Economic Community in nineteen fifty-eight. A person could depend upon a national health insurance fund without knowing whether their government had signed a European treaty. Conversely, a factory could gain access to a larger market while the workers inside it still disputed wages and workplace safety.

The British example makes the social promise concrete. The National Health Service opened in July nineteen forty-eight to make treatment broadly available at the point of use. Creating it required far more than passing a law: hospitals, local practitioners, nurses, clinics, buildings and finance had to be assembled into a functioning system. The need for workers reached across the empire and Commonwealth. The NHS's own history records recruitment from the West Indies beginning soon afterward. That fact joins the story of public welfare to the story of migration. A system celebrated as a national achievement was sustained in part by people who had not been born in the nation it served.

What did universal care promise, and what did it not guarantee? Removing an immediate fee makes a difference to whether someone dares to seek treatment. It does not by itself ensure a nearby doctor, a safe workplace, housing without damp, or equal treatment for every patient. Women doing unpaid care in the home might still carry much of the burden of illness. Immigrant nurses could supply essential labor while encountering prejudice. A welfare state was not a machine that eliminated inequality once its doors opened. It was an arrangement under constant negotiation: who qualified for help, how it would be funded, and how recipients and workers would participate in deciding its standards.

## Coal, steel, and making war harder

Western European cooperation began with an economic problem that was also military. Coal powered industry; steel supplied railways, machinery and weapons. After two world wars, French and German leaders had reasons to fear what each other's factories could produce. The European Coal and Steel Community placed important parts of those industries under common rules. Its founding six members were France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg. The arrangement did not remove nationalism or guarantee peace in every circumstance. It made production and competition subject to shared institutions, so that preparing another war between central partners would be politically and economically more difficult.

In March nineteen fifty-seven, those six governments signed treaties in Rome creating the European Economic Community and Euratom, the atomic energy community. The economic treaty took effect in January nineteen fifty-eight. Its ambition exceeded coal and steel: governments sought a common market, reduced customs barriers, common rules and institutions through which commercial disputes might be addressed. Why would states agree to give up some freedom to act alone? Their leaders expected larger markets, coordinated rules and interdependence to support recovery and political stability. But integration also involved a bargain: countries that gained from expanded markets had to consider industries and regions that would face more competition.

The institutional detail can sound dry until we picture a lorry crossing a border. Customs officers can charge tariffs, apply quotas and delay shipments. A customs union aims to remove duties among members while maintaining a common external tariff. The original Community completed that step in July nineteen sixty-eight. This was not yet the fully developed single market of later decades; other barriers and national controls remained. The achievement mattered because it altered business decisions about where to make goods and sell them. It also created arguments about rules: if a border tax disappears, what happens to workers whose industry had relied on protection from foreign competition?

Economic integration did not settle the question of social rights, but the Treaty of Rome included a European Social Fund and an Investment Bank. The Fund was intended to improve job opportunities and workers' living standards. These were smaller tools than the comprehensive national welfare systems. At the same time, a different body, the Council of Europe, opened the European Social Charter for signature in nineteen sixty-one. The Council of Europe and the European Economic Community were not the same organization. The Charter recognized rights concerning work, collective bargaining, social security and assistance. Legal promises matter because they give people standards against which to challenge a government. But a declaration is not proof that each right was fully enforced.

## Compromises in the workplace and at home

West Germany's postwar economic revival supplied one powerful example of the period's trade-offs. Industry rebuilt, exports expanded, and employment grew. A U.S. diplomatic report from nineteen fifty-seven noted the relationship between that prosperity and the political stability of the West German government. But a report about aggregate growth cannot show the distribution of bargaining power inside a factory or household. The broader settlement involved private industry, public regulation, social insurance and organized bargaining in different combinations. Calling it a social market economy does not make every participant agree. Employers, trade unions, churches, political parties and governments disputed how much security and control workers should have.

A welfare settlement depends upon production and taxes, but it changes production too. An injured worker who can receive medical care or compensation is in a different bargaining position from one who must accept any conditions to survive. A family that can send a child to school or rely on a pension can make plans beyond the next harvest or paycheck. Yet social insurance historically often recognized continuous formal employment more readily than unpaid domestic labor, intermittent work or migrant work. Gender and migration are not optional decorations on this account. They help reveal who was visible to the institutions that promised protection, and who could be called upon to supply the care that those institutions failed to provide.

There was also political disagreement over who should make social decisions. Should hospitals be run by central ministries, municipalities, charities or professional associations? Should benefits be financed through general taxes, contributions tied to employment, or a mixture? Should unions negotiate with employers industry by industry or through national agreements? These are not merely technical alternatives. A contribution-based system may look fair to people whose work histories are steady, yet create gaps for people who cannot work full time. A national service may treat formal eligibility equally while remaining distant from rural communities. The postwar welfare states should be understood as many experiments in collective security, not one institution adopted uniformly across the continent.

## Food, farms, and the price of stability

The European Economic Community launched the Common Agricultural Policy in nineteen sixty-two. Its founding purposes included supporting farmers' livelihoods, stabilizing markets, improving production and ensuring food supplies at reasonable prices. For Europeans who remembered wartime shortages, dependable food carried political weight. But agriculture was more than a matter of enough calories. Millions of families depended upon land, livestock and rural businesses. A policy that protected farm earnings could preserve communities; it could also make consumers pay more, encourage production of surplus crops or advantage certain kinds of farms. Each outcome depended on its design and the differences among participating regions.

The shared farm policy revealed a practical tension within integration. A government could not easily create common trade rules for agricultural products while allowing every member to subsidize and price them in entirely different ways. Governments therefore developed common arrangements, including forms of price support. A higher guaranteed price might encourage a farmer to plant more or invest in equipment. That could serve the goal of reliable supply, but it could also create surpluses and costs. When historians discuss the success of the European project, they should ask whether the measure is total production, taxpayers' costs, rural employment, consumer prices or the environmental condition of the land. Those measurements answer different questions.

A useful comparison is between the farmer and the industrial worker. Both could claim to contribute to national prosperity. Both could face economic changes far beyond their control. But they often asked for different instruments of protection: a farmer might demand a viable selling price, while a worker might demand a secure wage, an insurance fund, or the ability to bargain collectively. Neither interest was the only public interest. Consumers needed affordable food; future generations would inherit the soil and water. The politics of the welfare state and the common market consisted partly of negotiating across these competing forms of security. It was not enough to announce that a policy served Europe; citizens wanted to know which Europe it served.

## The limits of the prosperity story

In nineteen seventy-three, Denmark, Ireland and the United Kingdom joined the European Communities, bringing membership to nine. Enlargement showed that the original six-member project had become attractive to other governments, but attraction did not imply agreement about its future. British political debate, Irish economic hopes and Danish attitudes toward farming and national regulation differed. The oil shock of nineteen seventy-three then exposed vulnerabilities in growth, prices and employment. In the middle of that upheaval, European institutions debated regional aid and the needs of poorer member regions. An economic community was being asked not only to remove barriers but to address the disparities that opening those barriers could reveal.

By the mid-nineteen seventies, European governments confronted inflation, energy costs and industrial restructuring. Industries built for an earlier mix of fuel prices and trade rules could no longer assume steady expansion. Social programs did not vanish, but arguments about how to finance and redesign them grew sharper. Who would carry the cost of adjustment: employers, taxpayers, public employees, pensioners or workers whose factories closed? The Community developed regional instruments intended to assist poorer areas. Those instruments demonstrated a growing recognition that the benefits of a common market were uneven. They could not independently solve every local problem, and their significance should be measured against conditions on the ground.

Political inclusion raised another question. In nineteen seventy-nine, people in member states voted directly for representatives to the European Parliament for the first time. Previously, the assembly's representatives had been drawn from national parliaments. The change made a European-level democratic connection more visible, although institutional authority remained divided among the Commission, Council, courts and national governments. Having an elected assembly did not mean that every important decision was taken by direct public vote. Neither did it make the institution meaningless. It altered who could claim a mandate to speak in the name of European voters, while leaving open the difficult question of how shared authority should be made accountable.

Europe was not entirely free or democratic through this period. Portugal remained under dictatorship until the nineteen seventy-four revolution, Greece's military regime ended in the same year, and Spain's political transition began after Franco's death in nineteen seventy-five. Countries under Soviet-aligned governments had different systems of economic planning, welfare provision and political control. To use the term postwar European consensus without qualification is to describe only selected societies and to flatten real conflicts within them. The social rights enjoyed by some Western workers coexisted with colonial violence, migrant exclusion, political repression elsewhere, and persistent gender inequalities. A history of cooperation must not mistake the map of participating governments for the map of everyone affected by their policies.

## What survived the shock

One way to assess the postwar settlement is to imagine three ordinary journeys. A nurse goes to work in a publicly funded hospital. A metalworker takes a train to a factory whose sales depend on trade across borders. A family shops for food whose price reflects agricultural decisions made nationally and collectively. Each journey crosses a different system of obligations. The hospital depends on professional labor, public finance and eligibility. The factory depends on investment, bargaining and market access. The food supply depends on production, trade and support for farmers. European integration connected some of these systems, but did not replace the national and local institutions that made them work.

What did Europe build after nineteen forty-five? Not a single welfare state, and not a finished political union. It built national compromises over health, income and work; international arrangements to make another continental war harder; and institutions through which countries could negotiate commerce and shared policies. The energy and economic crises of the nineteen seventies tested all three. Their endurance was not proof that every policy had been just or effective. Their importance lay in establishing durable arenas in which governments, workers, farmers and voters could contest what security and cooperation meant. The continuing question was no longer simply whether Europeans could rebuild. It was who would exercise power over the rebuilt economy, and who could demand a share in its protection.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Institutional and diplomatic sources establish decisions, claims and official chronology, not necessarily civilian experience or a complete causal history. Survivor testimony is attributed to named witnesses; no unverified composite eyewitness is invented. Publisher abstracts are labeled and not treated as if the full work were read. Comparative explanation and historiographic alternatives are distinguished from directly documented events. Lecture length is based on subject matter, not a duration rule.
