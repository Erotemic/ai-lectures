# When Uniforms Rule: Coups, Repression, and Democratic Openings

## The sound of a radio changing hands

A government can fall in several ways. Voters can remove it, a parliamentary coalition can collapse, or soldiers can take control of buildings, communications and weapons before civilians have any chance to vote. In Chile, on September eleventh, nineteen seventy-three, the armed forces overthrew the elected government of Salvador Allende. The military seized the national broadcaster and other centers of power, and the presidential palace was attacked. Within hours, the constitutional order had broken down. Allende died during the coup. That day did not automatically tell Chileans what the succeeding seventeen years would bring, but it removed the legal means by which disagreements over economic policy and political representation could ordinarily be settled.

Chile had been bitterly divided. Allende's government sought a socialist transformation through electoral politics; opponents challenged nationalizations, prices and its claim to govern, while supporters feared elite resistance and foreign intervention. Inflation, shortages, strikes and political violence intensified conflict. Recognizing this crisis is necessary for history, but describing it cannot justify arbitrary detention or torture. A crisis does not determine whether military officers must abolish democratic institutions. Declassified U.S. intelligence reports show officials in Washington following the plans and prospects of a coup. Other records document U.S. efforts to weaken Allende. Yet careful historians distinguish foreknowledge, political and economic pressure, encouragement of opponents and direct operational control of particular military acts. These are different claims requiring different kinds of evidence.

After the coup, a junta ruled under General Augusto Pinochet. Political parties and independent organizations faced suppression, the legislature was dissolved, and agencies of the state detained, tortured, killed and disappeared political opponents. The Chilean Museum of Memory and Human Rights documents these institutions and their victims. The word disappeared describes a particular cruelty: a person taken by agents who refuse to acknowledge custody, leaving relatives unable to obtain either release or truthful information about death. The attack is on the detained person, and also on everyone who must live with uncertainty. Fear can govern a neighborhood without a soldier standing on every corner when neighbors learn that ordinary questions may bring danger.

Families responded with organization. Chilean relatives of the detained and disappeared established associations to locate loved ones and demand accountability, carrying petitions into churches, courts and public spaces. Some women created textiles and other forms of testimony when public speech was heavily restricted. They were not simply mourning figures waiting for political parties to return. They made claims about evidence, legal responsibility and the rights of the living. A constitution can be torn up quickly; reconstructing trustworthy records of where a detainee was taken is slow, often painful work. This makes families' archival and legal labor part of the political history of Chile, not an afterword to the actions of generals.

## What a military government claims to protect

Chile was not alone in the Southern Cone. In Uruguay, conflict had intensified before the military and President Juan María Bordaberry dissolved the legislature in June nineteen seventy-three. A political archive at the University of Texas documents an already crowded landscape of elections, labor organizations, the left-wing Frente Amplio, conservative parties and the armed Tupamaro movement. Armed conflict and state repression were real; they did not make the later destruction of public freedoms inevitable. The regime restricted association and expression and imprisoned and tortured opponents. If we describe the dictatorship as a response to guerrillas alone, we overlook its broad assault on unions, students and political competitors, many of whom had never belonged to an armed movement.

Argentina's military seized power in March nineteen seventy-six. Its leaders named their government a process of national reorganization, but the term disguised a system of clandestine state violence. Armed revolutionary organizations and attacks had been part of the preceding political crisis. The dictatorship nonetheless targeted many more people than armed combatants. The former naval mechanics school known as ESMA in Buenos Aires became a secret detention and torture center. The UNESCO account of the site describes abduction, forced labor, sexual violence and the taking of children born to captive women. A person could vanish into this system without charges, an ordinary trial or public confirmation of imprisonment. The state's secrecy itself was an instrument of power.

The word security can sound reassuring when a government invokes it. But security for whom, against what threat and with which limits on officials? Under Argentina's junta, officers claimed exceptional power to decide who was dangerous, and then tried to hide how that power was used. The difference between a prison sentence imposed by a court and an unacknowledged disappearance is not a mere difference in paperwork. Public charges, independent counsel, judicial review and family access are barriers against officials making themselves their own judges. The very people who claimed to restore order dismantled many of the institutions by which their actions could be contested. That contradiction is essential to understanding military rule.

In April nineteen seventy-seven, a group of mothers gathered at the Plaza de Mayo in Buenos Aires, asking what had happened to their missing children. Azucena Villaflor and other women helped turn isolated searches into public pressure. Accounts of the early meetings describe police orders to keep moving, which prompted women to walk around the square rather than stand together as a prohibited gathering. Their visible presence challenged the regime's attempt to make disappearance invisible. They did not begin with control of the press or parliament. They began with a demand for information that the state itself had refused to provide. Even that demand could be dangerous; some organizers were later themselves abducted.

Another group, the Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo, searched for grandchildren taken from families or born while their mothers were held captive. The question became more than locating a missing body. It concerned identity, birth records, adoption, legal parenthood and a child's right to know their origins. Generations later, that work has depended upon testimony, archival reconstruction and scientific identification methods. The law and the historical investigation meet at the point where a state has tried to change a person's name and family history. The fact that relatives developed their own investigative methods should not obscure the original burden: the authorities had created the secrecy. But it demonstrates how public accountability can be pursued when official institutions are refusing to function honestly.

## An ocean away, another martial law

In the Philippines, President Ferdinand Marcos proclaimed martial law in nineteen seventy-two, using claims about communist insurgency, disorder and national reconstruction to concentrate power. The country had different social structures, a different political history and different relations with the United States from the Southern Cone republics. Its chronology should not be folded into one master story of Cold War coups. Marcos was already president before martial law, and his authoritarian consolidation occurred through decrees and institutions as well as direct force. Political opponents, journalists and activists faced arrests and abuses. A Philippine law passed decades later recognized and provided reparations for victims of killings, torture and enforced disappearance under the Marcos regime. Its language records the state's later formal acknowledgment of the violations.

A comparison across these regimes should focus on the mechanisms they shared without pretending their beginnings or purposes were identical. Exceptional executive power weakened courts and elected bodies. Censorship made it harder to know what was happening. Military and security agencies acted behind procedures that civilians could not inspect. Governments claimed to defend the nation against dangerous enemies and often broadened the category of enemy to include peaceful critics. The consequences were shaped by local societies, legal systems, economies and external alliances. Some dictatorships changed the ownership of industries; others defended existing elites or reorganized markets. The common problem was not one economic ideology. It was power shielded from public challenge.

The Cold War supplied language and resources to these struggles but cannot explain every decision as a command issued abroad. Washington's anti-communist priorities affected security relationships, and the Chile documents demonstrate American awareness and intervention in national politics. Latin American military officers, business leaders and civilian opponents had their own agendas. Philippine leaders likewise operated inside domestic institutions and rivalries. When seeking responsibility, identify the action, the actor and the evidence. Foreign support for a regime may be documented without proving the foreign state organized every abuse. Local autonomy does not remove foreign responsibility for assistance it knowingly gave. A rigorous history needs both perspectives rather than an automatic choice between them.

## The democratic openings of the same decade

The nineteen seventies also saw soldiers helping end dictatorships. In Portugal, the Armed Forces Movement overthrew the long-standing authoritarian regime on April twenty-fifth, nineteen seventy-four. The Portuguese constitution adopted two years later explicitly described the revolution as an end to dictatorship, oppression and colonialism. The movement grew amid exhausting colonial wars in Africa, where liberation movements had fought Portuguese rule. Those anticolonial forces were not passive beneficiaries of a decision in Lisbon; their struggles helped shape the crisis of the Portuguese state. Workers, political parties and neighborhood movements then contested what kind of society should replace the old order. The presence of military officers at the start did not dictate a permanent military government.

The Portuguese transition shows why the phrase return to democracy is sometimes too simple. Competing groups disagreed over ownership of land and factories, the future of colonial territories, the military's role, and the meaning of socialism. The new constitution was not just a signal that soldiers had gone back to barracks. It was an attempt to set procedures and rights for future conflict. Constitutions can make promises that practice fails to meet, but a rule allowing elected representatives to argue publicly is different from one declaring opposition illegal. Portugal's revolution also connected Europe to decolonization in Angola, Mozambique and other territories. A European democratic opening and African independence were related transformations, not separate chapters in which one continent acted and the other received.

In Greece, the military dictatorship established in nineteen sixty-seven collapsed in July nineteen seventy-four. The Hellenic Parliament's constitutional history records the restoration of democratic government under Konstantinos Karamanlis, elections that November, and a December referendum rejecting the return of the monarchy. The constitution of nineteen seventy-five established a new parliamentary republic. Greece's crisis was bound up with events on Cyprus, but the transition depended upon political organization inside Greece, too. The outcome reminds us that an army's loss of credibility can open space for civilian institutions, yet it does not specify how durable those institutions will be. The work of electoral law, judicial reform and public trust still remains.

Spain offers a different pace again. Franco died in November nineteen seventy-five, and the subsequent transition involved negotiations, mobilization and disputes about how far institutions inherited from dictatorship should change. Its path was not a military coup identical to Portugal's, nor a single moment of collapse identical to Greece's. The European Communities increasingly regarded democratic government as central to the possibility of future enlargement, but membership and democratization did not happen simultaneously. The comparison clarifies something important: a regime can end without all its networks disappearing, and citizens can obtain legal voting rights while still disagreeing over accountability for past abuses.

## The archive after the regime

The people who have survived state terror face a problem once a dictatorship ends: how can institutions prove acts those institutions once denied committing? Argentina's democratic government established the National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons, known as CONADEP, in nineteen eighty-three. Survivors and families gave evidence that informed the nineteen eighty-four report Nunca Más, meaning Never Again. The commission did not restore people who had been killed. It made the state's hidden practices subject to public investigation and helped establish a documentary foundation for later prosecutions. The ESMA memory site similarly preserves evidence connected to a building where officers had attempted to keep violence from public scrutiny.

Historical records do not become trustworthy simply because a new government opens an archive. The people who collected petitions, remembered arrest dates, followed vehicles, preserved photographs and testified after intimidation had kept evidence alive. Organizations of relatives in Chile and Argentina made that work political. Their knowledge sometimes conflicted with the government's account; sometimes it preceded the documents officials were willing to release. This is one reason history should attribute findings to the people who made them. A truth commission is important, but it often becomes possible only because survivors have insisted for years that public institutions cannot choose to forget what happened in their name.

Even the term dictatorship needs precision. Not every military government killed on the same scale or used the same detention system. Chile's political repression, Uruguay's imprisonment and exile, and Argentina's clandestine disappearance system must be studied through distinct case records. Their victims were not a uniform political bloc. Some were armed militants, some peaceful organizers, some uninvolved relatives, and some simply people designated suspect. The legal prohibition of torture and disappearance does not depend upon whether an opponent has admirable political beliefs. To retain that distinction is not to avoid discussing violence committed by insurgents. It is to reject the claim that one violence gives unrestricted license to the state.

The decade therefore offers no simple formula stating that military force always produced the same political result. Portuguese officers helped overthrow an authoritarian state and participated in a contested transition toward democracy. Greek military rule collapsed and opened space for constitutional government. Marcos in the Philippines used an existing presidency and martial law to extend authoritarian control. Southern Cone juntas dismantled elected institutions and attempted to suppress organized opposition. The explanatory questions have to be concrete: who controlled the armed forces, what legal institutions survived, how were citizens organized, what happened to the press, and who could punish officials for crimes? Answers differ across places, yet those questions can travel.

One measure of democracy is an election. Another is whether a person can ask where a loved one has been taken and receive an answer without risking their own disappearance. Another is whether a worker can join an association, a journalist can publish evidence, or a court can require the powerful to explain themselves. The nineteen seventies made these protections visible by showing what happened when they were removed, and how people fought to restore them. The story is not finally about uniforms or speeches in a palace. It is about whether a society's institutions leave ordinary people the power to say no, to seek reasons, and to insist that those who govern are bound by the same law.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Institutional and diplomatic sources establish decisions, claims and official chronology, not necessarily civilian experience or a complete causal history. Survivor testimony is attributed to named witnesses; no unverified composite eyewitness is invented. Publisher abstracts are labeled and not treated as if the full work were read. Comparative explanation and historiographic alternatives are distinguished from directly documented events. Lecture length is based on subject matter, not a duration rule.
