# The Day and the War: September 11 and Afghanistan, 2001–2004

## A morning and the people in it

On September eleventh, two thousand and one, four commercial aircraft became weapons. Nineteen hijackers affiliated with al-Qaeda seized the planes after departure from American airports. Two struck the World Trade Center in New York, a third struck the Pentagon near Washington, and a fourth crashed in Pennsylvania after passengers and crew resisted. The towers collapsed amid fires and structural damage. Two thousand nine hundred seventy-seven people were killed, excluding the hijackers. The number is worth saying carefully. It includes passengers, office workers, cleaners, cooks, emergency responders, military personnel, and people visiting places that happened to become targets. They had lived in many countries and belonged to many religions. Their deaths were the purpose of an organized terrorist attack, not an accidental consequence of an exchange of fire.

What people remember about that morning includes separate scenes: a stairwell full of smoke, a radio that cannot connect, a telephone call that ends, a fire crew entering a building while others try to leave, a family searching hospitals for a name. These scenes are not invented conversations; they are the broad categories of experience documented by investigations and memorial work. The attacks also changed the work of an ordinary day for people who survived. First responders and recovery workers faced dangerous debris and long-lasting health questions. Families confronted bereavement while public officials made decisions at extraordinary speed. When we turn to foreign policy, these people must not vanish behind the names of organizations or presidents. Yet understanding the tragedy also requires identifying who planned it and how they acquired the means to carry it out.

## The organization and its shelter

Al-Qaeda was not the Afghan state. It was a transnational militant organization, formed out of networks that had developed in the late nineteen eighties. Its leader, Osama bin Laden, and other organizers argued for violence against the United States and its allies, directed attacks on civilians, and sought to make those attacks into a political strategy. The September eleventh hijackers operated through recruitment, travel, money transfers, aviation training, and coordination across borders. The commission appointed to investigate the attacks reconstructed these links through documents, interviews, and government records. It also documented missed signals and institutional failures in American intelligence and aviation security. Explaining those failures is not the same as suggesting that American institutions caused al-Qaeda's decision to kill civilians. Responsibility for planning the attacks belongs to their perpetrators.

The Taliban was a different organization with a different history. It emerged from Afghanistan's civil wars after the withdrawal of Soviet troops and took Kabul in nineteen ninety-six, establishing rule over much of the country. Its leadership imposed severe controls on public life, including restrictions on girls' education and women's mobility, while rivals controlled other regions. Al-Qaeda used Afghanistan as a base and received protection from the Taliban authorities. That shelter mattered enormously: camps, communications, and the ability to meet without interference helped an international organization operate. But the Taliban's Afghan political and military project and al-Qaeda's international terrorism were not identical. Nor did millions of Afghans, including people living under Taliban control, become responsible for the September attacks by virtue of the territory in which they lived.

The American response joined several decisions that history should keep separate. Investigating the attacks and pursuing those directly involved were immediate tasks. Blocking money and communications and strengthening aviation security required different tools. The administration of President George W. Bush also decided to use military force against al-Qaeda and the Taliban authorities sheltering it. American leaders described a wider campaign against terrorism, but that phrase was capable of including many operations in many places. A declaration of political purpose is not a description of a single battle, nor does it explain what would count as victory. In Afghanistan, the apparent first aim was to deny al-Qaeda a secure base. A more ambitious aim soon appeared: replacing the Taliban government and helping construct a different Afghan political order.

## How the first campaign worked

On October seventh, two thousand and one, American and British forces began air attacks in Afghanistan. Small numbers of American special operations personnel and intelligence officers worked with Afghan anti-Taliban forces; U.S. aircraft struck Taliban positions, command facilities, and other targets. The Northern Alliance was not a foreign army delivered to Afghanistan after September. It consisted of Afghan factions already fighting the Taliban, with histories of alliances, rivalries, and at times serious abuses of their own. Their fighters supplied much of the ground force during the first phase. This division of labor helps explain the rapid change in territorial control. It also complicates the later claim that the new state could simply be designed by foreign governments, as if no armed Afghan organizations had their own constituencies and demands.

Mazar-e-Sharif fell to anti-Taliban forces in November, followed by Kabul and then other cities. The Taliban government lost its major centers of power by December. This was a dramatic battlefield reversal, but the defeat of a government is not the elimination of an armed movement. Taliban leaders and fighters could retreat, cross borders, blend into communities, or await conditions for renewed rebellion. Al-Qaeda operatives could likewise move rather than assemble conveniently for capture. For coalition commanders, the first phase showed that intelligence, air power, and local allied forces could overturn a regime quickly. For residents of contested districts, it did not answer who would police a road, settle a land dispute, or prevent armed men from taking revenge after a front line moved.

The bombing campaign also killed Afghan civilians. Human Rights Watch investigated particular air strikes near Herat, Kandahar, and elsewhere, including incidents involving cluster munitions. A cluster bomb scatters multiple smaller explosive units over an area; some fail to detonate when they land and remain dangerous when children or farmers later encounter them. In Qala Shater near Herat, an apparent errant strike killed residents and left unexploded bomblets. The report contains witness accounts and attempts to distinguish intended military targets from what hit homes. Those are findings about documented incidents, not a reliable total for all civilians who died during the war. The broader point requires no guess at a grand total: destroying a military position and protecting nearby inhabitants are separate operational problems, and decisions made far from a village can continue killing after a battle ends.

Afghan civilians were also threatened by the people who fought on the ground. Taliban authorities had repressed communities and punished those they identified as enemies. Some anti-Taliban commanders had records of looting, arbitrary detention, sexual violence, and attacks on rivals. A foreign coalition choosing local partners inherits a political problem with its tactical benefit: an effective fighter is not necessarily a legitimate police chief, provincial governor, or minister. People trying to reopen a school or a market had to negotiate the authority of armed men who often controlled checkpoints and aid distribution. That problem would return during efforts to draft a constitution. Military alliances made in a war are hard to dissolve at the moment a government is declared.

## Government by agreement

In December two thousand and one, Afghan factions met under United Nations auspices in Bonn, Germany, to establish provisional political arrangements. The agreement provided for an interim authority, a later emergency loya jirga, and a process toward a constitution and elected government. A loya jirga is a large Afghan political assembly, though its historical forms and claims to representation have changed over time. The agreement called for participation by women and equitable representation of ethnic and religious communities. These provisions mattered because they made a promise about political membership rather than merely naming a new leader. Hamid Karzai headed the interim authority. But the negotiations had not given every Afghan group an equal voice, and the Taliban were not among the parties designing the settlement.

The United Nations Security Council authorized an International Security Assistance Force in December to help maintain security in Kabul and its surrounding area. The mandate was a distinct legal and political arrangement from the combat campaign already under way. It rested on support for the new Afghan authorities and the Bonn process. Calling the whole intervention a 'United Nations mission' conceals operations with different mandates and chains of command. Some forces pursued counterterrorism and combat operations; others operated under an international security mandate; Afghan factions and newly forming state bodies had responsibilities of their own. Different legal authorities did not automatically produce coordination. A family whose district was unsafe could not improve its position simply by knowing which international resolution governed a particular contingent.

For Afghan women, the period opened real political and educational possibilities, but it did not begin with foreign armies bestowing a new desire for rights. Afghan women had organized and taught under severe restrictions, and many had demanded access to work, learning, and public decision-making before two thousand and one. Women entered the interim administration and subsequent political gatherings, sometimes facing threats from armed delegates. Human Rights Watch interviewed women who feared that powerful commanders would dominate assemblies meant to establish representative institutions. A later scholarly study of the Bonn process argues that bargaining over ministerial positions gave insufficient weight to gender inclusion. The existence of a women's quota or a ministry tells us something important about constitutional commitments; it tells us less about whether a teacher in a provincial town could travel safely to work.

At the emergency loya jirga in June two thousand and two, Karzai was chosen to lead the transitional administration. A subsequent constitutional process produced a new basic law in two thousand and four. Elections and ministries gave the state recognizable structures, but command over armed men was uneven and provincial power holders continued to shape outcomes. The early settlement had to balance representation, elite bargains, disarmament, local identities, and international financing. Every choice came with costs. Including an armed faction could reduce its immediate incentive to sabotage the new order while discouraging victims of that faction from trusting the state. Excluding it could make the agreement seem cleaner on paper but leave it with military force outside the system. The settlement's weaknesses were not invisible to Afghan participants at the time.

## Returning is not the same as recovering

As fighting changed and the Taliban government fell, Afghan refugees began returning from Pakistan, Iran, and farther away. UNHCR reported extraordinary movements in two thousand and two, with more than one and a half million people returning within months and numbers rising as the year continued. To speak of returns as if a single family simply crossed a border and resumed an interrupted life would miss much of what repatriation required. Houses might have been damaged. Fields could be mined. A child born in exile might know the route to a refugee settlement better than the road to an ancestral village. Schools, clinics, wells, land documents, and employment were often missing. Returning could be an act of hope and a new exposure to risk at the same time.

Aid organizations faced the problem of how to reconstruct services where war had repeatedly rearranged authority. A school needed teachers who could be paid, buildings where lessons could take place, and parents who believed the journey was safe. A clinic required medicines, trained staff, referral routes, and decisions about who would serve women patients in conservative communities. These were not secondary matters to be addressed after history's main political drama ended. They were central to whether a new government was experienced as a government at all. When armed groups controlled a local road or threatened a woman official, the constitutional language drafted in Kabul could become a promise that stopped at the edge of town.

## A widening question

The September attacks changed security practices in the United States and abroad, and the Afghan war rapidly became connected to a broader global campaign. But the categories used in that campaign carried their own dangers. Al-Qaeda was a specific terrorist organization with members, finances, and operations. Taliban insurgents were part of an Afghan conflict with changing local stakes. Civilians who shared a language, religion, or nationality with either group were not thereby belligerents. Policies that ignored those differences risked turning intelligence failures into collective suspicion and undermining the legitimacy of the very institutions the campaign said it intended to protect. Distinguishing enemies, bystanders, allies, and critics is not a luxury available only in peacetime. It is a requirement of reasoning about what force is likely to achieve.

What should we conclude from the first years? The Taliban lost its government; al-Qaeda lost some of its secure base; and new Afghan institutions and opportunities began to take shape. At the same time, civilians died in the campaign, local armed power remained strong, and agreements about representation did not guarantee safety or accountability. The September dead deserve accurate remembrance. Afghan civilians deserve history that does not make them scenery in someone else's response to those deaths. The hardest question left by two thousand and one was not only how a force could topple a hostile regime. It was how Afghans could build an order whose authority came from people living under it, rather than from the temporary alignment of foreign armies and armed commanders.
