# What Does It Mean to Leave? Brexit and Europe's Competing Futures, 2015–2020

## A border that was also a history

On the island of Ireland, a line on a map had long been more than a customs boundary. For much of the twentieth century, the border separated Northern Ireland, which remained within the United Kingdom, from the independent Irish state to its south. It divided jurisdictions during decades of political violence, conflicting claims of national belonging, and efforts to build a peace. By the twenty-first century, a person could cross at many roads without stopping at a checkpoint. Both the United Kingdom and Ireland belonged to the European Union. Their common membership helped make the movement of goods and people across the border less conspicuous, although the peace settlement depended on much more than economics. When British voters chose to leave the European Union, the question of how to keep that border open became one of the hardest problems in the negotiations. This was not an obscure technical complication. It showed what it means to leave a political institution after people have built ordinary lives around belonging to it.

The European project grew out of postwar efforts to make cooperation durable through economic and legal institutions. The United Kingdom joined the European Economic Community in nineteen seventy-three, after earlier debates over sovereignty, commerce, and the country's relationship with continental Europe. By the second decade of the twenty-first century, the European Union was not a single European government, but neither was it merely a free-trade pact. Its members negotiated shared rules governing goods, markets, movement, some environmental protections, and many other subjects. European courts could interpret obligations governments had accepted. National governments and elected representatives participated in making those rules. Arguments about how much authority should be exercised nationally and how much jointly were part of membership itself.

In Britain, those arguments took several forms. Some people viewed European cooperation as a source of economic opportunity, diplomatic influence, and rights to live or work abroad. Others believed decisions should be returned to the British Parliament, objected to free movement within the Union, or regarded European institutions as too distant from voters. Critics of membership did not all want the same future trade relationship, and supporters of membership did not agree on every Union policy. The word sovereignty could mean control over laws, over borders, or over the practical ability to influence rules affecting one's country. Those meanings could pull in different directions. A government might gain freedom to set its own regulations yet lose a direct vote over standards that its exporters still had to satisfy.

## One question, many possible futures

Prime Minister David Cameron promised an in-or-out referendum and, after the Conservatives won the general election in two thousand and fifteen, the government proceeded toward a public vote. The question placed before voters on June twenty-third, two thousand and sixteen was whether the United Kingdom should remain a member of the European Union or leave it. It did not present a detailed, binding blueprint for what leaving would involve. The Leave campaign included people who favored reducing immigration, restoring national legislative control, negotiating new trade agreements, or changing how public money was spent. Remain campaigners emphasized economic integration, cooperation, and the costs and uncertainty of departing. The rhetoric of the campaign included disputed forecasts and disputed claims about money and migration. A campaign argument should be understood as an argument made to persuade voters, not automatically as a fact about what would happen.

The official count recorded seventeen million, four hundred and ten thousand, seven hundred and forty-two votes for Leave and sixteen million, one hundred and forty-one thousand, two hundred and forty-one for Remain. That was fifty-one point nine percent against forty-eight point one percent of valid votes, on turnout of seventy-two point two percent. Those figures establish a clear result for the question asked. They do not establish that every Leave voter preferred the same form of departure, or that every Remain voter held the same views about European institutions. Historians and political researchers must keep the result, its interpretation, and later claims made in its name separate.

The geography of the vote mattered. Leave won majorities in England and Wales; Remain won majorities in Scotland and Northern Ireland. London voted differently from most English regions. Even those broad categories conceal differences among towns, generations, occupations, and neighborhoods. Scotland's government saw the result as an argument about its own constitutional future, while Northern Ireland's divided electorate faced a particularly immediate border question. A vote conducted across the whole United Kingdom produced a nationwide majority but not a common national experience of the decision. That tension was not resolved by repeating the total count.

A referendum also did not write a new treaty. Under British constitutional practice, Parliament had to authorize important legal steps. Under European Union law, a member seeking departure had to use Article Fifty of the Treaty on European Union, notifying the European Council and beginning a negotiating period. The United Kingdom's Supreme Court held in early two thousand and seventeen that ministers could not initiate that process by executive power alone without parliamentary authority. Parliament passed legislation, and Prime Minister Theresa May gave notice on March twenty-ninth. A two-year legal clock began, though the deadline could later be extended by agreement. The phrase take back control had now encountered the question of which domestic institution could exercise control, and by what procedure.

## The negotiations are about people, money, and rules

Negotiators did not begin with a blank sheet. The United Kingdom had participated in Union budgets, shared regulatory systems, and rights frameworks for decades. British people lived and worked in other European states; European citizens lived and worked in Britain. Universities, hospitals, farmers, and employers relied on these arrangements in different ways. The first phase of negotiations therefore concentrated on citizens' rights, financial obligations, and the Irish border. These were not decorative details to be sorted out after the great political decision. They were concrete commitments and dependencies created by membership.

For a family living in a country other than its country of citizenship, the meaning of withdrawal could turn on residence status. Could parents continue to work? Would children retain access to schooling? Would past years count toward permanent residence? The eventual Withdrawal Agreement included protections for many people covered by its terms and required new administrative arrangements. But the existence of a legal right did not make the paperwork automatic or eliminate every uncertainty. A legal settlement defines categories. A person has to demonstrate that they fit one. This is one way major constitutional changes are lived: through applications, records, deadlines, and decisions made by offices that rarely appear in campaign speeches.

Trade raised a second distinction: leaving the Union was not the same as ceasing to trade with European countries. The single market reduces barriers by coordinating rules and, in important areas, enabling free movement of goods, services, capital, and people. A customs union operates differently; it concerns common external tariffs and the treatment of goods crossing borders. The United Kingdom could seek access to European customers without accepting all the obligations of membership, but it could not unilaterally determine the terms offered by the remaining member states. Choosing a more independent regulatory policy could mean more checks on goods or reduced ease of market access. The negotiation was thus not a simple bargain between free trade and no trade. It was a bargaining process over the rules of interdependence.

The European Union had its own institutions and collective interests. The European Commission negotiated under guidelines agreed by the member states, and the European Parliament had a role in approving the withdrawal agreement. The remaining twenty-seven governments had to defend the integrity of their shared legal system while also limiting disruption. On the British side, the prime minister negotiated internationally but depended on support in the House of Commons. An agreement that looked workable to negotiators could be unacceptable to a parliamentary coalition, or to governments concerned about the Irish border. The deadlock in London was not merely personal rivalry. It reflected disagreement over goals that could not all be satisfied simultaneously.

## Why Ireland could not be an afterthought

The Good Friday Agreement of nineteen ninety-eight established a framework for power sharing in Northern Ireland, cooperation across the island, and recognition of different political identities. It did not erase disagreement over sovereignty, and it was not simply an agreement about trade. But the practical openness of the border was part of a transformed everyday landscape. If the United Kingdom left common customs and market arrangements while Ireland remained in them, there had to be some way to enforce differences in the movement of goods. The question became where those checks would occur, what rules would apply, and who would consent to them.

One proposed answer, often called a backstop, would have maintained specified arrangements to avoid a hard land border until another workable solution could be found. Theresa May's negotiated withdrawal package provoked fierce opposition in the Commons, including from parliamentarians who thought it kept Britain too closely tied to European rules and others who thought withdrawal itself was harmful. Some members worried about the democratic and constitutional implications for Northern Ireland. Parliamentary rejection did not mean there was no border problem; it meant the proposed method of addressing the problem lacked sufficient support. Several attempts to approve a deal failed, and the original withdrawal date was extended.

Boris Johnson became prime minister in two thousand and nineteen and reached a revised Withdrawal Agreement with the European Union. Under the revised arrangement, Northern Ireland would continue to follow a range of European rules for goods while remaining part of the United Kingdom's customs territory. This shifted some practical checks toward goods moving between Great Britain and Northern Ireland rather than restoring a routine hard border on the island. Unionists could object that it introduced differences within the United Kingdom; others could see it as necessary to protect cross-border commerce and the peace settlement. It was an institutional compromise, not a magic removal of borders. Later disputes over its operation belong to the longer history of Brexit, rather than being evidence that negotiators had never recognized the problem.

The repeated extensions of Article Fifty were themselves political decisions. They showed that setting a departure date and specifying a lawful, implementable agreement were distinct tasks. The December two thousand and nineteen general election gave Johnson's Conservative Party a parliamentary majority. The withdrawal legislation then passed, and the United Kingdom left the European Union on January thirty-first, two thousand and twenty. Yet for most practical economic purposes, an agreed transition kept many European rules operating until the end of December. Political departure and the restructuring of daily relationships occurred on different clocks.

## A departure without complete separation

The United Kingdom and European Union reached a Trade and Cooperation Agreement in late December two thousand and twenty, on the edge of the transition deadline. It provided a basis for continued trade and cooperation but not a restoration of membership, unrestricted free movement, or all of the former single-market arrangements. It also contained mechanisms for managing disagreements. At the start of two thousand and twenty-one, new border and administrative procedures affected traders, travelers, and employers differently. An exporter selling goods faced questions of origin and compliance; an individual hoping to move abroad encountered changed residence and work rules. A treaty may be written in legal chapters, but its consequences arrive through decisions made by firms, local authorities, households, and border officers.

Brexit was also a European story. It forced the other member states to make decisions about their Union's rules and direction, and encouraged renewed arguments within Britain over representation and national identity. It did not prove that European integration was inevitably collapsing, any more than the economic benefits claimed by supporters of membership made every criticism of the Union illegitimate. The European Union continued with twenty-seven members. Britain's internal constitutional questions remained. The future of Scotland, Northern Ireland, and the relationships among the nations of the United Kingdom could not be derived from the referendum's national arithmetic alone.

There is a temptation to reduce this history to a pair of slogans, one about freedom and another about economic cost. Neither gives an adequate account of what happened. A referendum selected a direction under a simple question. Parliaments, courts, negotiators, regional governments, and citizens then had to define and live with the resulting institutions. At every stage, different kinds of evidence answer different questions: election counts tell us how ballots were cast; legal texts define obligations; parliamentary records reveal contested choices; trade and household records show consequences that unfold over time. The difficulty of Brexit is not that the ballot had no meaning. It is that a democratic decision about belonging had to become workable rules for people whose lives crossed more than one border.
