# When the War Is Over, Where Is Home? Displacement after 1945

## The problem contained in the word home

Imagine that the war is officially over, but returning home requires a document that has disappeared, a railway that no longer runs, or permission from a government that no longer exists. A family might be told to go back to a country where their house has been taken, their neighbors have been murdered, or the authorities now regard them as enemies. Another family might be removed from a town where their grandparents lived because a new border classifies them differently. War does not merely destroy buildings. It changes what home means, who may enter it, and which institutions can recognize an individual’s claim to belong. In 1945, those questions confronted millions of people across Europe and Asia.

Administrative language can obscure the nature of the problem. Allied authorities in Europe spoke of displaced persons, or DPs: people who had been brought away from home as forced laborers, prisoners, persecuted civilians, or wartime evacuees, and now needed relief or a destination. The phrase sounds neutral, but different people had been displaced for radically different reasons. A survivor of a concentration camp, a conscripted worker from Eastern Europe, a child separated from family, and someone escaping a new postwar government did not all want the same outcome. The initial Allied preference for swift repatriation assumed that returning someone to a country of origin would usually restore a damaged life. For many, it could. For others, it could not.

Relief was organized on an enormous scale. The United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration, founded in 1943 before the United Nations organization itself formally existed, coordinated food, medical care, transport, and camps in the war’s aftermath. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum reports that Allied military authorities and UNRRA repatriated more than six million displaced people between May and December 1945. That figure is useful, but the category is a bureaucratic outcome counted over specified months. It does not mean that six million families returned to the homes they remembered, or that those who were not repatriated had no claims to care. The apparent success of a transport operation and the restoration of a life are different historical measures.

At a relief center, the material questions were relentless. Who had enough food to travel? Who needed treatment? Who could translate a form? How did a person prove nationality when the places printed on old documents had acquired new names or governments? What should authorities do when a person refused repatriation? These are not rhetorical decorations added to diplomatic history. They are ways of reading the documents and decisions that relief agencies produced. Repatriation was simultaneously transportation, classification, policing, welfare, and a negotiation with people whose own purposes could conflict with a government’s timetable.

## Survival did not settle belonging

For Jewish survivors of the Holocaust, liberation posed an especially painful contradiction. The Nazi regime that had attempted their destruction was gone; the communities, family networks, and property to which many might have returned were devastated. In parts of postwar Europe antisemitism and danger persisted. Many survivors did not wish to return to their old towns, and immigration destinations were limited by existing restrictions. In the Western Allied zones, hundreds of thousands lived for years in displaced-person camps or urban centers while seeking new countries. The Holocaust Museum records that the Jewish DP population reached roughly a quarter of a million in 1947. That number is not a single ideology: individuals had different histories, aspirations, and possible destinations.

In the summer of 1945, Earl Harrison investigated conditions for displaced people in the American zone and reported serious failures in the treatment of Jewish survivors. His findings challenged the assumption that grouping survivors in general national categories was an adequate answer to persecution. Jewish survivors themselves organized committees, schools, religious institutions, publications, training, and advocacy in the camps. They did not simply wait to be processed. After a regime had tried to eliminate their political and cultural existence, the practical work of education and collective representation became a way to insist on a future. The camps were places of deprivation and uncertainty, but also of organized activity.

Migration politics complicated these efforts. Restrictions limited admission to the United States and British-administered Palestine, where competing national claims and British policy made entry deeply contested. In 1948 the State of Israel was established amid war and displacement, and many Jewish DPs emigrated there; others sought homes in the United States and elsewhere. This history cannot be told merely as the successful movement of one persecuted population. The 1947–49 conflict in Palestine also displaced large numbers of Palestinian Arabs, whose own losses and political claims are distinct historical subjects. The fact that a person needed refuge did not make all consequences of a state-making process disappear.

People also searched for those who never appeared at an assembly center. The organization now known as the Arolsen Archives traces its origins to wartime efforts to gather information about Nazi persecution, prisoners, and forced laborers. In September 1945, a central tracing bureau became a responsibility of UNRRA. It began compiling a central name index the following month. Behind an index card could be an entire family’s unanswered question: was someone dead, living under another name, in a hospital, or on the other side of a border? A record can disclose a camp registration or a route; an absence from a record does not prove a death. The process of reconstruction therefore included building an infrastructure for asking questions.

## When borders turn residents into outsiders

Not everyone forced to move after the war was formally classified as a DP. In Central and Eastern Europe, border changes, retaliatory violence, state policy, and the collapse of Nazi occupation produced large movements of German-speaking people. Some had been residents of territories now placed within Poland; others lived in Czechoslovakia or Hungary. The Potsdam Agreement of August 1945 recognized that transfers to Germany would take place and instructed authorities that they should be orderly and humane. The sentence is striking because it recognizes large-scale removal as a political settlement while also trying to limit its brutality. The instruction cannot be taken as proof that the removals were carried out humanely.

These transfers need their historical setting. Nazi Germany had invaded, occupied, expelled, and murdered across Europe, seeking to transform the continent’s populations by force. The defeat of that regime was necessary to end those crimes. Yet collective identification with a defeated national group could become grounds for removing people whose individual actions and attachments differed. A farmer who had lived in a place for decades, a child with no political agency, and a former Nazi official were not morally interchangeable. Explaining a transfer through the logic of the victors does not settle whether the people transferred were secure or whether their property could be reclaimed. The postwar border was a state project imposed on individual biographies.

Housing, work, and food sharpened all of these disputes. A family returning to a damaged town might encounter other people already living in its former building. Someone who survived by taking a vacant apartment could be told to leave for an owner returning from the war. Rationing, unemployment, disease, and shortages made the question of who deserved assistance politically explosive. Even a victorious country could face prolonged scarcity: a University of Oxford history teaching resource records that Britain rationed bread from 1946 and ended its remaining food rationing only in 1954. Victory, then, was not a day after which civilian economic life resumed. Governments continued allocating limited supplies and households continued calculating what they could obtain.

## The end of a Japanese empire

Asia experienced another vast post-imperial movement. Japan’s surrender dismantled an empire built through conquest, colonization, and the movement of people across Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria, and occupied China and Southeast Asia. Japanese soldiers and civilians needed routes back to Japan; people who had been transported to Japan from its former colonies faced different choices, restrictions, and pressures. Migration scholar Jonathan Bull estimates in a 2026 Oxford encyclopedia abstract that nearly seven million Japanese nationals were repatriated from colonies and occupied territories, while more than one and a half million Koreans, Taiwanese, and Chinese moved from Japan toward places of origin. These are large retrospective aggregates, not a complete description of how any person traveled.

The historian Lori Watt calls attention to the process by which imperial residents were remade as repatriates. A Japanese civilian who had lived in a colony might lose the house, employment, or standing that imperial rule had made possible. Some Japanese experienced suffering during retreat, waiting, and resettlement. This should be described without forgetting why Japanese residents had lived in conquered territories or reducing formerly colonized populations to obstacles in a settler’s journey. The new label “returnee” could conceal the fact that a person had thought of a colony as home and that others had never consented to the empire that brought that person there. Reintegration into Japan was at once an economic problem and a reckoning with imperial history.

For Koreans, the movements were different in origin and meaning. Under Japanese rule, Koreans had migrated or been moved for labor across the empire, and in 1945 its collapse opened routes to the peninsula. Scholar Alyssa Park studies the institutions that categorized and cared for people returning to southern Korea after liberation, including military offices, local relief networks, and the emerging Korean government. Her work is a reminder that “refugees” are not only discovered by a relief organization; the category is also created through registrations, housing arrangements, and the distribution of aid. Korea’s division between Soviet and American occupation zones and its later war meant that arrival on the peninsula did not necessarily provide security or end movement.

We should resist treating return migration as a mirror image: some Japanese who left Korea had benefited from colonial structures, while some Koreans returning from Japan had been subjected to the restrictions and hierarchies of that same system. Those histories could overlap in transport queues and ports without becoming morally equivalent. Nor did every Korean person in Japan decide to move. Families, education, property, and the uncertain politics of the divided peninsula influenced different decisions. A good account asks who controlled the ships, who decided how much property could be carried, and how state agencies defined a person’s nationality. These details make the collapse of empire visible at the scale of ordinary lives.

## Reconstruction and the right to stay

The upheaval of the late 1940s was not limited to the countries defeated in 1945. When British India was divided into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan in August 1947, new borders cut through Punjab and Bengal. Violence and fear drove mass migration, separated families, and created claims to abandoned homes and property. The final boundary awards were published after the formal moments of independence. The British National Archives records this sequence; South Asian oral-history projects preserve the perspectives of people who experienced the consequences. This was not merely an appendix to a European refugee crisis. It was one of the defining human movements of decolonization, with distinct political causes and local histories.

A boundary announcement can describe which administration controls a district; it cannot state what a household should do when its members belong to different religious communities, or when someone has been threatened on a journey. A train ticket, a title deed, a ration card, and a family story are all different documents of belonging. Some records were produced to move people quickly; others were created years later to recover lost property or recognize a missing relative. Oral testimony may preserve experiences absent from official files, while memory can change over time. The historian needs both kinds of record and must ask what each can establish. The task is not to force an individual story into a ready-made total.

The international refugee framework developed in response to these problems, but slowly and imperfectly. The International Refugee Organization succeeded UNRRA in the late 1940s, and the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees was established in 1950. The 1951 Refugee Convention later set standards for qualifying refugees, including the principle against sending someone back to serious threats to life or freedom. But it was designed with postwar categories and constraints; it did not retrospectively provide a remedy to everyone who had lost a home. Even identifying someone as a refugee was a political and legal process, not simply a recognition that a person was suffering.

What, then, is reconstruction? It includes roads, housing, factories, schools, and food supply. It also includes a parent trying to establish a child’s identity, a survivor deciding where it is safe to live, a community building an institution in a camp, or someone demanding an explanation for a missing relative. The postwar record repeatedly separates formal peace from the practical possibility of living somewhere without fear or humiliation. If we remember only the rebuilt skyline, we miss the people barred from returning to it. If we remember only refugee totals, we miss the decisions, documents, and relationships through which a home could be recovered—or a different home painstakingly made.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

The displaced-person figures reported by USHMM have specific periods and administrative categories. The German transfers discussion analyzes the Potsdam text as an official policy document and does not assert it was implemented humanely. Japanese repatriation estimates come from abstracts or excerpts by Jonathan Bull and Lori Watt. Partition and Korea are introduced but require region-specific testimony for detailed life stories. No first-person individual refugee account is invented.

This transcript contains no fabricated oral testimony, private conversation, or counterfactual result presented as fact. Source records distinguish open historical pages, primary texts, excerpts and bibliographic abstracts. Narration has no fixed duration or word-count target.
