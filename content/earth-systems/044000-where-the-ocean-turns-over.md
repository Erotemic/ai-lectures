# Where the Ocean Turns Over: Deep Water, Mixing, and the Long Ocean Memory

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A storm can rearrange the surface of the sea in a day. A winter can cool a wide region of the North Atlantic. But the deep ocean may carry the consequences of surface weather for decades or centuries. Water that is far from sunlight can still hold oxygen acquired at the surface, carbon dissolved from the atmosphere, and a record of the temperature and salinity conditions under which it last mixed with air. The ocean's deepest layers are not motionless. Their movement is simply harder to see and often much slower than the weather above them.

To explore this hidden circulation, imagine that we could release an invisible tracer into the surface of the North Atlantic in winter and watch it for a century. Some would remain in upper currents and return toward lower latitudes. Some would be mixed downward as surface water became dense. Some would spread into deep boundary currents. Some would be carried by eddies across those currents. There would be no single conveyor belt carrying every molecule along the same route, and no common travel time at which they all emerged. What we would see is a network of pathways, mixing, and exchange among water masses.

Oceanographers use the word overturning for a circulation that exchanges water between upper and deeper layers. A familiar part is the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, often shortened to A M O C. Broadly, its upper branch carries water northward in the Atlantic, while a deeper branch carries water southward. This exchange helps transport heat, salt, oxygen, and carbon. Yet it is not identical to the Gulf Stream, which is a vigorous western boundary current with an important wind-driven component. Even a major change in overturning would not mean that every North Atlantic surface current simply stops.

Why would seawater move vertically at all? Pressure rises with depth, and a water parcel's density depends principally on its temperature, salinity, and pressure. Cooler water is generally denser than warmer water at similar salinity and pressure. Saltier water is generally denser than fresher water at similar temperature and pressure. In some high-latitude regions, surface cooling can make the upper water dense enough to mix with or sink beneath nearby water. Sea ice formation can reject salt into surrounding seawater, although the effect on convection depends on the local water and ice conditions. In other places, freshwater from rain, river discharge, or melting ice can increase surface buoyancy and make deep mixing more difficult.

These are tendencies, not instructions that a parcel must follow. A very cold surface layer can remain light relative to deeper water if it is fresh enough. If you pour freshwater above saltwater in a glass, the layers resist mixing even if the top layer is cooler. The useful quantity is density, not temperature alone. Real seawater has a nonlinear equation of state, which oceanographers calculate from measured temperature, salinity, and pressure. Simplifying that physics to 'cold water sinks' can conceal the reason a high-latitude region mixes deeply in one year and barely does so in another.

The North Atlantic contains several regions where winter cooling and wind-driven mixing help produce dense waters. As this water moves and mixes along different routes, it contributes to the deep southward branch of the Atlantic overturning. The process is spatially complex. Deep-water formation involves changes in water-mass properties over broad areas, exchanges across slopes, convection in selected regions, and downstream mixing. The water that leaves the surface does not necessarily plunge through the entire ocean depth in one vertical column and begin a journey along a predetermined track.

There is also a much colder, deeper part of the global system associated with waters formed around Antarctica. Dense water created in several Antarctic shelf and coastal environments can descend into the abyss and spread northward along the bottom of ocean basins. Antarctic Bottom Water is not simply the southern continuation of the Atlantic overturning branch. It is part of an interacting global circulation with its own source regions and sensitivity to sea ice, melting ice shelves, and regional winds. A change around Antarctica can influence oxygen supply and temperature in waters thousands of meters below the surface.

Something has to return deep water toward shallower layers. The Southern Ocean plays an important role because winds drive currents around Antarctica and help move water across density surfaces and toward regions where it can be brought nearer the surface. Large-scale wind stress, eddies, and mixing work together. Upwelling is not usually a deep-water geyser. In broad regions it is a slow redistribution of water and density surfaces, and its exact pathways depend on the dynamics of the Southern Ocean. The simplified story of a current sinking at one end and rising at the other omits much of the machinery that makes a three-dimensional circulation possible.

Even an ocean with no continents would not overturn indefinitely without energy. Winds put mechanical energy into upper-ocean motion. Tides move water over rough bottom topography and generate internal waves. Breaking internal waves and turbulence mix water across density layers. Surface heating and cooling change buoyancy. The global circulation is sustained by a combination of mechanical forcing and buoyancy processes. That is why ocean circulation cannot be reduced to the idea that dense water falls until it hits the bottom and later floats upward for no particular reason.

A second experiment helps. Picture two deep reservoirs of water of slightly different density, separated by a thin interface. Left alone, stable stratification makes large-scale vertical exchange slow. Stir the interface and the reservoirs exchange heat and salt. In the ocean, much of the equivalent stirring occurs through tiny turbulent motions generated by breaking internal waves or by flows around topography. The immediate mixing is small-scale. Its cumulative consequence is global: it helps determine how quickly deep layers communicate with the surface. Climate models must represent this mixing, sometimes through approximations for processes smaller than a grid cell.

This brings us to the word ventilation. Ocean ventilation is the process by which water acquires contact with the atmosphere and subsequently carries dissolved gases or other properties into the interior. A newly ventilated water mass may contain oxygen dissolved while it was near the surface. As it travels through the deep ocean, organisms consume some of that oxygen as organic matter decomposes. The dissolved oxygen measured at a depth is therefore influenced by both the water's history of contact with the surface and what has happened biologically along the way. Oxygen concentration by itself is not a simple clock, but it is a clue.

The same reasoning applies to carbon. Carbon dioxide enters the surface ocean, where its chemistry redistributes carbon among dissolved species. Some of the absorbed carbon moves into deeper waters when those waters are ventilated or mixed downward. Biological production and the sinking and decomposition of organic material supply another route for carbon transport. How rapidly the deep ocean exchanges with the surface helps determine how the ocean absorbs heat and carbon from a changing atmosphere. An ocean can have an enormous storage capacity and still exchange with the atmosphere too slowly to prevent significant near-term warming.

To measure ventilation, scientists look for tracers whose history is partly known. Certain industrial chemicals entered the atmosphere in increasing quantities during the twentieth century, then entered surface seawater. Their concentrations in the ocean can constrain how recently a water mass last exchanged with the atmosphere, though mixing creates distributions of ages rather than a single date. Radiocarbon offers information on other timescales, with corrections for how carbon enters and circulates through the ocean. Temperature, salinity, oxygen, and chemical tracer measurements together reveal more than any one alone.

But a full ocean circulation is not visible from one sample bottle. Scientists measure overturning by combining instruments. In the Atlantic near twenty-six and a half degrees north, the RAPID observing array began continuous monitoring in 2004. Its system uses moorings, measurements of the Florida Current and other observations to estimate transports across the basin. Oceanographers combine those components to estimate the northward upper-ocean and southward deep-ocean flows. The published data set covering April 2004 through March 2024 documents how much can be learned from persistent measurements of a place we cannot observe in its entirety with a single instrument.

To express those flows, oceanographers use a unit called the sverdrup. One sverdrup is one million cubic meters of water transported each second. That sounds impossibly large until you remember that an ocean current is an enormous cross-section of water, sometimes moving slowly but extending across hundreds of kilometers and great depths. If a current carries fifteen sverdrups, for example, that means fifteen million cubic meters pass its section each second. A sverdrup is a transport, not a speed and not a fixed volume of ocean water. The distinction helps when a graph reports a strengthening or weakening of a circulation.

How do we know whether the Atlantic overturning is slowing? There are several answers, and they must not be collapsed into one certainty. A continuous observing array captures short-term and decadal variability at a particular latitude, but its record is still short compared with some natural fluctuations. Proxies based on temperature patterns, sediments, or other indirect indicators extend the historical perspective but add assumptions and calibration uncertainty. Models simulate responses to changing greenhouse gases and freshwater inputs, but their representation of deep mixing and water-mass formation is imperfect. These lines of evidence can reinforce one another, and sometimes they disagree about a trend's size.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's sixth assessment concluded that the Atlantic overturning is very likely to weaken during the twenty-first century under the emissions scenarios it considered. It had much less confidence in the exact size of that weakening. It also assessed, with medium confidence, that an abrupt collapse would not occur before 2100. This is not a guarantee that an abrupt change is physically impossible. It is a calibrated statement about evidence and uncertainty under the assessed scenarios. More recent observations and research can refine those judgments, but a headline based on a single reconstruction should not be treated as a direct measurement of a global current.

What would a substantial change do? Heat transport within the Atlantic would change, potentially altering regional temperatures, rainfall patterns, and sea level along some coasts. Ocean carbon and oxygen pathways could change as well. These are not effects that follow immediately and everywhere from a switch being thrown. They depend on how the circulation changes, how the rest of the ocean and atmosphere respond, and over what timescale. Just as the Gulf Stream is not the entire overturning, European weather is not governed by a single ocean pipe.

The deep ocean is a memory system, but it does not have one memory length. Upper-ocean eddies can rearrange water quickly. Some newly formed deep waters may travel appreciable distances within years or decades. Abyssal waters can be isolated from the atmosphere for much longer. The measured concentration of a tracer in a sample combines formation, transport, mixing, decay where relevant, and biogeochemical reactions. If scientists say that an area of ocean has an apparent ventilation age, they mean a constrained inference under a model, not that all its water was last at the surface on one precise date.

The picture that emerges is richer than a conveyor-belt drawing. High-latitude cooling and freshwater can alter density. Winds and tides supply energy. The Southern Ocean helps connect deep and shallow water, and Antarctica produces waters that occupy some of the deepest ocean. Biological processes consume oxygen and redistribute carbon. Observing arrays track transport at a few crucial sections, while chemical tracers provide clues to pathways and history. Every piece measures something real, but no single one is the entire global circulation.

If the surface ocean is the planet's immediate conversation with the atmosphere, the deep ocean is the long reply. To understand that reply we must follow heat, salt, motion, and chemical tracers across depths and decades. Only then does it make sense to ask how a warming world might change the circulation that carries so much of Earth's past into its future.

## Scientific companion: overturning is a transport field, not a single conveyor

**Density and stability.** Seawater density depends on temperature, salinity and pressure. Cooling can increase density, while freshening can decrease it. Whether convection reaches depth depends on the ambient stratification, surface forcing, winds and water-mass properties. The linear heuristic $\Delta\rho\approx-\rho_0\alpha\Delta T+\rho_0\beta\Delta S$ is only a local approximation; rigorous calculations use the seawater equation of state.

**Overturning versus named currents.** The AMOC is a basin-scale meridional exchange between upper and deep flows. The Gulf Stream includes important wind-driven geostrophic circulation and is not synonymous with the AMOC. The Southern Ocean and Antarctic Bottom Water participate in a related but distinct global overturning network. Mixing across density surfaces requires energy supplied by winds, tides and buoyancy-related processes.

**Transport unit.** $1\,\mathrm{Sv}=10^6\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$. A hypothetical $15\,\mathrm{Sv}$ section transport is $15$ million cubic meters per second; it is not a speed in meters per second. The RAPID observational system near 26.5 degrees north combines several measurements to infer a cross-basin estimate.

**Uncertainty.** The IPCC AR6 WGI assessed a very likely twenty-first-century AMOC weakening, low confidence in precise magnitude, and medium confidence that no abrupt collapse would occur before 2100. Direct observations, proxy reconstructions and model projections are distinct. A spatially limited, roughly two-decade observational record cannot alone establish a centuries-long global trend.
