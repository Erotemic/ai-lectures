# When Pacific Rain Moves the Jet Stream: The Distant Reach of ENSO

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A wet season fails in part of Indonesia. During the same broad season, the equatorial central Pacific sees thunderstorms over water that is usually less favorable for towering clouds. Months later, the winter storm track across the North Pacific takes a different average route. It is tempting to draw a line on a map and imagine a storm moving from one place to the other. That is not what happened. The link is carried by the atmosphere's response to heat released in tropical rain clouds. A shift in the place where air rises can change winds, pressure and the pathways available to storms thousands of kilometers away.

This is the problem of teleconnections. The word means a statistical and physical connection between climatic behavior at widely separated places. El Niño and La Niña provide a major example because their characteristic changes in tropical Pacific temperatures alter the location of deep convection. The ordinary western Pacific warm pool favors large rainfall systems over and near the Maritime Continent. During many El Niño episodes, enhanced convection extends or shifts toward the central or eastern equatorial Pacific. Other regions experience suppressed rising motion. La Niña often strengthens the more usual westward concentration of tropical convection. But a temperature anomaly is not itself a traveling storm, and a teleconnection is not a machine that guarantees one location's weather.

Think about a tall thunderstorm in the tropics. Water vapor evaporated from a warm surface rises, condenses, and releases latent heat. This heating changes the vertical temperature structure and the motion of the surrounding air. The air must respond: mass cannot continually accumulate in one column, and pressure differences develop. In the tropics, broad zones of ascent and descent are connected to upper- and lower-level flow. A shift in concentrated tropical rainfall therefore acts like a change in the heating pattern imposed on the atmosphere. Because Earth's rotating atmosphere is a connected fluid, that change can project onto wind patterns far from the original clouds.

The lower atmosphere in the tropical Pacific adjusts rapidly to the distribution of convection. When the strongest rising motion shifts east, the familiar Walker circulation changes. Regions once favored for rising air may receive more sinking motion, inhibiting cloud development. Near-surface and upper-level winds change their average strength and direction. The shift feeds back on the ocean through wind stress, which is the same coupling that helps drive ENSO itself. But tropical heating has a second reach: it can excite large-scale atmospheric wave responses that extend into the subtropics and higher latitudes.

To see why, recall that air moving north or south on a rotating globe changes its relationship to planetary rotation. The Coriolis effect depends on latitude. A broad current of air also carries its own rotation, or relative vorticity. Changes in vorticity and the latitude-dependent effect of Earth's rotation support Rossby waves: large undulations and pressure patterns in the atmosphere. They need not look like a line of individual clouds on a satellite photograph. Sometimes they appear as persistent ridges and troughs in the middle and upper troposphere, with alternating centers of pressure influence.

Heating in the tropical atmosphere changes the divergent outflow aloft. Where that outflow interacts with regions of strong horizontal wind and vorticity gradients, it can help force Rossby wave patterns. The response depends on where the heating occurs and on the preexisting jets, not merely on the total amount of tropical rain. A warm anomaly near one longitude can produce a different distant response from a similar anomaly farther east or west. This is one reason that two El Niño episodes with similar central-Pacific temperature indices may have distinct effects on the rest of the world.

Over the North Pacific, changes in the large-scale circulation can shift or alter the subtropical jet stream and the tracks along which extratropical storms develop. The jet is a fast high-altitude current associated with horizontal temperature gradients and the balance of pressure and Earth's rotation. It is not a hose that sprays rain wherever it points. Storms form, intensify and move within a changing flow. If the jet and average pressure fields shift, the probability of certain storm tracks can increase or decrease. But the actual storms depend on many disturbances, including weather systems arising locally and upstream, that ENSO does not dictate one by one.

In the Northern Hemisphere winter, El Niño has often been associated with a more active or more southerly Pacific storm track and wetter odds across parts of the southern United States. Some regions in the northern United States and southern Canada more often experience relatively mild conditions. The words 'often' and 'parts' do important work. A single winter does not have to conform to a textbook map. Atmospheric blocking, the Arctic Oscillation, the position of the jet, regional sea temperatures, and random weather all play roles. A strong El Niño does not imply that every neighborhood along a colored map will have a wet winter.

La Niña frequently tilts these odds in the other direction, with some of the southern United States drier in winter and different average patterns of jet position and storm activity over the North Pacific. Again, this is a seasonal composite, not a deterministic switch. The connection is much more evident in some seasons than others and more robust in some geographical regions. A winter teleconnection map should not be used uncritically to describe a July thunderstorm or to infer that a particular town will receive a particular number of inches of precipitation.

The Southern Hemisphere and tropical lands are part of the same story, but they do not receive the same response. When convection moves away from the Maritime Continent during El Niño, parts of Indonesia and Australia are more likely to experience suppressed rainfall during their relevant seasons. During La Niña, the shifted rainfall distribution can favor wetter conditions in parts of these regions. In some episodes this influences drought risk, river flow, crop production and fire weather. Yet drought requires a deficit sustained through time, and a damaging wildfire also depends on fuel, ignition, wind, land management and exposure. ENSO changes some of the conditions; it does not by itself cause every subsequent loss.

Along the Pacific coast of tropical South America, warm eastern Pacific waters and altered convection can produce heavy rainfall in areas where the normal climate is much drier. The biological ocean also responds when the thermocline deepens and the nutrient supply to surface waters is reduced. But coastal Peru and Ecuador are not interchangeable with the full basin. Local coastal ocean-atmosphere events can behave differently from basin-wide ENSO, while the most extensive basin events do not have identical impacts at every coastal point. A careful lecture must keep the scale of the phenomenon aligned with the scale of the evidence.

Consider the Indian summer monsoon. Seasonal rainfall depends on the heating contrast between land and ocean, atmospheric moisture, the position of regional rain belts, and several modes of variability. ENSO can influence the monsoon, with El Niño historically linked to greater odds of deficient rainfall in some analyses and La Niña to wetter odds. But the relationship varies across events, decades and parts of India. The Indian Ocean, the configuration of tropical convection and the monsoon's internal variability all matter. It would be inaccurate to promise drought merely because an El Niño is present, or to describe the monsoon as simply the western end of the Walker circulation.

The tropical Atlantic provides another instructive connection. El Niño often promotes atmospheric patterns that increase vertical wind shear over parts of the Atlantic hurricane development region. Shear is a change of wind with height; sufficient shear can make it harder for a tropical cyclone's organized circulation and thunderstorms to remain aligned. On average this can suppress Atlantic hurricane activity, while La Niña tends to favor less hostile shear in some regions. But warm Atlantic water, humidity, Saharan dust, wind patterns and transient disturbances still influence the season. A seasonal reduction in the expected number of storms says nothing certain about whether an individual coast will be struck.

What evidence justifies these claims? We can start with observations collected before computers could run modern global circulation models. Researchers compared records of rainfall, pressure and temperature from widely separated stations. In the early twentieth century Gilbert Walker and collaborators studied large-scale fluctuations in air pressure, partly motivated by the challenge of understanding the Indian monsoon. They identified the Southern Oscillation as a pattern of connected pressure variations. They did not have the modern satellite view of the equatorial ocean, so the oceanic mechanism remained incomplete. Later work connected the pressure pattern to the warm and cool phases in the Pacific.

In 1969 Jacob Bjerknes studied how tropical Pacific temperature anomalies related to changes in winter atmospheric circulation. That was not simply a discovery of a repeated local rain event. It identified the importance of the equatorial ocean as a contributor to distant atmospheric behavior. In 1987 Chester Ropelewski and Michael Halpert analyzed monthly rainfall records from more than seventeen hundred stations to look for regional patterns associated with ENSO. Their results identified coherent rainfall relationships in a number of tropical and subtropical regions. The analysis was statistical; it did not claim that all locations within each region always behave the same way in every event.

How would we reproduce an analysis like that? We would begin with a collection of ENSO events identified by some consistent ocean-atmosphere measure. Then we would calculate seasonal rainfall departures at each observing station relative to an appropriate local climatology. Next, we would compare events and look for patterns that recur more often than they would in the absence of ENSO. We would also examine different months, since the delayed response of a region matters. The strength of the relationship can be tested by withholding events, comparing independent records, or using physically based models. One event on a map is not sufficient to discover a reliable global teleconnection.

A recurring problem is that correlations are conditional. A region's response can be stronger when El Niño peaks in winter than when it peaks in another season. The location of anomalous central-Pacific convection matters, so an event classified by one sea-temperature index may not predict the entire atmospheric response. Some rainfall series have changed their observing networks and coverage over time. Long-term warming, land-use changes and other climate modes can also affect the recorded outcome. Statistics without a causal mechanism may mistake coincidence for connection; a plausible physical mechanism without data may mistake possibility for reliable predictability. Both are needed.

One should also distinguish the average rainfall over a season from an extreme day. A season can be wetter than average because rain falls on more days, because a few particularly heavy storms occur, or both. ENSO might alter the likelihood of the broad pattern without providing a reliable forecast of the particular flood that damages a bridge. Even when probabilities shift substantially, losses depend on what has been built in a floodplain, whether hillsides have vegetation, how reservoirs are operated and whether warnings reach people. The climate influence and the vulnerability of a community are different links in a chain.

We can think of an ENSO teleconnection as an experiment the Earth repeats with variations. The Pacific supplies a changing pattern of sea-surface temperatures and tropical heating. The atmosphere responds with altered circulation, including wave patterns and storm-track tendencies. Observers compare many events to identify robust regional relationships. Real events differ because the heating pattern differs, the seasons differ, and weather remains variable. The result is predictive information, but it is information about probabilities, not a map of preordained storms.

The consequence is significant. A water manager may use seasonal odds to consider several possible reservoir inflows. Agricultural planners may evaluate risks to planting or irrigation under different rain scenarios. Public-health agencies may watch for environmental conditions that favor disease vectors without treating an El Niño announcement as a disease forecast. These are not direct readings from a thermometer in the Pacific. Each decision requires local evidence, a careful forecast model, and an account of consequences if the forecast is wrong.

The distant reach of ENSO is therefore neither mystical nor mechanical in the crude sense. Its influence is carried by real physical changes in atmospheric heating, mass circulation and waves. But a physical influence is not a guarantee. The Pacific can shift the loaded dice of seasonal climate without choosing the result of every throw. That distinction is the foundation for understanding what climate prediction can accomplish, and where the ocean's memory eventually gives way to uncertainty.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Mechanism and interpretation

- **Atmospheric bridge:** tropical SST shifts influence tropical convection and latent heating; upper-level divergent outflow and Rossby-wave forcing help alter remote circulation patterns.
- **Seasonal composite:** an average over events aligned by phase or season. It is not an event-by-event guarantee and should never be presented as a literal rainfall map for a particular year.
- **Storm-track response:** changes in mean jet and pressure patterns alter probabilities of extratropical storms. Individual storms and extreme outcomes retain large internal variability.
- **Attribution:** physical mechanisms, observational associations, local vulnerability, and actual event causation must be distinguished.
- **Event diversity:** warming centered in different Pacific regions can shift tropical heating longitude and alter the remote signal.

### Sources and limits

- Bjerknes (1969), *Atmospheric Teleconnections from the Equatorial Pacific*. Historical observational links between equatorial SST and North Pacific winter circulation. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493(1969)097%3C0163:ATFTEP%3E2.3.CO;2
- Ropelewski and Halpert (1987), *Global and Regional Scale Precipitation Patterns Associated with the El Niño/Southern Oscillation*. More than 1,700 station records, coherent but conditional regional precipitation composites. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493(1987)115%3C1606:GARSPP%3E2.0.CO;2
- Wallace and Gutzler (1981), *Teleconnections in the Geopotential Height Field during the Northern Hemisphere Winter*. Describes recurring Pacific-North American pressure-height patterns, not an event-level deterministic response. https://doi.org/10.1175/1520-0493(1981)109%3C0784:TITGHF%3E2.0.CO;2
- NOAA, *Understanding El Niño & ENSO*. Regional tendencies and explicit caveat that teleconnections are likely, not certain. https://www.noaa.gov/understanding-el-nino
- NOAA Atlantic Oceanographic and Meteorological Laboratory (June 2026), *How does El Niño Impact Atlantic Hurricane Season*. Influence through vertical wind shear, not a prediction of individual hurricane landfall. https://www.aoml.noaa.gov/how-does-el-nino-impact-atlantic-hurricane-season/
- NOAA PSL, *ENSO Information*. Regionally defined ocean indices and atmospheric indicators; different indices represent different aspects of the coupled system. https://psl.noaa.gov/enso/
