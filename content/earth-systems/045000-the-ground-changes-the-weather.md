# The Ground Changes the Weather: Soil, Plants, Heat, and Feedback

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Take two fields beneath the same summer sky. They receive nearly the same sunlight. The air above them is warm, and the wind is light. One field has damp soil and active vegetation. The other has dry soil and sparse cover. By afternoon, the dry field may be much hotter at the surface. The difference is not because the Sun has chosen one field over the other. It is because the two surfaces have divided the incoming energy differently. One is spending more of its energy evaporating water. The other has less water available and can send more energy into warming the ground and the air.

This difference is the beginning of a conversation between land and atmosphere. Weather determines rain, sunlight, temperature, and wind over a landscape. The landscape responds by changing soil moisture, plant growth, surface temperature, and evaporation. Those changes then affect the atmosphere's heat and moisture supply. In some circumstances, the atmosphere feeds back onto the land, making a dry spell hotter or a wet period more persistent. Earth-system scientists call these land-atmosphere interactions. Their importance becomes clear when we stop treating land as a flat, passive lower boundary for weather.

A useful starting point is the surface energy budget. Sunlight reaches the ground, some is reflected, and the surface exchanges thermal radiation with its surroundings. The remaining energy can be partitioned into several pathways. Some warms the air directly; this is sensible heat. Some evaporates water or drives transpiration through plants; this is latent heat. Some moves into the soil or is stored in vegetation and other materials. All are energy transfers, but they affect the air differently.

When water evaporates, it takes energy from the surface. A wet field can therefore stay relatively cool even as it supplies water vapor to the air. When little water is available, evaporation may be constrained, and a larger share of available energy can heat the surface and nearby air. The dry field is not creating extra solar energy. It is changing the route that energy follows. That is a crucial distinction. The same surface radiation budget can produce different near-surface temperatures depending on soil and vegetation.

Water vapor does not rise from bare soil and leaves in exactly the same way. Bare-soil evaporation depends on soil moisture near the surface, temperature, vapor-pressure gradients, and the resistance of the soil and air to water movement. Plants move water from the soil through roots and stems to leaves, where it exits mainly through microscopic openings called stomata. This process is transpiration. Together, evaporation and transpiration are called evapotranspiration. Two landscapes with the same total water loss may divide it differently between soil evaporation and plant transpiration, and that distinction matters for how water is taken from the ground and how plants respond to stress.

Stomata provide a connection between biology and the planetary water cycle. A leaf opens stomata to admit carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, but opening also allows water vapor to escape. If air is hot and dry, the difference in vapor pressure between a moist leaf interior and the surrounding air can be large. A plant may reduce stomatal opening to conserve water, depending on species, soil conditions, and physiology. That response can lower transpiration and alter the surface energy budget. The atmosphere changes the leaf's decision; the collective behavior of leaves changes the atmosphere's supply of moisture and heat.

There is an additional complication: plants do not all access the same water reservoir. Shallow-rooted grasses can respond quickly to a dry upper soil layer. Trees with deeper roots may continue taking up water after the surface has dried, though they too have limits. Leaf area, rooting depth, phenology, and plant hydraulic strategies alter how a landscape uses water over a season. A field with green leaves in spring may act differently in a summer drought even if its soil and topography have not changed. When a model treats all vegetation in a grid cell as the same green surface all year, it can miss this evolving exchange.

Now picture a dry spell. Rain stops arriving, but clear skies keep delivering energy. As soil water is used or lost, evaporation may decrease. With less evaporative cooling, surface temperatures rise. Warmer air can have greater evaporative demand, making it easier for exposed soil and plants to lose water where water remains available. This can further deplete soil moisture. In regions where evaporation is limited by water supply, such a soil-moisture feedback can amplify heat extremes. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has assessed strong evidence that dry soil moisture anomalies favor summer heat waves in these settings. The feedback is conditional: in a persistently humid, energy-limited environment, more sunlight may matter more than a modest change in soil water.

That conditional behavior is worth dwelling on. Imagine an area already so wet that water is readily available for evaporation. A little extra rainfall may not change daytime cooling much, because sunlight and the atmosphere's capacity to accept vapor are the limits. Now imagine a semi-arid region at the transition from wet to dry soil. A small additional loss of stored water can sharply reduce evaporation and raise sensible heating. This intermediate regime can be especially sensitive to soil-moisture anomalies. We cannot assume that the same millimeter of rain changes surface temperature equally in a rainforest, a grassland, and a desert.

The air immediately above the surface is called the atmospheric boundary layer. During the day, heating can build turbulence that mixes air upward. Strong sensible heating tends to deepen this mixed layer under suitable conditions. Water vapor from soil and vegetation enters it and is diluted or concentrated as the layer grows and exchanges air with its surroundings. These changes influence cloud-base height, near-surface humidity, and the possibility that rising parcels will reach levels where condensation begins.

Does a dry, hot surface produce more thunderstorms because it makes stronger thermals? Not necessarily. A vigorous rising bubble needs adequate moisture and a favorable larger-scale atmospheric profile. Dry land may supply sensible heat but relatively little vapor. Wet land may supply vapor but maintain a cooler surface and a different pattern of convection. A boundary between wet and dry patches can create local temperature and pressure contrasts that help organize low-level winds. In some situations that boundary may favor convection; in others, large-scale subsidence or dry air aloft prevents storms. Stronger surface heating is one ingredient, not a self-sufficient thunderstorm recipe.

Land cover adds another dimension. A dark forest canopy can absorb more sunlight than a lighter grassland or snow-covered clearing. It can also provide shade, store water, make the surface rougher, and exchange more vapor through its leaves. A forest's effect on local temperature is therefore not determined by color alone. In snowy high latitudes, a dark evergreen canopy can mask bright snow and lower the area's reflectivity. In warmer places, substantial transpiration can cool the surface while the canopy influences clouds and rainfall. The balance differs by latitude, season, water availability, and atmospheric circulation.

Surface roughness matters because wind has to move past obstacles. Trees, tall crops, shrubs, and buildings exert drag and generate turbulence. The roughness length in a land-surface model describes how a landscape exchanges momentum with air; it is not merely an aesthetic measure of how bumpy the view looks. If land cover changes from forest to low crops, near-surface winds, mixing, and evaporation can all change. Any assessment of land-use change has to consider radiation, water, and momentum together.

This is why a claim that planting trees always cools the climate is too simple. Trees can store carbon, change albedo, alter evapotranspiration, and affect snow cover and cloud formation. Their effects depend on the existing vegetation and environmental conditions. Planting trees in a snowy high-latitude landscape may darken the surface and increase absorbed sunlight, while a change in a warmer region can have a different biophysical effect. Carbon storage and the local surface-temperature response are related but distinct questions. A careful analysis needs both.

Consider irrigation. Adding water to cropland can increase evaporation and cool the irrigated surface under suitable conditions. That cooling can be measured locally, but it does not mean the irrigation water is free or that the entire region's climate must cool. The water may have been pumped from an aquifer, diverted from a river, or drawn from a reservoir. Its use changes water availability elsewhere and perhaps the atmosphere's humidity downwind. Once again, a local energy benefit and a watershed-scale water cost can coexist. An Earth-system explanation follows both budgets rather than reporting only the cooler afternoon.

Fire creates another set of interactions. Hot, dry weather can make vegetation more flammable, while the amount and condition of fuel depend on earlier growth and moisture. A fire alters canopy cover, soil exposure, dark or bright surface properties, and sometimes water infiltration. It releases gases and aerosol particles that can influence radiation and clouds. Later regrowth changes transpiration and carbon exchange again. Yet fire is not merely a climate feedback. Ignition sources, fuel continuity, land management, and weather extremes all influence whether a fire occurs and how far it spreads. A plausible feedback loop is not proof that climate caused every ignition or every burned hectare.

The Sahel provides a case in which vegetation, rain, and aerosols meet. Rainfall shapes the growth of grasses and shrubs, which alters soil exposure, dust emission, evaporation, and surface properties. Those changes can feed back on the atmosphere. But West African monsoon variability is also influenced by ocean temperatures and large-scale energy contrasts, including aerosol forcing. It would be a mistake to describe Sahel rainfall as simply the result of local vegetation loss or restoration. Local and remote processes operate together, and their relative importance must be evaluated with data.

The same caution applies to claims about the Amazon. Forests contribute substantial moisture through evapotranspiration, and land-cover changes can affect regional energy and moisture cycling. Some of that water falls again as rain after transport through the atmosphere. But a statement about the proportion of rainfall 'made by the forest' needs a defined region, season, and moisture-tracking method; vapor may arrive from the Atlantic, pass over many surfaces, and be recycled several times. The physical reality is a set of coupled transports, not an isolated pump operated by a single forest.

How do scientists measure land-atmosphere exchange? A soil-moisture probe records conditions at a selected depth. Satellite sensors can estimate near-surface moisture or vegetation conditions over broad areas, but those observations do not directly reveal the water accessible to deep roots. Towers equipped for eddy covariance measure turbulent exchanges of heat, water vapor, and carbon dioxide between a surface and the air. Weather stations measure radiation, wind, humidity, and temperature. Stream gauges and water budgets constrain where rainfall goes. Models combine these observations with equations for surface energy, vegetation, soil water, and atmospheric motion.

A good field experiment compares more than two hot afternoons. Did the surfaces receive similar incoming radiation? Was one site windy and the other sheltered? How much water was stored in each soil profile? How much energy went into evaporation rather than heating the air? Did a hot day dry the soil, or did an existing soil-moisture deficit contribute to the heat? Observed correlation is not enough to determine the direction of cause. Researchers use controlled comparisons, coupled models, and measurements over time to identify feedbacks rather than merely simultaneous changes.

Land-surface models face a problem of scale. A grid cell may include forests, irrigated fields, bare soil, wetlands, and rock. Each patch exchanges heat and water differently, and its behavior changes as plants grow, soil dries, snow melts, or a fire passes through. A model that averages a patch of wet soil with a patch of very dry soil may predict neither patch's actual evaporation because the response to moisture is nonlinear. Scientists therefore use subgrid representations, observations, and alternative parameterizations to test how much the unresolved patchwork matters.

There is also a difference between a feedback and a forcing. A new irrigation system is an intervention that alters the land surface directly. A drought that dries soil can create a feedback by amplifying heat, which may increase further evaporative demand. Rising carbon dioxide acts as an atmospheric forcing and also changes plant physiology, potentially affecting stomata and leaf area. The same variable can participate in several pathways at once, so a complete assessment must say which cause and which feedback it is considering.

Return to our two fields. If we measure only air temperature, we might decide that one was simply hotter. If we measure soil water, leaf conductance, radiation, sensible and latent heat, and the boundary-layer response, the difference becomes an explanation. We can see how water was used to transfer energy into evaporation, how vegetation controlled that transfer, and how the altered exchange may influence clouds and later weather. The land was not merely receiving a forecast from the sky. It was helping determine what the sky would do next.

The largest lesson is that Earth's climate does not stop at the ground. The boundary between atmosphere and land is crossed continuously by energy, vapor, carbon, and momentum. Soils remember earlier rain. Plants respond to today's humidity and yesterday's drought. A change in land cover can alter a river, a cloud, and an energy budget at once. Understanding these pathways is essential because the ground beneath us is part of the climate system, not the stage on which climate happens.

## Scientific companion: surface energy and a moisture-limited feedback

**Surface energy partition.** A simple daytime land-surface budget is $R_n=H+LE+G+\Delta S$, where net radiation $R_n$ is partitioned into sensible heat $H$, latent heat flux $LE$, ground heat flux $G$, and changes in storage $\Delta S$ (with an explicit sign convention). Water-limited evaporation often decreases $LE$ and can increase $H$ for a similar $R_n$, but advection, cloudiness and other budget terms also affect the result.

**Scale example.** Evaporating one millimeter of water over one square meter corresponds to approximately one kilogram of water and about 2.45 megajoules of latent energy near ordinary surface temperatures. Spread over twenty-four hours, this equals about 28 watts per square meter. It is a budget conversion, not an assertion that irrigation necessarily produces a 28-watt cooling of local air.

**Coupling regimes.** In energy-limited wet settings, extra soil water may have little effect on evapotranspiration; in moisture-limited dry settings, soil moisture can limit latent heat. Feedbacks can be strongest near transitions between regimes. Whether dry soils enhance or suppress convection depends on moisture, lifting, atmospheric stability, mesoscale contrasts and boundary-layer growth.

**Vegetation tradeoffs.** Stomata exchange carbon uptake for water loss; root access, leaf area, vapor pressure deficit, CO2 concentration and species physiology modify transpiration. Forest and grassland comparisons must account for both surface albedo and latent heat, as well as roughness and snow, not just carbon sequestration.

### Questions and investigations

1. If two fields receive similar net radiation, how can the wet one remain cooler than the dry one?
2. Why could a dry surface increase boundary-layer turbulence but still fail to trigger a thunderstorm?
3. Compare the effect of replacing forest with grassland in a snow-covered northern region and a humid low-latitude region. Which competing terms would you examine?
4. How can irrigation cool a field while increasing pressure on a regional groundwater or river system?
5. Design observations that could distinguish soil moisture amplifying a heat wave from the heat wave merely drying the soil.
