# Why High Tide Is Late: Gravity, Basins, and Moving Water

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The Moon rises above a harbor. The people on the pier can see it plainly, yet the water is still going out. Several hours later the tide reaches its highest point. If the Moon is pulling the sea upward, why isn't the high tide directly beneath it? In another harbor, on the same planet, the water rises twice a day; farther along the coast, the two high tides are unequal; on some shores, one rise dominates the day. The tide is perhaps the most familiar clock in the ocean, and also a warning against imagining the ocean as a motionless covering that simply bulges toward the Moon.

To understand this clock, separate the forcing from the response. The forcing is astronomical. The response belongs to moving water in a world of coastlines, basins, shallow shelves, friction, and rotation. These are related but different problems. Knowing the Moon's location does not, by itself, tell you what a tide gauge will read in a particular harbor.

The gravitational field of the Moon is not equally strong at every place on Earth. Points nearer to the Moon experience a slightly stronger attraction than points farther away. The Sun also creates a differential gravitational field, despite being much more distant. Ordinary gravitational attraction moves Earth and its oceans together; a tide arises from the small differences in that attraction across the planet. In the reference frame moving with the Earth–Moon system, these differences can be represented by a tide-generating potential. Its spatial pattern has two broad lobes, one roughly toward the Moon and another away from it.

The far-side lobe is sometimes explained by saying that the Moon pulls water off the far side, or that a second independent force mysteriously lifts it. Neither picture is necessary. The Moon accelerates the Earth's center more than material on the far side; relative to the Earth's center, that material is left behind. The mathematical description compares accelerations at different locations. The two-lobe equilibrium-tide picture follows from that comparison on an ideal, ocean-covered Earth. But an equilibrium shape is not the actual daily tide. It is a useful starting hypothesis for a much more complicated fluid response.

Earth's rotation moves a shoreline through the changing astronomical forcing. The Moon also moves eastward in its orbit, so the time between successive passages beneath the Moon at one longitude is about twenty-four hours and fifty minutes: the lunar day. If a coast were dominated by two similar lunar tidal peaks each lunar day, their spacing would be about twelve hours and twenty-five minutes. The stronger astronomical lunar semidiurnal constituent is conventionally called M two. A solar semidiurnal constituent is called S two. Their periods differ slightly, so their contributions alternately reinforce and oppose each other.

When the Sun and Moon are roughly aligned around new and full moon, the principal semidiurnal effects tend to reinforce. Oceanographers call the resulting larger tidal range a spring tide. Spring here refers to a rising or jumping of the tide, not the season. Around quarter moons, the principal components tend to work against one another, producing neap tides with a smaller range. Exact timing depends on local dynamical response and other constituents. On a tide chart you may see the largest range occur after, rather than precisely at, the astronomical alignment.

Imagine water in a long bathtub. Tip the tub at regular intervals and the water sloshes. If you choose a forcing period near the water's preferred oscillation period, the motion grows. If you make the same motion at a different rhythm, or introduce friction and obstacles, the response changes. An ocean basin can behave similarly, though its waves travel on a rotating Earth and interact with far more complicated geometry. Water has inertia; it cannot appear instantly at the point the tide-generating force favors. As tidal waves cross shelves and turn into bays, they are delayed, accelerated, reflected, dissipated, or amplified.

That is why the Bay of Fundy, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, makes such a good experiment. At the head of the bay, tidal ranges can approach sixteen meters. The Moon is not sixteen times more forceful there than elsewhere. The shape and length of the bay, its narrowing shallows, and the natural oscillation of the Gulf of Maine and Fundy system combine to magnify the incoming semidiurnal tide. Canada's hydrographic service identifies the inner Bay of Fundy and Ungava Bay as places with Earth's largest ranges. The amount is a range between high and low water, not the height of a single wave crest traveling along a beach.

In other places, the tide is muted. At an amphidromic point, the amplitude of a particular tidal constituent is very small, while the phase rotates around the point in a wider region. On maps of predicted tidal height, bands of equal phase can appear to spin around such centers. Nothing requires the ocean surface there to stand absolutely still: other constituents, wind and pressure still affect the water. The pattern reveals that tidal forcing is launching a network of waves, not arranging a pair of static hills in the sea.

The rotation of Earth matters for those waves. A shallow-water gravity wave travels with a speed controlled primarily by gravity and water depth. In an ideal long-wave approximation, its speed is the square root of gravitational acceleration times depth. Over four thousand meters of water, such a long wave can move extremely rapidly. On a continental shelf it slows, and its interaction with the bottom grows. Coriolis acceleration can turn a long wave into a coastally trapped response, with sea-level changes and currents that vary from place to place. The wave can amplify in a funnel-shaped bay, but it can also weaken through bottom friction and energy loss.

A tide gauge records the elevation of the sea surface relative to a reference. That is not the same as the speed of the tidal current. At an estuary mouth, the strongest inflow may occur well before high water. The current may turn near high or low water, but only if the local geometry and tidal dynamics permit such an approximation. In complicated straits, current reversal and tidal height can have very different schedules. Water can be moving fast even when the surface elevation changes slowly. A navigation chart showing the height of the water and a current table showing its horizontal motion answer different questions.

The sea surface also moves for reasons unrelated to the Moon. A persistent wind may pile water against a coast. Low atmospheric pressure can contribute to a rise in sea level, and storms can drive surge. Seasonal heating changes water density; ocean circulation changes coastal sea level. The astronomical tide is comparatively predictable because its astronomical forcing can be calculated. Still, a prediction for high tide should not be mistaken for a forecast of the total water level during a hurricane. Surge and tide may combine, though their interaction can itself be nonlinear in shallow estuaries.

How do we learn the local response rather than guess it? Keep a long record. A tide gauge measures water level against a surveyed reference and reveals repeated oscillations with different periods. Harmonic analysis represents the observed tide as a sum of constituents with characteristic frequencies, amplitudes, and phase lags. The lunar M two component is one, the solar S two component another, and additional components account for daily inequality, elliptical orbits, and local shallow-water interactions. Fit those components from observations, then use astronomical predictions to forecast when they will add and subtract in the future.

This works because a harbor often responds in a sufficiently regular way to known periodic forcing. It is not perfectly linear, and the coastline can change. Dredging, harbor walls, sedimentation, rising average sea level, and new channels can alter the response. A tide table is therefore a model grounded in long observation, not a perpetual law written directly into the Moon's path. Tide gauges and satellite altimetry reveal different aspects: gauges follow a fixed coastal site, while satellite measurements map sea-surface height over broader ocean regions and require careful referencing.

The choice of reference level matters. A depth written on a nautical chart is not automatically measured from the same zero as a civil engineer's land elevation. Mean sea level, mean lower low water, and other tidal datums are calculated over specified periods and have different practical uses. If a coastal community is assessing flooding, mixing incompatible vertical datums can turn a useful estimate into a misleading one. The statement that a storm reached two meters means little until we ask two meters above what.

Tidal currents do more than move boats. As water rushes through an inlet it carries sand and mud, scours channels, and deposits tidal shoals. Flood currents may carry sediment landward; ebb currents may export it seaward. The net transfer over a lunar cycle can be small compared with the volumes going back and forth. In estuaries, flood and ebb need not be symmetrical. One phase may be faster or shorter, and the difference can affect whether fine sediment accumulates or escapes. A tidal flat is the exposed surface of such repeated exchanges, changed by organisms and storms as well as by the Moon.

Tidal energy can also be extracted. But a turbine cannot harvest power without modifying the flow that supplies it. The exploitable energy depends on the hydrodynamics of the entire channel or basin, environmental constraints, and the effect of extraction on water levels, sediment, and ecological exchange. A claim based simply on the mass of water passing a harbor twice each day misses those limits. The same principles that make Fundy's tides so large also make the system sensitive to changes in its boundaries and friction.

The high tide that arrives after the Moon is overhead is no failure of gravity. It is the signature of a forced dynamical system with memory. The Moon and Sun supply rhythms; the basin selects which rhythms grow, fade, or arrive late. Wind, storms, and changing landforms add their own signals. The shore's daily advance and retreat is not merely astronomical. It is a conversation between celestial motion and local geography, conducted in moving water.

## Scientific companion: tides as a forced dynamical system

For a uniform shallow-water depth $h$, a long gravity wave has approximate phase speed $c=\sqrt{g h}$. Tidal motions involve rotation, basin geometry, nonlinear bottom friction and more than one forcing frequency; that simple expression alone does not predict local tide heights. A harmonic prediction may be represented as $\eta(t)=Z_0+\sum_j A_j\cos(\omega_j t-\phi_j)$, with constituents fitted from measured water levels. Here $\eta$ is the predicted sea-surface elevation relative to a specified datum.

**Tidal range versus flood risk.** Tidal range is high minus low water. Total water level includes astronomical tide plus meteorological and oceanographic departures, with non-additive interactions possible in shallow water. Height and horizontal current are separate variables.
