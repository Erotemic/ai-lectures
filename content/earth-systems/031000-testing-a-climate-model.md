# When a Climate Model Is Wrong: Testing Skill, Uncertainty, and Trust

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose two climate models predict the same rise in global mean temperature over the coming decades. One produces too much tropical rainfall, puts its storms in the wrong ocean basins, and has a poor seasonal cycle. The other captures those patterns much better. Have the models agreed? On one number, yes. On the system that produces that number, perhaps not. A climate model can get the right answer for the wrong reasons, and it can get an individual weather event wrong while still representing important climate statistics well. Evaluating it requires deciding what question it was meant to answer, what evidence could challenge it, and what forms of disagreement matter.

Start with a familiar experience: the weather forecast predicts rain tomorrow, and the sun shines. If the forecast gave a ninety-percent chance of rain at your location, the outcome is surprising but not impossible. One failure does not establish that the probabilities are badly calibrated. Across many comparable forecasts, however, rain should occur on about nine out of ten days assigned a ninety-percent chance, using the forecast's precise event definition. Calibration tests the statistical meaning of a probability. Sharpness asks whether the system can provide informative probabilities rather than always returning fifty percent. Skill compares its performance with a reference forecast, perhaps climatology or a simpler operational method. These are different qualities.

Climate projections are harder to evaluate because they extend across decades and centuries, under specified external conditions rather than fully predicted human behavior. We can still test physical components, seasonal cycles, past responses, and shorter initialized predictions. We can ask whether atmospheric models reproduce large-scale circulation, whether ocean models transport heat plausibly, whether sea ice varies with season and forcing, and whether a simulated temperature pattern matches independent observations. We can make historical hindcasts and analyze how they were initialized and tuned. We can test idealized physical problems with known answers. No single score establishes that a model is correct for every future quantity of interest.

At the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge, Massachusetts, Edward Lorenz gave this issue a deeper foundation in a 1963 paper on deterministic nonperiodic flow. In nonlinear systems of the sort relevant to fluid dynamics, very small differences in initial conditions can grow until trajectories diverge. This is often summarized through the butterfly effect, but the important point is more precise: the governing equations can be deterministic while long-term details of the realized weather remain difficult or impossible to forecast accurately. Failure to predict a storm years ahead is not by itself evidence that a model cannot estimate how changing greenhouse forcing shifts the distribution of temperatures or precipitation patterns.

Weather and climate differ in their predictability, not in the equations that govern them. A roulette wheel can have unpredictable individual outcomes while showing stable statistics under repeated similar conditions. The analogy has limits—the climate has feedbacks, slow reservoirs, and changing forcings—but it helps separate a particular realization from a distribution. Klaus Hasselmann's 1976 work on stochastic climate models showed how rapidly changing weather disturbances can act as an effective random forcing on slower climate components such as the ocean. The slow system integrates this fluctuating input and acquires its own memory. That framework helps explain why internal variability persists even without changing external forcing and why the climate response to forcing can be analyzed statistically.

Now consider a model with a systematic bias. Suppose it puts the tropical rain belt too far south. That can change cloud radiation, trade winds, and ocean temperatures, which feed back on the rain belt. If a scientist evaluates only global-average precipitation, the displaced pattern might look fine because total rainfall remains similar. A map of regional bias reveals the error. A time series of daily precipitation reveals a different error: perhaps too many light rain days and too few intense ones. The model could pass one test and fail the others. This is why evaluation protocols include many fields, regions, timescales, and relationships among quantities.

An especially important relationship is energy conservation. Imagine that a model simulates the observed global-average surface temperature but maintains an incorrect net heat input at the top of the atmosphere. Its ocean may slowly accumulate heat for the wrong reason, or an error in cloud reflection may compensate for an error in outgoing infrared radiation. Observed top-of-atmosphere radiation, changes in ocean heat content, and modeled surface temperatures help distinguish these hypotheses. Agreement on temperature alone would hide the compensating errors. The same logic applies to water and carbon cycles: get both the stocks and the fluxes as right as the available evidence allows.

There are several ways to test a model without pretending that its future can be observed today. In a process test, compare a modeled physical relationship with measurements: for example, the change in evaporation with atmospheric demand under particular soil conditions. In an idealized test, run the numerical method on a mathematical fluid problem whose behavior can be checked against theory or a more accurate calculation. In a hindcast, initialize or force the model using information from the past and compare its predictions against subsequent data. The degree of independence matters. If the historical measurements were used to tune the model, the hindcast may still be informative, but it is not a pristine out-of-sample test of every aspect.

For an initialized seasonal forecast, a fair assessment may run many past forecasts using only information that would have been available at each start date. A retrospectively improved observing system can complicate this comparison. Forecast quality may also depend on which regions and seasons are chosen. A system can skillfully predict some features of tropical sea-surface temperature while being less skillful for regional summer rainfall. Showing average performance without its geographic variation can mislead users. A useful verification report says which variable, region, event definition, lead time, and reference forecast were used.

Long-term projections introduce several kinds of uncertainty. The first is internal variability: different realizations of weather and ocean fluctuations under the same physical model and forcing. The second is model-response uncertainty: physical and numerical choices affect the magnitude and pattern of the response. The third is scenario uncertainty: the future pathway of emissions, land use, and other external influences is not known. The relative importance of these terms changes with forecast horizon, geography, and variable. For near-term regional rainfall, internal variability may dominate. Over longer periods, scenario differences become increasingly important for global temperature outcomes.

Ed Hawkins and Rowan Sutton published a widely cited analysis in 2009 of the potential to narrow uncertainty in regional climate predictions. One lesson is that a forecast's uncertainty should not be described by a single permanent band around a line. A model may explain some of the uncertain spread through better physical understanding and observations, while other variability remains inherent to the system. Improving model physics does not eliminate the fact that the exact future sequence of storms cannot be known for decades in advance.

Ensembles are central to estimating the spread of plausible responses. But there are several kinds. An initial-condition ensemble runs the same model with slightly different starting states. A perturbed-parameter ensemble changes uncertain coefficients within a modeling framework. A multi-model ensemble combines systems built by different groups, often with different dynamics, parameterizations, and resolutions. These are not equivalent samples. A large initial-condition ensemble can map variability within one model without proving that its cloud feedback is accurate. A multi-model ensemble can expose structural spread, but its members may share important scientific ideas, software, or observational calibration data.

Suppose five model runs agree closely. The first interpretation is that five independent modeling teams have reached a similar result using genuinely different methods and assumptions. The second is that a single model has been run five times from slightly different starting states. The third is that five models share nearly the same code or parameterization. In the latter cases, agreement does not provide five fully independent checks on model physics. In 2017, Reto Knutti and colleagues described a method for weighting climate model projections using both performance and interdependence. The point is not that one universal set of weights solves uncertainty, but that independence and skill matter when interpreting ensembles.

There is a danger in giving an ensemble range a meaning it does not possess. Imagine that twenty models project one result and ten project another. The fraction of models is not necessarily the probability that the future will follow either result. Modeling centers do not constitute a random sample drawn from all physically possible Earths. Their output depends on shared assumptions, choices of forcings, tuning, computational constraints, and which centers participate. Probability statements can be made under explicit statistical assumptions and constraints, but a raw multi-model histogram should not automatically be read as an objective betting market on climate futures.

What should a scientist do when different evaluations point in different directions? One model may simulate present-day tropical clouds well but respond too strongly to warming. Another may reproduce the historical global mean temperature but have biased ocean heat uptake. Rather than choosing the most visually pleasing comparison, scientists examine the causal link between each test and the prediction of interest. To evaluate future changes in the frequency of heat waves, for example, we care about temperature distributions, land-atmosphere coupling, atmospheric circulation, and forced warming—not only today's average temperature. The evaluation target must be chosen before interpreting which model is better for that question.

Evidence beyond contemporary observations also matters. Paleoclimate archives constrain how climate systems responded to past forcings and large reorganizations, though the uncertainties and boundary conditions differ. Satellite radiation measurements test energy exchange. Ocean observations constrain heat uptake. Laboratory studies and theory inform microscopic cloud, radiation, and chemical processes. No one source can independently validate a global Earth-system model. Converging evidence from methods with different weaknesses provides stronger support than repeated measurements of the same quantity under shared assumptions.

What about running at much higher resolution? In principle more detail can improve representation of coastlines, currents, topography, and organized convection. But more grid cells do not automatically improve every quantity. If a parameterization was calibrated at a coarser scale, it may need to change. If a simulation still lacks realistic aerosols or ocean mixing, fine spatial detail can coexist with serious climate biases. Increased resolution can improve aspects of extreme weather even while long-term energy imbalance remains imperfect. The correct evaluation is not how impressive a rendered map looks, but how the simulation performs on independently justified scientific tests.

A mature model-evaluation practice welcomes failures with diagnostic power. If a model misses a known seasonal phase shift, identify the mechanism. If its top-of-atmosphere energy balance drifts, check the physical budget and numerical coupling. If a projected regional response varies wildly among ensemble members, report how much comes from internal variability and how much from structural differences. If a result is sensitive to an uncertain cloud parameter, show that sensitivity. The purpose of evaluation is not to defend a computer product. It is to discover what our physical understanding can and cannot presently support.

We can now return to the two models that agreed on global warming while disagreeing about tropical rain. They may both support a conclusion about the large-scale energy response under a specified forcing. They do not thereby provide equal evidence for tropical precipitation or downstream flood risks. Agreement should be interpreted at the level of the variable, mechanism, and timescale actually tested. When scientists and decision-makers make those distinctions, uncertainty becomes informative rather than merely an excuse for indecision.

An Earth-system model is a set of physical hypotheses translated into computation. Its cells and numerical methods express conservation and motion; its parameterizations express uncertain small-scale processes; its coupler makes the planet's subsystems interact; data assimilation turns observations into estimates of present states; experimental protocols make counterfactual comparisons possible. Every layer introduces assumptions and creates new tests. Trust in a model is therefore not a yes-or-no judgment about computers. It is a documented conclusion about which mechanisms the model captures, which questions its outputs support, and where independent evidence still disagrees. That is how a simulated Earth becomes a scientific instrument rather than merely a convincing picture.

## Scientific companion: interpreting model skill and ensembles

### Evaluation dimensions

| Metric family | Question answered | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean-state bias | Is the time-average field approximately right? | Proper variability, extremes, or feedback response |
| Variability and teleconnections | Are correlations, spectra and patterns plausible? | Correct forcing response or causal mechanism |
| Conservation budgets | Do mass, energy, water and carbon balance? | Correct geographic distribution of effects |
| Hindcast skill | Does it anticipate withheld subsequent observations? | Out-of-sample skill if it used future data to tune or initialize |
| Process comparison | Does a hypothesized mechanism match independent evidence? | Correct behavior of every coupled feedback |
| Scenario ensemble | How do specified assumptions affect climate outcomes? | An empirical probability of future socioeconomic choices |

### Separating uncertainty sources

It is helpful, but approximate, to distinguish internal variability, model-response uncertainty, and scenario uncertainty. Their relative importance depends on horizon, variable, and region. An ensemble from a common model chiefly samples internal variability when physics and forcing are held fixed; multiple structurally distinct models additionally sample some model response uncertainty. Neither is necessarily an independent random sample from a known probability distribution of future Earths.
