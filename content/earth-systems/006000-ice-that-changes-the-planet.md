# Ice That Changes the Planet: Snow, Sea Ice, Glaciers, and Ice Sheets

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine flying north in the spring and seeing a white ocean crossed by dark leads of open water. The contrast looks simple: ice against sea. But those two surfaces differ in how much sunlight they reflect, how they exchange heat with the atmosphere, how waves move, and how salt and freshwater circulate. Farther inland, snow is accumulating on an ice sheet so thick that it moves under its own weight. A change to the ocean ice and a change to that land ice can look similar in a photograph while having very different consequences for sea level. We need a vocabulary that distinguishes frozen surfaces by what they are made of, where they rest, and how they move.

The cryosphere includes snow, sea ice, lake and river ice, mountain glaciers, vast ice sheets, ice shelves, and frozen ground. These are not interchangeable parts. Sea ice begins as ocean water and floats from the beginning. Glacier ice generally starts as snow falling on land, compressing through firn and eventually into dense ice. A glacier flows downhill under gravity; an ice sheet spreads over an enormous area and can reach the coast through fast outlet glaciers. An ice shelf is floating glacier ice still attached to land ice upstream. Permafrost, finally, refers to ground that remains at or below freezing for at least two consecutive years; the ground can contain sediment, rock, organic matter, and varying amounts of ice. The name describes a thermal state, not a single slab of solid ice.

Why does this collection matter to the planetary energy budget? Start with reflectivity, or albedo. Bright snow reflects a large share of incoming sunlight; dark open ocean absorbs most of it. The National Snow and Ice Data Center describes an approximate contrast: open ocean reflects only a small fraction of sunlight under typical conditions, while bare sea ice commonly reflects roughly half to two thirds and fresh snow can reflect still more. These are illustrative ranges, not fixed optical constants: sun angle, cloud, snow grains, melt ponds, and surface roughness change the actual reflectivity. If warming removes snow or ice, the newly exposed darker surface can absorb additional energy, encouraging further warming and loss of ice. This is the ice-albedo feedback.

A positive feedback does not mean that every patch of melted ice causes runaway collapse. It means an initial change can reinforce itself within particular conditions and timescales. Winter polar night brings little sunlight, so an albedo change then has a different immediate effect from the same change in midsummer. Fresh snowfall can temporarily brighten the surface. Clouds modify incoming and outgoing radiation. Ocean circulation can deliver heat under ice even when surface air remains cold. A feedback must be specified with its seasonal setting, affected area, and competition from other processes.

There is another kind of energy accounting that is easy to overlook. Ice absorbs heat when it melts without necessarily warming above its melting temperature; the energy goes into changing phase. This is latent heat of fusion. Conversely, freezing releases latent heat. Snow can also insulate the ground and the ice below it, reducing winter heat loss. A deep snow layer may protect soil from the cold air even as the snow surface reflects sunlight. Ice can therefore be reflective and insulating, and it can store or release energy through phase change. One familiar quantity, surface temperature, cannot summarize all three roles.

Consider sea ice forming in a polar winter. The ocean surface cools to its local freezing point, which depends on salinity, and ice begins to grow. During freezing, salt is largely rejected into the surrounding water or concentrated in brine pockets. The remaining water can become saltier and denser, sometimes helping to drive mixing or deeper-water formation. As ice melts, relatively fresh water enters the surface and may stabilize a light upper layer. Ice growth, drift, deformation, and melt respond to winds, currents, ocean heat, and atmospheric weather. It is possible for ice to be pushed together and thickened in one region even when the total area of sea ice is declining elsewhere. A map of ice extent cannot by itself give the total mass of floating ice.

You may hear that sea ice is 'irrelevant to sea level because it floats.' The useful physics behind that shorthand comes from Archimedes: floating ice displaces seawater with the same weight as the ice. Melting the ice therefore adds approximately the amount of water already displaced. Because freshwater and saltwater have different densities, the direct sea-level effect is not mathematically exactly zero, but it is tiny compared with the effects of land-ice loss and warming seawater. That is the distinction to retain. Sea ice matters strongly for reflection, air-sea heat exchange, ecology, and the way the ocean is insulated or exposed; it is not the main direct supplier of additional water to the ocean.

A glacier on land faces a different budget. Snow adds mass; surface melting, sublimation, wind transport, and sometimes ice breaking into the sea or a lake remove it. The balance is not simply 'did the glacier's snout advance this year?' Ice is continually moving through the glacier. The terminus may be responding to changes that began years earlier, and local geometry affects the response. Glaciologists measure accumulation and ablation at points with stakes and snow pits, compare surface elevations, and use geodetic observations to estimate glacier-wide mass changes. They often express the balance as a depth of water equivalent, allowing fluffy snow and dense ice to be compared by mass.

The flow itself is a major part of the story. Glacier ice deforms under stress. Some glaciers also slide over their beds, their movement influenced by the pressure and arrangement of water at the ice-bed interface. A mountain glacier may move only a modest distance in a year, while an outlet glacier can send large volumes of ice toward the sea. Where a glacier descends through a valley, it can grind rock, transport debris, and leave moraines. Meltwater can carry fine sediment far beyond the ice front. The hydrology of the surrounding valley changes as snow and ice storage grow or diminish. A glacier is not merely a frozen reservoir; it is a moving participant in a watershed and a landscape.

Greenland and Antarctica hold the only present-day continental-scale ice sheets. Their mass budgets depend on snowfall, surface processes, and the discharge of ice through outlet glaciers. In Greenland, summer surface melt and runoff are important contributors to mass loss, alongside ice discharge. Over much of Antarctica, the interior is cold enough that surface melt is not the dominant mechanism; ice flow to marine margins and ocean-driven changes beneath floating ice shelves are critical. It would be a mistake to tell a single story in which warmer air melts the top of every ice sheet in the same way.

At the edge of Antarctica, a floating ice shelf can act as a mechanical support for the grounded glacier upstream. Think of it not as a solid dam, but as a stressed extension of flowing ice. Contact with embayment walls, islands, and shallow seabed highs can resist the shelf's motion. When a shelf thins or breaks apart, that resistance can diminish. The ice that was already floating does not directly add much to global sea level by melting, yet its loss can allow grounded ice to speed up and add new mass to the ocean. Observations after the Larsen B Ice Shelf disintegrated on the Antarctic Peninsula in 2002 showed substantial acceleration of the glaciers that had fed it. This was a measured dynamical response, not merely an inference from the appearance of the vanished shelf.

The line at which land-based ice begins to float is the grounding line. Its position depends on ice thickness, bed shape, ocean level, and the stress transmitted through neighboring ice. Where an ice sheet sits on bedrock that deepens toward its interior, retreat of a grounding line can, in certain configurations, expose progressively thicker ice to the ocean and promote further retreat. This is the proposed marine ice sheet instability mechanism. But the word 'instability' is not a prediction that an entire continental ice sheet will suddenly vanish. Shelf buttressing, bed topography, ocean forcing, and numerical treatments of ice deformation affect the rate and even the circumstances of retreat. Assessments distinguish observed present-day change from possible longer-term dynamic responses and from the uncertainty of the most extreme projections.

The ice sheets are losing mass in the satellite era, though the losses differ in space and from year to year. The NASA-German GRACE and GRACE Follow-On missions infer changes in Earth's mass distribution by measuring tiny variations in gravity. Their records reveal large-scale changes in Greenland and Antarctica and require adjustments for other processes, including changes in the solid Earth. A different set of instruments, including ICESat-2, measures surface elevation using the travel time of reflected light. An elevation change must be interpreted with snow density, firn compaction, and ice dynamics before being converted to a mass change. Neither gravity nor elevation measurement sees the future; both constrain what has happened.

Now add sea level. When grounded ice is transferred to the ocean, it increases ocean water mass. Warming seawater also expands, even without additional water. Satellite altimetry measures the changing height of the sea surface; gravimetry constrains changing mass; temperature and salinity profiles constrain expansion. These measurement families complement one another. Global mean sea level is a useful average, but local relative sea level also depends on land movement and regional ocean effects. Losing a large ice mass even changes gravity and the distribution of seawater, so the regional sea-level pattern is not a simple uniform rise everywhere.

Ice changes water availability far inland as well. Mountain snow and glaciers store winter precipitation and release water seasonally. A basin with glaciers may experience increased meltwater runoff for a time as ice storage is lost, followed eventually by reduced glacier contribution once less ice remains. The pattern depends on climate, glacier size, rain, snow, groundwater, and the period being compared. Snow can accumulate in one storm and disappear in a warm week; a glacier's long-term response may unfold through decades. Managing water with only a yearly precipitation total overlooks that timing.

Frozen ground introduces another connection between ice, water, and carbon. When permafrost warms, the ground can settle as ice within it melts, changing drainage and sometimes forming ponds or eroding slopes. Organic matter previously protected by cold conditions may become available to microbial decomposition. The gases produced depend partly on oxygen conditions: carbon dioxide commonly dominates in aerated settings, while methane can be important in waterlogged settings. A warmer, drier landscape can also experience changed fire regimes. But the carbon response is not a predetermined instantaneous emission of all frozen organic matter. Plant growth, soil moisture, microbial activity, fire, and the depth of thaw interact. That complexity is why frozen-ground carbon feedbacks require field measurements and models, not an assumption that a single temperature threshold releases the entire store.

The cryosphere contains many clocks. Sea ice can form and melt within one year. Snowpacks change from storm to storm. A glacier records decades of changing snowfall, melt, and motion. Ice sheets can respond on human timescales at their coastal margins while retaining the memory of climates thousands of years old in their interiors. Some consequences of sustained ice loss persist long after the initiating climate change, because rebuilding a large ice mass requires a long surplus of snowfall over losses. It is possible to measure an accelerating present-day retreat while remaining uncertain about the exact response centuries ahead. The certainty of an observed direction and the precision of a distant projection are not the same claim.

Return to the aerial view of the white sea. The open lead absorbs sunlight differently, transfers heat and moisture to the air, and can change the conditions for making ice nearby. Beyond the coast, a glacier moves across a rocky bed toward a floating shelf. Beneath it, the ocean transfers heat to ice, while gravity and the shelf's geometry govern the speed of ice upstream. Farther north, a river's flow depends partly on how much winter snow and glacier ice is released in summer. One word, 'melting,' hides all of these exchanges. Ice changes the planet not because everything frozen behaves alike, but because each kind of ice occupies a different position in Earth's linked budgets of energy, water, mass, and carbon.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Four ice categories and their budget consequences

| Component | Origin and geometry | Principal budget variable | Main Earth-system connections |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sea ice | Seawater frozen in place or drifted; floating | Area, thickness, concentration, seasonal mass | Albedo; insulation; brine rejection; freshwater stratification |
| Land glacier | Accumulated snowfall, flowing under gravity | Mass balance, discharge, terminus and elevation | Freshwater timing, landscape erosion, sea level if delivered to ocean |
| Ice sheet and ice shelf | Vast land-based ice sheet; shelves are floating extensions | Interior accumulation, marginal losses and grounded-ice discharge | Long-term sea level, ocean forcing, buttressing and grounding-line dynamics |
| Permafrost | Ground at or below 0 C for at least two consecutive years | Ground temperature, ground ice, thaw depth | Hydrology, infrastructure, vegetation and soil-carbon processing |

A glacier's **mass balance** is accumulation minus ablation and discharge as defined for its measurement boundary. Glacial terminus motion is not a direct one-to-one substitute for annual mass balance.

### Floating ice and sea level

Archimedes' principle gives displaced seawater volume \(V_d = m_{\mathrm{ice}} / \rho_{\mathrm{sea}}\). Melted freshwater occupies approximately \(m_{\mathrm{ice}}/\rho_{\mathrm{fresh}}\), slightly greater volume because freshwater is less dense. Thus the direct contribution of melting floating sea ice is small, **not mathematically exactly zero**. Floating ice-shelf changes can affect the sea level indirectly by releasing the flow of grounded ice.

### Questions worth thinking through

1. Why would sea-ice decline strongly affect the energy budget while contributing little directly to sea-level rise?
2. How can a floating ice shelf increase global sea level *indirectly* when it breaks apart?
3. What does a change in ice-sheet gravity reveal that a surface-elevation map does not measure directly?
4. What conditions make grounding-line retreat self-reinforcing, and why does that not specify a unique rate?
5. How can frozen ground release different greenhouse gases under different moisture and oxygen conditions?
