# The Chemistry Between Clouds: Sunlight, Ozone, and the Air That Cleans Itself

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A weather report may describe clear skies, light winds, and a warm afternoon. An air-quality report for the same place may warn about ozone. It sounds contradictory if we imagine pollution as smoke that must be visible. But the atmosphere can manufacture a pollutant from gases that, taken separately, are difficult to see. Light from the Sun drives some of the reactions. Other reactions take place at night. Air that has drifted from a city into a rural valley can be chemically different from the air that left the city hours earlier.

The atmosphere is not simply a container for gases. It is a chemical reactor with changing ingredients, temperatures, sunlight, clouds, and mixing. Some gases enter from vehicles, industry, plants, soils, fires, or oceans. Others are made by reactions in the air itself. Molecules can be transformed into new substances, removed to the surface, dissolved into clouds, or transported far from their sources. To understand what we breathe and how the atmosphere influences climate, we need to follow both transport and chemistry.

Start with ozone, a molecule containing three oxygen atoms. Ozone has a peculiar reputation because we need some of it and should avoid breathing too much of it. High in the stratosphere, a region above much of our weather, ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation that would otherwise reach Earth's surface. Near the ground, ozone is a reactive oxidant that can damage living tissues and contribute to smog. It is the same molecule in two places, with different consequences because exposure and atmospheric role depend on where it is. There is no good version and bad version at the molecular level.

How is ozone made? In the stratosphere, sufficiently energetic ultraviolet radiation can split ordinary oxygen molecules, which contain two oxygen atoms. Some of the free oxygen atoms then combine with oxygen molecules, in collisions with a third body that carries away excess energy, to make ozone. Other ultraviolet wavelengths can break ozone apart. Formation and destruction operate together. The balance is influenced by sunlight, temperature, motion of air, and catalytic chemical cycles. The famous ozone layer is therefore not an immobile roof. It is the outcome of ongoing reactions and circulation.

Near the surface, sunlight also helps make ozone, but the dominant polluted-air pathway needs a different cast of chemicals. Consider nitrogen dioxide, one member of a family called nitrogen oxides. In sunlight, nitrogen dioxide can split into nitric oxide and an oxygen atom. The oxygen atom can then help form ozone. At first this sounds as though nitrogen dioxide emissions should automatically cause ozone to accumulate. There is a catch: nitric oxide can react with ozone, turning back into nitrogen dioxide while consuming the ozone molecule. If those were the only reactions, production and removal could largely cancel. We need another reaction pathway that converts nitric oxide back into nitrogen dioxide without destroying ozone.

That pathway is supplied by the oxidation of other gases, including carbon monoxide, methane, and volatile organic compounds, or V O C's. Some of those compounds are released by fuel combustion and solvents; others come naturally from vegetation. Their reactions make peroxy radicals. Those radicals can convert nitric oxide to nitrogen dioxide without consuming an ozone molecule. Sunlight can split the newly formed nitrogen dioxide and make more ozone. The system can now produce ozone in excess of the rapid cycle that merely swaps nitrogen oxides back and forth. That is the chemical reason ground-level ozone depends on sunlight, nitrogen oxides, and an adequate supply of other reactive carbon compounds.

Ozone responds nonlinearly to its chemical precursors, so cutting one ingredient need not produce a proportional drop in concentration. Imagine a sunny urban street with very high nitric oxide emissions. Nitric oxide can consume ozone near the street, even as nitrogen oxides support ozone production farther downwind. Reduce those emissions a little, and the immediate roadside ozone might rise because less nitric oxide is available to destroy it. Meanwhile, a different mix of emission reductions could lower ozone over the wider region. Atmospheric chemists distinguish regimes in which ozone production is primarily limited by nitrogen oxides from regimes in which it is strongly sensitive to volatile organic compounds and radical chemistry. The controlling regime can change between a city center, its suburbs, and the rural air downwind.

So the statement that ozone is produced by pollution is correct but incomplete. Ozone concentrations are a history of emissions, sunlight, temperature, transport, chemical reactions, and removal. You cannot diagnose the right emission-control strategy from one ozone measurement without knowing the surrounding chemistry. This is one reason atmospheric models include chemical reaction networks rather than assigning every molecule a fixed fraction of a source's emissions.

Where do the radicals that begin many of these reactions come from? One of the most important is the hydroxyl radical, usually written as O H: an oxygen atom bonded to a hydrogen atom, with an unpaired electron that makes it highly reactive. Some hydroxyl is produced when sunlight breaks ozone and the resulting excited oxygen atoms react with water vapor. Other reaction pathways can produce or recycle it. A hydroxyl radical may survive for a very short time before reacting. Yet the chemistry sustained by an enormous succession of such radicals is central to the atmosphere's capacity to transform and remove reactive gases.

Scientists sometimes call hydroxyl the detergent of the atmosphere. The metaphor is helpful only up to a point. A household detergent takes dirt away without necessarily making it into something harmful. Atmospheric oxidation can move a molecule toward products that are more easily removed, but it may also create new pollutants along the way. Oxidizing a volatile organic compound can produce oxygenated gases, ozone-forming radicals, and material that later contributes to airborne particles. Oxidation changes the chemical identity of emissions; it does not guarantee their harmlessness.

Methane illustrates the global consequences. A methane molecule can remain in the atmosphere long enough to mix across continents before chemical oxidation removes it. Much of that removal is initiated by reactions with hydroxyl in the troposphere. Methane influences climate directly as a greenhouse gas, and its chemistry also affects ozone, water vapor in higher atmospheric layers, and the atmosphere's oxidizing capacity. If the concentrations of reactive gases change, the abundance of hydroxyl can change too. The lifetime of methane is therefore not a perfectly fixed countdown attached to each molecule. It depends partly on the chemical environment through which the methane travels.

Not all removal happens through chemistry in the free atmosphere. Some gases dissolve in raindrops and leave with precipitation; that is wet deposition. Others are absorbed at soil, water, plant, or built surfaces; that is dry deposition. A leaf may take up ozone through its stomata, while the same ozone can injure the plant's tissues and interfere with photosynthesis. Rain can remove soluble gases and particles from the air, but those substances then enter water and soil chemistry. If a chemical vanishes from the atmosphere, it has often moved to another part of the Earth system rather than vanished from existence.

Now return to the upper atmosphere. Ozone destruction in the stratosphere can proceed through catalytic cycles involving chlorine and bromine. The atoms participate in reactions that destroy ozone and are regenerated, allowing a small amount of the catalyst to affect many ozone molecules. This is why compounds containing chlorine and bromine, once widely used in industrial products, had such large consequences after they reached the stratosphere and released reactive species. In the cold polar stratosphere, reactions on polar stratospheric cloud particles help make chlorine especially effective when spring sunlight returns. The dramatic Antarctic springtime ozone depletion was not a literal open hole through which all ultraviolet passed. It was a marked reduction in column ozone over a geographic region and season.

The history of ozone protection is also a history of separating evidence from policy. Scientists measured changes in the atmosphere, established the role of ozone-depleting substances, and built chemical and circulation models. Countries negotiated the Montreal Protocol in 1987 and subsequently strengthened its controls. The 2022 international scientific ozone assessment found that the decline of controlled ozone-depleting substances has been contributing to upper-stratospheric recovery. It projected return of near-global total ozone to roughly 1980 values around the middle of this century, while warning that the timing varies by region, scenario, and atmospheric variability. A projection is not a promise that every spring will have a smaller Antarctic ozone hole than the one before it.

The distinction between stratospheric and ground-level ozone is especially important here. Policies that reduce ozone-depleting substances in the upper atmosphere address a different chemical problem from local controls on nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds. Some reactions are shared across atmospheric chemistry, but the relevant sources, heights, timescales, and health implications differ. You can have progress in protecting stratospheric ozone while still having serious ground-level ozone episodes. Conversely, a clear, cool urban day with low ground-level ozone tells you little about the amount of protective ozone far above.

What about climate? Ozone absorbs radiation and is a greenhouse gas, though its effects depend on where in the atmosphere it changes. Nitrogen oxides, methane, carbon monoxide, and volatile organic compounds can influence climate indirectly through ozone and other chemical processes. Temperature affects reaction speeds, and sunlight and cloud cover affect photolysis. Heat waves often create conditions favorable to some ozone pollution episodes, but the relationship is not a single temperature-to-ozone rule. Winds, drought, biogenic emissions, and the chemistry of the emission mixture matter. The weather and the chemistry change one another's operating conditions.

To study the system, scientists combine surface monitors, aircraft sampling, balloons, and satellite instruments. A monitor near a highway captures what reaches that particular site. A satellite can help reconstruct regional distributions of some gases but generally does not measure human breathing-level concentration directly. Instruments often observe radiation or atmospheric columns and require retrieval models to infer chemical amounts. A chemical transport model then follows winds, mixing, sunlight, reactions, emissions, and deposition across space and time. The model can be evaluated against independent measurements and used to ask which process explains an observed pattern.

Imagine two communities on the same day. One is upwind of a city; the other is downwind. The downwind community may experience higher ozone even if its own emissions are smaller. During the journey, radicals from emitted organic gases have converted more nitric oxide to nitrogen dioxide, sunlight has released oxygen atoms, and ozone has accumulated. At night, photolysis slows or ceases, but other reactions continue and near-surface mixing often changes. Tomorrow's concentration begins with the transport and chemistry inherited from today. This is why a map of emissions and a map of ozone are not interchangeable.

There is a broader lesson in all this. Air pollution is not always something released into the air in its final form. Emitted molecules can react into other molecules. Some of the most consequential substances are secondary products made after transport. The same physical laws that drive winds also determine where reactants meet and how long they remain together. A molecule's path through the atmosphere is both a journey and a chemical history.

Clear sky is not chemically empty sky. It is a sunlit laboratory whose results affect lungs, forests, radiation, and the chemistry of rain. Once we learn to follow reactions rather than only sources, we can ask better questions about what is in the air, why it is there, and which changes are likely to improve it.

## Scientific companion: two ozone problems, one molecule

**Fast nitrogen oxide photochemistry.** Illustrative reactions: $\mathrm{NO_2+h\nu\rightarrow NO+O}$; $\mathrm{O+O_2+M\rightarrow O_3+M}$; $\mathrm{NO+O_3\rightarrow NO_2+O_2}$. By themselves these reactions provide a rapid cycle. Oxidation of VOCs, CO, and methane creates peroxy radicals ($\mathrm{RO_2}$ and $\mathrm{HO_2}$) that convert NO to NO2 without consuming ozone. The full chemical mechanism has competing loss channels and varies in regime; reducing NOx does not everywhere yield the same immediate local ozone response.

**Ozone layer versus surface pollution.** In the stratosphere, UV photolysis of molecular oxygen and catalytic loss by halogens are central. In the troposphere, precursor emissions, radical chemistry, photolysis, mixing, and deposition control pollution episodes. Ozone is chemically identical in both places; location determines protection against UV or direct exposure harms.

**Hydroxyl chemistry.** A major OH source is the reaction of electronically excited oxygen atoms from ozone photolysis with water vapor. OH reactions initiate oxidation of many atmospheric gases. Atmospheric 'cleansing' may yield secondary pollutants and particles. Methane's atmospheric lifetime is controlled by multiple sinks, primarily tropospheric OH, and changes with oxidizing capacity.

**Observational caution.** Surface monitors, retrieved satellite columns, and chemical transport models answer different questions. A strong causal attribution of ozone change to one emission source requires more than a correlation at one monitor.

### Questions and investigations

1. Why does photolysis of nitrogen dioxide alone not necessarily lead to net ozone accumulation?
2. Why can an area downwind of a city have more ozone than a busy street within it?
3. How can a chemical species disappear from the air while still affecting another part of the environment?
4. What is chemically identical and what is physically different between ozone in the stratosphere and near the ground?
5. What measurements would you combine to distinguish changes in precursor emissions from changes in weather and transport?
