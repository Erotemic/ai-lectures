# The Air You Cannot Eat: Nitrogen's Microbial Routes

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Pick up a handful of soil from a field in spring. It contains grains of mineral, decaying leaves, a thin film of water, living roots, and communities of organisms too small to see. Overhead, nearly four-fifths of the dry air consists of nitrogen gas. A plant growing in that soil needs nitrogen to build proteins and genetic material. Yet the enormous nitrogen reservoir above it is not, by itself, a ready supply of plant food. The central puzzle of the nitrogen cycle is not where nitrogen atoms are. It is which chemical forms those atoms occupy, and who can transform one form into another.

Nitrogen gas consists of pairs of nitrogen atoms bound tightly together. Breaking that bond is energetically demanding. Most organisms cannot take atmospheric nitrogen and directly construct an amino acid from it. They take up nitrogen that has already been made chemically accessible, especially ammonium or nitrate, or they consume other organisms that contain nitrogen in organic molecules. The atmosphere is rich in nitrogen; an ecosystem can still be limited by usable nitrogen. Abundance in a reservoir does not guarantee accessibility at the point of need.

There are microorganisms that solve the bond-breaking problem. Their nitrogenase enzyme complex reduces nitrogen gas to ammonia, a form that can enter biological chemistry. The reaction requires energy and reducing power. Nitrogenase is also sensitive to oxygen, creating a physiological problem for microbes that live in an oxygenated world. Some protect the enzyme by living in low-oxygen settings; some organize the timing or location of fixation; some form partnerships with plants. The nitrogen supplied to a legume is not something the plant roots extract from the air through ordinary respiration. It comes from a biological relationship with microbes that perform a reaction the plant itself cannot perform.

Imagine a bean plant growing beside a grass. The bean may host rhizobial bacteria in root nodules. The plant provides sugars made by photosynthesis, while the bacteria provide newly fixed nitrogen. This is an exchange, not free fertilizer: the partnership costs energy, and its effectiveness depends on soil conditions, the partners, and how much accessible nitrogen is already present. The grass may draw more heavily on nitrogen released by organic matter, supplied in fertilizer, or fixed elsewhere and transported into the soil system. Neither strategy overrides conservation of matter. The nitrogen atoms in new leaves must arrive by a physical or chemical pathway.

The word fixation can mislead. Nitrogen fixation does not mean that nitrogen remains fixed in a soil forever. It means converting relatively inert nitrogen gas into reactive compounds. Once the new nitrogen is in the food web, the story is movement, storage, reuse, and loss. Roots absorb forms of inorganic nitrogen. The nitrogen becomes proteins, pigments, nucleic acids, and other molecules. Animals eat plants and other animals. Waste and dead organisms return organic nitrogen to soil or sediment. Decomposers release some of it as ammonium. This conversion of organic nitrogen back into inorganic form is often called mineralization or ammonification. It is a step in recycling, not a return to the atmosphere.

Ammonium is a positively charged ion in many environmental conditions. It can be retained on negatively charged surfaces in soil, taken up by organisms, converted into new organic matter, or transformed by microorganisms. When oxygen is available, different microbes can gain energy by oxidizing ammonia or ammonium through nitrite toward nitrate. We call the overall process nitrification. The classic textbook diagram gives one organism the first step and another the second, but the living network is more flexible. Some organisms carry out complete ammonia oxidation, and many microbial groups overlap in their environmental roles. The existence of these different strategies matters because nutrient transformations depend on the organisms and conditions actually present, not on the labels printed on a diagram.

Nitrate behaves differently from ammonium. It is negatively charged and often more mobile in water moving through soils. If plants or microbes do not capture it before rain carries it downward, it can move toward groundwater, streams, and eventually the sea. This is why the chemical form of a nutrient affects where it can travel. Two soils containing the same total amount of nitrogen need not release the same amount to a river. Texture, rainfall, root uptake, microbial activity, drainage, and timing all matter. The total inventory tells us how much nitrogen exists; the flux tells us how much is on the move.

Follow a nitrate ion into waterlogged soil. The pores that recently held air are now filled with water. Oxygen cannot be replenished as rapidly, while roots and microbes continue to consume what is there. When local oxygen becomes scarce, some microbes use nitrogen oxides as electron acceptors in energy-yielding reactions. Through denitrification, nitrate or nitrite can be converted step by step into gaseous forms of nitrogen, potentially ending as nitrogen gas that returns to the atmosphere. The path can also release nitrous oxide. This is not the same gas as nitrogen dioxide, and it is not ordinary molecular nitrogen. Nitrous oxide is a long-lived greenhouse gas, so the precise products of a microbial pathway matter for climate as well as for fertilizer budgets.

Not all oxygen-free nitrogen removal is denitrification. In the twentieth century, researchers working with wastewater systems encountered a reaction that did not fit the expected bookkeeping. Ammonium was disappearing without oxygen, and the missing nitrogen was emerging as nitrogen gas. Experiments published in 1995, including work by Alexander Mulder and colleagues and by A. A. van de Graaf and colleagues in the Netherlands, established anaerobic ammonium oxidation, now called anammox. In simplified terms, anammox microbes combine ammonium-derived and nitrite-derived nitrogen into nitrogen gas. They do not have to follow the classic denitrification sequence. The discovery changed our understanding of nitrogen removal in wastewater treatment and in low-oxygen marine environments.

Why should anyone studying Earth systems care whether a microbe uses nitrate, nitrite, or ammonium? Because each reaction has a different environmental budget. Some make biologically available nitrogen; some recycle it; some remove it from the local ecosystem; some release a greenhouse gas. A wetland can retain part of a nutrient load and transform another part. A stream can receive nitrate from its watershed and lose some downstream to microbial reactions. A marine oxygen-minimum zone may contain niches where several of these transformations occur centimeters apart. Mapping the nitrogen cycle is therefore an ecological and physical problem as much as a chemical one.

A crucial distinction is between a chemical reaction and the environmental conditions that permit it. A microbe may possess the genes for denitrification, but the reaction depends on available organic matter or other electron donors, nitrate supply, oxygen, temperature, and competing processes. A field can be a net source of nitrous oxide during one period and a different kind of nitrogen processor during another. Soil is not a single uniform reactor. It is a landscape of tiny gradients, continually disturbed by rain, roots, drying, freeze-thaw, and cultivation.

There is also a human path into the nitrogen system. Early in the twentieth century, Fritz Haber demonstrated methods for combining nitrogen and hydrogen to make ammonia under high pressure and with a catalyst. Carl Bosch and a large engineering effort made this practical on an industrial scale, with a plant operating in 1913 in Germany. The Haber–Bosch process did not invent nitrogen fixation; it provided a new, industrial route alongside the biological one. Nitrogen fertilizer from industrial ammonia helped make large agricultural yields possible, while the same ability to manufacture ammonia also had military applications. The historical story is not simply one of beneficial chemistry or harmful chemistry. It is an expansion of human power to redirect a major planetary element.

The amount of reactive nitrogen people introduce to landscapes now changes ecosystems far from a fertilizer factory. A farmer applies nutrients to grow crops. Some are harvested and exported as food; some remain in soils or residues; some are lost in gases; some move in drainage or runoff. Those losses can stimulate plant or algal production downstream, alter species composition, acidify environments, and contribute to greenhouse-gas emissions. But the size of the effects depends on where nitrogen goes, which forms it takes, and what other nutrients, especially phosphorus, are available. Fertilizer application is not the same quantity as nitrogen reaching a distant estuary.

We should be careful with the phrase nitrogen pollution as well. Nitrogen is indispensable to life. A deficiency can limit crop growth, natural productivity, and food production. Excess reactive nitrogen in the wrong place, or at the wrong time, can be damaging. The goal is not to eliminate nitrogen from biological systems. It is to understand the routes through which nitrogen becomes available, is transferred into biomass, and escapes the intended system. In a crop field, the desired flux may be into harvested grain. In a constructed wetland, it may be into nitrogen gas. In a groundwater aquifer, the question may be whether nitrate is accumulating over decades despite changes in surface farming.

Measuring these flows is hard, but it is not guesswork. Scientists measure nitrate and ammonium concentrations, observe changes during storms, analyze gases emitted from soil, and incubate water or sediment under controlled conditions. They also use nitrogen isotopes. A tracer with an extra neutron behaves enough like ordinary nitrogen to follow many of the same chemical routes, while its distinct mass allows researchers to identify reaction products. If labeled ammonium and labeled nitrite produce a particular combination of isotopes in nitrogen gas, that can distinguish anammox from some alternative routes. An isotope experiment does not magically show every microbe responsible, but it constrains the chemical transformations that must have occurred.

A nitrogen budget needs both stocks and rates. Suppose a wetland receives ten units of dissolved nitrogen over some interval, stores three in growing vegetation and sediment, releases four downstream, and transforms three into nitrogen gas. At that scale, the budget balances. But to know how it will behave next spring, we must ask whether stored nitrogen will stay stored, whether an exceptionally wet year will carry nitrate through too quickly for reaction, and whether the same microbes will remain active when temperatures or water levels change. The snapshot is a balance sheet. The forecast requires understanding the process.

In many Earth system diagrams, atmospheric nitrogen sits at the top, soil microbes at one side, animals at another, and denitrification makes a neat arrow back to the air. That picture is useful as a first inventory of transformations. But it hides the thing that actually makes the cycle work: an interconnected microbial network whose reactions respond to oxygen, energy, water, and geology. A drop of rain can change which reaction dominates in a soil pore. A fertilizer decision can change the nitrogen carried by a watershed. An ocean current can deliver oxygen to a region where nitrate would otherwise be consumed. The nitrogen cycle is a moving arrangement of opportunities for chemistry.

The same principle will recur with other elements. A phosphorus atom has a very different route through rock and water, while oxygen can be both a resource for respiration and a condition controlling which microbes function. Understanding nitrogen is a beginning because it shows how to read the planetary cycles. Do not only ask where the atoms are. Ask what form they are in, who can use that form, what energy makes their conversion possible, and which flow carries them into the next place.

## Scientific companion: nitrogen transformations

### Mechanistic accounting

- **Fixation:** nitrogen gas to reduced reactive nitrogen, catalyzed biologically by nitrogenase or industrially by Haber–Bosch synthesis; it requires energy.
- **Assimilation and mineralization:** inorganic nitrogen enters biomass and returns through decomposition to ammonium. These are not equivalent to nitrogen-gas removal.
- **Nitrification:** oxidation of ammonia toward nitrite and nitrate. Canonical two-step models are incomplete because complete-ammonia-oxidizing organisms also exist.
- **Denitrification:** reduction of nitrate/nitrite through nitrogen oxides; the products can include N₂O and N₂. Extent and leakage depend on microenvironment.
- **Anammox:** ammonium and nitrite are substrates for production of N₂ under anoxic conditions. Do not conflate with denitrification or claim the original 1995 study discovered a single named microorganism.
- **Nitrate mobility:** mobility depends on soil mineral surfaces, flow paths, uptake and residence time; it is not an invariant for all soils.

### Distinguishing evidence from interpretation

The cited 1995 anammox work established that an oxygen-free conversion of ammonium to N₂ was a microbiological process using controls and isotopic analysis. The 2018 Kuypers–Marchant–Kartal review emphasizes interdependent microbial networks rather than a strictly sequential nitrogen cycle. The early-twentieth-century Haber–Bosch history concerns an industrial production method, not the invention of biological nitrogen fixation.

### Questions

1. Why can a field be nitrogen-limited when the atmosphere is mostly nitrogen gas?
2. What is the distinction between a nitrate ion leaving a watershed and a nitrogen atom returning to the atmosphere as N₂?
3. What evidence lets a laboratory distinguish anammox from simple abiotic loss or ordinary denitrification?
4. Why might the same wetland be a nitrogen sink during one season and a source of nitrous oxide during another?
5. What changes when nitrogen is considered as a network of chemical forms and rates rather than as one number in a soil test?
