# An Island That Moves: Dunes, Storms, and the Barrier Coast

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On some maps a barrier island is a thin yellow line of land separating the open ocean from a broad lagoon. It looks permanent enough to name towns, build roads, and draw property boundaries. Then a storm crosses the coast. The ocean cuts through the island and pours sand into the lagoon. The dunes are flattened. The shoreline has moved. From the perspective of a person living there, this is destruction. From the perspective of the sediment system, it can be both destruction and a way the island moves to a new position.

This is a difficult distinction, because it is easy to mistake a physical explanation for a judgment about what ought to happen. A house is not less valuable because a barrier island migrates naturally. But a barrier island does not remain in place just because a road is expensive. To make choices about a changing coast, we need to understand how an island made mostly of unconsolidated sediment can persist while its material is continually rearranged.

A barrier island commonly consists of an ocean-facing beach, dunes, vegetation, washover surfaces, marshes or back-barrier flats, and tidal inlets. The precise combination differs from coast to coast. It may receive sand from longshore transport, older geological deposits, river sources, or material eroded from its own shoreface. Waves reshape its seaward edge. Wind takes dry sand inland and builds dunes when vegetation or other obstacles trap it. Tides exchange water and sediment through inlets. Storms can push sand across the island. These processes compete; no single one builds all barriers.

We might expect a shore to retreat by having its front edge erased while its back edge stays where it was. Sometimes that happens. But a barrier can also migrate landward. Overwash carries sediment from the ocean beach and dunes across low parts of the island into the back-barrier environment. Tidal inlets can move, open, or close. Deposits build the landward side while the ocean side loses ground. In a simplified picture the entire sand body rolls slowly toward the mainland as sea level changes. The migration is not smooth, and its rate is not fixed. One storm can do much of the sediment moving that would otherwise take years.

To see the mechanics, imagine a broad sandy island with a dune ridge. Waves and rising water during a storm first attack the beach; as the water reaches the dunes, they may erode, supplying sand to the surf zone. A larger event can send water over a dune crest. The water slows on the far side and deposits a fan of sand called an overwash fan. Where an inlet or breach forms, a tidal current can move sand into shoals on either side. The island has lost height in one place but may have gained material on the back side. Whether those gains are enough to offset losses cannot be known from one photograph of the dune line.

In 2012, Hurricane Sandy changed parts of the New Jersey barrier coast dramatically. The U.S. Geological Survey documented breaches and overwash on Island Beach near Mantoloking, including deposition toward the estuary. That observation makes the dynamic visible: some sand that had helped form the ocean-facing barrier was transferred across it. The site-specific pattern matters. A general rule that every storm only takes sand offshore would miss the landward transport, while a general rule that storms always save barrier islands by building them upward would miss other losses.

A second observation is particularly instructive. During Hurricane Dorian in 2019, the North Core Banks in North Carolina were affected strongly by water coming from Pamlico Sound, on the landward side of the barrier. A 2023 USGS study combined high-water marks, model results, and comparisons of aerial imagery before and after the event. It reported large seaward-directed erosional washout channels driven by sound-side inundation. The storm was not merely an ocean wave assault. Depending on winds, basin geometry, and surge pathways, a barrier can be damaged from the back.

These events challenge a tempting diagram: ocean storm comes in, overwash carries sand inland, barrier moves landward. That diagram is a real mechanism, but it is not an exhaustive model. Sand may move seaward across the shoreface, may accumulate in a tidal inlet, may travel alongshore, or may cross the island in either direction depending on water-level gradients. Storm history, island width, back-barrier depth, dune height, and sediment grain size all affect the outcome. Water can cross a barrier without delivering enough sediment to raise its surface meaningfully.

Researchers increasingly try to close a sediment budget across the entire barrier, not just the part visible above water. In 2026, a USGS-associated study published in Nature Communications examined five years of shoreface-inclusive sediment changes after an extreme storm and found that flux patterns varied strongly with island geomorphology. It reported relatively low overwash flux compared with some seaward and inlet-related transport, and incomplete post-storm recovery despite intervention. The result is not that overwash never helps islands adjust. It is that a model which assumes enough sand is carried landward during every event can overestimate how readily a particular barrier keeps pace with rising relative sea level.

A barrier's future is a competition among vertical and horizontal processes. Rising water level increases the area exposed to flooding. Dunes can build through windblown sand, but only if there is sand available and a setting that allows it to remain. Marshes can gain elevation through mineral sediment and organic matter accumulation. Shoreface sand can migrate into different compartments. If the land underneath is subsiding, the same global ocean rise creates a larger local change in relative sea level. A barrier must adjust to that relative change, not just to a global average.

A barrier can narrow even when its beach is seasonally replenished, if the back-barrier shoreline retreats faster. It can widen from inlet shoals without becoming safer from overtopping, if the new land remains low. It can maintain a high dune ridge while the submerged shoreface steadily loses sediment. Different measurements answer different questions: shoreline position, volume of the beach and dunes, minimum island width, maximum elevation, water depth in the back-barrier basin, and the size of sediment reservoirs available for future change.

Humans alter almost every part of that balance where the coast is densely settled. Beach nourishment adds sand; dredging removes and relocates it. Stabilized inlets can hold navigation channels open and change sediment bypassing. Seawalls can constrain retreat at the front, though waves can still remove sand in front of the wall. Roads and houses can change dune building and vegetation patterns. These choices may protect particular assets while limiting some of the movements by which a natural barrier would adjust. Calling an island 'stable' after fortification can mean its position is fixed for a time, not that the physical sediment budget is balanced indefinitely.

A further complication is that storm activity and recovery are episodic. Suppose a barrier loses part of its dune volume in one storm, rebuilds some of it through wind and mild weather, and is struck again before recovery is complete. The sequence can lower a threshold for overwash or breaching. But a long calm interval may allow a different outcome. The average number of storms over a century is not enough to reconstruct the order and size of the individual events. Coastal systems possess memory because the state left by one event changes how the next will act.

That memory can also be ecological. Dune grasses trap sand and tolerate burial, marsh plants slow currents and retain sediment, and vegetation can return after saltwater and sand disturbance if the physical setting permits it. Yet plants cannot manufacture unlimited mineral sediment. If prolonged inundation kills vegetation and soft peat becomes exposed to waves, erosion can accelerate. Biological feedbacks can strengthen a landform, but they operate inside the constraints of sediment supply, water movement, and elevation.

Why not simply predict where the shoreline will be in fifty years? Scientists do make projections, but precise local outcomes depend on uncertainties that accumulate: future storms, relative sea-level change, sediment supply, inlet changes, and intervention. Some models estimate probabilities of overwash and dune erosion under particular waves and water levels. Others simulate long-term barrier migration. Each model resolves different processes. A physically credible estimate should state which measurements constrain it and which outcomes depend on assumptions about the future.

The map at the beginning of this story shows a fixed ribbon of dry land. The real island is a moving exchange among beach, dune, shoreface, inlet, lagoon, and marsh. Storm damage and geomorphic adaptation can occur in the same event. Some islands can migrate and persist; others may fragment, drown, or transform as constraints change. Understanding the process does not make the human consequences less serious. It makes the consequences more intelligible, because a barrier island is not a wall built once. It is a landform continually being assembled and taken apart by the coastal system around it.

## Scientific companion: barrier sediment pathways

An idealized landward-migration model involves sand eroded from the ocean-facing beach and deposited on the back-barrier side. It should not be substituted for a full sediment budget that includes the submerged shoreface, dunes, tidal inlets, alongshore transport, offshore sinks, and engineered sediment transfers. A barrier can change position, surface elevation, width and volume independently.

**Relative sea level** is sea-surface elevation measured relative to the land at a site. It includes changes in the ocean and vertical land motion. Overwash does not guarantee vertical accretion at the rate of relative sea-level rise.
