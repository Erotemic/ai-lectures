# How to Forecast an Ocean: Measuring ENSO and Living With Uncertainty

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A forecaster in June is asked whether the coming winter will bring an El Niño. She cannot travel to January, but she has a remarkable alternative: a Pacific-wide network of observations and models that follow the present state of the coupled ocean and atmosphere. Satellites survey sea-surface temperatures and sea level. Moorings measure winds and the ocean at multiple depths. Floats, ships and weather analyses add information where satellites cannot see. Computer models attempt to evolve this measured state into several possible futures. The result is not a single line marked 'what will happen.' It is a distribution of possibilities, and even a good forecast can miss the eventual outcome.

To see what is being predicted, we should begin with what is observed. El Niño and La Niña are not discrete substances that appear in the ocean. They are phases of a pattern involving temperature, pressure, winds and rain. Observers need indices that compress this high-dimensional field into understandable numbers. An index can help compare episodes or communicate an outlook, but it is not the coupled system itself. Choosing a number always means deciding which region, which measurement, which reference period and which timescale to use.

One common measure starts with sea-surface temperatures in a strip of the central-eastern equatorial Pacific known as the Niño three-point-four region. It spans five degrees north to five degrees south of the equator and longitudes from one hundred seventy to one hundred twenty degrees west. The region is large enough to summarize an important part of basin-scale warming and cooling, but it cannot describe where every warm pool boundary or rain cloud lies. An anomaly is a departure from a reference average; because the ocean's normal seasonal cycle is strong, comparing a winter temperature to the right seasonal baseline matters.

The traditional Oceanic Niño Index, or ONI, uses a smoothed three-month measure of sea-surface temperature anomalies in that region. Positive anomalies generally indicate warming, negative anomalies cooling. Operational definitions have often used half a degree Celsius in magnitude as a familiar threshold. But a threshold is a convention for monitoring an evolving physical process; it does not make the ocean instantly switch regimes at the precise moment a decimal changes from point four nine to point five zero. A diagnosis of coupled ENSO conditions also needs evidence that the atmosphere is responding consistently, not only a warm patch of water.

There is an additional complication when we measure these anomalies over many decades. The tropical oceans are warming under human-caused climate change. A fixed or slowly updated reference period affects what the word 'anomaly' means. If the entire tropical ocean is warmer than it was several decades ago, a positive departure in one Pacific region might include both a broad tropical warming signal and a specifically Pacific east-west contrast. For ENSO, that relative contrast is often more closely tied to where tropical convection can organize than the absolute change relative to a historical mean. This is one reason investigators use multiple indices.

In February 2026 the United States Climate Prediction Center announced a shift toward the Relative Oceanic Niño Index, abbreviated RONI, for its ENSO monitoring and outlooks. RONI starts from the Niño three-point-four anomaly, subtracts the average sea-surface temperature anomaly of the global tropical belt from twenty degrees south to twenty degrees north, and adjusts the resulting series to retain a familiar measure of variability. The goal is to track how the central-eastern Pacific differs from the broader tropics, while reducing some dependence on a moving historical baseline. The threshold convention of plus or minus half a degree remains recognizable. This index change did not change the physics of the ocean; it changed a diagnostic lens.

The same program also distinguishes an episode that lasted through multiple seasons from a provisional warning about conditions now forming. In its historical catalog, NOAA uses at least five consecutive overlapping three-month periods meeting the threshold to identify a sustained warm or cool episode. In operational discussions, forecasts of persistence and supporting atmospheric observations matter too. That distinction prevents a one-month temperature spike from being automatically labeled a complete historical El Niño. It also explains why the names on a historical event timeline can be revised when data or methods improve.

Now add the atmosphere. The Southern Oscillation Index uses the difference in sea-level pressure anomalies between Tahiti and Darwin, Australia, after standardization. Its negative phase commonly accompanies El Niño-like conditions, while its positive phase commonly accompanies La Niña-like conditions. Wind anomalies near the equator and the longitude of enhanced convection are further clues. An ocean temperature anomaly with no corresponding wind or pressure response may be a transient oceanic condition, not a fully established coupled episode. Likewise, an atmospheric burst can precede the slower sea-surface change it helps produce.

We also need to know what is happening beneath the surface. A satellite measuring infrared radiation from the sea primarily sees the skin of the ocean, not the hundreds of meters of stored warm water below. Sea-surface height gives clues about ocean pressure and expansion, but it is not a direct temperature measurement. Ocean moorings sample temperature and in some places velocity at known depths. The tropical moored observing array that developed in the 1980s and reached broad Pacific coverage in the 1990s was designed in part to make this missing subsurface information available. It later became widely known as the TAO/TRITON array through cooperation across observing agencies.

Imagine an anchored mooring near the equator. At the surface it may measure wind and air temperature. Below it, sensors record temperature at a series of depths. If a downwelling Kelvin wave passes, the depths associated with particular water temperatures can change in a coordinated way before the sea surface has fully warmed. A forecaster who sees only a satellite image might miss that evolving subsurface structure. A forecaster who sees both can assess whether the ocean contains an anomaly likely to support further change. Even then, sensors are point measurements, and a mooring can fail or drift; gaps in the observing network matter.

The first famous ENSO forecasting challenge of the modern satellite era came in 1982 and 1983. A very strong El Niño developed, and the observing and forecasting system of the time was not prepared to track its evolution as comprehensively as later systems could. The event helped motivate sustained tropical ocean observations and research programs. By the 1997 and 1998 event, the tropical mooring network and satellites provided a far richer picture of wind bursts, eastward-propagating ocean disturbances and changing subsurface temperatures. The lesson of this history is not that one model suddenly became infallible. It is that predictions require an observing system as well as a theory.

A numerical forecast model attempts to approximate the physical laws for ocean and atmosphere: conservation of mass and energy, fluid motion, mixing, heating, water vapor and cloud processes. The atmosphere and ocean exchange heat, moisture and momentum at the surface. A coupled model calculates these interactions repeatedly as time advances. But no model can simulate every turbulent eddy, cloud droplet or breaking wave at global resolution. Processes smaller than the computational grid need simplified representations, and model errors can accumulate. Ocean currents may be slightly too strong, convection may shift too readily, or the average simulated Pacific temperature gradient may be wrong. These biases affect how the model responds to the same disturbance nature experiences.

The initial state is itself uncertain. A mooring measures its location and a satellite covers only the variables to which its instrument is sensitive. Scientists combine observations with the physically consistent state estimated by a model; the procedure is called data assimilation. It is not the replacement of measurements with a preferred answer. It is a disciplined method for blending incomplete evidence with dynamical constraints, subject to assumptions and errors in both. Different assimilations can yield different estimates of the depth of the thermocline, equatorial heat content and atmospheric circulation at a particular instant.

So forecasters use ensembles. One run starts from one plausible state; another starts from a slightly different state or uses a different model. If most members evolve toward a sustained warm-phase pattern, the probability assigned to El Niño can rise. The spread among members gives one indication of uncertainty, though an ensemble can still be too confident if its members share the same systematic error. Multi-model forecasts attempt to benefit from independently developed representations of the ocean-atmosphere system. Statistical forecasts can also learn relationships between observed indices, ocean heat content and later ENSO state. Neither class is inherently always superior at every lead time and season.

The point at which a forecast is issued matters. ENSO forecasts often struggle most when they must cross boreal spring, a feature known as the spring predictability barrier. In spring, the relationship between the current observed state and the coming winter's state can become less reliable. The seasonal cycle changes the strength of coupled feedbacks, and unpredictable wind events can alter the future trajectory. The barrier is a tendency measured in forecasting skill, not an iron law declaring all forecasts issued in April useless. Skill depends on the model, the event, the target season and how far ahead the forecast is made. Recent evaluations comparing dynamical and statistical models have shown precisely this variation.

What does a forecast probability mean? Suppose a well-calibrated system assigns a seventy percent chance of El Niño next winter, a twenty percent chance of neutral conditions and a ten percent chance of La Niña. It is not saying the ocean will be seventy percent El Niño. It is expressing a judgment over several mutually exclusive categories. If the twenty-percent neutral outcome occurs, the forecast was not necessarily irrational; an event with a twenty-percent probability should sometimes happen. To evaluate forecasting quality we must consider many forecasts and ask whether the stated probabilities align with the frequency of later outcomes and whether sharper, better calibrated predictions outperform simple baselines.

One baseline is persistence: suppose today's condition continues. Another is climatology, the historical frequency of each category for a given season. A sophisticated model is useful when it predicts better than these simpler approaches for the conditions being evaluated. Verification should use forecasts made without knowledge of the eventual outcome. Retrospective model simulations, often called hindcasts or reforecasts, can help estimate seasonal skill, but they can also overstate performance if calibration or model selection quietly uses information from the events being tested. Independent verification and published uncertainty are therefore central to trustworthy long-range prediction.

Finally, there are two different forecasting problems. The first asks which ENSO state the Pacific is likely to occupy. The second asks what rainfall, heat, storms or river flows a particular region will experience if that state occurs. A forecast of El Niño may be confident while a local precipitation outlook remains only weakly shifted away from the usual odds. A strong event can increase confidence in some remote tendencies, but not guarantee them. Regional forecasts must combine the ENSO signal with other information about the background climate, atmospheric variability and local processes. Converting an ENSO outlook directly into a town's weather forecast skips most of the difficult work.

A final distinction concerns climate change. ENSO is a form of natural variability in the coupled Pacific. Human-caused warming changes the background climate against which ENSO acts. The two are not competing explanations for any warm year. An El Niño can temporarily affect the global average temperature, while the long-term warming trend can continue across El Niño, La Niña and neutral years. Scientists investigate whether the statistics and impacts of ENSO themselves are changing, but different aspects of that question have different levels of confidence. We should not treat a single unusually strong event as proof of a permanent change in frequency or intensity.

An operational ENSO page may show colored probability bars, a forecast cone, historical event classifications and a current ocean-temperature map. Now we can ask the right questions of each element. Does the chart show a temperature anomaly, a relative temperature anomaly, or a full coupled diagnosis? Is the number observed, reconstructed or predicted? How many months are being averaged? Is the outlook for a broad equatorial ocean category or a local seasonal rainfall distribution? What uncertainty has been quantified, and what uncertainty has not? Those questions make the forecast more useful rather than less.

The power of seasonal prediction comes from a particular physical fact: the upper ocean remembers. Heat-content anomalies and wave adjustments evolve more slowly than many individual weather disturbances, so some aspects of the future are constrained by today's state. But the memory is incomplete, because winds, convection, mixing and external disturbances keep modifying the path. The most scientifically mature result is neither perfect prediction nor helpless uncertainty. It is a reasoned probability, tied to observations, physical models and verification, accompanied by an explanation of what can still go differently.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Indices and operational distinctions

- **Niño 3.4 region:** 5 degrees north to 5 degrees south and 170 to 120 degrees west. A regional sea-surface temperature diagnostic, not a complete ENSO state vector.
- **ONI:** overlapping three-month mean SST anomaly in Niño 3.4 relative to the index's climate reference; the historical operational label should not be mistaken for a physical phase transition.
- **RONI (NOAA CPC transition in 2026):** Niño 3.4 anomaly minus the tropical mean SST anomaly, then variance-adjusted to ONI-scale variability; retains familiar thresholds. The underlying data product/reference period can be revised and recent values can be provisional.
- **SOI:** standardized Tahiti minus Darwin surface-pressure anomaly, sign generally opposite warm-phase SST indices.
- **Episode versus conditions:** the historic CPC duration threshold of five overlapping three-month seasons differs from operational evaluation of present coupled features and expected persistence.
- **Forecast verification:** reliability/calibration, discrimination, lead-time and season-specific skill, reference baselines, and proper out-of-sample validation; one miss is not sufficient to classify a probabilistic outlook as unreliable.

2. What does RONI remove from the traditional index, and what does it preserve?
3. Why are ocean subsurface measurements valuable even when satellites already measure SST?
4. How would you evaluate a forecast that says there is a seventy percent chance of El Niño and the event does not occur?
5. Why can seasonal ENSO state predictability be greater than the predictability of an individual region's rainfall?
