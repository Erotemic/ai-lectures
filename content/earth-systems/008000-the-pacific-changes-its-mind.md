# The Pacific Changes Its Mind: El Niño, La Niña, and the Ocean-Atmosphere Oscillation

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Stand on the equator in the middle of the Pacific, far from any coast, and there is almost no landmark by which to judge what the ocean is doing. Its surface looks flat compared with the size of the basin. The daily weather comes and goes. But beneath a few degrees of sea-surface temperature lies a rearrangement of winds, rain clouds, currents, and an enormous volume of warm water. A change that begins in this ocean can alter the odds of rain on other continents months later. The process is called the El Niño-Southern Oscillation, or ENSO. To understand it we must stop treating the sea and the atmosphere as separate machines.

The familiar names are El Niño, the warm phase, and La Niña, the cool phase. Both names describe departures from the ordinary pattern in the tropical Pacific, not a permanent state of the whole planet. There is also a neutral range, and neutral does not mean that the Pacific has stopped moving. ENSO is a recurring, irregular exchange between an ocean with thermal memory and an atmosphere that responds rapidly to the distribution of tropical heat. Some episodes are strong, some are weak; they last different amounts of time, and they do not recur by a strict calendar.

Begin with the Pacific on an average day. The sun supplies heat throughout the tropics, but average surface temperatures are not uniform along the equator. The western Pacific, around Indonesia and New Guinea, holds a broad region of very warm surface water. The eastern equatorial Pacific is typically cooler, with a cold tongue extending westward from the waters off South America. At first this might sound like a consequence of sunlight, yet both regions are near the same latitude. The difference is maintained by the interaction of ocean currents, winds, surface heat exchange, and the vertical structure of the upper ocean.

The prevailing tropical winds are the easterly trade winds: they blow toward the west along much of the equator. Their frictional stress pushes surface water westward. Warm surface water accumulates in the western part of the basin, raising sea level slightly there relative to the east and deepening the layer of warm water. Across the basin the boundary between the warmer upper ocean and colder deep water, the thermocline, slopes: typically deeper in the west and shallower in the east. This slope is a central part of the mechanism. It changes the temperature of water that can reach the surface when currents stir and lift water from below.

At the equator the Coriolis effect changes sign. Wind-driven surface currents on either side can diverge, allowing deeper water to rise toward the surface. Along the eastern boundary of the Pacific, winds and coastal geometry can also favor upwelling. But the effect of upwelling depends on what water is available below. Where the thermocline is shallow, relatively cool water can mix into the sunlit surface layer. Where the thermocline is deep, an upward-moving parcel may still be warm by the time it reaches the mixed layer. Thus the rate of upwelling and the temperature of its source water are two separate questions. Saying that El Niño simply switches off all upwelling misses this distinction.

Warm ocean surfaces usually support more evaporation and, when atmospheric conditions permit, deep convection. In the western Pacific warm pool, moisture can rise in towering clouds and release latent heat as vapor condenses. Higher in the atmosphere air spreads eastward and elsewhere, while near the surface there is a tendency for air to flow back from the central and eastern Pacific toward the active convection region. This large east-west overturning pattern is called the Walker circulation. It is not a rigid conveyor belt that always occupies the same longitude; it is a useful average description of pressure, wind, rising motion, and sinking motion across a vast region.

Here is the essential loop. The trade winds help keep the eastern surface cool. The east-west temperature contrast helps organize the tropical pressure and rainfall pattern that sustains the trades. The ocean affects the atmosphere, and the atmosphere sustains the ocean pattern. In the 1960s Jacob Bjerknes brought these linked observations into one explanation, relating abnormal Pacific sea temperatures to the pressure fluctuations earlier studied as the Southern Oscillation. The mechanism is now called the Bjerknes feedback. We can understand its warm direction without a single equation.

Suppose the equatorial trade winds become weaker over a sufficiently broad stretch of the Pacific. The usual westward push on the surface ocean slackens. The tilt of the thermocline begins to relax. The central or eastern Pacific gains access to warmer upper-ocean water; the eastern cold tongue weakens. As a result the difference in sea-surface temperature between east and west shrinks. The pattern of tropical convection can move farther east. Its altered heating and pressure gradients tend to weaken the easterly winds further. An initially small disturbance may reinforce itself through the atmosphere and ocean acting together. That is the growth part of an El Niño episode.

Reverse the disturbance. If the easterly winds become stronger, the warm surface water is pushed westward more effectively. The thermocline becomes shallower in the east and deeper in the west. Colder subsurface water can influence the eastern surface more strongly. The east-west temperature contrast may increase, with convection favoring the western warm pool and the easterlies reinforcing the cooling. That is the corresponding feedback for La Niña. The two directions are related, but their details are not perfect mirror images. Ocean temperature, convection thresholds, geography, and the background seasonal state can make warm and cool episodes develop differently.

A positive feedback is only half an oscillator. If reinforcement were the entire story, the Pacific would head toward one extreme and never return. What allows the disturbance to turn around? One answer lies in how much warm water is stored in the equatorial upper ocean, and where it is distributed. Winds do more than change the surface temperature. They rearrange sea level and the depth of the thermocline, and disturbances in this layered ocean can travel as waves. The equator provides a special guide for some of them, because Earth's rotation acts differently on either side of it.

A westerly wind burst, an episode in which winds blow anomalously toward the east, can launch a downwelling equatorial Kelvin wave. This is not a curling wave breaking at the beach. It is a broad, mostly subsurface displacement of layers and pressure that moves eastward across the Pacific, often over weeks to a few months. As it passes, it can deepen the thermocline in the central and eastern basin. That makes it easier for the eastern surface to become warm and harder for ordinary upwelling to bring cold water into contact with the surface. These waves are among the ways an atmospheric event produces an ocean response that persists after the wind burst has faded.

Kelvin waves do not carry the entire story by themselves. Other equatorial and off-equatorial wave motions, including Rossby waves, help move pressure and heat-content anomalies through the basin. Water also shifts meridionally, toward and away from the equator. The result is that an El Niño can gradually discharge the equatorial region of some of the excess upper-ocean heat content that previously favored its growth. A later cool event can help recharge it. Fei-Fei Jin's 1997 recharge-oscillator model made this delayed ocean adjustment a clear conceptual explanation for why the warming and cooling can reverse. It is a model of important dynamics, not a promise that every episode follows an identical cycle.

Consider a thought experiment with two thermometers. One measures the temperature right at the ocean surface in the eastern Pacific. The other measures the depth of a particular subsurface temperature boundary, such as the depth at which the water reaches twenty degrees Celsius. Two moments might show the same surface temperature but very different subsurface heat structures. In one case a thick warm layer lies beneath the surface, so sustained cooling would require removing considerable stored heat. In the other, cold water sits close beneath the mixed layer, ready to affect the surface if the winds change. The second thermometer can reveal a potential for change that the first cannot.

This is why oceanographers measure upper-ocean heat content rather than merely identifying warm-colored patches on a satellite map. A few centimeters of sea-level variation across a huge basin can help indicate the redistribution of water and heat when interpreted alongside other information. Anchored instruments measure subsurface temperatures and currents; satellites measure sea-surface height and temperature; drifting instruments and ships provide additional samples. None observes the entire three-dimensional ocean perfectly. The important physical state is reconstructed from complementary observations and model-based estimates, with uncertainties that vary in space and time.

How does an event begin? There is no universal first domino. Wind bursts, ocean heat content, tropical convection, the seasonal cycle, and disturbances arriving from outside the equatorial strip all influence the state. Some proposed explanations emphasize delayed wave adjustment; others emphasize recharge and discharge. These are not necessarily mutually exclusive. One describes ways information travels through a basin, the other describes changing stored heat. An honest account of ENSO does not claim that a westerly wind burst always causes an El Niño, or that a warm ocean reservoir always produces one. The state of the coupled system determines how strongly any particular disturbance grows.

The seasons matter. Many El Niño and La Niña episodes reach their most recognizable amplitude toward the end of a calendar year and weaken during the following boreal spring, although there are exceptions. This tendency is called phase locking to the annual cycle. Yet the oscillation is not the annual cycle itself. The equatorial Pacific's seasonal shifts change how susceptible the coupled feedbacks are to a disturbance. ENSO may evolve over many seasons, while the strength of the background winds, sunlight, mixing and convection alters month by month. These two clocks overlap without being identical.

The name El Niño has an older, more local history. Along the Peruvian coast, fishers used a name associated with the Christmas season for episodes of warm coastal water. The term later came to designate large-scale warming in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific, coupled to atmospheric changes. A brief coastal warming near Peru need not qualify as a basin-wide ENSO event. Nor does every El Niño generate the same temperature pattern along every part of the coast. This distinction matters when we connect historical reports, fishery records and modern climate indices.

The ocean's biological response helps make the physical changes visible. In the eastern Pacific, nutrient-rich deep water often fuels plankton production when upwelling reaches the sunlit layer. If the source water becomes warmer and poorer in nutrients, biological productivity can decline even when some upward motion persists. That can alter food webs and the distribution of fish. But a fishing crisis is not an index of ENSO strength: local management, fishing effort, food-web dynamics and coastal circulation can all matter. A physical shift changes conditions under which living organisms and people must operate; it does not determine one inevitable outcome.

ENSO is frequently described as a simple alternation between an El Niño year and a La Niña year. Observed sequences do not fit that pattern. Several years can pass in neutral conditions, a cool phase can persist across consecutive northern winters, and a warm event can end without a large cool event immediately replacing it. The familiar interval of roughly two to seven years describes a broad range of variability, not a schedule. Even the geography of warming varies. Some events concentrate more strongly toward the eastern Pacific, others farther west in the central basin. These differences can change the location of tropical rainfall and therefore the wider atmospheric response.

The essential lesson is not that the tropical Pacific has two settings. It is that a fluid system can build memory. The atmosphere responds to a warm ocean surface in days; the ocean's subsurface reservoir adjusts over months. Positive coupling amplifies anomalies, while delayed movement of water and heat changes the conditions that supported them. In a single physical story we have encountered winds, currents, buoyancy, tropical clouds, pressure gradients, planetary rotation and the ocean's heat budget. ENSO is a particularly clear example of why Earth science becomes more powerful when we follow the transfers between its parts rather than study each part in isolation.

So if a report says that El Niño is developing, the question is not simply whether a thermometer has crossed a threshold. We want to know where the water is warm, how the winds and convection have changed, whether the subsurface reservoir supports further warming, and how the ocean is likely to evolve. Those questions take us from the surface pattern to the mechanism. The next consequence is that changing tropical rain can reorganize atmospheric circulation far beyond the Pacific, but no distant region is handed a guaranteed forecast by the state of the equatorial ocean.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### The simplest coupled mechanism

In the mean state, easterly trades, warm-pool pooling, thermocline tilt, eastern cooling and Walker-circulation convection reinforce one another. A perturbation can grow through the **Bjerknes feedback**. A delayed response follows from equatorial-wave adjustment and the basin's changing upper-ocean heat content. Neither a pure feedback model nor a pure delayed-oscillator model explains every observed ENSO evolution.

- **Thermocline:** region of strong vertical temperature gradient; the 20-degrees-Celsius isotherm depth is often a practical indicator in the tropical Pacific, not an exact thermocline boundary everywhere.
- **Downwelling Kelvin wave:** moves eastward along the equator, depressing the thermocline. Upwelling waves have the opposite sign.
- **Equatorial recharge:** redistribution of upper-ocean warm-water volume in and out of the equatorial strip, distinct from a uniformly rising global ocean heat content.
- **Upwelling:** upward motion does not vanish universally during El Niño; the temperature and nutrient content of upwelled source water can change profoundly.
- **Anomaly:** departure relative to a chosen climatological reference; a warm anomaly does not mean a single fixed absolute temperature across the whole ocean.
