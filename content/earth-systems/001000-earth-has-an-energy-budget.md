# Earth Has an Energy Budget: Light In, Heat Out

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine trying to account for the energy of the whole Earth without ever touching it. You cannot put a thermometer around a planet, and an average temperature alone would not tell you where its energy comes from or where it goes. But you can draw an imaginary boundary around the atmosphere and ask two questions. How much energy crosses that boundary inward? How much crosses it outward? The difference is the rate at which the Earth system gains or loses energy. It is a surprisingly powerful first move, because it does not require that we know the temperature of every forest, ocean trench, cloud, or desert.

Sunlight enters. Some sunlight returns to space without having heated the planet. Earth and its atmosphere also emit their own radiation to space. At a stable long-term temperature, those two outward streams must together match the incoming sunlight. If they do not, heat accumulates somewhere, or stored heat is lost. This is an accounting statement, not yet an explanation of the atmosphere. It is the constraint every plausible explanation has to satisfy.

Start with sunlight. The solar energy arriving at a patch perpendicular to the rays is much more concentrated than the same energy averaged over an entire spherical planet, half of which is dark at any instant. Scientists use roughly three hundred and forty watts per square meter for the long-term global average at the top of the atmosphere. The figure already accounts for the fact that sunlight falls on a disk while the planetary surface is a sphere. It is not the intensity a sunlit field receives at noon, and it is not a promise that every latitude receives the same amount.

Earth is not a perfect absorber. Clouds, aerosols, snow, ice, oceans at shallow angles, and some land surfaces reflect sunlight. On global average, close to three tenths of the incoming solar energy is reflected to space. That fraction is called planetary albedo. The rest is absorbed by the surface or atmosphere and can change the energy of the system. A bright cloud can make the planet more reflective even while a different cloud traps additional infrared radiation. Clouds therefore cannot be assigned a single warming or cooling effect without specifying their altitude, thickness, particles, and location.

We have to distinguish two forms of light. The Sun, whose visible surface is much hotter than Earth's, delivers much of its energy at short wavelengths, including visible and near-infrared light. A comparatively cool Earth emits radiation at much longer, thermal infrared wavelengths. The terms shortwave and longwave are useful because different substances interact differently with them. Atmospheric gases and clouds can be relatively transparent at some wavelengths and absorbing at others. This is why an atmosphere can admit a great deal of solar energy and nevertheless impede the escape of thermal radiation.

A body at a fixed temperature is continuously emitting thermal radiation. Making the body warmer increases that emission. In the simplest idealized calculation, emission increases with the fourth power of absolute temperature. We should not apply this idealized law to the whole complex planet as though every place had one temperature and one emissivity. Yet the law explains a fundamental negative response: as the Earth warms, it generally radiates more energy to space. Without an additional influence, this response works against continued warming.

Now comes a confusion worth resolving carefully. If a gas absorbs infrared light, it does not normally hold the energy forever, and the atmosphere does not operate like a lid that physically stops radiation. Absorbed energy is exchanged through radiation and molecular collisions. Air emits infrared radiation as well as absorbing it. Some of that radiation travels upward; some travels downward. From space, a significant share of the infrared leaving the Earth comes from the atmosphere and clouds rather than directly from the ground.

Imagine looking down at the planet through an infrared wavelength absorbed strongly by a greenhouse gas. The radiation that ultimately escapes tends to emerge from a level high enough that the atmosphere above is relatively transparent at that wavelength. Higher layers of the troposphere are generally colder than the surface. A colder emitting layer ordinarily emits less radiation. Increase the concentration of an infrared-absorbing gas while keeping temperatures initially unchanged, and the radiation escaping to space at affected wavelengths can decrease. The planet is then receiving more energy than it is releasing. As temperatures adjust, outgoing infrared emission increases toward a new balance. This is a central physical account of the greenhouse effect, not simply an analogy to garden glass.

Water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, ozone, and other constituents contribute to infrared absorption, with clouds also important. Their behavior differs because they absorb in different spectral bands and are distributed differently in altitude and geography. Water vapor is especially responsive to temperature: warmer air can maintain more vapor before condensation becomes likely, and a warmer climate typically has more atmospheric water vapor. Carbon dioxide, by contrast, can be altered substantially by fossil fuel combustion and land-use changes independently of immediate local temperature. This makes it useful to distinguish a forcing from a feedback. Added carbon dioxide can change the radiation budget first; the resulting warming can then increase water vapor and amplify the response. Neither term is an accusation or a judgment. They describe causal roles in a coupled physical model.

The atmosphere also trades energy with the ground in ways that a top-of-atmosphere diagram can conceal. Evaporation takes energy from a surface to separate liquid water molecules into vapor. When that vapor condenses in a cloud, latent heat is released into the air. Rising warm air and turbulent mixing transport sensible heat. The ground emits infrared radiation upward, and the atmosphere emits infrared downward. A surface can therefore receive substantial energy from two directions while maintaining balance through several outgoing routes. Confusing the surface budget with the planetary budget leads to the false idea that every downward atmospheric infrared photon must be a violation of conservation of energy. It is not; the two budgets have different boundaries and include exchanges in both directions.

Consider a tropical ocean under active convection. Sunlight enters the mixed surface layer. Wind and evaporation take heat away from the water, and moisture enters the atmosphere. A cloud system later releases latent heat as vapor condenses. Some energy is carried away by atmospheric motion. Some is radiated to space. Some stays in seawater and may be transported toward higher latitudes. The initial sunlight is not labeled, molecule by molecule, for one final destination. Yet every pathway belongs in the budget. The words climate system are useful precisely because no one component owns that energy.

A comparison with a bathtub can clarify the budget and also show the limits of the comparison. Water flows in from a tap and leaves through a drain. The water level changes if inflow and outflow differ. On Earth the quantity being stored is thermal energy, and the capacity is dominated by the oceans, not by an imaginary tank of air. More importantly, the size of the drain changes with temperature, cloud behavior, and the atmosphere's composition. Even after an imposed change in incoming or outgoing energy, the climate takes time to respond because heat must be redistributed through a deep, moving ocean. An equilibrium calculation tells us the direction of adjustment, not how the next five years of weather will unfold.

How do we observe this rather than simply draw arrows? Above the atmosphere, instruments can measure incoming sunlight, reflected sunlight, and outgoing thermal radiation. The Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System instruments, known as CERES, have produced records used to study those top-of-atmosphere fluxes. The satellite measures radiative quantities; it does not place a thermometer in every cubic meter of ocean. To understand accumulation of energy, researchers combine the radiation record with independent observations of ocean heat content and other stores. The ocean is so massive and has such a high heat capacity that most of the excess energy associated with modern planetary heating is entering ocean water.

Here is an important measurement distinction. A satellite instrument may have enough consistency to reveal that an imbalance is changing over time while the absolute imbalance is harder to determine precisely from radiative measurements alone. Small differences between two large energy flows require careful calibration, instrument cross-comparison, and independent checks. Ocean profiles offer an alternative form of evidence: if the oceans as a whole gain heat over a period, that heat must have come from the energy budget, with smaller changes occurring in the atmosphere, land, and ice. These two approaches are complementary, but they are not mathematically identical observations of the same thing.

The measurements now support a sustained positive Earth energy imbalance. That means more energy is being absorbed than escapes to space over the measured period. It does not mean every year is warmer at every location, or that temperature rises uniformly with each annual measurement. Weather changes the location of heat. Ocean processes can bring colder water to a coast while the ocean as a whole gains energy. Volcanic aerosols can alter the sunlight absorbed. Snow and cloud patterns can change the reflection. A good explanation does not ask the global budget to predict the timing of every cold snap.

The reflectivity of Earth itself can change as the planet warms. Snow and sea ice are generally brighter than the ocean or ground they cover. Melting can expose darker surfaces, increase absorption of sunlight, and reinforce regional warming. That is ice-albedo feedback. The same logic invites care with clouds: clouds can increase reflected sunlight, but they also affect outgoing infrared radiation, and the balance depends on cloud type and distribution. Some responses are better constrained than others. Treating all feedbacks as equally uncertain would be as misleading as pretending every detail is settled.

There is also a difference between weather and climate that can be expressed in budget language. A storm draws on horizontal temperature contrasts, moisture, and the changing state of the atmosphere; predicting its path requires initial conditions and dynamics. The long-run planetary budget places a constraint on the average state within which storms develop. Understanding why climate can shift under an altered energy balance does not grant perfect weather forecasts. Conversely, the inability to forecast a particular rainstorm months in advance does not erase the energy accounting that controls longer-term change.

The budget is a method for asking better questions. If the planet absorbs more radiation, where is the extra energy stored? If an ocean region cools, where has heat been carried or released? If cloud cover changes, what happened to both reflected sunlight and emitted infrared? If a region becomes wetter, did moisture convergence change, or did evaporation change, or both? Each question identifies a measurable flux instead of assigning the entire outcome to a slogan about temperature.

Return to our imaginary boundary around the atmosphere. It has not told us everything. There are volcanoes, currents, vegetation, ice sheets, topography, and turbulent storms inside. But it has exposed a conserved quantity and the principal routes by which that quantity moves. The Earth's climate is neither an enclosed furnace nor a passive rock in sunlight. It is a changing system that absorbs, reflects, transports, stores, and emits energy. We cannot understand its wind, rain, oceans, or living landscapes until we first understand that accounting. And having learned the budget, we are ready to ask why energy refuses to remain where it first arrives.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Boundaries and signs

Let the positive sign denote energy entering the combined Earth system at the top of the atmosphere. Then

\[
N_{\mathrm{TOA}} = S_{\downarrow} - S_{\uparrow} - L_{\uparrow}.
\]

Here, \(S\) refers to shortwave solar radiation and \(L\) to outgoing longwave radiation. The expression is a **rate per unit area**, not a temperature. A positive \(N_{\mathrm{TOA}}\) represents energy gain, and long-run storage is concentrated in the ocean. A surface-only budget adds downward atmospheric longwave emission, upward surface emission, turbulent sensible heat flux and latent heat flux; do not insert these internal exchanges into the top-of-atmosphere budget twice.

A global mean of about 340 W/m² incident sunlight is a geometrical mean, not a local noon intensity; approximately 29–30% is reflected in commonly used climatological budgets. Exact annual and multiyear values vary and the radiation-record calibration is an active subject of research. Avoid turning one measurement period into a timeless number.

### Model distinctions

- **Radiative forcing:** an imposed radiative perturbation under defined conventions, such as changed greenhouse-gas concentration.
- **Feedback:** a radiative or other response caused by a temperature change, such as a temperature-associated water-vapor increase or sea-ice-albedo change.
- **Thermal inertia:** energy storage and transport, especially in the ocean, delaying the approach to equilibrium.
- **Climate sensitivity:** a property of a specified long-term climate response, not the observed temperature change during a single El Niño or storm.
- **Top-of-atmosphere versus surface budget:** two control volumes with different inputs, outputs, and internal exchanges.

### What the instruments actually measure

CERES radiometers constrain reflected solar and emitted thermal radiation at the top of the atmosphere. They do not measure integrated planetary heat content by themselves. Ocean observations, including Argo profiles, constrain changes in temperature through depth; estimating global ocean heat storage requires spatial coverage, density and heat-capacity assumptions, and corrections for incomplete sampling. Comparison supports a planetary energy imbalance but should retain uncertainties in both observation systems.

### Questions worth testing

1. Why does radiation absorbed and emitted by a molecule inside the atmosphere not violate energy conservation?
2. How can the surface receive more downward infrared radiation than direct sunlight yet not be generating energy?
3. What would be measured if global temperature were constant but ocean heat content continued to rise?
4. Why can the ocean delay a temperature response without cancelling the ultimate effect of a sustained energy imbalance?
5. How do cloud reflection and cloud infrared emission pull the energy budget in different directions?
