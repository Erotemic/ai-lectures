# Who Keeps the Air Breathable? Oxygen on Three Timescales

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Stand at the surface of a lake on a summer morning. Green plants and algae are receiving sunlight. If the water is productive, photosynthesis can release oxygen into the water. A little deeper, fish and bacteria consume dissolved oxygen. Deeper still, the same lake may contain water that has scarcely exchanged with the surface for months. Its oxygen concentration can be very low even though the air directly overhead remains rich in oxygen. Here is a paradox worth understanding: oxygen can be abundant in the atmosphere and scarce only a few meters below it. The difference is a matter of transport, consumption, and time.

Oxygen is both an element in many minerals and molecules and, in one particular form, a gas that organisms use for aerobic respiration. Oxygen atoms occur in water, rocks, and carbon dioxide, but a fish cannot breathe the oxygen chemically bound in a water molecule. A fish needs dissolved molecular oxygen. Likewise, a human breathes oxygen gas, not the oxygen atoms locked inside atmospheric carbon dioxide. As with nitrogen and phosphorus, an element's location and chemical state determine its biological role.

The most familiar source of molecular oxygen is oxygenic photosynthesis. Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria use light energy to drive reactions in which water is split and electrons are transferred through the photosynthetic machinery. Molecular oxygen is released as a product. Carbon dioxide is incorporated into organic compounds through connected processes. In simplified classroom diagrams, photosynthesis takes in carbon dioxide and water and produces organic matter and oxygen. That is useful for stoichiometry, but the oxygen gas released by oxygenic photosynthesis originates from water, not directly from the oxygen atoms of carbon dioxide. This distinction was established through isotope-tracing experiments and is important when following the atoms rather than just balancing the equation.

It is tempting to conclude that any sunny, green ecosystem continuously adds oxygen to the atmosphere. That conclusion is wrong. The same organic matter produced by photosynthesis can be consumed through respiration and decomposition. In aerobic respiration, oxygen is used while organic carbon is oxidized, often returning carbon dioxide to the environment. If photosynthesis and subsequent oxidation are balanced over an interval, there may be substantial oxygen production and oxygen consumption but little net accumulation in the atmosphere. Oxygen production is a gross flux. Net long-term oxygen gain requires additional accounting, including whether reduced carbon or other reduced material escapes reoxidation by burial or other pathways.

Consider one leaf. During daylight it may produce more oxygen than it consumes. At night photosynthesis stops but respiration continues. Over a growing season, an entire forest can accumulate biomass. Later, some of that biomass is eaten, decomposes, or burns. The oxygen budget depends on where the carbon goes and over what timescale. A forest can affect atmospheric carbon dioxide profoundly without causing the atmospheric oxygen fraction to change measurably in step with its daily photosynthesis. The atmosphere is a large reservoir, and oxygen's long-term history is governed by sources and sinks accumulated over geological time.

That long history is remarkable. For much of Earth's early existence, the atmosphere contained very little free oxygen compared with today. Microbial metabolisms developed in an environment with different chemical constraints. Oxygenic photosynthesis eventually became a major source of oxygen, but a source alone does not imply immediate atmospheric accumulation. Oxygen reacts with reduced iron, sulfur compounds, organic carbon, and other materials. The early Earth offered many sinks. Only when the balance between sources and sinks changed could atmospheric oxygen increase persistently.

Geologists identify a major rise in atmospheric oxygen roughly two point four billion years ago, usually called the Great Oxidation Event. Do not imagine it as one afternoon when the sky suddenly acquired today's composition. The oxygenation history was prolonged, geographically varied, and not necessarily monotonic. Geological evidence indicates that oxygen became increasingly important in surface environments while many parts of the ocean remained oxygen-poor. Later changes in ocean and atmospheric chemistry occurred over hundreds of millions of years. We are reconstructing a sequence of altered chemical budgets, not reading a direct thermometer or air sample from the Precambrian.

How can researchers infer oxygen in an atmosphere that vanished billions of years ago? They study chemical signals preserved in rocks. One particularly important kind of evidence concerns sulfur isotopes. Under low-oxygen atmospheric conditions, ultraviolet-driven chemistry can produce mass-independent fractionation patterns in sulfur isotopes that are preserved in sedimentary rocks. The disappearance or marked change of those patterns helps constrain when the atmosphere changed enough to suppress that chemistry. Other proxies use iron minerals, chromium isotopes, and trace elements whose mobility or oxidation state depends on oxygen availability. Each proxy responds to a set of reactions, not to an exact percentage of oxygen in every ocean basin.

There is a danger in speaking as though these proxies were ancient gas meters with a digital readout. A sulfur-isotope signal provides evidence about atmospheric photochemistry, and mineral assemblages provide evidence about particular environmental conditions. Scientists combine them with sedimentology, geochemical models, and age determinations to evaluate possible histories. Different proxies can disagree because they record different places and processes, or because assumptions about preservation are incomplete. The history of oxygen is not a single line drawn from one rock sample. It is a reconstruction from partial, cross-checked evidence.

Oxygen also created new opportunities for metabolism. Aerobic respiration allows organisms to use oxygen as a strong electron acceptor, enabling efficient energy extraction from certain organic substrates. But oxygen can also damage cellular components through reactive oxygen chemistry. Life evolved defenses and regulatory systems alongside the advantages of oxygen use. These changes connect planetary chemistry to biological evolution. Yet one should not say that oxygen caused every major evolutionary innovation by itself. Ecology, genetics, nutrients, environmental change, and many other constraints matter. The oxygen story is one part of a larger, entangled history.

Now return to the modern ocean. Surface seawater exchanges gases with the atmosphere. Its dissolved oxygen concentration depends on temperature, salinity, air pressure, mixing, and biological activity. Colder water can generally dissolve more oxygen than warmer water under otherwise comparable conditions. But that simple solubility effect is not enough to understand oxygen far below the surface. Deep and intermediate waters acquire oxygen where they last contacted the surface, then travel through the circulation while organisms consume oxygen by oxidizing organic matter. The oxygen remaining at depth reflects how water was ventilated and how much respiration occurred along its path.

In the productive surface ocean, phytoplankton use sunlight and nutrients. Some organic material is eaten and recycled near the surface. Some sinks as particles or is transported downward in other ways. Microbes and animals consume much of this material, using dissolved oxygen as they respire. If oxygen consumption is strong and renewed oxygen arrives slowly, an oxygen minimum can form. The eastern tropical Pacific and parts of the northern Indian Ocean have pronounced low-oxygen regions associated with this combination of biology and circulation. These regions are not merely patches of water containing less dissolved gas because they happen to be warm. They are the product of a three-dimensional transport-and-reaction system.

A water mass is not labeled with the date it last touched the atmosphere, but scientists can estimate ventilation ages and pathways using dissolved gases, chemical tracers, temperature, salinity, and circulation models. Measurements of oxygen profiles reveal the concentration at each depth. Repeat observations reveal change over time. Autonomous platforms can sample large regions, although no single platform measures the entire global inventory. To estimate global change, researchers integrate many observations and account for gaps, instrument differences, and unequal spatial sampling. An oxygen trend is an inference from a distributed observing system, not the reading of one sensor in the middle of the sea.

Researchers Sunke Schmidtko, Lothar Stramma, and Martin Visbeck assessed the global ocean oxygen inventory in a study published in 2017. Using observations spanning decades, they found an overall loss of more than two percent of ocean oxygen content since 1960, with substantial regional differences and uncertainties. The precise number should be understood as the result of that analysis and period, not as a timeless constant. Later assessments, including the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, also identify widespread ocean deoxygenation while emphasizing the variation among depths and regions. A global average hides differences that matter profoundly to a particular marine ecosystem.

Why would a warming climate contribute to oxygen loss? First, solubility: warmer water tends to hold less dissolved oxygen at saturation. Second, circulation: warming and freshening can change density structure, mixing, and the rate at which oxygen-rich surface water reaches the interior. Third, biological processes: changing productivity, respiration, and nutrient supply can alter where oxygen is consumed. These effects can reinforce or oppose one another locally. A shallow coastal bay receiving excess organic matter has a different oxygen problem from an open-ocean intermediate water mass that is ventilating more slowly.

Organisms have different oxygen requirements; there is no single dissolved-oxygen threshold for all life. A fast-swimming fish with high aerobic demand may need more dissolved oxygen than a tolerant worm or microorganism. Some microbes thrive where free oxygen is absent by using nitrate, sulfate, or other electron acceptors. Low-oxygen conditions change habitats and chemical cycling, but they do not make the water chemically or biologically empty. The phrase dead zone can communicate an ecological crisis, yet it obscures the activity of organisms that continue to metabolize under low-oxygen conditions.

When oxygen is scarce, nitrogen and phosphorus cycling can shift. Nitrate may be consumed through processes that release nitrogen gas or nitrous oxide. Iron minerals in sediments can change oxidation state and release bound phosphorus under some conditions. Sulfur metabolism may produce sulfide. The oxygen budget, therefore, is not independent of the nutrient budgets. The availability of an electron acceptor can determine which pathways organisms use, and those pathways feed back on the nutrients available for new organic production. What looks like a problem of breathing turns into a problem of whole-system chemistry.

We have arrived at three clocks. Photosynthesis and respiration can change oxygen conditions across the day. Ocean mixing, biological production, and the aging of water masses operate over seasons to centuries. The rise of atmospheric oxygen and the burial of reduced material unfold over immense spans of geological time. They involve related chemistry, but they cannot be substituted for one another. An algal bloom releasing oxygen this afternoon is not evidence that the atmosphere has gained the same amount permanently. A warming ocean losing dissolved oxygen is not evidence that the air above it has become unbreathable.

The value of an oxygen budget is that it forces those clocks apart. We can ask how many oxygen molecules are present, how many are entering through exchange or photosynthesis, how many are consumed, and whether transport replenishes a depleted reservoir quickly enough. We can then ask what organisms and chemical reactions are affected when those rates change. Earth has a breathable atmosphere because of a long history of biological production and geological balances. A particular fish has breathable water only if local oxygen supply keeps pace with demand. The same element serves both stories, but the explanation depends on scale.

## Scientific companion: oxygen budgets and proxy evidence

### Three distinct oxygen questions

1. **Daily aquatic oxygen:** gross photosynthesis, respiration, gas exchange, mixing and storage change dissolved concentration within hours.
2. **Ocean oxygen inventory:** solubility, ventilation and consumption change the distribution of dissolved O₂ over years to centuries. Ocean deoxygenation is not a claim that atmospheric O₂ is rapidly disappearing.
3. **Geological atmospheric oxygen:** preservation of reduced carbon and sulfur, weathering and oxidation, and long-term volcanic/tectonic fluxes constrain persistent changes in free O₂.

### Measurements and uncertainties

Water oxygen at the surface is approximately linked to atmospheric equilibrium but can depart from it with photosynthesis, respiration, warming, and rapid mixing. The ocean interior cannot be inferred from sea-surface temperature alone. Paleoproxies (sulfur mass-independent isotopes, iron and chromium records) do not constitute direct measurements of Precambrian oxygen percentages and require geological interpretation.
