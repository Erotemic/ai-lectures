# A River in the Sky Meets a Watershed: Atmospheric Rivers and Floods

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In February 2017, storms crossed the Pacific and struck northern California. The Sierra Nevada already held a deep snowpack. One pulse of incoming air was cooler and added snow in the mountains. Another was warmer, bringing rain to elevations where snow had accumulated. Water poured toward Lake Oroville. Rising reservoir levels, combined with serious damage to spillway infrastructure, led to a major emergency and large-scale evacuations. What matters for our story is that the runoff could not be understood from the rainfall total alone. The water released from snow already on the mountain was part of the flood-producing budget.

The atmospheric feature carrying the moisture was an atmospheric river: a long, narrow region of especially strong horizontal water-vapor transport, often embedded in a moving weather system. The term is vivid, but a literal river would mislead us. The atmosphere is not a pipe of liquid water, and it is not necessary for a narrow strip of clouds to look like a river from space. Most of the transported water is invisible vapor. The amount actually delivered as rain or snow depends on how the moving air rises, cools, and releases moisture along its route.

Why are atmospheric rivers so consequential? Start with two properties: how much water vapor a column of air contains, and how fast that column moves horizontally. A very humid but nearly stationary layer may transport relatively little moisture across a coastline. A strong wind carrying moderate humidity through a deep layer can deliver a great deal. Meteorologists therefore study vertically integrated water vapor transport, or IVT. In words, they add up the horizontal movement of water vapor through the atmospheric column. The resulting quantity has both magnitude and direction. A map of it reveals corridors through which the atmosphere is transferring water.

Atmospheric rivers are often associated with extratropical cyclones and frontal systems. Winds organize moisture into a corridor that can extend thousands of kilometers. Some moisture originates through tropical or subtropical evaporation; some is picked up along the way. The phrase pineapple express refers to a familiar North Pacific configuration that can transport moisture from around Hawaii toward western North America, but not every atmospheric river follows that path. Similar features affect western Europe, Chile, New Zealand, and many other coasts and mountain ranges. The phenomenon is global even though the Pacific examples have received much of the public attention.

Transport does not automatically mean precipitation. Imagine moist air flowing parallel to a coastline over relatively flat ground. Some vapor may move past without much condensation. Now imagine the same flow confronting a mountain wall. Air is forced upward. As it expands, it cools, and cloud water and precipitation can form. On the windward slopes, rain or snow may be concentrated. The leeward side can receive much less, because rising air has already lost part of its moisture and descending air warms. This is orographic precipitation. The strength and direction of the moisture corridor relative to the terrain help decide where the water falls.

The temperature profile decides what form it takes. If much of the mountain is cold enough, precipitation accumulates as snow and stores water above the valleys. If the freezing level is higher, rain can fall over a larger part of the catchment and reach streams quickly. Snow is not merely frozen rainfall in the budget. It is a reservoir with a delayed release mechanism, and the delay changes with temperature, solar radiation, wind, humidity, and the supply of energy during the storm itself. A mountain watershed can receive the same water equivalent in two storms but respond very differently depending on how much arrives as rain and how much remains in snow.

Here is a useful thought experiment. Two storms each deliver one hundred millimeters of water equivalent to a mountain basin. The first arrives during a cold period, leaving much of it as snow that may melt gradually over future weeks. The second arrives when rain falls up to the highest ridges. Streams may rise quickly after the second even though the precipitation total is identical. Now put the second storm over deep preexisting snow and sufficiently warm, wet air. Some stored water may be released as well. The runoff response can exceed what a rainfall gauge alone suggests, although rain on snow does not always produce large melt. The entire snowpack and energy balance have to be considered.

This is what researchers examined after the Oroville emergency. Studies led by Brian Henn and colleagues combined atmospheric observations with estimates of snow water equivalent, precipitation, and melt. They found that the early-February 2017 sequence generated exceptionally large runoff, partly because unusually high snowmelt added to the rain-fed water supply. The study estimated that snowmelt increased watershed-wide water available for runoff by about thirty-seven percent relative to rain alone, with a substantial uncertainty range. This is an estimate for a particular event and basin, not a universal multiplier for every atmospheric river.

Even when no mountain snow is involved, the land has its own memory. The first rain of a storm may infiltrate into relatively dry soil and replenish small storage spaces. After days of rain, the soil may be much wetter. New rainfall then encounters less storage capacity and more readily generates runoff. But soil infiltration is not controlled by wetness alone: texture, vegetation, cracks, frozen ground, and the intensity of rainfall all matter. An intense shower can exceed the rate at which even initially dry soil can accept water. Hydrologists distinguish infiltration-excess runoff from saturation-excess runoff because the two arise through different mechanisms.

Water moving through a watershed also has several clocks. Rain falling on an impervious street may reach a storm drain rapidly. Water on a forested slope may be delayed in shallow soil or surface depressions. Groundwater may support a stream during dry weather. River channels connect distant places, so a flood crest can arrive downstream after local rain has stopped. Different subcatchments deliver their peaks at different times; sometimes their contributions reinforce each other, sometimes they do not. Flood severity depends partly on this timing, not only on the total volume that fell across the basin.

Infrastructure inserts another set of conditions. Reservoirs can store part of a flood, but they have finite capacity and must be operated under competing goals that include water supply, hydropower, environmental flows, and protection against future storms. Levees can redirect water while changing where it accumulates. Roads, culverts, and urban drainage systems impose bottlenecks. The Oroville event is therefore both an atmospheric-hydrological case and an infrastructure case: the storm and runoff created a severe loading situation, while the spillway damage and reservoir-management context shaped the emergency. It would be wrong to treat the physical rainfall as the sole explanation for the evacuation.

When hydrologists say a flood was a hundred-year flood, the phrase can be heard as a promise that the next such event cannot happen for a century. That is not what it means. Under a stationary model, an annual exceedance probability of one percent corresponds to an average recurrence interval of a hundred years. The chance is assigned to each year, not to a waiting schedule. Two such events can occur in consecutive years. And the estimated probability depends on the period of record, the measurement and model uncertainties, and whether the underlying climate and land use are changing. It is a statistical description of a chosen river-flow threshold, not a law of nature.

Atmospheric rivers have their own scale, developed by F. Martin Ralph and colleagues and published in 2019. The scale groups events into five categories using the magnitude of water-vapor transport and the duration of atmospheric-river conditions at a location. Weaker events may be mostly beneficial, replenishing water supplies and snowpack, while stronger or longer-lived events can be highly disruptive. The scale is valuable because a corridor that transports moisture for twelve hours differs from one that continues for three days. But it is not a direct flood-depth rating. Two basins under the same category can respond differently because of terrain, snow levels, antecedent wetness, and the land below.

In parts of the western United States, atmospheric rivers are both a water source and a flood hazard. Water managers depend on relatively few winter storms for a substantial share of annual precipitation in some basins. A series of moderate atmospheric rivers can help end a seasonal deficit and rebuild snowpack. Yet a sequence that fills reservoirs and saturates soils can leave the same region more vulnerable to the next event. The very process that supplies water can produce floods when the rate, timing, and storage conditions align. Atmospheric rivers are not intrinsically disasters. Their value or danger depends on the receiving landscape and how rapidly the water changes form and location.

How are they measured and predicted? Satellites estimate water vapor and cloud structures across wide areas of ocean. Weather balloons and aircraft dropsondes provide vertical profiles. Coastal radar, precipitation gauges, snow sensors, stream gauges, and reservoir observations measure different parts of the water budget. Computer models combine these sources to predict the evolving moisture corridor and precipitation. The forecast then has to be translated into likely runoff through hydrological models that represent land cover, soil, snow storage, channel flow, and reservoirs. A good forecast of IVT is not automatically a good forecast of water depth at a specific intersection.

There is an important source of uncertainty in the phrase snow level. The height at which precipitation switches from snow to rain can move rapidly during a storm. It depends on the vertical temperature structure, the melting of falling particles, and the local atmosphere in mountain valleys. A shift of several hundred meters may expose a large new area to rain instead of snowfall. If a forecast describes an atmospheric river's moisture content accurately but gets the freezing level wrong, the forecast river response can still be wrong. Oroville illustrates why a detailed vertical temperature forecast can be as consequential as knowing that a large moisture corridor is approaching.

What happens as climate warms? A warmer atmosphere can hold more water vapor near saturation according to well-understood thermodynamics, often summarized as about seven percent more saturation vapor pressure per degree Celsius around ordinary Earth temperatures. But that is not a rule saying precipitation in every storm rises seven percent per degree. Moisture supply, winds, ascent, storm tracks, and the availability of condensation all intervene. Research using physically constrained model experiments has found climate-change enhancement of precipitation in some atmospheric-river cases, including a study of the 2017 Oroville storms. Such results are specific to their model design and the region and event being studied. They do not erase the role of natural variability or justify an identical number for every future storm.

The rising freezing level can produce another change: more mountain precipitation falling as rain instead of snow in some conditions, along with changes in melt timing. That affects the seasonal distribution of stored water. A wet year need not produce a healthy late-summer water supply if a smaller share remains in snow to melt gradually. A heavy atmospheric-river year can leave both replenished groundwater and damaged floodplain infrastructure. The result cannot be summarized by either good or bad weather without specifying which part of the water system and which time scale we care about.

Take the February 2017 mountain storms as the final picture. Water vapor was moved across an ocean. Winds forced it upward against the Sierra Nevada. The air's temperature determined rain and snow. Older snow supplied some of the runoff. Soil, streams, reservoir storage, and damaged engineering structures determined how the water became a crisis. A precipitation map would have captured only one layer of this history. To understand a flood, follow the water before it falls, while it is stored, and after it reaches the ground. The sky delivers a flux. The watershed produces the flood.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Water fluxes and distinctions

- **Integrated vapor transport (IVT):** vertically integrated horizontal flux of atmospheric water vapor; common units kg m^-1 s^-1. It includes wind and specific humidity throughout the column, not just precipitable water.
- **Atmospheric-river scale:** Ralph et al. (2019) proposed AR1-AR5 using maximum IVT and duration, with a threshold convention of 250 kg m^-1 s^-1 for defining AR conditions at a point. Local damage is not implied directly by category.
- **Rain-on-snow:** does not necessarily melt an entire snowpack; energy and water exchange, snowpack state, precipitation temperature and freeze level govern the net effect.
- **Flow:** precipitation, infiltration, soil and groundwater storage, snow accumulation/melt, evaporation and channel routing are separately modeled terms. Precipitation is not equal to streamflow.
- **Annual exceedance probability:** under a stationary assumption, a 1% annual chance is not a prediction of a century-long gap. Changing catchment and climate conditions challenge stationarity.
- **Clausius-Clapeyron:** saturation vapor pressure increases with temperature; actual precipitation changes depend additionally on atmospheric dynamics and availability of moisture.
