# The Landscape Is Moving: Weathering, Rivers, and the Making of Relief

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

After a heavy storm, two rivers meet. One is cloudy brown; the other is much clearer. The brown water is carrying tiny fragments of hillsides, roadsides, and streambanks, most of them too small to distinguish by eye. If we stood at the junction for only a minute, we might imagine that the dirt was always there. If we returned every month, we would see a different pattern. A flood can move a large fraction of the sediment that passes a measuring station in an entire year. The land upstream has entered the river, and the river will deliver some of it to a lake, a floodplain, an estuary, or the sea. A landscape is not a backdrop against which Earth systems act. It is a moving inventory of rock, soil, sediment, and dissolved substances.

Let us make three distinctions before traveling downstream. Weathering changes material where it sits. Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces; chemical weathering alters minerals by reaction. Erosion is the removal and transport of material from a place. Deposition occurs when that transported material is left somewhere else. These processes are linked but not synonymous. A rock can chemically weather in place without immediately being carried away. An unweathered boulder can fall from a cliff and be transported by a stream. Fine grains can settle on a floodplain and remain there until a later flood picks them up again.

Walk up from the river onto a hillside. The rock is crosscut by cracks. Water enters those openings; changes in temperature, growing roots, and in some climates freezing and thawing may widen them. Gravity can pry apart already weakened blocks. Meanwhile water and dissolved gases act chemically on minerals. Some feldspars react to form clay minerals and dissolved ions. Iron-bearing minerals can oxidize. Carbonate rock may dissolve along fractures and produce caves or springs. These are not just different ways of making smaller rocks. Chemical reactions change what the particles are made of and what they can carry dissolved in water.

The soil on top is neither simply pulverized bedrock nor merely dead leaves. It is a mixture assembled by mineral weathering, biological activity, movement of water, organic matter accumulation, and transport. A soil can have distinct horizons because minerals, organic compounds, and fine particles are produced or moved at different depths. Roots stabilize some slopes while also driving fractures and altering local chemistry. Microbes affect decomposition and the balance of carbon and nutrients. Soil may form slowly yet disappear rapidly when exposed to strong runoff. To describe whether a hillside is losing soil, we need a mass balance of production, arrival, removal, and storage, not only a photograph of the ground surface.

Return to the river. Flowing water exerts forces on its bed and banks. A grain is more likely to move when the drag and lift of the flow overcome its submerged weight, friction, and whatever binds it to surrounding particles. Coarse grains may roll, slide, or hop along the bed, a collection of movements called bedload. Finer particles can remain suspended in turbulent water and travel far downstream. Dissolved ions, invisible in the muddy color, are carried with the water itself. One river transports these three loads simultaneously, and each responds differently to a storm. The mud we can see is not a measurement of the river's total chemical export.

Why does a river sometimes cut a narrow gorge and elsewhere wander across a wide floodplain? Slope, discharge, sediment supply, bed material, vegetation, geological structure, and past changes all matter. Steeper water has more potential energy to convert into motion and work, but the actual capacity to erode depends on channel geometry and resistance. A fast flow may entrain gravel that a slower flow leaves in place. In a broad low-gradient reach, deposition can build bars and floodplains, and the channel may shift across material it previously deposited. Rivers can undercut valley walls, trigger slope failures, and move the resulting debris onward. The river inherits the geology of its watershed while repeatedly reshaping that watershed.

Now imagine looking only at a calm summer day. The river may carry clear water and little visible sediment. You might conclude that geological change is negligible. Then a strong storm raises discharge and produces torrents that scour banks, move cobbles, and deliver a pulse of suspended matter. The relationship between water discharge and sediment transport is not always linear. There may be a threshold for moving coarse material, or an early burst of soil and channel sediment that dwindles before the flood peak. One US Geological Survey explanation notes that a storm period can account for more than half of a year's sediment transfer at some river locations. It is not claiming that every river behaves that way. It shows why short observation windows can be misleading.

A useful measurement program therefore pairs water discharge with suspended-sediment sampling or optical turbidity observations. A sampler measures particle concentration in a known volume; combining concentration with discharge estimates how much suspended material passes the station per unit time. River chemistry samples measure dissolved constituents. Bedload needs additional methods because coarse grains move near the bottom, not in the water column sampled for suspended sediment. We must also choose a cross-section carefully. Sediment concentrations vary across a channel and through a flood. Even a well-maintained station does not reveal every individual rock's source or final destination. It constrains an integrated flux at one place.

The mountain above the stream has its own clock. Tectonic forces deform and uplift Earth's crust over long periods. When rock is exposed at higher elevations, rain, ice, chemical reactions, and gravity begin taking it apart. Erosion can reduce the mountain's height, but removing rock also unloads the crust, which may rise in response through isostatic adjustment. Uplift and erosion are therefore not exact opposites that cancel one another at a simple rate. A landscape may be gaining elevation in some places and losing rock mass at the same time. Measurements of surface elevation alone cannot distinguish rock uplift from the removal or accumulation of material without further information.

Glaciers enter this story as moving tools and reservoirs. Ice can quarry blocks from a bed, abrade rock with embedded debris, and move sediment into moraines. Meltwater may transport fine glacial flour down a valley, turning a river milky. When ice retreats, newly exposed slopes can be unstable and sediment supply can change. Rivers can incise the sediment that glaciers once left. Across seasons and centuries, the geography of snow and ice affects when water arrives and how much mobile rock is available. The hydrological and geomorphic systems are coupled: the same storm can fill a reservoir, erode a bank, and destabilize a hillside.

A landslide is one of the most conspicuous examples of motion, but it is not a single mechanism. Rockfalls, slides, slumps, and debris flows move in different ways. Gravity drives them, while several conditions affect resistance. Water entering pores may increase pore pressure and reduce the effective stress that holds grains together; it can also add weight. A river may remove support at a slope's foot. An earthquake can produce sudden acceleration. Frozen ground may thaw, or vegetation may be removed by fire. The question is usually not simply 'what triggered the landslide?' but 'why was this particular slope susceptible when the trigger arrived?' The US Geological Survey organizes landslide types by both movement and material, precisely because the hazards have different physics.

Consider a mountain drainage after an earthquake. The first photographs may show vast fresh scars, and it is natural to expect the river to export a matching surge of sediment and carbon. Yet the connection is not automatic. Material can remain stored on hillslopes, be trapped in valleys, or move only in later storms. A study led by Lena Märki of a central Himalayan basin across the 2015 Gorkha earthquake followed several monsoon seasons of water, sediment, and carbon flux. Its interpretation did not reduce to counting landslides: the integrated carbon pathways included export of young biological organic matter, weathering-related ions, and other sources. The observed large-scale river carbon budget did not change in a simple one-to-one proportion with the earthquake-triggered slides. This is a reminder that moving solid material and exporting dissolved or organic carbon are related but separate measurements.

The chemical side of erosion reaches into planetary climate. Atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolves in rainwater and soil water, participating in reactions that alter silicate minerals. Rivers carry some reaction products toward the ocean. On long timescales, weathering and subsequent carbonate formation can withdraw carbon dioxide from the fast atmosphere-ocean system. In 1981 Walker, Hays, and Kasting described how climate-sensitive silicate weathering might help stabilize surface temperature over geological spans. But an actual mountain contains multiple kinds of rock and carbon. Weathering a carbonate rock is not equivalent to weathering a silicate. Oxidation of old organic matter or sulfide minerals can introduce other chemical pathways. A mountain building phase can create reactive surfaces while also changing the exposure and oxidation of ancient carbon.

The familiar story that mountain uplift 'cools the planet' leaves out several competing processes. Exposed silicate minerals and runoff can contribute to long-term carbon dioxide removal. But the global outcome also depends on the climate, erosion rate, availability of fresh minerals, biological carbon burial, and chemical sources of dissolved ions. Uplift is not a dial that always produces one sign of atmospheric carbon change. Nor should we expect a silicate-weathering feedback that operates over hundreds of thousands of years to erase additional fossil carbon on the timescale of a human lifetime. The mechanisms are connected, but their clocks are different.

Where does the sediment end up? Some is stored in river bars, lakes, reservoirs, and floodplains. Some continues to an estuary and delta, where fresh water slows, mixes with salt water, and deposits material. Sediment delivered to a coast can help maintain low-lying land against subsidence and waves, while dams, channel changes, and coastal engineering alter the supply and distribution. Some fine material crosses the shelf and settles in deeper basins, where new sedimentary layers may eventually become rock. This is part of how a hill can become sediment at the sea floor without ceasing to be part of Earth's mass. The path is branching and full of delays, not a direct conveyor from summit to ocean trench.

To study such a system we need more than one kind of map. Satellite imagery records surface change over time. Surveyed river sections and sediment gauges constrain transport. Chemical analyses distinguish dissolved minerals and carbon pools. Geodetic measurements can detect changes in crustal position, while rock ages and sediment records establish longer histories. Landslide inventories tell us where failures occurred; they do not by themselves measure what a downstream estuary received. The strength of an Earth-system account comes from connecting observations with clear boundaries and units: rock removed from a slope, particles crossing a river section, ions delivered to the sea, carbon remaining buried.

Return to the meeting of the two rivers after the storm. A bright brown plume is not merely a sign that the water is dirty. It is a visible fragment of a larger budget: the production of fragments on hillsides, their transport in rare high flows, their storage and later remobilization, and the invisible movement of dissolved chemistry beside them. The rocks beneath our feet change on geological clocks, but their motion also comes in sudden events. The land is not fixed while air and water move across it. The land moves with them, and through weathering and sediment transport it changes the chemistry, carbon cycle, and shape of the planet.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Processes and conserved quantities

- **Weathering:** physical comminution or chemical alteration in place; may change mineral composition and dissolved species.
- **Erosion:** removal and transport from a location; can be mechanical or involve dissolved products.
- **Deposition:** storage of previously moved materials. Remobilization can occur much later.
- **River sediment discharge:** \(Q_s = Q_w C_s\) for a concentration \(C_s\) measured in compatible units; careful cross-sectional and temporal integration is needed. Bedload and dissolved loads are not included in a suspended-sediment estimate unless measured separately.
- **Carbon budget:** separate silicate chemical weathering, carbonate reactions, oxidation of ancient organic matter, and burial of recent biological carbon. A large solid sediment flux does not automatically imply a proportional net atmospheric carbon sink.

### Why a single erosion rate can mislead

The river can respond to a few threshold-crossing events rather than smooth average transport. The exposure age of bedrock, production of weathered material, temporary sediment storage, and tectonic forcing operate on different timescales. River load measured at a gauge is not the same quantity as total hillslope erosion upstream if intermediate storage is changing.

### Questions worth thinking through

1. Why can a river have high sediment transport during one storm even if its annual average flow is modest?
2. How do suspended load, bedload, and dissolved load differ in their measurement and destination?
3. Why does an earthquake-triggered landslide not guarantee an equally large immediate sediment pulse at the river mouth?
4. Which additional observations would be needed to turn 'rapid erosion' into a statement about net carbon sequestration?
5. How can crustal uplift and net loss of rock mass occur simultaneously in a mountain range?
