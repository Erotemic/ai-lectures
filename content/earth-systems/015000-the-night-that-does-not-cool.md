# The Night That Does Not Cool: Heat Waves, Cities, and Attribution

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On the last days of June 2021, temperatures across the Pacific Northwest and southwestern Canada reached levels far beyond those normally expected for the region. In Lytton, British Columbia, the recorded temperature reached forty-nine point six degrees Celsius. The heat affected homes, roads, forests, farms, and the people inside buildings built for a cooler climate. There were deaths and hospitalizations. The event has become a case study not just in extreme temperature, but in two different scientific questions: what atmospheric sequence made the heat possible, and how did the warming background climate change its likelihood and intensity?

A heat wave is not simply an afternoon when the thermometer rises high. Definitions vary by region and purpose, but they generally involve a period of abnormally hot conditions relative to local climate. Duration matters because the atmosphere, buildings, soils, and living bodies retain memory. A place accustomed to hot summers may have different infrastructure and habits from a place where comparable temperatures are rare. We therefore need to distinguish the meteorological intensity of the episode from the exposure and vulnerability of the organisms and people experiencing it.

Let us begin with the atmosphere. Solar radiation heats land and the lower atmosphere. Near the surface, energy is divided among several paths: warming the ground and air, evaporation of water, and exchanges with buildings and vegetation. Clouds change how much sunlight reaches the ground, while water vapor and other greenhouse gases influence the escape of infrared radiation. Winds move air masses across great distances. A local temperature record depends on this entire energy and transport budget, not on sunlight alone.

Many persistent hot spells form under strong, slow-moving high-pressure systems. The phrase heat dome is often used for one such pattern. Under a broad upper-level ridge, air may descend, compress, and warm. The circulation can suppress clouds and allow strong sunshine. A blocked or slowly moving pattern can persist for days instead of being replaced by the next weather system. The metaphor of a dome is helpful if it reminds us of persistent circulation; it is misleading if it suggests a transparent physical lid trapping hot air like a closed greenhouse. The atmosphere remains a moving fluid.

Another part of the mechanism can be air descending from mountains. When air moves down a slope, it encounters greater pressure, compresses, and warms. If it has already lost moisture on the windward side, it may arrive relatively dry and warm. Regional topography can therefore add a substantial temperature anomaly to a large-scale warm pattern. The 2021 Pacific Northwest event combined an exceptionally strong upper-level ridge with subsidence and regional flow effects, and research has explored the contribution of soil dryness as well. The record did not emerge from a single cause called a heat dome; several pieces aligned.

Soil moisture can change a hot day profoundly. A wet landscape can use part of the available energy to evaporate water. Evaporation carries energy away as latent heat rather than directly heating the near-surface air. If surface water becomes scarce, less energy goes into evaporation and more may warm the land and air. Plants also alter the surface energy balance through shading, transpiration, and their effect on roughness and wind. This helps explain why a hot and dry landscape can reinforce its own heat, and why the preceding season sometimes matters to a later heat wave.

But do not infer that every dry patch makes the air hotter in the same way. A forest, irrigated field, bare soil, and paved district exchange heat and water differently. Strong winds may advect air from elsewhere. Clouds may arrive. Irrigation may cool the surface while consuming scarce water supplies. The partition between sensible heat and latent heat is constrained by energy and water availability, but the real atmosphere moves both quantities around. A heat wave is a coupled land-atmosphere event, not a thermometer responding only to the Sun.

Night introduces another clock. During daylight, the surface receives shortwave energy. After sunset, the surface can cool by emitting infrared radiation and by exchanging heat with the air. Wind, humidity, clouds, and the temperature of surrounding surfaces influence this cooling. If a heat wave persists, buildings and pavement can accumulate heat and release some of it later. A humid or cloudy night may reduce radiative cooling. The result can be a sequence of nights when temperatures remain high enough that people, animals, and buildings have little recovery time from the previous day.

Cities modify this cycle. Dark roofs and roads absorb radiation; building materials store heat; street canyons change how energy and wind move; and less vegetation often means less evaporative cooling. Air conditioners move heat from rooms to the outside while using electricity, adding waste heat locally. The urban heat-island effect varies with urban form and weather and is often particularly apparent at night. There is no universal number of extra degrees for every neighborhood. Even within one city, tree cover, building density, surface moisture, and access to cooling can create sharply different exposures.

That brings us from meteorology to physiology. A human body produces metabolic heat even when resting. It exchanges heat with the environment through radiation, convection, conduction, and the evaporation of sweat. If the surrounding air is cooler than the skin, moving air can help remove sensible heat. If the air is hotter than the skin, that pathway can reverse. Sweating can still cool the body, but only when water evaporates effectively. High humidity reduces the gradient that drives evaporation. Hot, dry air may permit substantial evaporation while also placing large radiant and convective heat loads on the body.

This is why air temperature alone does not describe thermal stress. The heat index combines air temperature and humidity under specific assumptions, generally for shaded conditions. Wet-bulb globe temperature is another measure designed to incorporate humidity, radiant loading, and wind exposure; it is useful when assessing work or exercise in the sun. The ordinary wet-bulb temperature is yet another quantity, related to evaporative cooling of a wetted thermometer under specified conditions. These measures are not different names for the same number, and neither replaces the body's own energy budget.

A famous theoretical discussion uses a wet-bulb temperature of thirty-five degrees Celsius as a potential upper limit for sustained human heat balance under highly simplified assumptions. It should not be taught as a sharp line below which everyone is safe. Laboratory studies in healthy adults found that uncompensable heat stress can occur at lower wet-bulb temperatures, especially under hot-dry conditions, and physiological modeling indicates that age, activity, sunshine, and individual characteristics matter. Heat-related illness and death occur in many settings far below thirty-five degrees wet bulb. The meaningful physical question is whether the body can shed heat at least as quickly as it gains and produces it over the actual duration of exposure.

Think of two nights with equal outdoor air temperature. On one, a person can sleep in a well-ventilated, shaded room that cools as soon as the Sun sets. On the other, an upper-floor apartment has absorbed heat all day, windows cannot provide useful airflow, and power outages prevent mechanical cooling. The weather station reports the same temperature, but the indoor exposure is not the same. A heat disaster cannot be read solely from a regional temperature anomaly. The distribution of cooling access, housing conditions, occupation, health, and social support changes its consequences.

Return to the Pacific Northwest in June 2021. Meteorologists reconstructed an unusual large-scale upper-atmospheric ridge and regional descending flow. Some studies emphasized how the location and strength of the ridge combined with topography, solar heating, and dry soils. These are explanations of the event's immediate weather mechanisms. They ask why the temperature grew so high at that particular time and place. Without the circulation pattern, the event would not have looked the same, no matter how warm the climate baseline had become.

Climate-attribution scientists ask a different question. Suppose we could observe many plausible evolutions of the weather with today's greenhouse-gas concentrations and compare them with a counterfactual world that had not undergone human-caused warming. How frequently would each world produce an event at least as hot as the one that occurred? Or how much hotter is an event of a chosen rarity in the present climate? These are statistical questions about distributions, not declarations that a single weather pattern has just one cause.

An international team using World Weather Attribution methods published a rapid assessment of the June 2021 heat wave. It concluded that an event this extreme would have been exceptionally unlikely without human-caused climate change. A subsequent peer-reviewed analysis led by Sjoukje Philip developed the statistical argument, while openly discussing a major difficulty: the observed temperatures lay so far outside the historical record that estimating an exact return period was unstable and dependent on assumptions about the tail of the distribution. The headline was not the precise number of years until repetition; it was that a warmer background substantially shifted the odds and intensity of extreme heat.

Other research can emphasize somewhat different numerical contributions or mechanisms, because it defines the event, counterfactual, or diagnostic variable differently. One study might hold the observed circulation pattern approximately fixed and ask how much warmth human-caused climate change added. Another might ask how frequently a circulation-temperature combination could arise in a long set of simulated worlds. They are related but not identical estimands. A careful reading looks for the exact temperature metric, spatial area, duration, baseline period, models, and uncertainty. It does not turn one modeled ratio into a property of every heat wave everywhere.

A broader body of evidence is clearer than any single exceptional case. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment concluded that human influence has increased the frequency and intensity of hot extremes across much of the world, and it projects further increases with additional warming. This is a statement about the changing distribution of temperatures, supported by observations, physical reasoning, and models. It does not imply that every city warms by the same amount or that any two record events have the same immediate weather causes. Global change and regional variability coexist.

Extreme heat can also combine with other hazards. When soil moisture declines, heat and drought can reinforce one another. High temperatures can increase evaporative demand and stress vegetation, while the lack of available water reduces evaporative cooling. Under appropriate wind, fuel, and ignition conditions, hot dry weather can contribute to fire danger; but heat alone does not ignite a wildfire. A power outage during an extended heat wave can increase indoor exposure. A hot spell following a flood can affect displaced people whose access to cooling is disrupted. The word compound describes interactions that make the total risk different from a list of independent hazards.

How should a scientific forecast communicate these possibilities? A short-term weather forecast can predict the approach and persistence of a ridge, high daily temperatures, and nighttime minima. Heat-health warnings add impact thresholds informed by local conditions. Seasonal outlooks can express the probability of generally warmer-than-normal conditions but cannot promise exactly which week will be dangerously hot. Climate projections describe how distributions may change over decades under different forcing pathways. All three products may be useful, yet they solve different problems and carry different kinds of uncertainty.

The difference between hazard and disaster is especially clear with heat. The atmosphere sets part of the thermal exposure. The built environment modifies it. Bodies have finite capacity to regulate internal temperature. Public health systems, housing, access to cooling, and the ability to change activity determine whether an exposure becomes fatal. Because these factors can change, two heat waves of similar meteorological intensity may produce different consequences. And because the climate baseline can change, the same vulnerability can become more dangerous even without a change in a city's buildings.

A heat wave is therefore a meeting of several budgets. There is the planet's radiation budget, which governs the warming background. There is the atmospheric energy budget of a persistent ridge and descending air. There is the surface budget, divided between evaporation and warming. There is a building's storage of daytime heat. Finally there is a living body's need to release metabolic and absorbed energy. Heat becomes extreme when these budgets align in ways that keep temperatures high and cooling insufficient. To understand why an event happened, we must trace its weather dynamics. To understand how its probability changed, we must study the climate distribution. To understand its human consequences, we must look at the places and lives in which it occurred.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Definitions and methodological limits

- **Heat wave:** a duration-and-anomaly-based category relative to a defined regional and seasonal reference; no single universal meteorological threshold.
- **Blocking ridge:** a persistent large-scale circulation feature associated with subsidence, warming and changed winds; not a physical impermeable dome.
- **Heat index:** combines temperature and relative humidity under specified shaded assumptions. **Wet-bulb globe temperature** includes radiant and wind effects. Neither is simply ordinary wet-bulb temperature.
- **Uncompensable heat stress:** core heat storage grows because heat production and gain exceed heat loss. The threshold depends on metabolism, clothing, physiology, humidity, radiation, wind and duration.
- **Event attribution:** model-observation comparison between factual and counterfactual climates. Risk ratios and changes in intensity answer different questions. Extreme tails can make quantitative return periods particularly uncertain.
- **Hazard / exposure / vulnerability:** meteorological extreme versus who or what is present and how susceptible it is, including cooling access and built environment.
