# A Second Earth for Experiments: Controls, Forcings, and Climate Projections

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

What would Earth's climate have been like if the atmosphere contained less carbon dioxide, but everything else had followed the same history? Or if the volcanic eruption of a particular year had not happened? The obvious scientific experiment would require two Earths, identical until the moment we changed one of them. We possess one. A climate model is not a substitute planet in the literal sense, but it gives us a way to ask controlled counterfactual questions under a stated set of physical assumptions. That is a different scientific achievement from predicting next Tuesday's rain.

Start with an experiment small enough to fit in a notebook. Imagine a simple planet represented by its average temperature and a heat reservoir. Sunlight enters; heat radiates to space; the reservoir responds slowly; a parameter describes how much extra radiation escapes when the planet warms. Now increase a greenhouse-gas forcing in the equations while leaving the other assumptions the same. The model warms until its altered outgoing radiation and feedbacks approach a new balance. You can repeat the calculation with a different heat capacity or feedback strength and learn which assumptions control the transient response. The model has simplified away clouds and continents, but it makes causal structure visible.

A full Earth-system model can run analogous experiments with geographic detail and many interacting variables. One experiment might keep external forcing nearly fixed to characterize internal variability and residual drift. Another can impose a step increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide and track warming, ocean heat uptake, and feedback responses. Another can simulate the past using changing solar activity, volcanic aerosols, greenhouse gases, land use, and other estimated historical influences. Still another can apply a possible future forcing pathway and examine the range of responses across models and initial conditions. These experiments answer different questions. A control is not a history simulation; an imposed step change is not a forecast of when such a change will actually occur.

The control run is particularly important. Imagine observing that an experimental model warms a tenth of a degree over several decades. Is that caused by a proposed external forcing, by unforced internal variability, or by the numerical model's slow adjustment from its initial state? A long preindustrial-style control simulation can help characterize the system's natural fluctuations and its drift under approximately constant boundary conditions. A companion forced run starts from a comparable model climate but changes a specified input. Comparing the two helps isolate the response, though the precise difference between two individual trajectories still contains internal weather and ocean variability. Multiple initial-condition members can improve estimation of the average forced signal.

A striking experiment is the abrupt increase of carbon dioxide to four times a reference concentration. Nobody should mistake this for a realistic emissions schedule. It deliberately produces a large radiative perturbation that reveals model response times and feedbacks. Early decades contain the influence of fast adjustments and ocean uptake. Later decades show slower equilibration of heat reservoirs. Researchers can examine how the change in top-of-atmosphere radiation relates to global temperature change and infer a useful approximation to the model's effective climate sensitivity. That method has assumptions, including possible time-varying feedback strength; it is not the same as directly measuring a constant of nature.

Now consider an experiment in which carbon dioxide concentration grows by one percent per year, compounded. Its purpose is not to assert that the world will follow that exact path. Because the idealized forcing grows in a specified way, comparisons across models are easier to interpret. Around the time carbon dioxide has doubled, the simulation provides a conventional way to characterize the transient climate response, which includes the effect of ocean heat uptake and feedbacks during the warming. Transient response and eventual equilibrium response are not interchangeable. The ocean's delay is part of the science being measured.

Historical simulations ask a different question. Can the model reproduce broad changes over periods for which we have measurements, when forced with estimates of the external influences that actually occurred? A model that reproduces both observed warming and volcanic cooling offers evidence that it captures relevant physical mechanisms. But a close historical temperature match is not by itself decisive. Researchers may have adjusted some parameters using observations from part of that history. Several compensating errors can also yield a plausible global average. Independent evidence from the ocean, radiation measurements, geography, the seasonal cycle, and other variables helps test the mechanism more demanding than a single temperature curve does.

How should we describe a future run? The word projection is often more precise than prediction. Suppose a simulation explores a pathway with particular greenhouse-gas concentrations, land use, and aerosols. The model estimates the climate response conditional on those inputs and its physical formulation. It does not know which energy technologies, policies, conflicts, or economic choices societies will actually make. A climate projection therefore has at least two layers of uncertainty: uncertainty about the external pathway and uncertainty about the Earth-system response given that pathway. Internal variability adds uncertainty to the timing and geography of some outcomes, particularly over shorter intervals and smaller regions.

We must distinguish concentrations from emissions. In one setup, the model receives a prescribed atmospheric concentration of carbon dioxide. It calculates the radiative consequences but need not determine how emissions and carbon sinks combined to create that concentration. In an emissions-driven experiment, the model receives a specified emissions pathway and calculates atmospheric concentration through carbon exchanges involving the ocean and land, subject to the components included. If warming weakens a natural carbon sink, emissions-driven and concentration-driven calculations expose different parts of that feedback. It matters which experiment we are reading before comparing its output to a policy question.

A second distinction is the meaning of scenario. A scenario is a conditional description of possible future inputs, often including socioeconomic assumptions or emissions and land-use trajectories. It is not a probability forecast unless probabilities have been established separately. We can compare a higher-emissions and lower-emissions pathway to show how physical outcomes depend on human choices, but the simple existence of two lines on a graph does not tell us the odds that the world will follow either one. That would require information about future behavior beyond the physical climate equations.

Experiments also use volcanic eruptions as opportunities for testing. A large eruption can inject sulfur-containing gases into the stratosphere, producing sulfate aerosols that reflect sunlight and influence the radiation budget for a limited period. Researchers can compare observed responses with simulations that include estimated volcanic forcing and with control or otherwise matched simulations that omit it. Real volcanic events are not randomized trials: other climate fluctuations are happening at the same time, and aerosol properties and distribution are uncertain. Nonetheless, they offer independent perturbations that help evaluate radiative processes and the ocean-atmosphere response.

To organize comparisons among many models, the climate community uses standardized experiments. The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, known as CMIP, asks participating modeling groups to run agreed experiments and to document their outputs. This does not mean every model is identical. Quite the opposite: different numerical methods, physical representations, grids, and parameter choices can respond differently to the same forcing protocol. Standardization makes those differences easier to diagnose. It also allows many researchers outside the originating modeling centers to investigate shared datasets.

There is a historical thread here. CMIP began in the nineteen-nineties and developed through successive coordinated phases. The sixth phase, described in its experimental design by Veronika Eyring and colleagues in 2016, emphasized common diagnostic experiments, historical simulations, and specialized intercomparison projects. The seventh phase, set out by John Dunne and collaborators in 2025, continued this work with a more flexible structure and new coordinated experiments. Specific operational lists and submission schedules can change, so it is more important for a listener to understand why the experiment protocol matters than to memorize a temporary list of official run labels.

Consider one of the strongest uses of a shared protocol: separating changes due to model construction from changes due to forcing. Imagine model A and model B both receive a comparable carbon dioxide step increase. If their warming differs, scientists can examine cloud feedbacks, ocean heat uptake, water vapor, sea ice, and other processes. If model A alone is supplied with different forcing data, the source of the difference is harder to determine. Shared experiments are a way of controlling the question, not of guaranteeing that every answer is equally accurate.

A related tool is the initial-condition ensemble. Take one model with identical physics and forcing, but begin each run from a slightly different plausible climate state. After some time, its simulated storms and ocean fluctuations evolve differently. The members form a range of internal variations around the response to the same external pathway. This range helps us distinguish the model's average forced signal from the particular sequence of weather and ocean variability occurring in one realization. It does not sample uncertainty in the model's physics. For that, we need different parameterizations, model structures, or parameter perturbations, as well as independent observations.

Another useful contrast is between the question 'How much global warming occurs under a specified forcing?' and 'Will a particular river basin have a severe flood in a certain year?' Large-scale global temperature responses can be robust even when the precise locations of storms diverge among members. The second question may depend on internal variability, fine-scale topography, soil and land-use details, and hydrological modeling beyond the global climate simulation. A projection with credible global warming does not by itself yield a precise local flood forecast. Good practice preserves the chain of inference from global forcing to regional processes to local impacts.

Of course, an experiment run on a computer can be wrong. It can use an inaccurate cloud scheme, mishandle ocean mixing, miss a natural feedback, or be initialized inconsistently. The purpose of an idealized experiment is partly to expose those weaknesses. If one model warms much more than another under the same carbon dioxide perturbation, that disagreement guides investigation of sensitivity and feedbacks. When researchers bring in observed radiation, ocean heat uptake, paleoclimate evidence, and theoretical constraints, they can test which interpretations remain plausible. The models are not self-justifying witnesses merely because their simulated worlds look convincing.

Return to the two Earths we wished we had. We cannot conduct a randomized planet-scale intervention. But a model lets us change one specified cause while holding a defined set of conditions constant, or lets us run many versions of internal variability under the same cause. The validity of the result depends on the equations, the approximations, the forcing, and the independent checks. When these are explicit, a counterfactual simulation is a scientific experiment: an instrument for thinking about what would follow if the world were pushed in a particular way. Its strength is the clarity of the question. Its limitation is the fidelity of the modeled world.

## Scientific companion: the experimental matrix

### Common experiment meanings

| Experiment | Controlled input | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long preindustrial-like control | Approximately fixed external forcing | Background variability, equilibration and model drift |
| Abrupt CO₂ multiplication | Idealized step change in concentration | Feedbacks, adjustment timescales and effective sensitivity |
| Compounded 1% annual CO₂ | Standardized gradual concentration change | Transient climate response near doubling |
| Historical simulation | Estimated real-world time-varying forcings | Plausibility of hindcast climate response; not a blind forecast by default |
| Future concentration-driven scenario | Prescribed greenhouse-gas concentration/other boundary conditions | Conditional physical climate response |
| Future emissions-driven scenario | Prescribed emissions, interactive carbon-cycle components | Conditional concentrations and resulting physical response |

### Idealized balance and transient response

A global energy-balance approximation $C\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dt = F(t)-\lambda T$ illustrates transient adjustment. At constant forcing and in the simplified equilibrium state, $\Delta T_{eq}=F/\lambda$ if $\lambda>0$. A multi-layer system adds an ocean uptake term; feedbacks can be nonlinear and time-dependent. This toy relation is not a replacement for spatially resolved experiments.
