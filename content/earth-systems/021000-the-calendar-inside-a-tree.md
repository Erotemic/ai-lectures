# The Calendar Inside a Tree: Rings, Weather, and Missing Years

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A tree cannot remember a thunderstorm. It has no notebook in which to write down a hot summer, a dry spring, or the year insects consumed its leaves. But it does react to its circumstances by growing, or by failing to grow as much as it otherwise would. In many seasonal climates it lays down wood in a recurring annual pattern. When researchers compare those patterns across trees, they can construct a calendar of growth. The surprising part is that this is more than counting rings. An old stump with a thousand rings may still tell a less reliable story than a careful set of overlapping samples from many trees.

Why is crossdating necessary? Imagine cutting a round from a pine tree. Near its center are narrow rings, and near its outside wide ones. Between them is a sequence that appears to contain a particularly narrow ring, then a few wider rings, then two narrow rings together. A second pine from the same hillside shows a similar sequence, but not at the same distance from its center. Each tree began growing in a different year. If the pattern came from shared weather, however, the corresponding narrow and wide years should line up when the samples are placed on a common calendar. The recognizable pattern is more informative than the location of the rings within a particular trunk.

Researchers call the method crossdating. They inspect wood under magnification, compare ring-width patterns, and test the matches statistically. A tree may omit a visible growth ring in part of its circumference during a severe year. Another may form a false band after an interruption in growth within one season. A poor match can signal a missed, false, or locally absent ring. An isolated sample is therefore not a calendar merely because its bands look annual. Crossdating becomes particularly important when scientists want to give an exact calendar year to a piece of ancient timber whose outermost living ring has not survived.

In North America, dendrochronologists developed overlapping chronologies from living and preserved wood. A long-lived living tree can establish recent dates. A dead log with a distinctive pattern may overlap the older part of that living record, extending the chronology backward. Archaeological timbers and buried wood can add further segments if their overlaps are strong enough. The construction is not a neat tower of ever-older trees. It is more like matching fragments of a melody recorded by different musicians, with distinctive sequences providing the alignments. Ambiguous overlaps must be rejected or marked uncertain rather than forced to fit the desired historical date.

The calendar is not yet a climate reconstruction. Ring width measures growth, which depends on temperature, moisture, sunlight, soil nutrients, competition, insects, disturbance, and the physiology of the tree. Warmth might increase growth for a tree near a cold upper treeline. The same warmth might reduce growth at a drought-prone lower-elevation site by increasing evaporative demand. A ring of equal width can have different causes in these settings. The important question is not, 'How wide was the ring?' It is, 'Which environmental variable constrained this tree's growth, in this place, during this part of the season?'

Suppose a research group wants to reconstruct summer drought. They could sample many trees in a semi-arid landscape, crossdate the cores, and compare recent growth with independently measured precipitation, soil moisture, and evaporative demand. If a stable relationship emerges, it can be used to estimate earlier conditions when the instruments did not exist. But the modern relationship is an empirical model, not a law of nature. It should be tested against years excluded from fitting, and ideally against independent records such as streamflow or historical lake levels. An impressive fit within the years used to build the model is weaker evidence than performance on years it has not seen.

Tree-ring series have several kinds of biological trends. A young tree adding wood to a small trunk may have wider rings than the same tree late in life, even if the amount of wood produced in a season is similar. Canopy competition can change when a neighbor falls. A fire can damage a tree, leaving scars and years of altered growth. Researchers often remove age-related growth trends when estimating climate, a process called standardization or detrending. But the choice is consequential. Remove too little and changing tree age can masquerade as climate; remove too much and genuine century-long climate changes can disappear. Different reconstruction goals demand different treatments of slow variability.

Width is not the only tree-ring measurement. In some cold regions, the density of wood formed late in a growing season can provide a temperature-sensitive signal. Cell structure, stable carbon or oxygen isotopes, and the balance between earlywood and latewood can offer other clues. Their interpretations still depend on biology and site conditions. Even a strong temperature response may correspond mostly to warm-season conditions, not a complete annual average. A network of northern trees does not automatically reconstruct tropical ocean temperatures. When a graph says 'past temperature,' we must ask which season, region, and statistic it actually represents.

A much longer chronology can be built from ring patterns in wood that remains well preserved for centuries or millennia. One benefit is chronological: in favorable settings, researchers can attach calendar dates to growth rings and then measure the radiocarbon in those dated years. Radiocarbon is produced naturally in the atmosphere, enters living plants, and decays after carbon exchange with the environment ends. Its abundance in an ancient object is related to its age, but atmospheric radiocarbon production and exchange have changed through time. Without calibration, a radiocarbon age is not necessarily an exact calendar age.

Tree rings help solve that problem. If a ring formed in a known calendar year, its remaining radiocarbon provides a calibration point. A long set of such points documents fluctuations in atmospheric radiocarbon that would otherwise distort age estimates. The international IntCal20 calibration curves, published in 2020, integrate precisely dated tree-ring measurements with other independently dated archives farther back in time. The point is not that every radiocarbon-dated object comes from a tree. The point is that a securely dated tree can help calibrate the radioactive clock used to study pottery-associated charcoal, buried plant fragments, and many other organic remains.

Consider a mistake that could change the story of an ancient drought. If a sample is given a radiocarbon age of, say, three thousand radiocarbon years before present, treating that as exactly three thousand calendar years would neglect atmospheric variations in radiocarbon. Calibration may give a different central calendar age and, in some intervals, multiple plausible ranges. Those ranges can be narrower or wider depending on the shape of the calibration curve, measurement precision, and additional chronological information. A transparent report gives the calibrated range and the model assumptions instead of converting one laboratory number into a falsely exact historical birthday.

Tree rings can also preserve evidence of extremes that a seasonal average would hide. A severe fire may leave a scar in living wood. Certain frost events can damage developing tissues. A sudden failure of summer moisture may produce a particularly narrow ring in sites where growth responds strongly to dryness. But the existence of a fire scar does not by itself explain the weather that produced the fire. Fuel, ignition, land use, and suppression matter. A record of reduced growth during a known drought may support the reconstruction of older droughts, yet its inference still depends on tree physiology and the stability of the climate-growth relationship.

Scale introduces another trap. A thousand trees sampled from one mountain valley provide detailed knowledge of that valley and of shared regional climate signals. They are not the equivalent of a thousand independent measurements of global mean temperature. Trees near one another share soils, storms, and species-specific responses. Researchers constructing continental drought atlases or hemispheric temperature histories account for the geography of the networks and differences among proxies. Large sample counts are helpful, but geographic coverage and representativeness matter as much as how many cores were drilled.

There is also a survival bias. Trees that are alive and available to sample may have survived conditions that killed their neighbors. A chronology assembled from exceptional long-lived trees can overrepresent particular environments. Old timber collected from buildings may reflect choices people made about which species and which tree sizes were worth cutting. Fossil wood may be preserved preferentially in certain sediments. The archive is shaped by ecology and by the history of collecting, not just by the climate itself. That does not make it useless. It tells us to describe the sample before generalizing from it.

A particularly persuasive result comes when different kinds of evidence agree. If independent tree-ring networks show a broad dry interval, lake sediments indicate low water levels, and historical documents from the same region report crop failure, the combined interpretation may be much stronger. But agreement must be inspected, not merely counted. If all three records were dated by copying the same tree chronology, their dates are not independent evidence. If written records survive mainly from drought-stricken settlements, their spatial sampling is different from that of trees. The purpose of comparison is to discover where the evidence overlaps and where it does not.

The strength of the tree-ring record is therefore twofold. First, at suitably seasonal sites with successful crossdating, its calendar can be exact to the year, allowing comparisons that would be impossible in a sediment core with centuries of dating uncertainty. Second, its growth measurements can reconstruct aspects of climate if the local climate–growth relationship is understood and tested. Its weakness is that a tree is a living organism responding to several constraints at once. It does not grow a meter of 'global climate' into its trunk each year.

If you encounter a tree-ring reconstruction, read the caption as carefully as the plotted line. Which trees were sampled? What year does each ring represent? Which variable limits growth in that environment? How was the modern calibration established? What happened to long-term growth trends? What is the season and region of the reconstructed climate? These questions transform the rings from a charming metaphor for memory into a quantitative historical method. The tree does not know the calendar. Investigators construct the calendar by matching patterns; then they learn what the tree's growth can, and cannot, tell them about the weather it endured.

## Scientific companion: exact dating and climate calibration

### Crossdating and proxy inference are different operations

1. Identify ring boundaries and crossdate multiple trees. Missing and false rings are possible; matching needs independent replication and statistical checking.
2. Measure raw ring width, wood density, isotopic composition or other properties, preserving site and species information.
3. Remove non-climatic trends only as justified by the reconstruction target. Choices about standardization can alter low-frequency variability.
4. Calibrate a chosen climate variable against instrumental observations and evaluate performance with withheld data or independent records.
5. State spatial and seasonal representativeness; sample size is not the same as effective independent climate coverage.

### Chronology and radiocarbon

- Radiocarbon ages require calibration because atmospheric radiocarbon has varied. IntCal20 combines dendro-dated rings and older independent archives, with separate issues for marine and Southern Hemisphere contexts.
- 'Before present' in conventional radiocarbon practice refers to 1950 CE; calendar years and radiocarbon years should not be silently interchanged.
- A single calibrated radiocarbon determination may yield multiple plausible age intervals; chronological priors can affect the modeled result.
