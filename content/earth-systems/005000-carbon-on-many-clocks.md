# Carbon on Many Clocks: The Exchange That Never Stops

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On a mountain in Hawaii, instruments draw a line through time. The line climbs, falls, and climbs again. Every northern spring the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air is often near a seasonal high. During the growing season, plants take up enormous amounts of carbon, and the line descends. When growth slows and respiration and decay continue, it rises. Yet each year's descent ends higher than the comparable point decades earlier. There are two stories in the same curve. One is the recurring transfer of carbon between air and life. The other is the accumulation of carbon that was not part of that seasonal exchange before people extracted and burned fossil fuels.

Charles David Keeling began his sustained measurements at Mauna Loa in 1958. The resulting record is powerful partly because it is so plain: an instrument measures the concentration of a gas at a particular place over time. But the number is not a direct measurement of photosynthesis, a total global emissions inventory, or the rate at which carbon enters the ocean. To explain the curve we need to distinguish three things: the size of a carbon reservoir, the rate of exchange with its neighbors, and the net difference between flows entering and leaving it. Confusing these is perhaps the most common error in reasoning about the carbon cycle.

Picture a reservoir fed by a large river and drained by another equally large river. Both carry an extraordinary volume of water, but the reservoir's level stays constant if the flows balance. A small additional inflow can raise its level year after year. Carbon reservoirs work similarly. The atmosphere, leaves and wood, soils, surface ocean, deep ocean, and sedimentary rock contain different quantities of carbon. Their exchanges range from hours to millions of years. A very large gross movement in one direction does not imply a net accumulation in that direction. What matters for the changing atmospheric inventory is the sum of all sources minus the sum of all sinks.

Start with a green leaf. It takes carbon dioxide from the air and incorporates carbon into organic compounds using energy from sunlight. This is not merely gas storage: photosynthesis builds chemical structures, which become sugars, cellulose, oils, and countless molecules of living tissue. The carbon may pass from leaf to insect to bird, or from fallen leaf to fungus to soil microorganism. Respiration returns much of it to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide. A tree can stand for centuries, but many of its individual leaves are replaced within a year. Carbon in a rapidly turning-over root may spend weeks or months in the plant; some carbon associated with soil minerals can persist much longer. Calling an entire forest a carbon sink is a statement about its changing net stock, not a claim that the forest only absorbs carbon.

We can make this practical. Suppose a forest fixes one hundred arbitrary units of carbon through photosynthesis in a season. Plants themselves respire some of the fixed carbon. Microbes release more from litter and soil. Animals consume and respire a share. If total returns are ninety-nine units, the ecosystem has gained one unit over that interval; if returns are one hundred and two, it has lost two. Whether the stored carbon remains in living biomass, roots, soils, or exported material matters for how long that gain persists. A newly growing forest, an old forest, and a drained peatland can have very different balances even under similar sunshine.

How could we actually measure this? Scientists operate towers with rapid sensors for vertical wind and gas concentration. Turbulent swirls carry carbon dioxide both upward and downward, and the covariance between vertical motion and concentration gives an estimate of the turbulent exchange. Networks such as FLUXNET combine these measurements with meteorology, soil conditions, and methods for handling missing intervals. The tower measures a net flux over its surrounding footprint. Partitioning that net number into photosynthetic uptake and respiration requires additional assumptions and modeling. No single tower represents all forests, and an afternoon's uptake tells us little by itself about the annual carbon balance.

Now move to the ocean. Carbon dioxide crosses the air-sea interface when the dissolved gas and the air are out of equilibrium, with wind and turbulence affecting the rate. The surface sea can absorb carbon in one region and release it in another. Cold water can generally dissolve more carbon dioxide than warm water, but a water parcel's carbon chemistry, biological activity, and circulation history also matter. In the ocean, much of the carbon is not present as freely dissolved carbon dioxide. It is converted among dissolved carbon dioxide, bicarbonate, and carbonate ions. These chemical forms allow the ocean to store much more inorganic carbon than a simple bottle of carbonated water would suggest.

The chemistry has consequences. When the ocean absorbs additional carbon dioxide, reactions tend to increase the concentration of hydrogen ions and reduce the availability of carbonate ions. The average ocean remains alkaline, but its pH decreases: this is ocean acidification. Some organisms build shells or skeletons from calcium carbonate. Changes in the carbonate balance can make the building or maintenance of those structures harder, although responses depend on species, life stage, local water chemistry, and other stresses. The atmosphere is thereby partly buffered by an ocean sink, but the buffering is not cost-free and it is not unlimited.

A surface water sample is only the beginning. Marine organisms fix carbon near the illuminated surface. Some of their organic matter sinks and is decomposed deeper down, where the resulting dissolved carbon can remain isolated from the atmosphere for considerable periods. This is one part of the biological carbon pump. There is also a solubility-driven pathway: dense waters formed in certain high-latitude regions carry dissolved inorganic carbon into the ocean interior. Mixing and upwelling eventually bring some of it back. None of these is a one-way escalator. The circulation of carbon is linked to the ocean's changing circulation of heat and water.

We should be careful with a phrase such as 'the ocean takes up thirty percent.' The percentage refers to a specified accounting interval and specified human emissions, not to every possible year or every carbon flux in the sea. In the IPCC assessment of the decade from 2010 to 2019, around forty-six percent of human carbon dioxide emissions accumulated in the atmosphere, roughly twenty-three percent entered the ocean as a net sink, and about thirty-one percent accumulated in land reservoirs as a net sink. These are estimated averages with uncertainties, not fixed natural constants. A year of severe drought, fire, or unusual ocean circulation may change the partition. The accounting still follows the same conservation principle.

Why does the atmosphere accumulate carbon at all when vegetation and seawater both remove it? Because combustion transfers carbon from a geological store into the relatively fast surface system. Coal, oil, and gas formed from ancient organic matter that had been separated from ordinary respiration for geological spans of time. Burning them joins carbon to oxygen and releases carbon dioxide. Cement manufacture also emits carbon dioxide when carbonate minerals are processed. Land clearance and soil disturbance change the inventory of carbon in the living landscape. The natural processes of respiration, decomposition, and ocean exchange remain immense, but prior to the industrial perturbation their global net balance was much closer to zero. Adding a persistent input to a system with large nearly balancing flows changes its stock.

The evidence that the extra carbon is human-derived is stronger than a coincidence between two rising curves. Inventories of fuel extraction and combustion establish a source. Carbon isotopes carry a chemical signature because fossil organic carbon has a different isotopic composition from atmospheric carbon, and radiocarbon in very old fossil carbon has decayed. Atmospheric oxygen observations also fit combustion. The geographical pattern of concentrations, ocean uptake, and direct atmospheric measurements provide independent constraints. The carbon budget is assembled from evidence with different errors rather than from an instrument that sees every exchange at once.

The carbon clock extends much farther back than forests and oceans. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide and can react with minerals in exposed rock. Silicate weathering moves dissolved material through soils and rivers toward the sea. Over geological time, some of the products are incorporated into carbonate sediments that can become rock. Tectonic processes bury, alter, and eventually return carbon through volcanism and metamorphism. Weathering of silicate rocks followed by appropriate carbonate formation can consume carbon dioxide on a long-term net basis. But not every kind of rock dissolution has the same carbon balance. Dissolving carbonate rock can redistribute carbon without producing the same lasting removal, and oxidation of carbon-rich rock can release carbon dioxide. A river's dissolved load must be chemically identified before it is called a carbon sink.

In 1981 James Walker, Paul Hays, and James Kasting proposed a particularly influential climatic connection. A warmer, wetter world can, under suitable conditions, accelerate silicate weathering. If that reaction draws carbon dioxide down, the greenhouse effect weakens and tends to counter the initial warming. Conversely, colder conditions can slow weathering and allow volcanic carbon dioxide to build up. This is a negative feedback in the technical sense: it resists a perturbation. It is not a thermostat with a knob and a set temperature. Fresh mineral supply, soils, biological processes, runoff, continental arrangement, and tectonic activity all influence the response. Most importantly, this feedback operates over much longer timescales than the accumulation of carbon from a century or two of industrial activity.

The landscape can change the feedback's speed and even the sign of a local carbon budget. Uplift exposes fresh rock. Erosion supplies reactive minerals, but it can also uncover ancient organic matter that oxidizes. Rivers export both dissolved inorganic carbon and particulate organic carbon, some of which will be buried and some decomposed. Lena Märki and colleagues studied a Himalayan catchment after the 2015 Gorkha earthquake; their work illustrates why measuring a mountain's carbon budget is not equivalent to counting landslides: different carbon pathways respond differently, and a particular perturbation need not create a large change in the basin's integrated flux. A photograph of bare rock and a muddy river cannot by itself tell us whether the mountain system is taking carbon from the air.

Carbon locked in rock may remain separated from the atmosphere for millions of years. Carbon in a leaf can return within days. Carbon taken up by the surface ocean may equilibrate with air rather quickly, while carbon transported into the deep ocean can be isolated much longer. Carbon released by the thaw of previously frozen soils has its own complications: microbes can convert some material to carbon dioxide in oxygen-rich conditions or methane in some oxygen-poor environments, and the rate depends on temperature, moisture, substrate, and microbial communities. The clock of a reservoir and the clock of an individual carbon atom are not identical. An atom may exchange several times before a net perturbation is removed.

This leads to a subtle point. There is no single short half-life for an added pulse of carbon dioxide that works the way a radioactive decay half-life does. Some excess carbon is absorbed relatively soon by land and surface ocean; a portion persists while deep-ocean equilibration and carbonate compensation unfold; a smaller residual can require rock-weathering processes over very long intervals. Saying that carbon dioxide 'remains for a hundred years' as though every emitted molecule expires on schedule misses the multiscale physics. The relevant question is how the concentration caused by a pulse diminishes, not how long any particular molecule remains airborne before exchanging with a leaf or seawater.

To see these clocks, scientists combine observations. Mauna Loa records a precise local atmospheric history against the background of global measurements. Flux towers observe turbulent ecosystem exchange. Oceanographers sample dissolved inorganic carbon, alkalinity, oxygen, and other tracers at different depths. Satellites such as OCO-2 measure the absorption of sunlight by carbon dioxide along atmospheric columns. A satellite column is not a direct flux measurement: inferring the sources and sinks that produced the pattern requires atmospheric transport calculations. Ice cores contain old air and extend the concentration history beyond the instrumental era. Geochemical records and models address still longer spans. Each instrument has a domain, and agreement among partly independent domains gives the carbon-cycle picture its strength.

Go back to the Hawaiian mountain. The seasonal wave is not a contradiction to the accumulating trend; it is one of the mechanisms visible beneath it. Living landscapes and the surface ocean exchange carbon rapidly, yet their net uptake has not matched the additional geological carbon placed into the active system. Deep ocean and rock offer further pathways, but on clocks that run more slowly. The carbon cycle is not a single circle with an arrow returning to the start. It is an interconnected network of stocks, exchanges, transformations, and delays. To understand what the atmosphere will contain tomorrow, a decade from now, or thousands of years from now, we must ask not only where carbon goes, but how long it stays and what becomes of it on the way.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Stocks, gross fluxes, and the anthropogenic perturbation

For the atmospheric carbon inventory \(C_a\), use a defined sign convention:

\[
\frac{dC_a}{dt} = E_{\mathrm{fossil}} + E_{\mathrm{land-use}} - S_{\mathrm{ocean}} - S_{\mathrm{land}} + \epsilon.
\]

Here the anthropogenic source terms and net land/ocean sinks are defined consistently; \(\epsilon\) is a residual reflecting imperfect independent estimates. It is **not** a claim that natural respiration is absent from the equations: photosynthesis and respiration are already included in the *net* land term. The IPCC 2010-2019 partition (46% atmosphere, 23% ocean, 31% land) applies to its stated emissions and averaging period, not a timeless constant.

### Ocean chemistry and long-term weathering

At the schematic level:

\[
\mathrm{CO_2(aq) + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-},\qquad
\mathrm{HCO_3^- \rightleftharpoons H^+ + CO_3^{2-}}.
\]

Equilibrium constants vary with temperature, salinity, and pressure. 'Acidification' denotes a decrease in pH, not that seawater is usually below neutral pH. A simplified silicate-weathering plus marine carbonate-burial end member is

\[
\mathrm{CaSiO_3 + CO_2 \longrightarrow CaCO_3 + SiO_2},
\]

which omits intermediate aqueous chemistry and requires appropriate carbon and charge accounting. **Do not** assume carbonate dissolution, sulfuric-acid weathering, oxidative weathering, and silicate weathering have identical net carbon outcomes.

### Questions worth thinking through

1. How can the gross seasonal CO2 exchange with plants be much larger than net annual atmospheric accumulation without contradicting that accumulation?
2. What does a flux tower measure directly, and what requires extra partitioning assumptions?
3. Why does additional CO2 decrease seawater pH while the ocean remains alkaline?
4. Why is a fixed 'lifetime of a CO2 molecule' the wrong description of the atmospheric response to an emissions pulse?
5. Which evidence separates fossil-carbon addition from a hypothetical change in the natural seasonal cycle?
