# The Ocean Is Not a Bathtub: Circulation, Heat, and the Deep Sea

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Off the coast of California, a cold ribbon of ocean water can rise toward the sunlit surface. It may bring nutrients that support a burst of plankton production, which then feeds animals up the food web. The ocean has not become colder everywhere. Instead, wind, rotation, coastline geometry, and the structure of water beneath the surface have changed which water reaches the light. A local ecological consequence begins with a physical redistribution of water.

The ocean is sometimes imagined as an enormous bathtub that warms when the air warms. That image misses nearly everything interesting about how it responds. The ocean has moving surface layers, dense deep waters, wind-driven currents, tides, mixing, eddies, and boundaries that make water circulate on many different timescales. It absorbs and releases heat, carries moisture by evaporation, and exchanges gases with the atmosphere. It is also the planet's dominant reservoir for accumulating excess heat. Understanding the ocean requires tracking both motion and storage, neither of which is visible from a single surface temperature measurement.

Begin with density. Water's density depends on temperature, dissolved salts, and pressure. In much of the ocean, colder or saltier water is denser than warmer or fresher water at similar pressure. This tends to create a stable arrangement with less dense water over denser water. In actual seawater the relationship is nonlinear, and high pressure matters at depth, so simple fresh-water intuition is not enough for precise calculations. Yet the essential picture is clear: stratification resists vertical mixing. It takes energy or changes in buoyancy to move water across a strong density boundary.

A surface layer warmed by the sun can sit over colder, denser water. Wind can stir that layer, but it does not immediately mix the entire ocean from top to bottom. The temperature may change sharply through a zone called the thermocline. Salinity can change sharply through a halocline. These gradients may reinforce or oppose one another in their effect on density. The ocean is therefore a stack of connected but unequally mixed layers, not a giant vessel with one temperature.

Wind is a second driver. It transfers momentum to the sea surface through stress. Because the Earth rotates, the movement of ocean water is deflected relative to the surface wind, and friction communicates the motion downward. In a simplified steady Ekman layer, the integrated upper-ocean transport is to the right of the wind in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. The exact angle and vertical structure of real flow vary with stratification, turbulence, local acceleration, and coastlines. The principle still predicts a powerful effect: a wind that blows along a coast can move surface water away from the land. Water from below rises to replace it. That is coastal upwelling.

Along parts of the California Current, equatorward winds favor offshore transport of surface water in the Northern Hemisphere. Colder, deeper water rises near the coast, often bringing nutrients that had accumulated beneath the sunlit layer. Phytoplankton can grow when light and nutrients are both available. This does not mean every day of wind immediately produces a plankton bloom. Winds vary, the source depth matters, and sunlight, grazing, and other limits can intervene. The important connection is that circulation shapes biological opportunity. An ecosystem cannot be understood solely by listing its organisms without asking how nutrients arrive.

Elsewhere, winds and the rotation of Earth help organize large surface-current gyres. Picture the North Atlantic: the broad ocean basin, the prevailing wind belts, and the restoring influence of planetary rotation together support a circulation with a swift western boundary current, the Gulf Stream. It is more useful to think in terms of momentum balances and a rotating basin than to imagine a river of warm water poured through the sea. Western boundary intensification follows from the way rotation varies with latitude and from the need for broad ocean circulation to close its transport balance. The rapid current on one side is paired with much broader, slower return flows and recirculations.

When the ocean carries warm water northward, it can release heat to the atmosphere at higher latitudes. The atmosphere can then transport some of that heat farther. We therefore should not make a climate statement such as 'the Gulf Stream alone heats Europe' without accounting for atmospheric circulation, prevailing winds, the ocean's wider circulation, and geography. Ocean heat transport matters greatly, but regional climate is a coupled atmosphere-ocean result, not the direct effect of one identifiable current.

The deep ocean introduces a different set of pathways. Surface water can become denser when it cools or when freshwater is removed by sea ice formation and the remaining seawater becomes saltier. Under suitable conditions, dense water may sink and spread at depth. In the North Atlantic, deep-water formation is associated with cooling and water-mass transformation in high northern latitudes. Around Antarctica, very dense bottom water can form on continental shelves and flow into deep basins. These are not the only origins of deep circulation, nor are wind and density neatly separable: winds, mixing, topography, and buoyancy exchange all contribute to the three-dimensional overturning of the ocean.

You may have seen a diagram labeled the global ocean conveyor belt. It shows a single continuous pathway connecting surface and deep currents around the world. That picture helped convey the idea that distant seas communicate through overturning circulation. But the real ocean is not a single closed loop that every parcel follows on a predetermined schedule. It has branches, eddies, recirculation, and mixing; surface and deep routes differ among ocean basins and change with time. The phrase overturning circulation is more honest than a literal moving belt. Scientists quantify it as water transport through defined latitude-depth or density sections rather than by following one colored line forever.

The Southern Ocean is especially important because it connects the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific basins around Antarctica. Strong westerly winds, a great circumpolar current, density fronts, and steeply tilted layers allow exchange between deep waters and the surface. In some regions, deep water returns toward the surface and can exchange heat and carbon dioxide with the atmosphere; elsewhere, surface waters are converted into intermediate or bottom waters. The distribution of these transformations affects oxygen, nutrients, and carbon storage through the ocean interior. The details depend on mixing, sea ice, winds, and the changing freshwater budget, so it is not a single vertical elevator operating everywhere at once.

What does all this mean for heat? Water has a high specific heat capacity: changing its temperature requires substantial energy for a given mass. The ocean's huge mass makes it a formidable thermal reservoir. Yet how much heat reaches the deeper ocean depends on circulation and mixing. Surface warming may be communicated downward slowly, and the rate differs by region. A single map of sea-surface temperature can show warm patches, cold upwelling zones, and seasonal patterns without revealing the total heat stored through two kilometers of water. The distinction between temperature and heat content is essential. A small temperature increase spread through a vast mass can represent more energy than a large change in a very thin surface layer.

The ocean also moderates the atmosphere. During a summer afternoon over land, the air can warm rapidly because soil and rock have a limited actively mixed thermal layer. A nearby sea may change temperature more slowly because heat is distributed through water and evaporative cooling is effective. Over seasons, that thermal memory can affect coastal temperatures, fog, storm tracks, and air-sea heat exchange. On larger scales, changes in tropical sea-surface temperatures, such as those involved in El Niño and La Niña, reorganize atmospheric convection and circulation. An ocean anomaly can therefore influence precipitation far beyond the water that first warmed or cooled. Such connections are studied probabilistically; they do not provide a deterministic weather forecast for a particular town.

The exchange between air and sea is not only thermal. Evaporation takes freshwater from the ocean surface while leaving most dissolved salts behind, tending to increase surface salinity. Rain and river inflow add relatively fresh water and tend to reduce salinity. Ice formation and melting can alter both salinity and buoyancy. These freshwater changes influence stratification, mixing, and sometimes the formation of deeper water masses. Biological processes and air-sea exchange also move carbon dioxide and oxygen. The ocean's physical circulation transports these chemicals to different depths, where their return to the atmosphere may be delayed by decades, centuries, or longer depending on the pathway. That is why ocean circulation matters for both marine life and the global carbon cycle.

How do scientists see below the surface without mapping every cubic meter? Traditionally, ships lowered instruments that measured conductivity, temperature, and pressure. Conductivity helps determine salinity; pressure helps determine depth. Repeated sections revealed structure but sampled only narrow tracks at particular times. Satellites observe sea-surface height, surface temperature, ocean color, winds, and related properties. They can map great areas quickly, but ordinary satellite infrared measurements do not reveal deep-ocean temperature directly. They tell us about the surface and help constrain the flow and its consequences, especially when combined with in-water observations.

The international Argo program transformed subsurface coverage. Thousands of autonomous floats drift in the ocean and repeatedly descend and rise, recording temperature and salinity profiles. A standard core Argo float typically profiles the upper two thousand meters on an approximately ten-day cycle. Those records are widely shared and have enabled much more systematic estimates of upper-ocean change. Extensions of Argo seek to measure deeper waters and biogeochemical properties. The instruments are remarkable, but not omniscient: ordinary floats do not densely resolve every coastal current or small eddy, and coverage of the deep ocean and seasonally ice-covered regions has been incomplete. Results about ocean heat content are estimates built from observations plus methods for handling those gaps.

The evidence from sustained ocean observations shows that the ocean is gaining heat overall as Earth experiences a positive energy imbalance. It is not heating by the same amount at every depth and place. Currents redistribute heat, and waves and weather add short-term variability. A transient cool patch of surface water does not contradict a global increase in integrated ocean heat content. This is another place where conservation accounting and dynamics answer different questions: one establishes an energy gain, the other explains where the energy travels.

There are consequences at the shore. Warming seawater expands, contributing to sea-level rise; melting land ice adds water and is a separate contribution. Local relative sea level also depends on land movement, ocean circulation, and gravitational and other effects. Marine heat waves can affect species even if average deep-ocean temperature has changed only a little. Upwelling can bring nutrients to the coast while also delivering lower-oxygen or more acidic waters under some conditions. No single sign, 'warming' or 'cooling', is adequate for every ecosystem or coastline. We need profiles, chemistry, and an understanding of changing circulation.

Return to that cold upwelling ribbon off California. Wind has moved surface water away from the coastline, but what replaces it is not just cold; it carries the chemical history of a different depth and pathway. Nutrients help govern new biological production. The water exchanges heat and gases with the air. It may join a current and move onward. Far away, other water is sinking, mixing, or storing an increment of the planet's excess energy. A bathtub can be described with one faucet, one drain, and one thermometer. The ocean requires a map of connected motions and reservoirs. That map is never finished, but each observation lets us ask more precisely where Earth's heat, freshwater, and dissolved materials have gone.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Four circulation mechanisms and their coupling

1. **Wind stress:** inputs momentum to near-surface waters; friction, rotation, and pressure gradients organize boundary layers, gyres, and coastal upwelling.
2. **Buoyancy changes:** cooling, heating, evaporation, precipitation, and sea ice modify density and can transform water masses.
3. **Mixing and topography:** allow cross-density-layer exchange, constrain pathways, and are indispensable to overturning budgets.
4. **Tides:** produce oscillatory currents and contribute to mixing; particularly important in shallow regions and some deep-ocean mixing locations.

Density is a function \(\rho(T,S,p)\) of temperature, salinity, and pressure; detailed oceanography uses seawater thermodynamics rather than a linear fresh-water approximation. Ekman transport direction is an idealized steady result; instantaneous currents need not be exactly perpendicular to the wind.

### Heat storage and observational limits

Ocean heat content relative to a specified reference can be expressed as

\[
H = \int_V \rho\,c_p\,(T-T_0)\,\mathrm{d}V.
\]

The reference does not affect *changes* in heat content when the control volume and conventions are consistent. Surface temperature alone cannot determine the integral. Core Argo usually samples down to ~2,000 m; Deep Argo and ship measurements cover important deeper and coastal gaps. Satellite sea-surface height is also sensitive to thermal expansion and redistribution, but disentangling contributions requires other data.

### Avoid these shortcuts

- “Thermohaline” circulation is not independent of winds, mixing and topography; there is no single parcel pathway around a universal conveyor.
- A local cool sea-surface patch is not a measurement of globally integrated ocean heat content.
- North Atlantic sinking and Antarctic bottom-water formation are different processes with different geography and seasonal controls.
- Coastal upwelling can supply nutrients while simultaneously altering oxygen and acidity; consequences depend on water chemistry and biology.
- Global mean sea-level rise has multiple contributions, including ocean thermal expansion and land-ice mass loss.

### Questions worth testing

1. Why does the presence of a cold coastal current not imply that the ocean as a whole is losing heat?
2. How can winds produce upward motion of water even though wind stress acts mainly horizontally?
3. Why is a loop diagram of the global conveyor inadequate as a literal travel itinerary for one parcel?
4. What can an Argo profile reveal that a satellite sea-surface temperature map cannot?
5. How can the same upwelling event benefit plankton while changing oxygen or acidity conditions for other organisms?
