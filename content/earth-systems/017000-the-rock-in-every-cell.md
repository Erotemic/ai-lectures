# The Rock in Every Cell: Phosphorus and the Long Way Back

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A grain of phosphate-bearing rock can be older than the landscape that surrounds it. Water and roots slowly change the mineral, and eventually some of its phosphorus enters a river, a plant, a fish, or a person. Inside a cell, the element becomes part of genetic material, membranes, and the chemical machinery that transfers energy. Later, that phosphorus may return to soil or sediment. If it reaches the sea and becomes buried, its route back to dry land may require geological time. Phosphorus links the fastest activities of living cells to the slowest movements of rock.

Phosphorus is not simply a minor ingredient in fertilizer. DNA and RNA contain phosphate groups in their backbones. Cell membranes use phosphorus-bearing lipids. Adenosine triphosphate, often called ATP, contains phosphate groups and participates in energy transfer. A growing organism cannot build all these structures without phosphorus atoms. Yet living things cannot manufacture a phosphorus atom. It has to arrive from a source in the environment or from another organism. This is the same material constraint we saw with nitrogen, but the planetary routes are quite different.

For nitrogen, the atmosphere offers a vast reservoir and specialized microbes can make nitrogen gas accessible. There is no comparably large, rapidly exchanged atmospheric gas reservoir of phosphorus. The principal long-term stores are in rocks, sediments, soils, and the ocean. Phosphorus moves mainly through mineral weathering, biological uptake, decomposition, water transport, and sediment burial. It may travel on suspended particles or as dissolved phosphate. Some phosphorus-bearing dust moves through the air, but there is no atmospheric return loop on the scale of the nitrogen-gas cycle.

Begin with apatite, a family of calcium phosphate minerals. Exposed rock encounters rainwater, acids from roots and soil organisms, and changing temperature. Weathering can release phosphorus-containing compounds. The release is often slow compared with the demands of an actively growing forest or field. This makes phosphorus availability depend partly on the history of a landscape. A young soil newly supplied with weatherable minerals can behave differently from an old, strongly weathered soil in which much of the readily available phosphorus has already been transformed or carried away. Temperature and rainfall matter, but so do the minerals present and the time water has been moving through them.

Now imagine measuring total phosphorus in a spoonful of soil. That number is not the same as the amount a plant can absorb this week. Phosphate ions can bind to mineral surfaces, including iron and aluminum oxides. They can be incorporated into organic molecules, precipitate with other minerals, or remain dissolved in soil water. Roots and their fungal partners can alter local chemistry and explore large soil volumes. Microbes release enzymes that help make some organic phosphorus accessible. A plant's access depends on the chemical form, soil acidity, root distribution, moisture, and competing organisms. Large inventory, slow release: this is another case where stock and usable flux must be distinguished.

An experiment in a lake makes that distinction vivid at the ecosystem scale. In northwestern Ontario, Canada, researchers at the Experimental Lakes Area studied how nutrient additions changed lake productivity. David Schindler and collaborators became central figures in whole-lake experiments that tested the consequences of adding phosphorus and nitrogen. A long-running experiment in Lake 227 maintained phosphorus additions while progressively reducing nitrogen additions. Over thirty-seven years, the lake remained highly productive and eutrophic even when phosphorus was supplied without added nitrogen. Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria became part of the response, providing an additional source of usable nitrogen. The result gave strong evidence that cutting nitrogen input alone could fail to control eutrophication in that kind of lake when phosphorus remained abundant.

That result should not be inflated into a universal rule that nitrogen never matters in aquatic ecosystems. Estuaries and coastal seas often respond strongly to nitrogen availability; some waters are co-limited or change limitation with season. What the Canadian whole-lake experiment established is that nutrient limitation is a property of a functioning ecosystem, not a simple conclusion from how many kilograms of each element are present. An organism can obtain more of one limiting nutrient through fixation, but it cannot do the same for phosphorus by pulling a large reservoir of phosphorus gas from the air. The ecosystem has different ways to relieve different constraints.

Phosphorus can support life or cause trouble in an inconvenient location. In a field, phosphate fertilizer may help a crop that would otherwise lack sufficient phosphorus. After rainfall, some phosphate can wash away dissolved in runoff, attached to eroded particles, or through other drainage paths. In a lake or reservoir, enough extra phosphorus can support dense algal or cyanobacterial growth. When the resulting organic matter decomposes, microbes consume dissolved oxygen. Some blooms also involve organisms capable of producing toxins. These are related but distinct problems. A visible bloom does not prove that toxins are present; a toxic bloom does not necessarily require the same conditions that produce deep-water oxygen depletion.

Lake Erie gives us a historical example of both improvement and persistence. During the twentieth century, wastewater and other sources carried large phosphorus loads into the lake. Growing public concern led to binational efforts by Canada and the United States. Under the Great Lakes Water Quality Agreement and associated controls, changes to wastewater treatment and detergent phosphorus helped reduce the loads, and important aspects of water quality improved. The success was real. It showed that a large lake could respond when human nutrient inputs changed.

But the story did not end there. Extensive blooms reappeared in western Lake Erie in later decades. Agricultural runoff and the form and timing of phosphorus delivery became central concerns. In August 2014, a harmful cyanobacterial bloom contributed to a drinking-water advisory in the Toledo region after microcystin was detected in treated water. A lake that had responded to one class of phosphorus controls still faced challenges from other sources and pathways. The point is not that earlier cleanup failed. The point is that watersheds have several kinds of phosphorus storage, transport, and biological response, and an intervention in one pathway may not control them all.

Why can runoff remain nutrient-rich even after a farmer reduces new phosphorus applications? One reason is legacy phosphorus. For years, fertilizer and manure may have supplied more phosphorus than crops removed. Some accumulated in soil. Additional phosphorus can be stored in stream sediments, ditches, wetlands, and lake bottoms. When new inputs decline, these stores do not vanish at once. Phosphorus can leave them gradually through erosion, desorption, decomposition, changes in water chemistry, and remobilization during storms. A field can become better managed while a river continues to carry some of the consequences of older decisions.

There is a timing problem hidden in that sentence. The phosphorus reaching a monitoring station this spring may have entered the watershed as fertilizer years earlier. Alternatively, it may have come from a recent rainstorm over newly fertilized ground. The river mixes paths of different lengths, while dissolved and particle-bound phosphorus may follow different transport routes. Scientists therefore measure more than total annual phosphorus. They measure the fraction dissolved, the fraction associated with suspended sediment, event-by-event fluxes, and concentrations at different places and depths. In Lake Erie, concern about dissolved reactive phosphorus reflects the importance of the form readily available to phytoplankton, not a claim that all other phosphorus is harmless.

Even a lake bottom can change the chemical meaning of the phosphorus it stores. In oxygen-rich sediment, phosphate may bind to iron minerals. If oxygen becomes depleted, microbes can reduce some iron oxides, changing the mineral surfaces and potentially releasing phosphate back to water. This is one mechanism of internal loading. It is important but not universal: sediments vary in iron availability, sulfur chemistry, calcium binding, water mixing, and biological activity. A sediment store can be a sink in one season and a source in another. The history of oxygen conditions matters to the future phosphorus budget.

There is also a global mining and distribution problem. Farmers often add phosphate derived from mined rock. The ore bodies are not distributed evenly across countries, and mining, processing, and transport move phosphorus from geological stores into agricultural landscapes. Harvests transport more of it into cities and food systems. Wastewater carries some away. Recycling manure, crop residues, and properly treated waste streams can return phosphorus to fields, but the economics and contamination risks of each route differ. We are not just managing a natural cycle; we have built a large one-way redistribution through mines, farms, food chains, and sewage networks.

A useful phosphorus budget might begin with ten units entering a field in fertilizer and manure, then subtract the phosphorus removed in crops, lost in runoff, accumulated in soil, or exported through erosion. For a lake, the inputs may be rivers, groundwater, and the atmosphere; losses may be burial or outflow; uptake by algae shifts phosphorus within the lake but is not necessarily a permanent loss. These distinctions sound like accounting because they are accounting. A nutrient can be essential in one compartment and a pollutant in another. Every management claim should specify the compartment, timescale, and route.

The geological timescale returns at the end of the story. Marine particles containing phosphorus can settle into sediments. Some phosphorus is regenerated and returns to the water. Some becomes buried in forms that are relatively stable. Over very long periods, uplift and erosion can expose buried rocks again. This slow external supply is coupled to the amount of life the ocean can support, and therefore to the burial of organic carbon and other planetary chemical balances. We should not picture a single phosphate molecule continuously cycling on a convenient timetable. The system contains fast biological exchanges nested inside stores that can hold material for thousands or millions of years.

Phosphorus is a lesson in bottlenecks and memory. Living cells need it constantly, but rock releases it slowly. A small dissolved concentration can constrain an ecosystem, while a modest extra supply to a sensitive lake can shift that ecosystem into a different state. Past phosphorus inputs can remain influential after present decisions improve. The next time a lake turns green or a crop struggles, it is not enough to ask whether phosphorus is present. Ask which phosphorus, how available, where it came from, how long it has been stored, and how quickly the environment can release or remove it. Those questions connect a cell's chemistry to the rock beneath an entire watershed.

## Scientific companion: phosphorus stocks, fluxes and legacy

### Chemical distinctions

- **Orthophosphate:** a common form of dissolved reactive phosphorus available to organisms; analytical measurements and operational categories do not coincide with every molecular species.
- **Total phosphorus:** includes dissolved and particulate, organic and inorganic forms. Total loading and immediately bioavailable phosphorus are different quantities.
- **Sorption and release:** phosphate binding to mineral surfaces is pH-, mineral- and redox-dependent; internal loading is not a uniform response in every lake.
- **Long geological residence:** weathering of phosphate-bearing minerals supplies phosphorus; sediment burial removes some from fast exchange, but not all sedimentation is permanent burial.
- **Nitrogen versus phosphorus limitation:** nutrient limitation varies with ecology and timescale. The whole-lake Lake 227 result supports sustained importance of phosphorus control in those conditions, not a rule that nitrogen reduction is irrelevant everywhere.

### Case study and evidence boundaries

The Experimental Lakes Area Lake 227 experiment used long-term whole-ecosystem manipulation rather than flask-scale extrapolation. The Lake Erie narrative concerns changing sources and chemical forms of phosphorus; the 2014 Toledo advisory was associated with microcystin in treated drinking water. A harmful cyanobacterial bloom, cyanotoxin occurrence and hypoxia are three variables that should not be used interchangeably.
