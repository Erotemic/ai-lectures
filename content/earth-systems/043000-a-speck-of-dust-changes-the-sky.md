# A Speck of Dust Changes the Sky: Aerosols, Clouds, and Climate

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The blue of a clear sky seems to suggest that there is nothing between us and the horizon but gas. In fact, almost every breath contains microscopic particles suspended in the air. Some are salt from breaking waves. Some are desert dust. Some are soot from burning fuels or biomass. Some were not emitted as particles at all: they formed when gases reacted and the products condensed. Many are too small for us to see individually. Together they can change how much sunlight reaches the ground, how droplets form inside clouds, and how long chemical substances remain in the atmosphere.

The name for these suspended particles is aerosols. Strictly speaking, an aerosol is a collection of particles in a gas; in ordinary atmospheric science the word often refers to the particles themselves. They are not one chemical substance. Sea salt, mineral dust, organic material, sulfates, nitrates, and black carbon differ in size, composition, shape, and optical properties. That variety is the first clue to why one statement about aerosols and climate is rarely enough.

Consider dust lifted from a dry landscape. Wind stress can dislodge small particles, which may stay suspended for hours or days and travel far from where they formed. Large particles settle faster under gravity; smaller ones can stay aloft longer if turbulence and vertical circulation support them. A storm in the Sahara can send dust over the Atlantic. Other plumes carry particles from fires or cities. Aerosols move with the weather, but they can also change the weather by influencing radiation and clouds. Their effect depends on where a plume goes, how high it rises, and what happens to the particles along the way.

Primary aerosols enter the atmosphere already in particle form. Sea spray makes droplets, many of which leave salt behind as water evaporates. Fire releases soot and complex organic particles. Wind lifts mineral fragments. Secondary aerosols form in the atmosphere after gases react. Sulfur dioxide can be oxidized into sulfuric acid and sulfate-containing particles. Nitrogen oxides and ammonia help form nitrate aerosols under suitable chemical conditions. Reactions of organic gases can yield compounds with low enough volatility to join existing particles or form new ones. This means that a reduction in gaseous emissions can alter airborne particles even when the particles were never visible at the source.

Size is as consequential as chemistry. Think of one large particle and many tiny particles made of the same total mass. The small particles collectively present a much larger surface area for a given material volume. Surface chemistry can therefore proceed differently, and light interacts with the population differently. Particle size also determines how efficiently a particle can be inhaled or removed by rain. When an atmospheric scientist reports a mass concentration of fine particulate matter, the measurement captures one aspect of the aerosol population, not its full number, shape, chemistry, or cloud-making ability.

What happens when sunlight meets an aerosol? A particle can scatter light, redirecting it from its original path, or absorb light, converting some of its energy to heat. Bright sulfate or sea-salt particles often scatter sunlight efficiently. Black carbon is an effective absorber of visible light. Whether the ground cools or warms, and how the atmosphere itself heats, depend on particle optics, the underlying surface, cloud cover, and vertical position. A reflective particle above dark ocean has one radiative effect; an absorbing layer above a bright cloud can have a rather different one.

That is the direct radiation interaction. There is a second family of effects involving clouds, and these require understanding the birth of a droplet. Air can cool until its water vapor reaches saturation, but vapor does not ordinarily make a large population of stable liquid droplets simply by filling empty space. It condenses onto tiny preexisting particles called cloud condensation nuclei. The particle's soluble material and size affect how much supersaturation is needed for a droplet to activate. A salt particle may be especially good at attracting water. A particle's ability to nucleate a cloud droplet is not the same as its ability to scatter sunlight, although both depend on size and composition.

Imagine a rising parcel of moist air. As it expands in the lower pressure above, it cools. Water vapor begins condensing on suitable particles. At first the droplets are tiny. As the parcel continues to rise, condensation adds water and droplet populations evolve. Later, some droplets may grow by colliding and merging into raindrops. Ice formation introduces additional pathways in cold clouds. The number of available condensation nuclei can alter the size distribution at an early stage of this process, but the eventual rain depends on the whole cloud's dynamics, water supply, temperature, entrainment, and microphysics.

Here is a useful idealized experiment. Give two clouds the same amount of liquid water, but provide one cloud with many more activated droplets. Its water is divided into a greater number of smaller droplets. Smaller droplets can present more total area for scattering light, making the cloud more reflective under appropriate conditions. This is often called the cloud-albedo or first aerosol indirect effect. The qualification matters. The experiment holds cloud liquid water constant; real clouds respond to particles with changes in mixing, evaporation, precipitation, and water content. A physical explanation for one effect is not a prediction that every polluted cloud will behave the same way.

Could a cloud with many small droplets rain less readily? Sometimes. If collision and coalescence become less efficient, warm rain may be delayed. But a cloud is not just a bucket whose droplets wait for a collision. It mixes with surrounding dry air. It can develop strong updrafts or collapse. In deep convective clouds, freezing and melting processes matter. In some environments aerosols alter cloud lifetime or precipitation, and in others the response is small or complicated. Scientists have learned a great deal about aerosol-cloud interactions, yet their net climate influence remains more uncertain than the direct warming effect of well-mixed greenhouse gases.

It is tempting to divide aerosols into a cooling group and a warming group. The distinction helps but also conceals important complexity. Reflective aerosols often cool the surface by reducing incoming sunlight. Absorbing aerosols can warm the layer of air in which they reside, possibly changing stability and cloud development. Soot deposited on snow darkens it, increasing absorption of sunlight and potentially influencing melt. A sulfate plume can alter cloud brightness and the radiation budget over ocean. The sign of an aerosol effect cannot be inferred from the word aerosol alone.

In June 1991, Mount Pinatubo erupted in the Philippines. Sulfur dioxide reached the stratosphere, where it contributed to a widespread sulfate aerosol layer. Unlike many aerosols in the lower atmosphere, which are efficiently removed by precipitation on much shorter timescales, stratospheric particles can persist and spread around the globe. Satellite and ground-based observations documented a large perturbation in optical depth. The reflective layer reduced the sunlight reaching parts of the lower atmosphere and contributed to a temporary decrease in global mean surface temperature over the following years. NASA's Earth-observation account describes cooling of roughly half a degree Celsius over about two years. That number concerns the climatic response to this particular event; it is not a universal conversion factor between volcanic sulfur and temperature.

The Pinatubo episode offered scientists a natural experiment in how atmospheric particles alter radiation. But a volcano is not an exact analogue of industrial aerosol emissions. Stratospheric particles have different residence times and spatial distributions from most tropospheric pollution. They also affect stratospheric chemistry, including ozone reactions. In contrast, particles from factories, vehicles, fires, and cooking may interact immediately with nearby clouds, deposit on surfaces, or change local air quality. A controlled comparison must distinguish where the material is and which process is being measured.

There is another difference between aerosol particles and long-lived greenhouse gases. Carbon dioxide emitted in one region mixes sufficiently through the atmosphere that its warming influence is global, although the response varies geographically. Many aerosols remain concentrated closer to where their sources and atmospheric transport place them. Their spatially uneven cooling or heating can change atmospheric circulation. Cooling in one hemisphere relative to the other can help shift tropical rainfall. That is one reason some twentieth-century monsoon changes cannot be explained by greenhouse gases alone. A particle that persists for only days can influence a seasonal energy balance if emissions continually replenish it.

The interaction with monsoons illustrates how atmospheric chemistry crosses into physical geography. Suppose a region emits large quantities of scattering aerosols. Less sunlight reaches the ground, which can weaken some contrasts that help organize regional circulation. The location of deep convection may change, and so may moisture transport. Yet the result depends on ocean temperatures, cloud response, and circulation patterns beyond the aerosol source region. It is not valid to conclude that reducing pollution must always increase or decrease rain at a particular city. The chain from particle emission to cloud and rain is real, but it crosses enough processes that regional observations and models are necessary.

How do we measure aerosols? Instruments on the ground can collect particles or measure how they scatter light. Sun photometers estimate how much radiation the whole column of air removes at different wavelengths. Satellites infer aerosol optical depth from reflected sunlight, with limitations over bright surfaces and clouds. Aircraft can sample size distributions, chemistry, and cloud activation processes at specific heights. Models represent emissions, chemical formation, coagulation, condensation, transport, deposition, and interactions with radiation and clouds. Each measurement samples a different property of a three-dimensional and evolving population. An optical-depth map does not by itself reveal the concentration of fine particles at breathing height.

There are difficulties even in estimating the average climate effect. Anthropogenic aerosols have on balance exerted a cooling influence over the industrial period, according to major climate assessments, partially offsetting some greenhouse-gas warming. But the amount attributable to particular aerosol-cloud adjustments carries substantial uncertainty. Clouds change for meteorological reasons that have little to do with aerosol emissions, and aerosols frequently arrive with the same weather patterns that affect clouds. Observing a bright cloud beneath a dusty plume is not enough to isolate a causal aerosol effect. Scientists compare natural experiments, cloud microphysics, multi-instrument observations, and model ensembles to separate influences as well as possible.

The physics becomes clearer if we distinguish three questions. Where did a particle come from, and what is it made of? How does it interact with light, water, and other substances? And how do those local interactions alter a regional or global climate system? A single particle's ability to seed a droplet is a microscopic fact. Whether that change will intensify a storm is a much larger question. Between the two lie turbulence, humidity, radiation, cloud structure, winds, and the history of the air mass.

When the sky looks empty, it is still populated by particles carried from oceans, deserts, forests, fires, and industry. They are tiny, but the collective effect of billions of them can be measured from the ground and from orbit. The lesson is not that a speck of dust controls the climate. It is that the atmosphere's smallest ingredients can alter the passage of sunlight and the behavior of clouds, and that understanding the whole system requires carrying those microscopic effects into the larger physics without pretending the answer is simpler than it is.

## Scientific companion: particle physics, cloud activation, and radiative effects

**Primary and secondary aerosols.** Primary particles include mineral dust, sea salt and soot. Secondary particles are produced by atmospheric chemistry, including sulfate from sulfur dioxide, nitrates formed under appropriate chemical conditions, and low-volatility products of oxidation of organic gases. The resulting mixtures can change through coagulation, condensation, and deposition.

**Droplet number thought experiment.** If a cloud's liquid water volume is held fixed and spherical droplet number is multiplied by eight, the average radius scales as $r\propto N^{-1/3}$ and is halved. Total geometric droplet area then doubles. This illustrates why a change in droplet number can influence reflectivity; it is not a quantitative cloud-albedo law, because scattering, cloud depth, droplet distributions and water content also matter.

**Three distinct effects.** Aerosol-radiation interactions concern scattering and absorption of radiation by the particles themselves. Aerosol-cloud interactions concern cloud microphysics and resulting radiative or precipitation changes. Surface-darkening effects such as black carbon deposited on snow involve deposition and surface albedo. Treating these as one forcing mechanism hides different spatial scales and uncertain responses.

**Volcanic contrast.** The 1991 Pinatubo eruption injected sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere; widespread sulfate aerosol persisted much longer than typical lower-atmospheric particles. Use the observed subsequent cooling as a case study, not as a coefficient applicable to any aerosol source.

### Questions and investigations

1. Why is a sulfate aerosol formed from sulfur dioxide different from a mineral dust particle even if they have similar mass?
2. Explain why increasing droplet number can increase reflectivity in the fixed-water idealization, without guaranteeing more or less rainfall.
3. Compare the fate of stratospheric volcanic sulfate with that of aerosols in the rainy lower troposphere.
4. Why can an aerosol concentration map, a satellite optical-depth map and a cloud-droplet count show different patterns?
5. Which specific observations would help distinguish a change caused by aerosol emissions from an ordinary change in cloud weather?
