**Status: complete, 16 lectures.** This is a finite, audio-first biology course. It is the molecular and cellular companion to *The Living World* (natural history) and *The Physical World* (physical principles). Manuscripts have one spoken region each; technical companions and readings are not sent to TTS.

The guided collection includes all sixteen in an ordered progression; focused routes cover Genetics First, Cell Mechanisms, Beyond Mendel, and Renewal and Change. Every lecture is designed to be intelligible without listening to all previous lectures. The final synthesis develops a connected argument about energy, information, continuity, development and evolution instead of merely listing the earlier installments.

## Evidence and scope

The first twelve manuscripts retain their prose and claim-level source maps. Four new manuscripts add experiments in intestinal renewal, planarians, axolotl regeneration, reprogramming, horizontal transfer and CRISPR; genomic methodology; and a cross-scale closing argument. Primary research, reviewed references and orientation links are separated from narration. `metadata/sources.json` contains source records and `metadata/ID.json` contains per-lecture descriptions, source anchors and tag paths.

Modern discovery metadata is attached at the **lecture level**: specific `tags`, hierarchical `tag_paths`, and useful descriptions. The track retains only broad inherited labels, so a lecture on cell membranes does not automatically acquire the names of cancer pathways or genome-wide association methods. Places, named eras and chronology are deliberately not assigned just because a scientist or a study year appears in the narration; they describe relevant geographic or historical subject coverage, not incidental citations. Specific scientific techniques and mechanisms carry topic paths instead.

Length follows the material; the word and duration fields are descriptive measurements, not targets. No audio or generated binary assets are part of this pack. Its sixteen-lecture curriculum is editorially complete, not a claim that the field of biology has been exhausted.
