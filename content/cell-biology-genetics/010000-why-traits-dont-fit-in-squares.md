# Why Traits Do Not Fit in Punnett Squares: Penetrance, Networks, and Quantitative Inheritance

The Punnett square is one of biology's most successful teaching devices. Put two possible alleles for one parent along the top of a grid, two for the other along the side, and the four boxes show the possible combinations in an offspring. For a simple dominant or recessive trait, it makes the arithmetic transparent. You can understand Mendel's pea experiments without being able to see a chromosome. But a grid that correctly predicts how alleles are transmitted does not automatically tell us exactly what an organism will look like, how it will behave, or which diseases it may develop.

The first thing to distinguish is inheritance from expression. A child may inherit a particular DNA variant with a probability that Mendelian logic describes precisely, while the resulting trait has a more complex relationship to that DNA. The variant acts inside a cell, and the cell acts inside a changing organism. Biochemical conditions, other genes, development, age, exposure and chance all intervene. The fascinating question is not whether Mendel was wrong. It is how Mendel's powerful account of transmission fits into a world in which traits arise through networks of interacting causes.

Begin with an imaginary plant. Suppose an allele changes the activity of an enzyme needed to make a purple pigment. If the enzyme is disabled, the flower may be white under the conditions of our experiment. In a genetic cross, an allele affecting the pigment can segregate in predictable ratios. Yet that ratio assumes a great deal about the biology: which allele variants are present, how much enzyme activity is enough, whether other reactions in the pathway are intact, and whether the flower experienced the conditions required for pigment production. In the classroom, we often hold those other factors constant to isolate one principle. Nature generally does not.

A classical extension is epistasis, in which variation at one locus alters or masks the effect of variation at another. Imagine one gene producing a pigment and a second gene directing pigment into a tissue. In an individual unable to make pigment, a variant affecting where the pigment goes may have no visible effect at all. Crosses involving both genes can produce ratios different from the famous nine-three-three-one of two independently assorted dominant-recessive traits. Mendelian segregation still operates at each locus. The pattern of visible traits changes because the molecular effects are connected.

Calling a gene 'for' a trait can conceal the chain of interactions that produces it. A gene does not ordinarily specify an isolated visible object. It may encode an enzyme, a transporter, a regulator or an RNA that changes the behavior of many other molecules. The resulting trait is an outcome of a chain of interactions. One gene can influence many traits, a phenomenon called pleiotropy. Many genes can contribute to one trait. And the effect of one allele can depend on which other alleles are present. An apparently simple single-gene result is often a biological system under unusually controlled conditions.

Now imagine two people who carry the same disease-associated variant, but only one develops the condition being studied. Geneticists call the probability that a particular genotype produces a specified phenotype its penetrance. If nearly all carriers express it under a clearly defined set of conditions, we speak of high penetrance. If only some do, the penetrance is incomplete. A statistic of this kind needs a definition of phenotype, an age range, the population sampled, and a way to recognize carriers who have not developed the condition. Without those pieces, a penetrance number can be misleading.

Expressivity answers a different question. Among individuals who do show a trait, how strongly or in what form does it appear? A variant can have high penetrance but variable expressivity. For instance, a developmental condition might occur in most carriers, but its physical signs vary in number or severity. Penetrance concerns whether a defined feature appears; expressivity concerns its pattern or degree. The boundary can blur if researchers use different thresholds to say what counts as affected. We must not treat these terms as magical properties of one DNA spelling independent of how they are measured.

There is also time. A variant that contributes to a condition developing mostly later in life may seem to have low penetrance in a young sample and higher penetrance among older carriers. Environmental exposures can change the relevant risks. Other variants can act as genetic modifiers. And a phenotype may be measured with errors or imperfect detection. When interpreting an inheritance study, asking 'How old were the participants?' and 'What counted as expressing the trait?' can be as important as looking at the family tree.

The body adds another layer: not every cell has to carry exactly the same genome or express the same inherited allele in the same way. Somatic mutation can create mosaic cell lineages. In mammals with more than one X chromosome, X-chromosome inactivation produces cellular mosaics in which different cells may use different X-linked alleles. Mitochondria carry their own DNA, inherited through a different route in most animals, and cells can contain varying mixtures of mitochondrial genome variants. These examples matter because the most familiar two-allele autosomal diagram is only one piece of genetic biology, not a complete model of it.

For some traits, the biggest puzzle is that no single gene has an overriding effect. Measure the height of a large population, for example. You will not ordinarily see two or three tidy height categories corresponding to a dominant and a recessive allele. Instead you get a broad distribution. Height depends on many biological processes: bone growth, cartilage, hormones, metabolism, timing of development and health. Variation in genes affecting these processes contributes to variation in height. Nutrition, illness and other environmental conditions matter as well. A person's measured height is the outcome of development, not a direct reading of a tally of 'tall' DNA letters.

Quantitative genetics gives us a disciplined way to reason about such continuous traits. Imagine measuring a characteristic across individuals in a particular population under particular conditions. Its total variance describes how spread out the measurements are. We can build statistical models that partition this variance into components associated with genetic differences, environmental differences and their interactions. In a simplified additive model, the expected contributions of alleles can be summed. This may be useful even when the underlying molecular mechanisms are complicated. Statistical additivity is a property of a model's predictive components, not a claim that the biology contains no interacting pathways.

The most misused word here is heritability. Heritability is a population-level statement about variation under specified circumstances. It is not the fraction of an individual's trait caused by genes. If a height trait has high heritability within one well-fed population, that does not imply that improving nutrition could not raise average height. It means that, within the environmental variation sampled, genetic differences statistically account for a substantial share of the differences between individuals. Move to a population with different diets, illnesses or social conditions and the measured heritability may change.

A thought experiment makes this point clear. Grow seeds from several genetically different plant lines in a greenhouse with tightly controlled light, water and nutrients. Differences in plant height may be highly attributable to genotype. Now grow genetically identical plants in pots with radically different water supplies. The variation within that second experiment may be almost entirely environmental. The same kind of trait can yield different variance partitions because we changed the sources of variation. Neither experiment determines the eternal percentage contribution of genes to every plant's height.

Even a trait that is nearly completely determined by genotype can be shifted by the environment in every member of a population. Conversely, a trait influenced by the environment may still exhibit large heritable differences. A high heritability estimate neither means a trait is fixed nor tells us how easily a change in the environment will affect the mean. And a low estimate does not mean the genes involved are unimportant for the trait's basic development. All the plants in an experiment need genes for photosynthesis, but if those genes barely vary among the plants, they may account for little of the observed differences.

Twin and family studies provide one approach to estimating genetic contributions to variation, though they rely on assumptions about shared environments and family resemblance. Breeding experiments provide another. Genomic data offer a more direct but still statistical approach: examine many DNA markers and ask whether the values of particular markers are associated with differences in a trait. Such analyses are called genome-wide association studies, or G W A S. The word association is deliberate. A marker can lie near a causal variant and be inherited with it, just as markers on chromosomes could be linked in a fruit-fly cross.

A large study of human height published in 2022 analyzed data from about five point four million individuals and reported more than twelve thousand statistically independent association signals across thousands of genomic regions. That is not evidence for twelve thousand independent 'height genes' in the everyday sense, and it is not a list from which you can read a person's eventual stature. It demonstrates the distributed genomic architecture of a familiar characteristic. Each association is a lead toward biological mechanisms, with effect sizes, linkage patterns, population structure and sampling choices that investigators must analyze.

Why can genome-wide studies mislead? Imagine two populations whose members differ, on average, both in genetic ancestry and in an environmental exposure that affects the measured outcome. A marker common in one group might appear associated with the trait even if it does nothing causally. Statistical correction for ancestry and study design can reduce such confounding, but no single procedure automatically solves every problem. It is also essential to distinguish a correlation estimated in one sampled population from a reliable prediction in a population with different genetic backgrounds and environments.

Polygenic scores combine the estimated effects of many variants into a statistical index. They can summarize inherited contributions to some measured outcomes, but the scores are not individual destinies. Their predictive performance varies with the phenotype, the population used for training, the population being assessed and the accompanying environmental information. They may be less transferable when genetic association studies disproportionately represent one ancestry group. A score may shift the estimated probability of an outcome without being useful as a stand-alone decision, and it should never be mistaken for a measurement of a person's worth or potential.

Now return from statistics to mechanism. An organism does not have a calculator summing thousands of allele effects and printing a trait. It has cells that make proteins, transmit signals, assemble tissues and respond to circumstances. Several variants may affect the same pathway; others influence entirely different steps. A gene that changes development early may alter the consequences of a gene that acts later. One allele's effect can be large only under particular environmental conditions. Gene-by-environment interaction means the relative effects of genotypes are themselves sensitive to context, not that genes and environment are separately acting forces that can always be assigned fixed percentages.

How can we move from a statistical signal to a causal explanation? We might compare a DNA variant with molecular measurements of RNA or proteins, edit the variant in cells or a model organism, and test whether a predicted change appears. We might introduce a gene variant into a different genetic background, or modify a suspected interacting gene to see whether the effect persists. If a result changes when we control diet, temperature or stress, that is evidence about environmental context. Different experiments answer different questions. Changing a base in cultured cells may establish an effect in those cells without proving the same effect produces the entire human trait.

This is why genetic explanations are richest when we resist both extremes. It is wrong to conclude that a complicated trait is not genetic because it doesn't follow a tidy Punnett square. It is equally wrong to conclude that a statistically heritable trait is fixed, inevitable, or explained by a single score. Inheritance tells us which biological variants can enter the next generation. Development tells us how those variants participate in making an organism. Population and quantitative genetics tell us how differences are distributed and predicted across many organisms. Those are related questions, but not interchangeable ones.

The Punnett square remains useful. We should keep it, precisely because its assumptions are visible. But the next time you see a grid with neatly filled boxes, remember what happens after an allele enters a living cell. It encounters interacting proteins, regulatory networks, a developing body, and an environment that never holds perfectly still. Genetics becomes more powerful, not less, when its predictions are properly conditional. A person inherits DNA. They do not inherit an already completed life.

## Technical companion: genotype, phenotype, and variance

**Penetrance and expressivity.** For genotype \(G\) and a specified phenotype definition \(Y\) observed by age \(t\), penetrance may be modeled as \(P(Y=1\mid G, t, E, \text{sampling})\). Expressivity characterizes the distribution of \(Y\)'s forms or severities among affected carriers; ascertainment and changing diagnostic thresholds can alter both.

**A simple quantitative model.** \(Y=\mu+G+E+G\times E+\varepsilon\) is a conceptual decomposition. In an additive model where the terms are appropriately defined and uncorrelated, \(V_P=V_G+V_E+V_\varepsilon\). With dominance, epistasis, covariance and interactions the variance decomposition requires additional terms. Narrow-sense heritability \(h^2=V_A/V_P\) concerns additive genetic variance in a defined population and environment, not an individual's percentage of genetic causation.

**Risk and prediction.** A polygenic score \(S_i=\sum_j \hat\beta_j x_{ij}\) combines estimated effects \(\hat\beta_j\) with individual genotypes \(x_{ij}\). Training-population allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium, effect estimation and environment all affect prediction. A relative-risk shift is not an absolute risk without baseline incidence and appropriate calibration.

**Causal validation.** Association studies nominate genomic regions. Orthogonal molecular assays, perturbation, rescue, and replication can test proposed mechanisms. Statistical significance alone cannot establish a particular regulatory or protein-level causal route.
