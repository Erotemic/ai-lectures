# Evolution in the Gene Pool: Selection, Drift, and the History Hidden in DNA

On a small island in the Galápagos, a drought changes which seeds are available to finches. Birds with different beak sizes do not meet the same challenge equally. Some can crack the seeds that remain. Others struggle. Researchers watch individual birds survive or disappear, then measure the next generation. It is a vivid demonstration that natural selection can take place within an ordinary human lifetime. But it also poses a less visible question. What happened to the inherited variants contributing to those traits, and how could we tell whether the change arose from selection rather than chance?

To answer, we need to change the unit of observation. A cell carries a genome. An individual receives alleles and develops a phenotype. A population contains many alleles in different frequencies, continually reshuffled and transmitted through reproduction. Evolution in the population-genetic sense involves changes in that inherited variation across generations. Natural selection is one cause. It is not the only one, and the others are not footnotes.

Begin with the simplest possible population. Imagine a diploid species, with two copies of a particular gene in each individual. At that locus, there are two variants, which we will call A and a. Suppose sixty percent of all allele copies in the population are A, and forty percent are a. Those are allele frequencies. They are not the same as the frequencies of individuals who have AA, Aa, or aa genotypes. To predict the genotype distribution from the allele frequencies, we need assumptions about how individuals reproduce.

If mating is random with respect to this locus, and if we idealize an enormously large population without selection, migration or mutation, a simple probability argument gives us the Hardy–Weinberg proportions. If the A frequency is zero point six, the chance that two independently sampled gametes both carry A is zero point six times zero point six, or thirty-six percent. The chance of two a copies is sixteen percent. The two different orders producing a heterozygote add up to forty-eight percent. The three possibilities sum to one hundred percent.

Why should we care about a prediction that assumes away so much of the living world? Because it supplies a baseline. If a sample departs from those genotype proportions, we have a question to investigate. We should not leap to the conclusion that selection is operating. Population structure, nonrandom mating, errors of measurement, departures from diploidy, or other violations of the model could be responsible. And allele frequencies can change over generations even when genotype proportions happen to match Hardy–Weinberg expectations within each generation. A null model is valuable because it tells us what explanation remains to be earned.

First consider selection. In a population where a heritable trait affects the probability of surviving and producing offspring, variants contributing to that trait can change in frequency. Suppose an insect population encounters a pesticide. Some inherited variants alter how the chemical is processed or interacts with its target. If carriers leave more descendants under pesticide exposure, those variants may become more common. But selection does not invent a mutation in response to the needs of the individual insect. A mutation can arise during reproduction or growth for reasons unrelated to whether it will later prove useful. Exposure changes which variants reproduce successfully.

That distinction was tested with extraordinary elegance by Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück in 1943. They grew many separate bacterial cultures, then exposed them to a virus that killed susceptible bacteria. If resistance arose only after the virus induced the necessary change, similarly sized cultures might be expected to yield roughly similar numbers of resistant survivors. If resistant mutations arose at unpredictable times before exposure, some cultures would contain many descendants of an early mutant while others would contain very few. The data showed the characteristic large fluctuations predicted by pre-existing mutation events. It was a statistical way to reconstruct something the experimenters had not watched happen cell by cell.

The experiment did not show that all mutation rates are fixed under every condition, nor that cellular stress can never affect mutation mechanisms. It established a crucial separation between when resistant variants arise and when the environment favors them. Variation, selection and the history of cell divisions are different parts of the causal story. The same discipline is necessary when we interpret antibiotic resistance or natural evolutionary change.

Selection can be estimated in the wild. Peter and Rosemary Grant and collaborators studied Darwin's finches over years, following birds on Daphne Major and measuring traits such as beak dimensions and body size. During the 1977 drought, birds with certain larger beaks were more likely to survive when harder seeds remained. A later study found selection on related traits across other difficult years, but it also showed how complicated the targets of selection can be. Because beak depth and body size are correlated, an observed survival difference does not automatically tell us which variable caused the advantage. Careful statistical analysis and changing ecological conditions matter.

Selection is also local and conditional. A trait that helps a bird survive one food shortage can carry costs when food types or competitors change. A variant that is advantageous in a particular environment may be neutral or disadvantageous elsewhere. Evolution does not climb a single mountain labeled 'better species.' It changes the distribution of inherited variants in the conditions where organisms actually reproduce.

Now imagine a more unsettling case. In a small population, some individuals fail to reproduce through pure chance. Perhaps a storm kills a nest before any offspring emerge. Some allele copies disappear from the next generation even if they had no negative effect on survival or reproduction under normal conditions. This is genetic drift, the random sampling of alleles across generations. In a large idealized population, random fluctuations at one locus tend to be comparatively small. In a small population, the same randomness can move allele frequencies rapidly or remove a variant entirely.

Consider our A allele at frequency sixty percent. If a new generation is formed from just ten independently sampled allele copies, one generation could accidentally contain eight A copies, five, or three. None of those outcomes needs selection. If millions of copies are sampled under the same model, the proportion will usually be much closer to sixty percent. The law of large numbers does not turn a population into a deterministic machine; it explains why sampling noise becomes relatively smaller as the number of sampled copies increases.

Drift can be amplified by demographic history. A population may crash in a bottleneck after a disease outbreak, fire or drought. Its survivors carry only a subset of its former variation. A few colonists can found a new population whose allele frequencies differ sharply from the source population. These are the bottleneck and founder effects. You cannot safely diagnose either one from a single odd-looking trait, however. Demographic history must be inferred from data and tested against alternatives.

Population size in these models is not always the number of animals a field biologist counts. Geneticists use effective population size, a parameter describing how strongly drift or genealogical coalescence behaves relative to a simplified reference population. If individuals differ greatly in reproductive success, generations overlap, or sex ratios and population sizes fluctuate, the effective size can differ sharply from the census size. It may even vary depending on which aspect of the data is being modeled. Quoting an effective size without its assumptions makes it sound more tangible than it is.

Migration adds another mechanism, often called gene flow. Suppose two neighboring plant populations contain different frequencies of an allele affecting flowering time. Wind-blown pollen from one population can fertilize plants in the other. The next generation receives alleles that did not originate in its local parental group. Gene flow may reduce differences between populations, but whether it does depends on migration rates and the other evolutionary forces. It can also bring variants into a population where local selection favors them. An immigration event changes more than the number of individuals on the hillside; it changes the genetic material available for future combinations.

What about mutation? It is the ultimate source of novel DNA sequence variants, but it is rarely the fastest cause of change in common allele frequencies over a short interval. Selection and drift can rearrange the frequencies of variants already present. Recombination can generate new combinations without creating new bases. Mobile elements and gene duplication can add and move genetic material, while point mutation changes individual nucleotides. A complete explanation requires asking not merely whether an allele is present, but where it came from, how it traveled, and what happened after it arrived.

Natural selection and drift often operate simultaneously. One population could favor a beneficial allele yet lose it by chance while it is rare. Another could keep a slightly harmful allele for a long time because its selective disadvantage is weak relative to random fluctuations. Some variants are effectively neutral under particular conditions. The word neutral does not mean biologically meaningless in every conceivable context. It means their effects on the modeled reproductive outcomes are sufficiently small that random processes can dominate at the scale being considered.

So far we have imagined looking forward, generation by generation. There is a second way to reason about populations. Take DNA sequences from several individuals living today and trace their ancestry backward. Two sampled copies of a region may have descended from the same ancestral copy in some past generation. As we move backward, their lineages merge at that common ancestor. Repeat this process for several sequences, and we obtain a genealogical tree. This is the central intuition of coalescent theory, developed in mathematically explicit form in the early 1980s.

Backward thinking reverses the question. Instead of asking which allele frequencies a population will have in a thousand generations, we ask which histories are compatible with the sequence differences seen now. Under an idealized neutral random-mating model, the chance that two lineages coalesce in a preceding generation is connected to the number of breeding copies in the population. Smaller effective populations produce more rapid coalescence on average; larger ones preserve older separate lineages. Mutation accumulates along branches, and present-day sequence differences provide noisy clues about the time and shape of the genealogy.

That is conceptually powerful, but there are complications. Recombination means different parts of a chromosome can have different ancestry trees. Selection changes the distributions of lineages and may make a sample look as though it came from a smaller or more rapidly changing population. Population structure and migrations create other patterns. Inferring ancient population size from genomes is an inverse problem: several different demographic histories can produce similar data. Model assumptions are not embarrassing caveats to hide; they determine what the inference actually means.

There is no reason to expect every stretch of our DNA to tell the same family story. One region may have its most recent common ancestor comparatively recently, another far earlier. A nearby advantageous variant can change the apparent history of linked neutral variants. Segments inherited from different populations carry distinct histories, and the categories we use to describe populations are approximations of continuing patterns of ancestry, mating, movement and sampling, not biological boxes with fixed boundaries.

This perspective makes a familiar human phrase problematic: 'this gene evolved because the species needed it.' At the molecular level, the genome changes through particular mutational and recombination events. At the individual level, variants can affect survival and reproduction. At the population level, the frequencies respond to sampling, migration and selection. Retrospectively, a successful variant can appear purposefully fitted to its environment. But no allele knows the needs of a future generation. Historical contingency and differential reproduction are enough to produce striking adaptation over time.

Return to the finches. The drought was not a selection dial set in a classroom. It was a change to a food web, experienced by birds of different ages, sizes, genotypes and circumstances. Researchers could measure survival and trait distributions, test whether beak dimensions had changed, and ask how the offspring inherited those differences. DNA studies add a molecular view, but they do not abolish the ecological questions. A genome sequence cannot by itself tell us why a bird survived a particular dry season.

The most important lesson of population genetics is that evolution is not one force with one direction. It is a family of processes acting on inherited variation, some directional, some stochastic, and often entangled. Hardy–Weinberg gives us a baseline. Selection asks which variants leave more descendants in particular conditions. Drift measures the power of chance. Gene flow connects populations. Mutation and recombination create and rearrange variation. Coalescent reasoning lets us turn present-day sequences into conditional statements about ancestry. Together these ideas make the gene pool something we can measure, model and investigate, rather than a metaphor for a species changing its mind.

## Technical companion: forward and backward population models

**Hardy–Weinberg baseline.** With two alleles at frequencies \(p\) and \(q=1-p\), random union of gametes yields expected diploid genotype frequencies \(p^2, 2pq, q^2\). For \(p=0.6\), the expected proportions are 0.36, 0.48, 0.16. This is an expectation under assumptions; genotype deviations do not uniquely identify a causal force.

**Sampling and drift.** In an ideal Wright–Fisher diploid population with effective population size \(N_e\), the number of A alleles in the next generation can be modeled as \(X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(2N_e,p)\), with \(E[p']=p\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(p')=p(1-p)/(2N_e)\). The formula isolates random sampling; it does not model all real breeding structures.

**Selection (one-locus example).** With genotype fitnesses \(w_{AA},w_{Aa},w_{aa}\), allele frequency after selection is \(p'=[p^2w_{AA}+pq w_{Aa}]/\overline w\), assuming random mating before selection and correctly defined fitness weights. Selection can oppose drift but does not guarantee an allele will fix.

**Coalescent intuition.** For a randomly sampled pair of gene copies under an ideal constant-size diploid model, the probability of immediate common ancestry one generation backward is about \(1/(2N_e)\), and the expected time to that event is about \(2N_e\) generations. Migration, structure, selection, and recombination alter these predictions; neighboring sites can have distinct genealogies.
