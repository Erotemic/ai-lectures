# Kate and the Acorn with a Long Plan

Kate the hedgehog found an acorn in the grass.

It was smooth and brown, with a rough cap that looked like a hat.

Kate liked hats, so she thought it was a fine acorn.

She carried it to the garden and set it in an empty flowerpot.

“You can live here,” she told it.

She waited.

The acorn didn't move.

Kate had a cup of water and came back.

The acorn still didn't move.

“Perhaps,” said Kate, “you are thinking.”

The gardener came down the path with a watering can.

“What are you waiting for?” the gardener asked.

“A tree,” Kate replied.

The gardener bent to look at the flowerpot.

“Ah. An oak tree. That is a very long thing to wait for.”

Kate looked at the acorn. It was small enough to fit between her paws.

“Will it be tall by supper?”

“Not by this supper.”

“Tomorrow's supper?”

“Not that one either.”

Kate considered this.

“How tall is an oak tree?”

“Come and see.”

The gardener led Kate down the garden path, through the open gate, and to the old oak near the meadow.

Its trunk was wider than Kate could stretch her arms. Thick branches spread overhead, and the leaves whispered in the breeze.

Kate stood beneath it and looked up.

She looked up farther.

Then she stepped backward so she could look up without tipping over.

“This is a very big acorn,” she said.

The gardener laughed.

“That oak was little once too,” the gardener said. “It began with an acorn.”

Kate looked down at the ground.

There were more acorns under the tree. Some had been eaten by animals. Some lay among the leaves. One had a tiny root pushing out where its shell had opened.

Kate crouched close.

“Is that the beginning?”

“It is one beginning,” said the gardener.

Farther along the meadow stood a little oak sapling, no taller than Kate's head. It had a thin stem and several green leaves.

“That one has been growing for a while,” the gardener said.

Kate looked at the tiny root, then the sapling, then the enormous old oak.

They were all different sizes, but she could see how they belonged together.

On the way home she carried her chosen acorn carefully in both paws.

The gardener helped Kate plant it in a pot of soil outdoors. They put the pot where rain could reach it and where it would not be knocked over.

Then they left it alone for the afternoon.

The next morning, Kate went outside to check.

Nothing green was there.

She checked the following morning.

Still nothing.

She checked on a day when the wind blew, and on a day when the rain pattered on the shed roof. The acorn had a place to grow, but it did not have to grow where Kate could watch every moment.

The weather turned cooler. The leaves on the big oak changed color, and some fell to the ground.

Kate spent the colder days doing other things. She made a new hat, built a cushion house, and watched birds search for seeds.

When warmer weather returned, she came to the pot again.

A little green shoot was pushing out of the soil.

Kate stared.

“You've been busy,” she whispered.

Over the next few weeks the shoot grew a little taller. Two small leaves unfolded. They were not enormous oak branches. They could not make shade for a picnic.

They were simply leaves, doing the work of a little tree.

The little plant had grown just two new leaves. The old oak still filled the sky.

Kate brought a small cushion and sat between the pot and the old oak in the distance.

She looked from one to the other.

One could shelter a bird. The other could barely shelter a beetle.

“Goodnight, little tree,” Kate said that evening.

She did not ask it to hurry.

The leaves rested in the cooling air, and the old oak moved softly in the wind beyond the garden.

Kate went inside to sleep, while both trees kept growing in their own good time.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** A seed, a sapling, and an old tree make long-term change visible without pretending a mature tree could grow during one bedtime.

**Optional prompt:** “What was the little tree before it had leaves?” A child may simply listen without answering.

Linder and Angela Eckhoff; National Association for the Education of Young Children, *Breaking Down STEAM for Young Children. Part 3: Questioning*](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/tyc/aug2020/breaking-down-steam). The first supports a specific science detail; the second supports only optional shared reading or conversation. Neither source evaluates this story or promises a bedtime or developmental effect.

**Narrator note:** Single calm narrator; no voice acting, music, sound effects or timed length requirement.
