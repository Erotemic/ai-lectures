# 029000 — The Little Red Wagon's Holiday

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original fiction for a single narrator. **Story shape:** A familiar garden wagon takes a very small holiday with one child and her grandfather.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# The Little Red Wagon's Holiday

At the end of Nia's garden stood a little red wagon.

It had four black wheels, one long handle, and a yellow scratch on the side.

All spring, the wagon had carried things.

It had carried soil for the flowerpots.

It had carried fallen sticks.

Once, it had carried Grandpa's enormous gardening boots, which looked like two sleeping bears.

On Saturday morning, Nia looked at the wagon and had an idea.

“Today,” she told Grandpa, “the wagon can have a holiday.”

Grandpa thought that sounded fair.

Nia brushed out the last little bits of soil.

Grandpa wiped the wheels with a cloth.

Then Nia spread a clean yellow towel inside.

A holiday wagon ought to be comfortable.

But where should it go?

Nia looked along the garden path.

Past the mint bush was a flat patch of grass.

Beyond the grass stood an old apple tree.

And beyond the apple tree was the vegetable patch, where Grandpa grew beans.

“That is a very long journey,” said Grandpa, looking at the whole garden.

Nia agreed. It was almost as far as lunchtime.

They packed the wagon carefully.

One water bottle for Nia.

One water bottle for Grandpa.

A little box of crackers.

And a soft cushion for the wagon itself.

Nia pulled the handle.

The wagon made its familiar rolling sound.

Rumble, rumble.

They reached the mint bush.

Nia stopped and sniffed the leaves.

Grandpa sniffed them too.

The wagon waited patiently.

Nia decided the mint bush was their first holiday stop.

At the patch of grass, they saw a ladybug resting on a blade of green.

They watched it crawl to the tip.

Then it opened its wings and flew away.

Nia did not try to follow it.

The ladybug had somewhere else to go.

The wagon had its own holiday to finish.

Rumble, rumble.

At the apple tree, Nia sat down and opened the crackers.

Grandpa sat beside her.

The wagon stayed in the shade with its cushion tucked neatly inside.

A breeze stirred the leaves overhead.

One apple leaf floated onto the yellow towel.

Nia picked it up and examined its little veins.

It looked like a tiny map of roads.

She tucked it into the wagon as a holiday souvenir.

Then they rolled onward to the bean patch.

There was a problem.

The path had a small bump where a tree root crossed it.

Nia pulled.

The front wheel stopped at the root.

She pulled again, gently, but the wheel still wouldn't go over.

Grandpa held the handle with her.

Together, they tipped the wagon just enough for the front wheels to pass.

Then the back wheels followed.

Over the bump they went.

Rumble, rumble, rumble.

The beans were growing in two long rows.

Their leaves made little green roofs above the soil.

Nia looked at the bean plants.

She looked at the old red wagon.

She thought the wagon had seen quite enough of the world for one day.

So they turned around and took the short way home.

At the end of the path, Nia parked the wagon in its familiar spot.

She took out the water bottles and the empty cracker box.

She shook the yellow towel and folded it.

Then she placed the apple leaf on the wagon's seat.

Grandpa gave the wagon a gentle pat.

Nia patted it too.

The wagon stood still in the late-afternoon shade.

Tomorrow it might carry soil again.

But today it had traveled all the way to the beans and back.

Nia went inside for supper.

Later, when the garden grew quiet, the little red wagon stood beneath the apple tree's shadow.

The yellow scratch was still on its side.

The four black wheels were still.

And one small apple leaf rested in its middle, like a picture from a very good holiday.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** A gentle pretend-holiday game makes an ordinary garden feel expansive. The story invites attention to concrete details and gives a small physical problem a cooperative, unhurried solution. A wagon does not literally need rest; that is Nia's pretend-play frame. The trip remains within a familiar garden with Grandpa nearby.

**Optional prompt:** “If your wagon went on holiday, what could it see?” The question can be left until daytime if the child is sleepy.

**Sources and limits:** [American Academy of Pediatrics, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old* (2021)](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx) suggests letting children connect stories to their own experiences and talk about what happens; [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years*, Parenting Tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) includes simple noticing and conversational prompts. These sources are guidance for shared reading and age-related experiences, **not** experiments on this wagon story or evidence that its plot improves resilience.

**Single-voice note:** Grandpa and Nia can be read in one voice; the recurring “rumble, rumble” is a quiet rhythm, not a sound effect requirement.
