# 036000 — Eddie's Upside-Down Bed

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator animal story. **Story shape:** A snail discovers that a bat's comfortable resting place looks very different from his own.

# Eddie's Upside-Down Bed

In an old oak tree lived a little bat named Eddie.

Eddie had a comfortable resting place high inside the hollow trunk.

He liked to hang by his feet from a rough wooden ledge.

His head pointed down.

His toes pointed up.

And when he folded his wings around himself, he looked a little like a tiny brown umbrella.

One evening, just as the sky began turning purple, a garden snail named Pete passed the tree.

Pete was on his way home to sleep beneath a broad leaf.

He looked up at Eddie.

Then he looked up again, because what he saw was very surprising.

“Eddie,” Pete called, “I think your bed is upside down.”

Eddie opened one eye.

“It's just right for me,” he said.

Pete looked thoughtful.

Pete slept curled snugly inside his shell, on a nice level patch of earth.

He could not imagine doing that upside down.

“What happens to your pillow?” he asked.

“I don't have a pillow,” said Eddie.

Pete looked even more thoughtful.

“No pillow?”

“No pillow.”

“Not even a very small one?”

Eddie shook his head.

That made his little ears wobble.

Pete decided Eddie might enjoy a pillow anyway.

He found a soft yellow leaf and pushed it toward the tree.

“Here you are,” he said. “A leaf pillow.”

Eddie thanked him.

Pete watched as Eddie tried to tuck the leaf beneath his head.

But Eddie's head was hanging in the air.

The leaf slipped away and floated down.

It landed on Pete's shell.

For a moment, Pete looked like a snail wearing a yellow hat.

Eddie smiled.

Pete tried again.

He placed the leaf on a low branch and asked Eddie to rest against it.

Eddie reached for the leaf.

The branch was too far away.

Pete looked at the branch.

He looked at Eddie.

He looked at his own perfectly comfortable shell.

“Perhaps,” he said slowly, “you really don't need a pillow.”

“Not for my bed,” said Eddie.

Pete thought about his favorite sleeping leaf. It was wonderful for a snail. It kept his resting place shady and comfortable.

But it would not be very useful hanging high in an oak tree.

Eddie stretched one wing, then the other.

The evening sky was growing darker, and Eddie was getting ready to go out into it.

Pete, meanwhile, was getting sleepier.

“How funny,” Pete said. “I'm going to bed, and you're just getting up.”

“That is funny,” Eddie agreed.

A little breeze wandered through the oak leaves.

It made a soft rustling sound.

Pete picked up the yellow leaf that had fallen onto his shell.

He carried it home.

Beneath his own broad green leaf, he tucked the yellow one beside him like a little mat.

It was exactly the right size for Pete.

High above, Eddie unfolded his wings and slipped gently into the evening air.

He made one quiet turn beside the oak tree before flying beyond the garden.

Pete watched him for a moment.

Then Pete pulled into his shell and settled on his leaf mat.

The tree stood still against the purple sky.

The garden path grew quiet.

And two friends, who liked very different kinds of beds, were both exactly where they wanted to be.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** The main animal fact is real: many bats rest hanging upside down. The characters' conversation and falling leaf are invented. Pete finds Eddie's position surprising but ultimately accepts that different bodies can have different comfortable arrangements. The story does not assign the child a moral to recite.

**Optional prompt:** “Where does Pete sleep? And where does Eddie rest?” Or simply enjoy their different goodnights without discussion.

**Sources and limits:** [Bat Conservation International, Kristen Pope, *Why Do Bats Hang Upside Down?* (August 21, 2023)](https://www.batcon.org/why-do-bats-hang-upside-down/) explains bat roosting, anatomy and the usefulness of hanging for flight. [U.S. National Park Service, *Bats — Yellowstone National Park*](https://www.nps.gov/yell/learn/nature/bats.htm), “Behavior,” also describes upside-down roosting. Not every bat species has exactly the same resting behavior, and neither source documents Eddie or Pete. [ZERO TO THREE, *Developing Thinking Skills From 24–36 Months*](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/developing-thinking-skills-from-24-to-36-months/) offers background on early awareness of differing perspectives; it does not prove that this story builds empathy.

**Single-voice note:** Eddie and Pete have distinct locations and repeated names, so one steady narrator can handle their short conversation. No squeaky bat voice is needed.
