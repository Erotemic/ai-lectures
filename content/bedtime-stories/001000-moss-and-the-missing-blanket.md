# 001000 — Moss and the Missing Blanket

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (written primarily for age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator bedtime fiction. **Story shape:** A small, solvable mystery in a familiar home.

# Moss and the Missing Blanket

Moss was a little rabbit who liked three things about bedtime.

He liked the warm water on his paws when Mama helped him wash.

He liked the round moon he could see from his window.

And most of all, he liked his blue blanket.

The blanket was soft at the edges and smooth in the middle. There was a little yellow moon stitched into one corner. Moss always kept that corner near his nose.

One evening, after his paws were clean and his teeth were brushed, Moss climbed onto his bed.

His pillow was there.

His stuffed mouse was there.

But his blanket was not.

Moss lifted his pillow. He found a feather that had slipped out of the pillowcase.

No blue blanket.

He looked at the foot of the bed. He found one small sock.

Still no blue blanket.

Mama Rabbit was folding a towel by the door.

Moss told her that the blanket had gone missing.

Mama put down the towel and sat beside him. They could look together.

First they looked under the bed.

Moss got down low, with his nose almost touching the floor. Under the bed were two wooden blocks and a round red ball.

He rolled the ball out with one paw.

There was no blanket under the bed.

Next they looked behind the big chair.

Moss found a book about ducks. He remembered the funny duck on the last page, the one with a leaf on its head.

He gave the book to Mama to put away.

But there was no blanket behind the chair.

They looked in the basket where the clean clothes went.

There was a shirt.

There was a pair of little brown trousers.

There was even a washcloth with a carrot on it.

No blanket.

Moss sat down on the rug.

He wasn't angry. He was thinking.

Mama asked him to remember what he had done that afternoon.

Moss remembered a snack. He had eaten two apple slices and one more little bite because the second slice was so good.

He remembered Hazel the hedgehog coming to play.

They had made a bridge from wooden blocks. They had put a little wooden boat beneath it.

A bridge needed a river, Moss had decided.

And what was blue enough to be a river?

Moss's ears stood up.

His blanket!

He padded into the sitting room. Mama followed with the little lamp.

The wooden bridge was still standing on the rug. The boat was waiting beneath it, just where Moss and Hazel had left it.

But the river was gone.

Moss walked around the bridge. He looked by the bookshelf. He looked beside the sofa.

There, under one corner of the sofa, was something blue.

Moss knelt down and pulled very gently.

Out came a soft blue corner.

And on that corner was a little yellow moon.

Moss had found his blanket.

He remembered now. When it was time to tidy up, he had rolled the river away from the blocks so no one would trip on it. The rolled blanket had slipped partly beneath the sofa.

Mama took one end. Moss took the other.

Together they carried the blue river back to his bedroom.

The boat stayed on its shelf, ready for another journey tomorrow.

Mama shook out the blanket once. It opened in the air like a small, soft sail.

Then she spread it across the bed.

Moss climbed in. He pulled the edge up to his chin and found the little yellow moon with his paw.

Now his pillow was there.

His stuffed mouse was there.

And his blanket was exactly where it belonged.

Mama tucked the last corner around his feet.

The house was quiet. The bridge could wait until morning. The boat could wait too.

Moss rested his nose against the yellow moon.

And the little blue river lay still.

## Author's companion — not spoken

**Experiment:** A gentle mystery with one missing familiar object, three unsuccessful searches, a remembered afternoon activity, and a clear physical resolution. The adult is present and helpful, but Moss makes the connection himself. No voice acting is needed: reported speech and scene narration carry the dialogue.

**Single-voice note:** The three bedtime preferences, the repeated searches, and the return to the pillow and stuffed mouse provide structure without character voice changes. Let the final two sentences stand on their own; avoid adding a moral or a second closing speech.

**Originality:** Newly written fiction; no external literary text or factual source is adapted.
