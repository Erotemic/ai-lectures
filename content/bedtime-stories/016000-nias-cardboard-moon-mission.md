# 016000 — Lisa's Cardboard Moon Mission

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A pretend journey ends exactly where bedtime began.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Lisa's Cardboard Moon Mission

Lisa found a cardboard box beside the hallway cupboard.

It was a good box.

It had four sides, two flaps, and room for one small person sitting with her knees tucked in.

Lisa climbed inside.

She looked to the left.

She looked to the right.

She looked up through the open top.

The box was still a box.

But perhaps, Lisa thought, it could also be a moon rocket.

Grandma was folding towels nearby.

She looked into the box and asked where Captain Lisa was going.

"To the moon," said Lisa.

Grandma said that sounded like a very far journey for a box that had just come from the cupboard.

Lisa considered this.

Maybe the rocket would need supplies.

She brought a little pillow for her seat.

She brought Teddy, who was excellent at sitting quietly.

And she brought one clean blue sock.

Grandma wondered why a moon rocket would need a sock.

Lisa explained that the moon might like a present.

The moon did look a little lonely in the picture above Lisa's bed.

Grandma folded a dish towel into a flag. Lisa put it beside Teddy.

Then Grandma sat on the rug and became the person at Mission Control.

"Ready, Captain?" she asked.

Lisa nodded.

Grandma counted very softly.

Three.

Two.

One.

And the cardboard rocket went absolutely nowhere.

Lisa giggled.

She rocked the box a tiny bit, keeping it flat on the floor.

Now it felt like a rocket.

Out the window, a cloud floated past the real moon.

Inside the room, Captain Lisa was making a pretend trip to it.

She passed the Pillow Mountains.

They were really two cushions by the sofa.

She passed the Towel Nebula.

That was Grandma's folded laundry.

Then she spotted a very strange planet.

It was round, brown, and had two ears.

Teddy.

Somehow Teddy had become a planet.

Lisa gave him a careful pat.

The moon was next.

Grandma put a round white cushion on the rug beside the box.

It was the moon's landing place.

Lisa stepped out of the rocket onto the ordinary carpet.

In her imagination, the carpet had become soft silver moon dust.

She took out the blue sock.

It was a fine present, but the white cushion had no feet.

Lisa tried the sock on one corner of the cushion.

It slid right off.

She tried it on the other corner.

Off it came again.

Lisa sat and thought.

Maybe the moon didn't need a sock to wear.

Maybe it needed a sock to keep something in.

So she tucked the dish-towel flag into the sock and laid it on the cushion.

A tiny moon parcel.

Grandma told her that was a very thoughtful delivery.

Lisa waved at the pretend moon and climbed back into the box.

The return journey was much shorter.

Past Teddy Planet.

Past the Towel Nebula.

Past the Pillow Mountains.

And home to the hallway rug.

Mission Control helped Captain Lisa stand up.

Grandma carried the box to its place beside the cupboard.

Lisa carried Teddy and the little pillow into her bedroom.

Through the window, the real moon shone above the rooftops.

It was much farther away than a cushion on a rug.

Lisa knew that.

But she liked her pretend visit all the same.

Grandma tucked the blanket around her.

Teddy rested beside her shoulder.

And Captain Lisa closed her eyes after a very long journey that had taken place without ever leaving home.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** This is a playful pretend journey, not an astronomy lesson. The story clearly distinguishes a real moon outside from an imaginary moon made of a cushion. The humor comes from a rocket that doesn't move and a very important sock.

**An optional conversation, not a bedtime assignment:** "What would you pack in a pretend rocket?" A child can also simply enjoy Teddy becoming a planet.

**Sources and limits:** [Harvard Center on the Developing Child, *Executive Function Activities for 3- to 5-year-olds*, p. 6](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf) discusses imaginary play, props, and shared storytelling. [ZERO TO THREE, *Developing Thinking Skills From 24 to 36 Months*](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/developing-thinking-skills-from-24-to-36-months/) describes familiar objects becoming pretend objects during play. These are contextual sources for an editorial choice, **not evidence that this particular rocket story improves executive function**.

**Single-voice note:** Keep the countdown gentle. The story must make sense with no rocket sound effects or different voice for Mission Control.
