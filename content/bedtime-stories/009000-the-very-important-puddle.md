# 009000 — The Very Important Puddle

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** One ordinary thing matters differently to different friends.

# The Very Important Puddle

After a little morning rain, a puddle appeared on the path beside the orchard.

It wasn't a very big puddle.

It wasn't a very deep puddle.

But it was very important.

At least, Pip the mouse thought so.

Pip had brought two tiny boats made from leaves.

One boat was yellow and one was green.

The puddle was just big enough for both of them.

Pip put the yellow boat in first.

It floated quietly.

Then Pip put in the green boat.

The two boats bumped noses and drifted apart.

Pip liked the puddle very much.

Soon, Moss the rabbit came along the path, carrying a small paper bag of carrots.

Moss liked carrots.

He liked dry paws, too.

And the puddle was right where he usually walked.

Moss stopped at the edge.

He looked at the water.

He looked at Pip's boats.

Then he looked at his own clean paws.

The puddle did not look quite as wonderful to Moss as it did to Pip.

Pip wanted the boats to keep sailing.

Moss wanted to get home without walking through the water.

Neither wish made the other one disappear.

Before they could decide what to do, Hazel the hedgehog came down the orchard path.

Hazel looked into the puddle.

She saw a cloud.

A small white cloud was floating across the water, although the real cloud was high in the sky.

Hazel moved a little to one side.

The cloud moved in the puddle, too.

Hazel liked the reflection.

Pip liked the boats.

Moss liked the dry path.

Three friends.

One puddle.

Three different things to notice.

Moss stepped back and looked around.

There was a strip of dry grass beside the path. It curled around the old apple tree and came back to the path on the other side of the puddle.

Moss could walk that way.

It would take a few extra steps, but his paws would stay dry.

Pip could keep the boats in the puddle.

Hazel could watch the cloud.

Moss tried the grassy way.

One step.

Two steps.

Three steps around the tree.

He came out on the other side of the puddle without a drop on his paws.

He turned around and looked back at his friends.

Pip's yellow boat was sailing toward the middle. The green boat had come to rest beside a blade of grass.

Hazel was watching the sky in the water.

Moss put down the carrot bag for a moment and came back along the grassy path.

He could look at the puddle without stepping in it.

From where he stood, he saw the same cloud Hazel had found.

The cloud looked small enough to fit inside the yellow leaf boat.

Pip liked that idea.

He moved the little yellow boat until the cloud seemed to sit inside it.

For one quiet moment, the cloud sailed across the puddle.

Then a breeze touched the surface, and the reflection wobbled away.

The friends laughed softly.

The puddle was still useful to Pip.

It was still lovely to Hazel.

And the dry grass was still there for Moss.

When the afternoon grew cool, Pip picked up the leaf boats. Hazel waved goodbye and carried her basket home.

Moss collected his carrots and took the grassy way around the tree one last time.

By evening, the puddle had become smaller.

Its water was still enough to hold a pale patch of sky.

The yellow and green boats were resting on Pip's windowsill.

Moss's paws were clean beside his bed.

And in the orchard, the very important puddle lay quiet beneath the apple tree.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What the story offers:** The puddle isn't labeled good or bad. It offers play, a reflection, and an obstacle, depending on whose experience matters. The friends don't force themselves to agree; an available dry path allows different wishes to coexist. A three-year-old need not articulate philosophical perspective-taking for the story to work.

**Optional conversation:** “Why did Pip want to keep the puddle?” and “Why did Moss walk around?” Two people can prefer different activities without anyone being unkind. A child may enjoy seeing their own reflection in a shallow puddle with adult supervision.

**Developmental reading and rationale:**

- [ZERO TO THREE, *Developing Thinking Skills From 24 to 36 Months* (2016)](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/parenting-resource-developing-thinking-skills-from-24-36-months/) discusses emerging appreciation that people can think and feel differently.
- [American Academy of Pediatrics, *Social Development in Preschoolers: Learning How to Share & Cooperate* (updated 2024)](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/Pages/Social-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx) describes developing social cooperation and the role of guidance in simple disputes.
- [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years* (2026)](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) recommends following children's interests in play, asking what they notice, and offering choices. This story's different uses of the puddle are an editorial illustration, not a CDC intervention.

**Narration:** The repeated “three friends, one puddle” structure is meant to be easy to follow with one narrator. No competing character voices. Keep the final scene gentle.

**Evidence boundary:** No cited source demonstrates that the story increases empathy or social reasoning. The goal is to create an inviting occasion for reflection and conversation.
