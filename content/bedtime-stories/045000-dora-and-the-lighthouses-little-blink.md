# Dora and the Lighthouse's Little Blink

Dora was a young seal who lived beside a sheltered cove.

In the daytime she liked to watch little boats cross the water. She liked the sharp cries of the sea birds and the way the waves lifted a piece of seaweed, then set it down again.

But her favorite time was evening, when her mother settled with her on the smooth sand above the water.

One evening the sun slipped below the far rocks. The sea turned from blue to dark silver.

Dora noticed a light.

Far away, on the point of land beyond the cove, a lighthouse blinked.

It was a tall white tower. Near its top was a small bright light.

Dora watched it shine.

Then the light went dark.

Dora lifted her head.

“Where did it go?” she asked.

“Watch a little longer,” said Mother.

Dora watched.

The sea moved slowly at the edge of the sand.

A gull settled on the far rocks.

The sky grew a shade darker.

Then the lighthouse shone again.

“There!” said Dora.

The light stayed bright for a moment, then disappeared once more.

Dora waited.

She counted the little waves that came close to her flippers.

One wave.

A second wave.

A third wave.

Blink!

The light had returned.

“It's playing hide-and-seek,” Dora decided.

Mother looked across the water.

“It's a lighthouse light,” she said. “It keeps flashing so boats can see the shore.”

Dora thought about that.

A little boat was moving far out in the darkening water. It had one square yellow window. The boat did not come into the cove. It stayed well away from the rocks.

Dora could see the lighthouse beyond it.

Bright.

Dark.

Bright again.

“The boat can see it too,” Dora said.

“Yes,” said Mother.

Dora tried blinking at the tower herself.

She closed her eyes tightly.

She opened them.

The lighthouse was dark.

She blinked again.

Still dark.

She tried a very long blink, closing her eyes while she counted one, two, three.

When she opened them, the tower was bright.

Dora gave a delighted little wiggle.

“I blinked at the same time!”

Mother watched her for a while as Dora tried again.

Sometimes the lighthouse shone when Dora's eyes were open.

Sometimes it shone while her eyes were shut, and she missed it entirely.

That did not seem to bother the lighthouse.

It kept its own steady pattern.

The little boat moved past the point and out of sight.

Dora nestled closer to Mother.

The light was there again, exactly when Dora had hoped to see it.

She did not blink back this time.

She simply watched.

Far across the dark water, the tower stood in the same place. It would still be there after Dora fell asleep.

The tide touched the lowest stones and slipped away.

A few stars appeared above the point.

Mother rested her head on the sand.

Dora rested hers beside it.

She saw one last flash beyond the cove.

Then she closed her eyes.

The sea breathed in, and the sea breathed out.

Dora no longer needed to count the blinks.

The lighthouse could look after its light.

And Dora could sleep.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** This story follows one gentle visual pattern at dusk. The lighthouse and visiting boat belong to a plausible coastal scene; the imagined greeting between Dora and the lighthouse is framed as her game, not literal communication.

**Optional prompt:** “What did Dora see each time the lighthouse blinked?” The child may also simply listen; nothing needs to be answered.

**Sources and limits:** [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx) and [National Association for the Education of Young Children, *Rocking and Rolling: Fostering Curiosity in Infants and Toddlers*](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/dec2020/fostering-curiosity-infants) provide background on shared reading, play, or observing change. These are parent-facing references about reading and observation, not sources for the original plot. No benefit from the specific story has been experimentally evaluated; invented scenes are fiction.

**Single-voice note:** Keep any character speech in the same narrator register. The narrated story needs no music, sound effects, or separate acting voices.
