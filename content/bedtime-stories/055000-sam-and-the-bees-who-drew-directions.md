# Sam and the Bees Who Drew Directions

Sam the mouse knew how to give directions.

If someone wanted to find his house, he said, “Go past the watering can, turn at the flowerpot, and look for the little blue door.”

If someone wanted to find his biscuits, he said, “Top shelf. Brown tin. Please leave me one.”

He was quite proud of this.

Then one afternoon Sam and Mum visited the garden nature center, where the bees had a house behind glass.

It was called an observation hive.

The glass let visitors watch the bees without opening their home.

Sam stood on a little step and peered in.

There were hundreds of bees moving over the honeycomb.

Some carried yellow pollen on their back legs. Others moved their heads into little hexagonal rooms. A few stood still for a moment before walking again.

Sam watched a bee come in from outside through a narrow passage.

It moved past one bee, then another, and began to do something very strange.

It walked forward, shaking its little body.

Then it turned and made a loop.

Then it walked forward, shaking again.

Then it made another loop.

Sam's whiskers twitched.

“That bee is dancing!”

A guide from the nature center came to stand beside them.

“Yes,” the guide said. “Watch the middle part.”

The bee wiggled along a short straight line, circled around, and did it again.

Sam watched carefully.

“Is there a party?” he asked.

“Not exactly. She's been out finding flowers.”

Outside the window were bright purple flowers beside a fence and yellow flowers near the gate. Bees flew between them and the hive.

“She's giving the other bees directions,” the guide said. “A bee can tell her neighbors where food is by the way she dances.”

Sam pressed his paws against the wooden rail below the glass.

“Without saying ‘past the watering can’?”

“Without saying a single word.”

Sam watched the bee do another wiggle and loop.

The guide explained that the direction of the wiggle and how long it lasted could help the other bees find the flowers.

Sam thought that was a marvelous idea.

All the way home he practiced.

First he wiggled straight ahead.

Then he looped around Mum.

Then he wiggled again.

Mum stopped walking.

“Are you trying to tell me something?”

“Yes,” said Sam. “I'm giving directions.”

“To where?”

Sam did another wiggle.

“Did that help?”

“I'm afraid not.”

Sam tried a bigger loop. He tried a smaller loop. He tried a very serious wiggle with his ears held flat.

Mum still could not guess.

At home Sam fetched his wooden train and hid it behind the curtain, where Mum could not see it.

“Now,” he said, “follow my dance.”

He wiggled across the rug, turned around the chair, and made a loop beside the table.

Mum followed him politely.

He made another loop.

Mum looked under the table.

“No train here.”

Sam tried one last wiggle, so vigorous that he nearly stepped on his own tail.

Mum laughed.

“Perhaps mouse directions need a pointing finger,” Mum said.

Sam pointed at the curtain.

Mum pulled it aside and found the train at once.

“There!” she said. “Very clear.”

Sam sat down with his train.

Bees could dance directions. Mice, it seemed, were very good at pointing.

He thought about all those bees inside the quiet hive, moving over the honeycomb while others flew among the flowers.

That night, when Mum tucked him into bed, she asked which way the kitchen was.

Sam lifted one paw and pointed through the bedroom door.

Then he gave a very small wiggle under the blanket, just for himself.

Mum smiled and switched off the light.

The house grew still. Sam's whiskers relaxed, and his little dance was finished.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** The bee dance creates an observable mystery and a comic comparison with how Sam gives directions. The activity takes place at an enclosed educational observation hive.

**Optional prompt:** “What did Sam do when he wanted to show Mum where his toy was?” A child may simply listen without answering.

**Background sources:** [North Carolina State University Extension, *The Honey Bee Dance Language*](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/honey-bee-dance-language); [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx). The first supports a specific science detail; the second supports only optional shared reading or conversation. Neither source evaluates this story or promises a bedtime or developmental effect.

**Narrator note:** Single calm narrator; no voice acting, music, sound effects or timed length requirement.
