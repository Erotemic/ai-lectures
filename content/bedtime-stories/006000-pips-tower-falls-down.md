# 006000 — Pip's Tower Falls Down

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A small disappointment leads to rest, a new plan, and something different from the first idea.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Pip's Tower Falls Down

Pip the mouse had a box of wooden blocks.

There were square blocks, long blocks, and one little triangle block that looked like a piece of cheese.

Pip liked the triangle best, even though it wasn't cheese at all.

One rainy afternoon, Pip decided to build a tower.

Not a tiny tower.

Not a medium tower.

A tower as tall as the window ledge.

Pip put down one square block.

Then another.

Then a long block, turned sideways.

Then another square block.

The tower grew.

It reached Pip's whiskers.

It reached Pip's ears.

It leaned a little to one side.

Pip put the cheese-shaped triangle on top.

The tower wobbled.

And then it fell.

Clack, clack, clack.

Blocks rolled across the rug.

The triangle slid all the way beneath a chair.

Pip sat very still.

He had worked hard on that tower.

He had imagined looking up at it from the floor.

Now there was no tower at all.

Pip felt cross, and a little sad.

He pushed one block away with his paw.

It rolled into the leg of the table and stopped.

Outside, the rain made a soft patter on the window.

Pip's grown-up came into the room with a small cup of water.

Pip told her that the tower had fallen.

She sat on the rug beside him. She didn't begin stacking the blocks for him. She just looked at the fallen pieces.

That was a lot of work to lose, she said.

Pip nodded.

For a while, he didn't want to build anything.

So they sat together and watched the rain make little paths on the glass.

One drop went straight down.

One drop bumped into another, grew bigger, and slid along the window.

When Pip felt ready, he found the triangle block under the chair.

He tried the tower again.

This time he put two square blocks at the bottom and a long block across them.

But the long block stuck out too far.

As Pip added the next piece, it tipped, and the tower fell again.

Clack, clack.

Pip took a slow breath.

This tower was harder than he had imagined.

He looked at the blocks.

He looked at the window ledge.

Then he looked at the little wooden mouse in his toy basket.

The wooden mouse didn't need a tall tower.

Perhaps, Pip thought, it needed a house.

Pip pushed two square blocks apart. He set the long block across them, like a roof.

The wooden mouse fit underneath.

There was room for a leaf blanket.

And a little pebble beside the door.

But the roof slid sideways when Pip touched it.

Pip asked his grown-up to hold one block steady while he tried again.

She held it. Pip placed the roof.

Then he added another square block to make the wall wide enough.

This time the roof stayed put.

It wasn't a tower as tall as the window ledge.

It was a little house, with a door big enough for one wooden mouse and a roof strong enough for one cheese-shaped triangle.

Pip put the triangle on top.

It stayed there.

He smiled.

The rain slowed. The room grew golden with lamplight.

Pip carried the wooden mouse into its new house and tucked the leaf blanket around it.

He left the extra blocks in their box. There might be a tall tower another day.

For tonight, the little house was just right.

Pip went to wash his paws.

When he returned, the roof was still standing.

The little wooden mouse was quiet beneath it.

And Pip, who had done quite enough building for one day, climbed into his own bed.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**What the story offers:** Pip is allowed to feel disappointment. The grown-up doesn't take over or insist on persistence. Pip rests, tests another arrangement, asks for a small amount of help, and ultimately chooses a *different* satisfying design. The aim is flexible problem-solving, not “never give up” or “success is guaranteed if you try hard enough.”

**Optional conversation:** “What else could Pip build with those blocks?” A child who is upset about a real failed tower may need comfort before discussing solutions.

**Developmental reading and rationale:**

- [ZERO TO THREE, *Parenting Strategies for a Child Who is Easily Frustrated* (2010)](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/parenting-strategies-for-a-child-who-is-easily-frustrated/) recommends acknowledging difficult feelings, offering a break, and coaching manageable attempts without taking the task away.
- [Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, *Executive Function Activities for 3- to 5-year-olds* (2014)](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Executive-Function-Activities-for-3-to-5-year-olds.pdf) discusses imaginative play, adapting a plan when it doesn't work, and adjusting adult support to the child's readiness.
- [NAEYC, *Rocking and Rolling: Fostering Curiosity in Infants and Toddlers* (2020)](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/dec2020/fostering-curiosity-infants) offers the block-tower example as an opportunity to observe, ask questions, and experiment.

**Narration:** No voice changes or dramatic crash effects. “Clack, clack” is ordinary narration and need not be loudly performed. Let the pause by the rainy window remain unhurried.

**Evidence boundary:** The sources discuss supportive strategies and opportunities for practice, not verified effects of hearing this story.
