# 051000 — Mona and the Butterfly That Was Not There

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator bedtime fiction. **Story shape:** butterfly transformation over days.

# Mona and the Butterfly That Was Not There

Mona was a little dragon who liked flowers, warm stones, and afternoon naps.

She also liked to know where things had gone.

If a ball rolled under the hedge, Mona wanted to know where it came out.

If a cloud passed behind the hill, she watched for it on the other side.

And one morning, beside the garden path, she met a caterpillar.

It was striped yellow, black, and white. It was sitting on a milkweed leaf, eating.

Nibble, nibble.

Mona crouched beside the plant.

“Hello,” she said.

The caterpillar did not look up. It ate another small piece of leaf.

“You're very busy,” Mona said.

She sat down to watch.

The caterpillar made its way to the edge of the leaf, tested it with its front feet, then turned and found another tasty spot.

Mona watched for so long that the sun moved across the path.

The next morning she came back.

The caterpillar was on another leaf, still eating.

The morning after that, it was farther up the plant.

Mona began to look forward to seeing her striped little neighbor.

One day, though, the leaf was empty.

Mona looked underneath it.

She looked on the stem. She looked on the next leaf and the next.

No caterpillar.

“Oh,” said Mona. “Where have you gone?”

She searched the milkweed plant once more, then sat on the path to think.

Her mother came into the garden carrying a watering can.

“What's the matter?” she asked.

“My caterpillar is missing.”

Her mother looked carefully around the plant and along the old wooden post beside it.

“Look here,” she said softly.

On the wooden post hung a little green chrysalis, still and closed.

It was about the size of the tip of Mona's thumb. There were a few tiny gold-colored marks near its top.

“Is that a seed?” Mona asked.

“No. It's the caterpillar, changing. The next part of its life is happening inside.”

Mona leaned close, but not close enough to touch it.

“Does it have its stripes in there?”

“We'll have to wait and see what comes out.”

So Mona waited.

Not all at once. She still played in the garden and helped carry small stones for a flower border. She ate breakfast and supper and went to sleep every night.

But each morning she visited the post.

On one morning the chrysalis was green.

On another morning it was still green.

On a third morning, Mona found a ladybug on the wooden rail beside it.

“Excuse me,” she told the ladybug, “I'm looking after a very quiet thing.”

The ladybug flew away.

A few more days passed.

Then one morning the little case looked darker, and Mona could see colors through its sides.

She hurried to tell her mother.

When they came back later, the case had opened.

Mona gasped.

The caterpillar was not there.

The green case was empty.

But just below it, clinging to the post, was a butterfly.

Its wings were orange and black, folded and soft. It looked as though it had only just finished making them.

Mona sat on the ground.

“Is that really the same one?”

“The very same,” said her mother.

The butterfly held on to the post while its soft wings slowly opened and dried.

Mona watched them spread wider.

She did not ask the butterfly to hurry. She had already waited many mornings; she could wait a little longer.

At last the wings moved once, then again.

The butterfly lifted from the post and passed above the milkweed leaves.

Mona stood up.

It flew over the garden path, past the watering can, and into the sunlight.

“Well,” Mona said, “you certainly went somewhere.”

She watched until the orange wings became a small moving mark above the flowers.

That night Mona's mother tucked her into bed.

“Did you find your caterpillar?” she asked.

“I did,” Mona said. “But it didn't look like I thought it would.”

She pictured the striped caterpillar, the small closed chrysalis, and the butterfly opening its wings.

Outside, the garden was quiet. The empty green case still hung on the post.

And somewhere beyond the flowers, the butterfly had found a place to rest.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** The story lets meaningful time pass and ends with an observed transformation rather than explaining it as a moral about change or growing up.

**Optional prompt:** “What was on the garden post before the butterfly came out?” It is fine if the child simply listens.

**Background sources:** [Florida Museum of Natural History, University of Florida, *Butterfly Life Cycle - For Educators*](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/educators/resource/butterfly-life-cycle/); [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx). The first supports a natural phenomenon; the second supports optional shared noticing or discussion. Neither evaluates the original plot or predicts a bedtime or developmental outcome.

**Narrator note:** Keep all dialogue in one clear narrator register; no character voice acting, music, sound effects, or timed narration target is required.
