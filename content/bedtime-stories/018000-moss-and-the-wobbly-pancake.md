# 018000 — Danny and the Wobbly Pancake

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A lopsided supper becomes a game of shapes.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Danny and the Wobbly Pancake

Danny the rabbit was helping Aunt Sue make pancakes.

Aunt Sue had a mixing bowl.

Danny had a wooden spoon.

And the kitchen smelled like something good was about to happen.

Danny stirred the batter once.

He stirred it twice.

He stirred it until a little bubble popped on the surface.

Pop.

Danny smiled.

Aunt Sue set the bowl beside the stove. She would do the part with the hot pan.

Danny could choose what shape each pancake would be.

That sounded easy.

A pancake was round.

Everybody knew that.

Aunt Sue poured the first spoonful into the pan.

It spread to the left.

It spread to the right.

Then one little bit of batter wandered toward the handle.

The pancake was not round at all.

It was a circle with a long, wiggly nose.

Danny stared.

"An elephant," he decided.

Aunt Sue looked again.

Perhaps it was an elephant.

Perhaps it was a pancake that had changed its mind halfway through becoming round.

Either way, it smelled delicious.

Aunt Sue lifted it onto a plate.

Danny watched as the next pancake began to cook.

This time the batter made a long shape with a bend at one end.

"A boot," said Danny.

But not a boot that anyone in the family could wear.

A very soft boot.

A breakfast boot.

Danny giggled so much that his ears shook.

The third pancake was nearly round.

Nearly.

It had one little point sticking out.

Danny held his paws behind his back and studied it like a great pancake expert.

Maybe it was a moon.

A moon wearing a tiny party hat.

Aunt Sue put the moon beside the elephant and the boot.

Now the plate looked like a very strange picture book.

Danny had wanted to make one perfectly round pancake.

He still did.

He asked if they could try again.

Aunt Sue said there was enough batter for one more.

She showed Danny how she poured slowly into the middle of the pan.

The batter spread into a small, almost-perfect circle.

Danny watched it carefully.

No nose.

No heel.

No party hat.

Then a tiny bump appeared along the edge.

It was the smallest bump Danny had ever seen on a pancake.

Danny let out a very small sigh.

Aunt Sue set the pancake on the plate.

She waited while he looked at it.

Then Danny leaned closer.

The tiny bump made the pancake look like a turtle.

A round little turtle with its nose poking out.

Danny liked turtles.

He liked them very much.

He looked at the elephant and the boot and the party-hat moon.

Then he looked at the turtle.

"Can I have the turtle?" he asked.

Aunt Sue put it on his plate.

They ate at the kitchen table while the last light of the day turned the window pink.

The elephant was surprisingly tasty.

So was the boot.

The moon disappeared in three bites.

And the turtle, despite being very nearly a circle, tasted exactly like a pancake.

Later, when Danny was tucked into bed, he imagined an entire breakfast parade.

An elephant.

A boot.

A moon with a party hat.

And a very round turtle, bringing up the rear.

It was a lovely parade.

Even if none of the pancakes knew how to march.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** The plot is mostly a food-and-shape joke. Danny briefly wants a perfect circle and is disappointed, but the story doesn't require him to announce a lesson about grit or positivity. An adult takes responsibility for the hot pan; Danny has a meaningful, age-appropriate role in stirring and choosing shapes.

**Optional prompt:** "Which pancake would you pick?" A child's preference can be completely different from Danny's.

**Sources and limits:** [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years*, “More Parenting Tips and Activities”](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) explicitly suggests letting children help with simple meal-preparation tasks such as stirring while adults handle unsafe steps. [ZERO TO THREE, *Parenting Strategies for a Child Who is Easily Frustrated*](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/parenting-strategies-for-a-child-who-is-easily-frustrated/) discusses acknowledging frustration and offering manageable support, including humor. These sources give **general caregiver guidance**, not experimental evidence for pancake-shaped fiction.

**Single-voice note:** The repeated guesses can be read with a small smile, without distinct character voices or elaborate food sound effects.
