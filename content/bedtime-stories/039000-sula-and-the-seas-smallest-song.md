# 039000 — Dora and the Sea's Smallest Song

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator coastal bedtime story. **Story shape:** A young seal notices several kinds of water sounds and finds a quieter place to rest.

# Dora and the Sea's Smallest Song

At the edge of a wide blue sea lived a young seal named Dora.

Dora liked to listen.

She listened to waves folding onto the sand.

She listened to little bubbles popping around the rocks.

And she listened to the sound her mother made when she slipped into the water.

Splash.

That was a very good sound.

One afternoon, Dora and her mother rested on a warm, flat rock near the shore.

Dora's whiskers twitched.

“The sea is singing,” she said.

Her mother looked toward the water.

“Which song do you hear?”

Dora listened to the open beach.

A wave rolled in.

Shhhhh.

Then another wave rolled in.

Shhhhh.

The waves made a long, sweeping sound, like a blanket being pulled gently across a bed.

“That is a big song,” said Dora.

Her mother nodded.

A little later they moved, together, along the edge of the beach to a cluster of round stones.

The sea sounded different there.

Water slipped between the stones and made soft, bright little noises.

Plip.

Plop.

Plip.

Dora listened carefully.

“That one has little pieces,” she said.

Her mother smiled.

Dora tried to copy the sound with her mouth.

“Plip. Plop. Ploop.”

The last sound was her own invention.

She made it again because it was funny.

“Ploop.”

A wave came in and covered the bottoms of the stones.

When it moved away, one small pool remained, sparkling in the sun.

Dora leaned over the pool, staying close to her mother.

She could see her whiskers reflected in the still water.

She moved her head a little to one side.

The whiskers in the pool moved too.

She gave them a very important nod.

They nodded back.

Then the breeze made a small ripple, and the reflected whiskers wiggled into a dozen tiny lines.

Dora laughed a soft seal laugh.

As evening drew near, the two seals made their way toward a sheltered little cove.

Here the water reached the sand in small, gentle waves.

There was room to stretch on the dry shore, with the sea close enough to hear.

Dora lay down beside her mother.

She listened for the big shushing sound of the open beach.

It was far away now.

She listened for the bright plip-plop of water among stones.

She could hear a little of that too, very faintly.

But nearest of all was the cove's own small sound.

Hush.

Hush.

Hush.

Dora rested her chin on the sand.

A gull crossed the sky and disappeared beyond the rocks.

The light on the sea turned from bright blue to pale silver.

Her mother settled beside her, warm and close.

Dora thought about the waves on the long beach.

She thought about her funny new word, ploop.

She thought about the whiskers that had wiggled in the little pool.

Then she stopped thinking about much of anything.

The sea kept moving, just as it had all afternoon.

The cove remained gentle.

And Dora listened to its smallest song until she was ready to sleep.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** This story offers a slow progression from a large sound to several smaller sounds, ending in a quiet place beside a caregiver. Its invented seal characters and exact sea sounds are literary devices, not an account of animal communication. There is no moral or sleep-effect claim.

**Optional prompt:** “Which sound did Dora think was funny?” A child might enjoy saying “ploop,” or may simply listen to the final hush and rest.

**Sources and limits:** [AAP, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx) includes following the child's comments and enjoying repeated phrases during shared stories. [NAEYC, *Rocking and Rolling: Fostering Curiosity in Infants and Toddlers*](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/dec2020/fostering-curiosity-infants) describes noticing and exploring everyday phenomena. These are background guides, not experimental evidence that this particular story improves attention, sleep, or emotional regulation. No biological claim about how seals sleep is intended.

**Single-voice note:** Read “Shhhhh,” “Plip,” and “Hush” gently as words in a story, not as loud sound effects. The ending should settle at the narrator's ordinary relaxed pace.
