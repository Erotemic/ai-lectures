# Lisa and the Planet with Ears

One evening Grandpa carried a long black tube into the garden.

He set it on three legs and turned it toward the sky.

Lisa came out in her slippers, holding a small blanket around her shoulders.

“What is it?” she asked.

“A telescope,” said Grandpa. “It helps us see things that are very far away.”

Lisa looked at the tube.

“Farther than the shed?”

“Much farther.”

“Farther than the hill?”

“Much, much farther.”

Lisa looked toward the dark hill beyond the garden. She couldn't imagine seeing anything farther than that.

Grandpa pointed toward a bright little dot above the trees.

“Do you see that?”

“Yes. A star.”

“It looks like a star from here. But that one is a planet. It's called Saturn.”

Lisa frowned a little.

“A planet?”

“A whole big world, moving around the Sun, just as Earth does.”

Lisa looked down at the grass under her slippers.

“This Earth?”

“This one.”

She looked back up.

The bright dot was small. She could cover it with the tip of one finger.

“How can a whole world fit in my finger?”

“It doesn't,” Grandpa said. “It only looks tiny because it's so far away.”

He helped her stand on the little step beside the telescope.

“Look through this end, with one eye. I'll hold it steady.”

Lisa looked.

At first she saw a dark, blurry spot.

“It's a smudge.”

Grandpa turned a little knob.

“Try again.”

Lisa put her eye to the eyepiece.

Something bright came into view.

It was a small bright ball with a thin shape stretching out on both sides.

She stared at it.

Then she pulled back and stared at Grandpa.

“It has ears!”

Grandpa laughed quietly.

“Have another look.”

Lisa looked again. The bright ball was still there. The shapes on either side seemed to join into one thin band around it.

“It's wearing a ring,” she said. “Like a big, flat hat.”

“Those are Saturn’s rings,” Grandpa said.

Lisa held still and looked for a long time.

Grandpa told her that the rings were not one solid piece. They were made of more little pieces of ice and rock than she could possibly count.

Lisa thought of the pebbles in the garden path.

“Are they all rolling around Saturn?”

“They go around it, out in space.”

She looked again.

How could so many little pieces look like one smooth ring from this far away?

When she stepped down, Saturn was still a bright dot above the trees. No one looking from the garden gate would know that it had rings.

“Does it keep its rings when we stop looking?” Lisa asked.

“Yes,” said Grandpa.

“Even when I have my bath?”

“Even then.”

“And while I'm asleep?”

“Yes.”

They folded the telescope and carried it indoors together.

Lisa warmed her hands around a mug of milk. She drew a small circle on a sheet of paper, then a long oval around it.

She showed Grandpa.

“It's Saturn. But it looks like a cup with handles.”

“That's rather like the way some of the first people who looked through telescopes described it,” Grandpa said.

Lisa drew two more circles: a tiny one for the moon and a big one for Earth. She put a little house on the big circle.

“That is where we are,” she said.

Grandpa looked at the drawing.

“Yes. Just here.”

When Lisa went to bed, she left the paper on the chair beside her blanket.

She knew that beyond the roof, beyond the hill, and beyond anything she could see from her window, a great planet was traveling around the Sun.

She had seen its rings herself.

The room was still, and the night was quiet.

Lisa pulled her blanket close, feeling the warm bed beneath her and the great wide sky above.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** The central reveal changes a featureless bright dot into a specific distant world, connecting a familiar backyard to the scale of the solar system.

**Optional prompt:** “What did Lisa think Saturn looked like through the telescope?” It is fine if the child simply listens.

**Background sources:** [NASA Science, *Saturn: Facts*](https://science.nasa.gov/saturn/facts/); [Sandra M. Linder and Angela Eckhoff; National Association for the Education of Young Children, *Breaking Down STEAM for Young Children. Part 3: Questioning*](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/tyc/aug2020/breaking-down-steam). The first supports a natural phenomenon; the second supports optional shared noticing or discussion. Neither evaluates the original plot or predicts a bedtime or developmental outcome.

**Narrator note:** Keep all dialogue in one clear narrator register; no character voice acting, music, sound effects, or timed narration target is required.
