# Lisa and the Elevator That Went Down First

Lisa went to the big town library with Grandpa on a rainy afternoon.

The library had books in long rows, a little table for drawing, and a tall window where the rain made dark lines.

But the picture books Lisa wanted were upstairs.

She knew because Grandpa had shown her the sign.

It had a big arrow pointing up.

“Up,” said Lisa.

“Up,” agreed Grandpa.

Beside the stairs was an elevator.

It was like a little room with shining doors and buttons on the wall.

Grandpa pressed the call button. The doors opened, and they stepped inside together.

There was a mirror at the back of the elevator. Lisa saw herself in her red coat. She saw Grandpa in his brown coat. And she saw one very small piece of fluff on Grandpa's sleeve.

She picked off the fluff and showed it to him.

“Thank you,” said Grandpa.

The doors closed.

The elevator began to move.

Lisa felt the floor shift gently beneath her feet.

She waited for the picture books.

But the elevator went down instead.

“Grandpa,” said Lisa, “the books are up.”

Grandpa looked at the buttons.

The button marked B was glowing.

“Oh dear,” he said. “I pressed the wrong one.”

Lisa looked at him with enormous surprise.

“Grown-ups can press the wrong button?”

“Certainly,” said Grandpa. “I have ten fingers. Any one of them can make a mistake.”

The elevator stopped. Its doors opened.

They were in the library's lower room, where a librarian was moving a trolley stacked with returned books.

The trolley was nearly as tall as Lisa.

Some books were thin. Some were fat. One had a picture of a bright yellow bus on its cover.

“Good afternoon,” said the librarian.

“Good afternoon,” said Grandpa. “We're going upstairs.”

“Then you've come to the right elevator,” said the librarian. “Just not the right floor yet.”

Lisa giggled.

Grandpa pressed the button for the upper floor. This time he looked at it carefully before taking his finger away.

The doors closed.

The elevator began to move again.

They passed the main floor.

They kept going up.

At last the doors opened onto a room with low shelves, bright chairs, and a soft rug with big round circles on it.

The picture-book room.

Lisa stepped out slowly, as if she were not quite sure the floor would stay where it was.

But it did.

She chose a book about a frog that had lost one rain boot, and another about a dog riding in a little red tractor.

Grandpa found a chair beside the window. Lisa sat next to him, and he read both books.

The frog found his boot under a lily pad.

The dog parked his tractor at home.

Then Lisa closed the books and looked toward the elevator doors.

“Grandpa,” said Lisa, “you made the book room take the long way.”

Grandpa smiled. “I suppose I did.”

Before leaving, they put the books on a trolley to be borrowed at the desk. Lisa checked the buttons when they rode down to the main floor.

This time the elevator went exactly where they expected.

Outside, the rain had stopped.

Grandpa held Lisa's hand as they walked home, with the two library books tucked safely under his arm.

Later, after supper and a bath, Lisa put the frog book beside her bed.

She had gone down, then up, then down again.

Now she was quite ready to stay in one place for the night.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** A commonplace elevator ride makes a tiny adventure with a grown-up’s harmless mistake, a simple down/up sequence, and a complete return home. It does not require a pretend setting or a lesson about reading.

**Optional prompt:** “Where did the elevator go before it went up?” The child may also simply listen; nothing needs to be answered.

**Sources and limits:** [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx) and [American Academy of Pediatrics, *Young Children Learn a Lot When They Play*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/young-children-learn-a-lot-when-they-play.aspx) provide background on shared reading, play, or observing change. These are parent-facing references about reading and observation, not sources for the original plot. No benefit from the specific story has been experimentally evaluated; invented scenes are fiction.

**Single-voice note:** Keep any character speech in the same narrator register. The narrated story needs no music, sound effects, or separate acting voices.
