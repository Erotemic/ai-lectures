# 057000 — Lisa and the Little Smudge in the Sky

# Lisa and the Little Smudge in the Sky

Grandpa took Lisa to the hill above the village to look at the stars.

They brought a blanket, a small torch with a red light, and a pair of binoculars.

The hill was dark enough that Lisa could see many little points of light above the trees.

There were bright stars, faint stars, and one that seemed to twinkle whenever she looked straight at it.

Lisa lay on the blanket and began to count.

“One, two, three, four, five...”

Then she stopped.

There were far too many to count while she was getting sleepy.

Grandpa lay down beside her.

“Do you remember when we looked at Saturn?” he asked.

“The planet with the ring,” said Lisa. “It looked like it had ears.”

“That's right. Tonight I have something different to show you.”

He picked up the binoculars and pointed to a patch of sky near a shape of stars.

Lisa looked through them.

She saw sharp points of light and more sharp points of light. Then, near the middle, she saw a little pale blur.

“Grandpa, there's a smudge on your binoculars.”

Grandpa smiled. “Let me see.”

He looked for a moment, then handed the binoculars back.

“Try again. Look just beside the fuzzy patch.”

Lisa looked.

The fuzzy patch was still there.

She moved the binoculars a little. The whole sky moved across her view, smudge and all.

“It isn't stuck on the glass,” she said.

“No.”

“Is it a cloud?”

“No. It's much farther away than a cloud.”

Grandpa pointed to the same place in the sky. Without the binoculars Lisa could barely make out anything there at all.

“That little smudge is a galaxy,” Grandpa said. “A great gathering of stars, much farther away than the stars we usually see.”

Lisa lowered the binoculars.

“How many stars?”

“A great many. More than we could count tonight.”

She looked again.

A gathering of stars. It seemed strange that such a big thing could look like a tiny pale mark.

“Is it little?” she asked.

“No. It's very big. It's so far away that all those stars together look small from here.”

Lisa pictured a long road stretching from the village to the sea. Beyond the sea she imagined more roads, then mountains, and beyond the mountains, more sky.

But the galaxy was farther away than any road she knew.

“Does it have a name?”

“Andromeda.”

Lisa tried saying it softly.

“An-dro-me-da.”

Then she looked through the binoculars again.

Grandpa told her that the light from those distant stars had traveled for about two and a half million years before reaching the hill.

Lisa did not know how long two and a half million years felt. She knew how long it felt to wait for breakfast. She knew how long it felt to wait for her birthday.

This was something much, much longer.

“Was the light already on its way when you were little?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“When your grandpa was little?”

“Yes, long before that.”

She held the binoculars still.

“The light started traveling toward us a very, very long time ago,” Grandpa said. “And tonight it finally got here.”

They were quiet together.

A faint breeze moved through the grass. Somewhere below the hill, a dog barked once and fell silent.

Lisa looked up without the binoculars.

Most of the sky was sprinkled with little bright dots.

The galaxy was almost too faint to find. But she knew where it was now.

Grandpa helped her draw its place on a small card: a few dots for the nearby stars, and a soft pencil oval where the galaxy had looked blurry.

“That's not a very good drawing,” Lisa said.

“Why not?”

“It's only a smudge.”

Grandpa looked at the card.

“It looks like what we saw.”

Lisa considered that. Then she put one more faint pencil line around the oval.

When it was time to go home, Grandpa folded the blanket and guided her down the path with the little red torch.

Lisa held the card against her coat.

At home she set it beside her bed. The little smudge did not shine like a star. It was just pencil on paper.

But when she closed her eyes, she could remember the dark hill and the pale, faraway light.

The stars were still above the house.

And Lisa, warm under her blanket, was ready to sleep.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** The night-sky observation is intentionally a dim smudge, not a spectacular invented view. The distance is made meaningful without asking a young child to calculate it.

**Optional prompt:** “Why did the faraway galaxy look so small and fuzzy?” A child may simply listen without answering.

**Background sources:** [NASA Science / Night Sky Network, *October's Night Sky Notes: Catch Andromeda Rising!*](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/night-sky-network/catch-andromeda-rising/); [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx). The first supports a specific science detail; the second supports only optional shared reading or conversation. Neither source evaluates this story or promises a bedtime or developmental effect.

**Narrator note:** Single calm narrator; no voice acting, music, sound effects or timed length requirement.
