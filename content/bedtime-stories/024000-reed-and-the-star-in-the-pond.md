# 024000 — Mike and the Star in the Pond

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A little otter notices a star reflected in water, then wonders where it went.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Mike and the Star in the Pond

Mike the little otter liked the pond best when evening came.

During the day, the pond was busy.

There were ducks with important places to go.

There were little insects skating along the top of the water.

And there was often a breeze making wrinkles across the surface.

But in the evening, the pond grew smooth.

It looked like a wide, dark mirror.

One evening, Mike walked to the bank with Mama Otter.

The first star had appeared above the willow tree.

Mike looked up at it.

Then he looked down.

There was another star in the pond.

It was just as bright.

Mike sat down on the grass.

He looked at the sky star.

He looked at the pond star.

Two stars, he thought.

One high up and one very low.

He was still thinking about this when a long willow leaf floated onto the water.

The leaf drifted slowly over the place where the pond star had been.

And the little star disappeared.

Mike blinked.

He looked up.

The star in the sky was still there.

He looked down.

The pond star was gone.

Mama Otter sat beside him.

Mike told her that he had seen a star in the water, and now he could not find it.

Mama Otter looked out across the pond.

She noticed the willow leaf.

Perhaps, she suggested, the water was showing them a picture of the sky.

And perhaps something was hiding part of that picture.

Mike watched the leaf move.

A slow current carried it past the willow's reflection.

There, in the dark water beside the leaf, the little light appeared again.

Mike leaned forward to look.

The light wobbled.

Then it grew still.

Mike was pleased.

But now he had another question.

What would happen if the pond had a great many wrinkles?

He did not throw anything into the water.

He only waited.

A breeze slipped between the willow branches and brushed the pond.

The smooth surface rippled.

The little bright point stretched into a long, shivery sparkle.

Then it broke into several tiny sparkles.

When the breeze passed, the point came together again.

Mike watched in silence.

It was as if the pond could draw the same star in different ways.

Just then a duck paddled quietly across the far end of the water.

The duck was very sleepy and did not seem interested in the stars at all.

Its reflection followed underneath it, upside down.

Mike noticed the duck in the water.

Then the real duck above it.

The upside-down duck made him smile.

He wondered whether the duck knew it had a second duck following along.

Probably not, Mike thought.

The duck looked busy being one duck.

Mama Otter stood and stretched.

It was time to walk back along the bank.

Mike took one last look.

The first star still shone above the willow.

Its light still rested in the pond when the water was smooth enough to show it.

Mike had not found a second star to take home.

He had found a very good place to look at the first one.

The grass felt cool beneath his paws as he followed Mama Otter home.

Behind them the willow branches stopped moving.

The duck found a sheltered spot near the reeds.

The sky grew darker.

And the pond held its quiet picture of the evening sky.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** A gentle observation mystery with no wrong or foolish guess. The otter compares an object and its reflection, notices what changes when the surface moves, and ends with the pleasure of looking. The story uses a simplified image of reflection; it is **not** a scientific account of optics or a suggestion for children to approach water unsupervised.

**Optional prompt:** “Where else can you see a picture of something without drawing it?” A mirror is an easy example if a child wants to continue the conversation, but there is no lesson to recite.

**Sources and limits:** [NAEYC, Sandra M. Linder and Angela Eckhoff (2020), *Breaking Down STEAM for Young Children. Part 3: Questioning*](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/tyc/aug2020/breaking-down-steam) explicitly distinguishes questions inviting observation from questions demanding a predetermined answer. [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years* (2026), “Parenting Tips and Activities”](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) suggests conversational questions about stories. Neither source evaluates this story, and supervision near water remains the responsibility of a trusted adult.

**Single-voice note:** Don't give the star or duck a voice. Let the changes from smooth point to wobbly reflection carry the curiosity.
