# Danny and the Lights Under the Sea

Danny had a small blue submarine made of wood.

It had a yellow window painted on the side and a propeller that turned when he pushed it with his paw.

One rainy morning, he drove it across the kitchen table.

“Down we go,” he said. “Past the seaweed. Past the fish. Down to the very bottom.”

Aunt Sue came in carrying two tickets.

“Would your captain like to see what the bottom of the sea really looks like?” she asked.

Danny picked up the submarine. “Yes, please.”

The tickets were for the little maritime museum beside the harbor.

Inside the museum were old ship bells, a rope with a knot as big as Danny's head, and a large glass case containing a model boat. Danny liked the boat, but he kept looking for the sea.

“It's through here,” Aunt Sue said.

They went into a room with blue walls and a screen almost as wide as the room. A bench stood in front of it.

On the screen was a film taken by a robotic camera, far below the ocean's surface.

At first Danny could see a cloud of pale sand and a round rock. The camera floated past slowly.

“Where is the sunshine?” he asked.

“Hardly any sunlight reaches so far down,” Aunt Sue said. “It's very dark there.”

Danny looked at his wooden submarine. Its yellow window had always seemed bright enough for any journey.

Then the camera's lamp moved away from a rock, and something surprising happened.

A little blue light appeared in the black water.

Another light flickered beside it.

One tiny blue line glimmered, and then disappeared.

Danny sat so far forward on the bench that Aunt Sue put a hand beside him to stop him sliding off.

“Stars,” he whispered. “There are stars under the sea.”

Aunt Sue smiled. “They do look like stars.”

On the screen, a small floating creature drifted past. A soft blue glow shone along its edge.

“That light is coming from the creature,” said Aunt Sue.

“Not from the camera?”

“Not that one.”

Danny thought about the sun above the ocean. He thought about the museum lamps above their heads.

“But who gave it a little lamp?”

“No one. Some sea animals can make light in their own bodies.”

Some of the lights had come from living animals, far below the sunny surface.

Danny watched a blue glow move slowly across the screen.

He had never met an animal with a lamp of its own. Unless fireflies counted. He had seen those once, over the grass beside Aunt Sue's garden.

“So the ocean has fireflies?” he asked.

“They're different animals, but they make their own light, just like fireflies.”

The camera turned. For a while the screen showed nothing but dark water.

Danny waited.

A little speck blinked. Then it was gone.

He waited again.

Another glow passed, as if somebody had drawn a blue line in the dark and rubbed it away.

Aunt Sue didn't hurry him. The room was quiet except for the soft humming sound from the screen.

After the film they went to a table with paper and pencils.

Danny drew the sea.

At the top he drew a boat, a sun, and three fish with very large smiles. Far below them he covered the page in dark blue crayon.

Then he took the pale blue pencil and made five tiny marks.

Aunt Sue looked over his shoulder.

“You've left a lot of room between those lights,” she said.

Danny looked at the little blue marks, then at the big empty space he had left around them.

“It's a big ocean,” he said.

On the way home, rain tapped softly on the car window.

Danny held his wooden submarine on his lap. It was too small to carry a real camera, and the yellow window was only paint.

That was all right. It could still travel very far when Danny was imagining.

At bedtime he put the submarine on the shelf beside his drawing.

The lights on the paper did not shine. They were only blue pencil marks.

But Danny knew what they stood for.

Somewhere far below the waves, where the sun could not reach, little living lights were glowing in the dark.

He thought about them for a moment, tucked the blanket under his chin, and went to sleep.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** The story contrasts a toy submarine with real deep-ocean footage. The surprising light has a physical source, while the imaginary conversation about it stays playful.

**Optional prompt:** “What did Danny first think the little lights were?” A child may simply listen without answering.

**Background sources:** [NOAA Ocean Exploration, *What is bioluminescence?*](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/bioluminescence/); [National Association for the Education of Young Children, *Rocking and Rolling: Fostering Curiosity in Infants and Toddlers*](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/dec2020/fostering-curiosity-infants). The first supports a specific science detail; the second supports only optional shared reading or conversation. Neither source evaluates this story or promises a bedtime or developmental effect.

**Narrator note:** Single calm narrator; no voice acting, music, sound effects or timed length requirement.
