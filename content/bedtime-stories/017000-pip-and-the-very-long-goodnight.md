# 017000 — Pip and the Very Long Goodnight

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A cumulative goodnight becomes a little joke before the whole garden rests.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Pip and the Very Long Goodnight

Pip the mouse was ready for bed.

His blanket was folded.

His pillow was plumped.

His little red leaf lay on the windowsill.

Pip looked around his room.

"Goodnight, pillow," he said.

Then he noticed the blanket.

"Goodnight, blanket."

Then he noticed his slippers.

"Goodnight, slippers."

It would have been a perfectly good bedtime right there.

But Pip had an idea.

Perhaps everything in the room deserved a goodnight.

He said goodnight to the chair.

He said goodnight to the rug.

He said goodnight to the crooked little picture above his bed.

The picture showed a carrot wearing a hat.

Pip had painted it himself.

"Goodnight, carrot with a hat."

The carrot did not answer.

That was all right. It was only a painting.

Pip looked at the windowsill.

"Goodnight, red leaf."

Then he saw a pebble beside the leaf.

"Goodnight, pebble."

The pebble was a good listener.

It listened to the entire goodnight without interrupting once.

Pip climbed into bed.

Then he remembered the kitchen.

The kitchen had a table.

And a kettle.

And a spoon that was a little bit bent.

Could he go to sleep without saying goodnight to the spoon?

Pip thought not.

So he called softly from his bed.

"Goodnight, kitchen table. Goodnight, kettle. Goodnight, bent spoon."

That seemed to settle the kitchen.

Then he thought of the garden.

Oh, the garden was full of things.

He said goodnight to the apple tree.

He said goodnight to the path.

He said goodnight to the daisy patch.

He said goodnight to the stone where Otis the snail sometimes sat.

The garden was very large for such a small mouse.

What about the other stones?

What about the blades of grass?

What about every leaf on every branch of the apple tree?

Pip began to imagine them.

Goodnight, first leaf.

Goodnight, second leaf.

Goodnight, third leaf.

He did not know how many leaves there were.

He suspected there were more leaves than he could count before breakfast.

Pip pulled the blanket up to his chin.

Perhaps, he thought, the leaves wouldn't mind one goodnight together.

"Goodnight, all the leaves."

There. That sounded quite nice.

"Goodnight, all the pebbles."

"Goodnight, all the daisies."

"Goodnight, all the sleepy little creatures tucked into their homes."

The room became quiet.

Outside, the apple tree stood still for a moment.

The red leaf on the windowsill did not move at all.

Pip gave his pillow one last little pat.

Then he noticed something.

He had said goodnight to a chair, a spoon, a pebble, and a great many leaves.

But he had forgotten Pip.

He tucked his paws beneath the blanket.

"Goodnight, Pip," he whispered.

And at last there was nobody left to answer except a very sleepy mouse.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** Repetition is the joke and the structure. There's no virtue to extract: a mouse says goodnight to an impractical number of things, then remembers himself. The grouping of leaves and pebbles gives the cumulative list a natural end without insisting on a life lesson.

**Optional prompt:** "What silly thing would you say goodnight to?" It's equally fine to leave the story at the last line.

**Sources and limits:** [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years*, “More Parenting Tips and Activities”](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) offers simple rhymes, counting games, and questions about books as age-related activities. [American Academy of Pediatrics, *Young Children Learn a Lot When They Play*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/young-children-learn-a-lot-when-they-play.aspx) discusses informal play and responsive interaction. Neither evaluates this fictional goodnight sequence or implies a child should complete a counting task at bedtime.

**Single-voice note:** Give the repeated goodnights room to breathe. No whispered character voices or mechanical sound effects are needed.
