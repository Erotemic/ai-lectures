# The Beetle Bus and the Crooked Road

At the bottom of a garden, behind the tallest cabbage leaf, was a bus stop.

The sign was made from a flat pebble.

On it were painted three little pictures: a rose, a stone, and a clover.

That was the whole bus route.

The Beetle Bus was made from a smooth yellow seedpod with four round wooden wheels.

It had a little roof and a bell no bigger than a pea.

Every afternoon the driver, a brown beetle, rang the bell and called, “Bus for Rose Corner, Pebble Stop, and Clover Hill!”

One afternoon, an ant came hurrying along with a crumb of bread.

“Rose Corner, please,” the ant said.

A ladybug climbed aboard next. She was carrying a folded paper fan. The fan was nearly as big as she was.

“Pebble Stop,” she told the driver.

Last came a small caterpillar. He was going all the way to Clover Hill.

The caterpillar looked at the bus.

He looked at the smooth yellow sides.

“Is the bus for eating?” he asked.

“No,” said the beetle. “The bus is for riding.”

“Oh,” said the caterpillar. “That's good too.”

They all climbed in.

The beetle rang the bell.

Ding!

The bus rolled away from the cabbage leaf, along a path between two blades of grass.

It turned past a white pebble.

It passed a red flower.

Then it stopped.

A broad green leaf lay across the road, from one side to the other.

It had fallen from a bean plant while the wind was blowing.

The beetle climbed out to look.

He tried to move the leaf, but it was as big as the bus roof.

The ant tried to help. The caterpillar pushed from the other side. The ladybug waved her paper fan in a very businesslike way.

The leaf did not move.

“Well,” said the driver, “we need a different road.”

He took a little map from his pocket.

The usual road was a short brown line.

Around the stone was a long curly line. It went past the thyme plant, under a bent twig, and back toward the rosebush.

“That looks very crooked,” said the ladybug.

“It is,” said the driver. “It goes all the way around that stone. Hold on tight!”

Everyone climbed in again.

Ding!

Around the white pebble went the Beetle Bus.

It passed the thyme plant, where a snail was taking a slow afternoon walk.

“Good afternoon,” called the beetle.

“Good af—” began the snail.

But the bus had already rolled around the bend.

It went underneath the twig. The caterpillar ducked his head, although the roof was nowhere near him.

And finally the bus came out beside Rose Corner.

The ant got off, carrying his crumb.

“Thank you,” he said.

Ding!

The bus followed a sandy curve toward Pebble Stop. The ladybug stepped down with her great paper fan. A light breeze caught one corner of it and turned her around once.

The ladybug laughed, folded the fan, and walked home.

Ding!

Only the caterpillar remained.

“Will the bus go all the way to Clover Hill?” he asked.

“Yes,” said the driver. “Even when the road has bends in it.”

Soon three clover leaves came into view, standing high above a little patch of moss.

Clover Hill.

The caterpillar stepped off and stretched his many feet.

He thanked the driver, sniffed a clover leaf, and went to his bed beneath it.

The beetle turned the empty bus around.

By the time he reached the cabbage leaf, the sun was low and the garden was still.

He parked the Beetle Bus behind its pebble sign and hung the tiny bell inside the driver's window.

The yellow seedpod stopped rolling.

The wooden wheels stopped turning.

There would be more passengers tomorrow, and perhaps another road to travel.

But tonight, the bus had finished its work.

Even a bus with four wheels and a little brass bell needed a good long rest.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** The miniature garden road and rolling seedpod bus are fantasy. The change of route supplies a concrete, low-stakes problem and gives a listener several landmarks to recognize. This is transportation comedy, not a lesson about public transit.

**Optional prompt:** “What was blocking the bus road?” The child may also simply listen; nothing needs to be answered.

**Sources and limits:** [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org; adapted from Melissa & Doug: Our Blog, *Playful Reading Tips: 4 Ways to Engage Your Child in the Magic of Books*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/playful-reading-tips-ways-to-engage-your-child-in-the-magic-of-books.aspx) and [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx) provide background on shared reading, play, or observing change. These are parent-facing references about reading and observation, not sources for the original plot. No benefit from the specific story has been experimentally evaluated; invented scenes are fiction.

**Single-voice note:** Keep any character speech in the same narrator register. The narrated story needs no music, sound effects, or separate acting voices.
