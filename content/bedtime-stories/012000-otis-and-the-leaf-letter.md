# 012000 — Pete and the Leaf Letter

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A snail's very small journey feels very large to him.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Pete and the Leaf Letter

Pete the snail lived beneath a wide green leaf in the garden.

His home was cool when the sun was warm and dry when the rain was light.

He had a smooth stone for a doorstep.

He also had a friend called Kate, who lived near the apple tree.

One afternoon, Pete decided to send Kate a letter.

He found a fallen leaf that was soft enough to fold.

With a little bit of berry juice, he drew a small purple star on it.

It was a picture of the evening star.

He wanted Kate to come and watch the sky with him when the day was done.

Pete folded the leaf carefully.

Then he realized something.

He didn't have a postbox.

He didn't have a bicycle.

And he certainly didn't have a delivery truck.

He did know how to travel. Slowly, steadily, along the garden path.

So Pete picked up the letter, settled it against his shell, and set off.

The journey began with a hill.

It was a pebble.

But from where Pete stood, it looked very much like a hill.

He went around it.

Next came a forest.

It was three blades of grass.

They bent together overhead and made a lovely green tunnel.

Pete went through it.

Then came an enormous lake.

It was one drop of water resting in a shallow dent in the path.

Pete went around that, too.

He had now traveled a very long way.

At least, it was a long way for Pete.

A beetle passed him on the path.

The beetle was going in the other direction, carrying a yellow petal.

They stopped to admire each other's parcels.

Pete liked the petal.

The beetle liked the leaf letter.

Then they continued on their separate ways.

After a while, Pete reached the old apple tree.

Kate's little house was nearby, with a round door and a flowerpot beside it.

Pete rested on the doorstep.

He had brought his letter all the way across the garden.

Now he only had to knock.

A snail's knock is a quiet sort of knock.

Pete touched the door gently.

Kate opened it and looked down.

There was Pete, with his folded leaf.

Kate unfolded the letter.

She found the purple star.

Pete pointed toward the sky.

The sun was low, and the first pale evening colors had begun to spread above the orchard.

Kate understood. She fetched a small cushion and followed Pete back along the path.

This time, the enormous lake was only a drop of water.

The forest was still three blades of grass.

The hill was still a pebble.

Kate had to look carefully to notice them at all.

Pete showed her each one.

She was glad he did.

When they reached his home, Kate set the cushion beside the wide green leaf.

They sat together in the cooling garden.

The apple tree made a dark shape against the sky.

A bird settled on a branch.

And at last, between two wisps of cloud, one little star appeared.

Kate looked at the star.

Then she looked at Pete.

She was glad he'd sent the letter.

Pete was glad he'd brought it.

The beetle's yellow petal floated down from somewhere above and came to rest by the smooth stone doorstep.

It looked rather like another little letter.

But this one didn't need delivering.

Pete tucked his folded leaf beneath the edge of his doorway.

Kate stayed until the garden was quiet.

Then she took the path back to her house, leaving Pete beneath his big green roof.

He had seen his friend.

He had seen his star.

And now his long journey had ended in the very place he liked best.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** This is primarily a miniature adventure and a scale joke. A pebble becomes a hill, three grass blades become a forest, and a water drop becomes a lake. It offers concrete, playful perspective without assigning the child a moral about patience or persistence.

**If a child wants to talk:** “What would look enormous to a snail?” A playful answer is sufficient.

**Related reading:** [CDC, *Milestones by 3 Years* (2026), “Parenting Tips and Activities”](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) recommends asking questions about children's interests and engaging in pretend play. This is broad caregiver guidance; it does not verify the snail's fictional experience or measure the effects of this story.

**TTS note:** Use the recurring pebble/grass/drop pattern to establish the journey. Maintain a single narrator's voice; let the return trip gradually quiet the pacing.
