# 047000 — Pete and the Lights in the Grass

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator bedtime fiction. **Story shape:** firefly evening observation.

# Pete and the Lights in the Grass

Pete the snail was on his way home when he saw a light in the grass.

It was not the moon. The moon was high above the field, round and pale.

It was not a lamp. There were no houses near the field.

The light was small and yellow-green, and it shone just above the clover.

Then it went out.

Pete stopped crawling.

“Hello?” he called.

The grass rustled. No one answered.

Pete took two more slow snail steps.

The light blinked again.

“There you are,” he whispered.

Then another light blinked farther down in the grass.

One was near a white flower. Another floated past a tall stem and disappeared behind it.

Pete turned his head left. Then right. Then left again.

“I'm going to need more eyes,” he said.

Kate the hedgehog was coming along the path with a small basket. She had been gathering the last ripe berries for supper.

“Kate,” Pete called. “I found lights, but they keep going away.”

Kate put her basket down beside a flat stone.

“Let's watch them,” she said.

She sat on the stone. Pete settled beside her, and they looked out across the field.

For a moment there was nothing to see but grasses and the soft dark shapes of flowers.

Then a light blinked near the path.

Another blinked by the clover.

And a third flashed just above the stone wall.

“There are lots,” Pete said.

“There are,” Kate answered.

A tiny beetle flew past them. For one brief moment its body glowed, and then it was dark again.

“It's a firefly,” Kate whispered.

Pete stared after it.

“A fly with a lantern?”

“A beetle with its own light.”

Pete looked down at his shell. It was smooth and brown, with pale stripes around its curve.

He waited. Nothing on his shell lit up.

“I'm a snail with no lantern,” he announced.

Kate smiled. “Would you trade your shell for a lantern?”

Pete considered this. His shell was indeed good. It kept the rain off his head and went everywhere with him.

Across the field a light flashed once.

A little farther down, close to the ground, a smaller light answered.

Pete leaned forward so far that one of his feelers almost touched the grass.

“Kate. Did you see? One blinked, and another blinked back.”

“I saw.”

“Are they playing?”

“The lights help them find one another,” Kate said.

Pete sat very still. He did not want to miss the next one.

Blink.

Dark.

Blink.

Dark again.

The field seemed larger than it had in the daylight. The tall grasses became dark towers. The white flowers were like little stars close to the ground. Between them, the living lights came and went.

A firefly passed above Kate's basket. Another flew out from behind a thistle. Two more shone near the distant hedge.

For a while, Pete tried to count them.

“One. Two. Oh, that one went out. Three. Wait.”

He gave up counting and simply watched.

The air was warm. Somewhere beyond the field, a frog made a low, sleepy sound. Kate's basket smelled faintly of berries.

After a long time, the flashing grew less frequent.

Kate picked up her basket.

“It's getting late,” she said. “Shall I walk with you to your leaf?”

Pete nodded.

They went slowly along the edge of the field. Every now and then, Pete stopped for one last blink.

At last they came to the broad leaf where Pete liked to sleep.

He turned to look back.

Near the hedge, a little light flashed once more.

“Goodnight,” Pete told it.

He tucked his head beneath the leaf, curled comfortably inside his shell, and listened to Kate's soft footsteps going home.

The fireflies were still somewhere in the field.

Pete could not see them from his bed.

But he liked knowing that, every now and then, a tiny light might be blinking in the summer grass.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Editorial intention:** The story builds from one light to several, with a pattern that can be heard and pictured. It treats the living insects as visitors to observe rather than objects to catch.

**Optional prompt:** “Did the little lights stay on all the time, or did they blink?” It is fine if the child simply listens.

**Background sources:** [National Park Service, Great Smoky Mountains National Park, *Synchronous Fireflies*](https://home.nps.gov/grsm/learn/nature/fireflies.htm); [American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, *How to Share Books with Children 2 and 3 Years Old*](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/How-to-Share-Books-with-2-and-3-Year-Olds.aspx). The first supports a natural phenomenon; the second supports optional shared noticing or discussion. Neither evaluates the original plot or predicts a bedtime or developmental outcome.

**Narrator note:** Keep all dialogue in one clear narrator register; no character voice acting, music, sound effects, or timed narration target is required.
