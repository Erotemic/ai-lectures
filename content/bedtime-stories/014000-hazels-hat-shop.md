# 014000 — Hazel's Hat Shop

**Collection:** Children's Bedtime Stories. **Age range:** Ages 3–4 (primarily age three). **Form:** Original, single-narrator fiction. **Story shape:** A hat maker discovers that many things look funny in hats.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# Hazel's Hat Shop

Hazel the hedgehog had a basket of little hats.

Some were made from leaves.

Some were made from scraps of soft cloth.

One was a blue flower turned upside down.

Hazel had made them on a rainy afternoon, just because she liked hats.

Now the sun was shining, and she wanted to see how they looked.

She tried the first hat on her own head.

It was a round green leaf with a hole in the middle.

The hole was quite useful.

Several of Hazel's hedgehog prickles poked through it.

The hat stayed on.

Hazel looked into a puddle and admired the reflection.

Then she tried a red hat.

It was too small for her head.

She put it on the round top of her watering can instead.

The watering can looked like it was going to a party.

Hazel liked that.

She tried a yellow hat on a pumpkin near the garden gate.

The pumpkin was very round.

The hat was also very round.

Together, they looked round enough to roll down the path.

They didn't roll, of course.

Pumpkins in gardens are usually quite happy to stay where they are.

But Hazel laughed at the idea.

Next she put the blue flower hat on a wooden fence post.

The fence post had never worn a hat before.

It looked surprised.

Or perhaps that was only because Hazel was imagining a face where the knots in the wood were.

Moss the rabbit came by with a basket of clean washing.

He saw the watering can in its little red hat.

He saw the pumpkin in its yellow hat.

He saw the fence post in its blue flower hat.

Moss decided that the garden had become rather fashionable.

Hazel offered him a soft brown hat made from folded cloth.

Moss put it on.

The hat settled between his ears.

His ears stuck straight up, one on each side.

He turned his head.

The hat turned, too.

He bent down to look at a daisy.

The hat slipped over one eye.

Moss could now see the world through one eye and a great deal of brown cloth.

Hazel adjusted it for him.

Then Pip the mouse arrived.

Pip wanted a hat as well.

Hazel looked through the basket.

All the hats were much too big for Pip.

Even the smallest leaf hat covered his whiskers.

Pip walked three steps and bumped gently into a cushion of moss.

The friends couldn't help laughing.

Pip took off the giant hat and looked about for something smaller.

On the garden path lay an empty acorn cap.

Pip set it on his head.

It fit perfectly.

He tilted it to one side.

Then he tilted it to the other.

He looked so pleased that Hazel declared it the finest hat in the shop.

The friends took a slow walk around the garden to admire their hats.

The watering can still wore red.

The pumpkin still wore yellow.

The fence post still wore its blue flower.

Moss wore brown.

Pip wore his acorn cap.

And Hazel wore the green leaf that let her prickles poke through.

As the sun lowered, the breeze grew cool.

They gathered the hats from the watering can, the pumpkin, and the fence post.

Moss put his brown one back in the basket.

Hazel carefully stacked the others on top.

Pip kept his acorn cap for a little while longer.

He wore it all the way home.

When he climbed into bed, he took it off and placed it beside his pillow.

It looked very small there.

Outside, the garden things rested without their hats.

The watering can was a watering can again.

The pumpkin was a pumpkin.

The fence post was still a fence post.

But Pip's little acorn cap waited on the bedside table, ready for another very fashionable day.

## For parents and caregivers — not spoken

**Why this one exists:** The fun is in the visual progression: a watering can, a pumpkin, a fence post, a rabbit, and a mouse all getting increasingly funny hats. There is no moral assigned. The observation about a fence post looking surprised is explicitly imagination, not a claim about objects having feelings.

**If a child wants to talk:** “What else would look funny wearing a hat?” For real-world play, use safe, appropriately sized dress-up materials and supervise small objects around younger children.

**Related guidance:** [American Academy of Pediatrics, *Young Children Learn a Lot When They Play* (2022)](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/young-children-learn-a-lot-when-they-play.aspx) discusses imaginative play generally. This is contextual reading, not a study of the story or a claim that playing with hats improves a measurable outcome.

**TTS note:** Let the listener picture one hat at a time. Keep dialogue indirect or brief, and slow down when the hats return to the basket.
