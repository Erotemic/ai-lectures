# Build continuous load paths in real wood

## How a Wooden House Carries Load: Members, Connections, and Moisture

Stand in an unfinished room and look up. You may see joists, studs, plates, sheathing, braces, and beams. At first these can look like a repetitive pattern of pieces of lumber. The interesting question is not what each piece is called. It is what would happen if one piece disappeared. Which forces would find another route? Which parts would bend more? Which connections would become overloaded? What would keep the house standing against wind as well as gravity?

Carpentry is an exercise in controlling forces with a material that was once a tree. Structural carpentry requires understanding the path from load to ground, the direction-sensitive properties of wood, and the connections that make separate pieces act together.

### Begin at the roof, then follow the force

Snow rests on a roof. Its weight acts downward. Roof sheathing distributes that load to rafters or trusses. Those members carry forces to supporting walls, beams, or other designed supports. Walls carry loads through plates and studs into floor framing or directly into the foundation. Foundations distribute loads into soil. This is a *load path*. Every connection in that chain matters because a load does not simply vanish at the end of a board.

Now reverse your attention. Wind may exert uplift on a roof or lateral pressure on a wall. The structural system must transfer those forces through connections and bracing to suitable supports. A house that carries gravity loads is not automatically secure against uplift or sideways deformation. Metal connectors, nails, sheathing patterns, and anchorage can be essential parts of the structure even though they occupy less visible space than the lumber.

A common mistake is to assume that anything vertical is load bearing and anything horizontal is decorative. A stud partition may or may not carry major vertical load, depending on what is above and the structural layout. A beam can support loads from a broad tributary area, while a short wall section may be part of the lateral-force-resisting system. Without seeing the larger building, the appearance of an isolated stud tells us too little to authorize its removal.

### Compression, tension, shear, and bending

A post under a centered vertical load is primarily in compression. A tie that holds two members from spreading may be in tension. A connection where one part tries to slide past another experiences shear. A floor joist under people and furniture experiences bending, with one region in tension and another in compression. The exact stress distribution depends on the member's shape and restraint.

Imagine a plank supported at both ends and loaded in the middle. The plank bends. Its upper fibers shorten and its lower fibers lengthen for ordinary downward bending. This explains why cracks, knots, and notches can be more consequential in some locations than others. The damage to load capacity cannot be inferred solely from how much wood was removed; location relative to stress matters.

Depth also matters. For an ideal rectangular beam of the same material and width, bending stiffness grows roughly with the cube of its depth, through the second moment of area. Doubling depth, all else equal, gives about eight times that geometric stiffness. But it also changes weight and load capacity in different ways, and actual framing is controlled by spans, materials, lateral restraint, connections, serviceability and the applicable design rules. This relationship is an explanation for why joists stand on edge, not permission to improvise a beam design.

### Wood is strong in particular directions

Unlike an ideal homogeneous material, wood has a grain. Many mechanical properties depend on whether a force acts parallel or perpendicular to the fibers. Moisture changes the dimensions of wood differently along and across the grain. A board usually changes much more across its width than along its length as its moisture content changes. Grain orientation, knots, checks, species, grade and defects all affect performance.

Consider a wide solid-wood tabletop glued rigidly across its entire width to a fixed frame. As humidity changes, the board tries to expand and contract across the grain. If the attachment prevents that movement, the table may crack, cup, or distort. The lesson is not that wood is unreliable. It is that wood moves predictably enough for good joinery to accommodate it.

Structural lumber is not generally rated by its appearance alone. Grade stamps and recognized design values relate to permitted uses and expected material properties. Engineered products such as plywood, oriented strand board, laminated veneer lumber, and engineered joists have different layer arrangements, design assumptions, and connection requirements. Their manufacturer details are not optional decoration.

### The joint determines what gets transmitted

A board sitting loosely on another board may carry compression by bearing but resist little uplift. A nailed connection may transfer a designed combination of forces, depending on nail type, number, penetration, edge distances, load direction, and the materials. A connection stiff enough for one loading condition may be flexible or weak in another.

This is why the visual impression of "lots of screws" is not a structural argument. A wood screw designed for cabinets may have different behavior than a structural fastener tested for specific framing applications. Ordinary fastener substitutions can change withdrawal capacity, shear behavior, ductility, and corrosion resistance. The required connection is part of the structural design.

The surface beneath a fastener matters too. Screw threads need effective engagement; nails need sufficient penetration and appropriate arrangement; bolts can crush wood locally if bearing stress becomes excessive. Edge distances matter because splitting may start long before the steel fastener itself reaches its capacity. Good construction is not just choosing a strong component. It is avoiding a weak mechanism in the combined assembly.

### Geometry is a form of strength

Try pushing the top of a rectangular frame made from four loosely hinged sticks. It can deform into a parallelogram while the sticks change very little in length. Add a diagonal brace and the shape becomes much harder to distort. A triangle resists this kind of racking because changing its angles generally requires changing a side length. Braced frames and shear walls use this geometric principle in engineered forms.

On a building, structural sheathing and its fastening pattern can provide lateral resistance. The sheathing cannot act independently of its boundary members and anchorage. A piece of plywood propped against studs is not the same thing as a properly fastened shear-wall assembly. Details at corners, openings, diaphragms, and foundations determine how forces connect.

### Reading a wall before altering it

Imagine you want to enlarge a doorway. The tempting task description is "remove a few studs and install a header." But the sound questions come earlier. What loads reach this wall? Where are the joists or trusses above? Is the wall part of a lateral load path? What supports the loads while work occurs? What is below the proposed opening? Are utilities passing through it? Which code, design documents, and permits apply?

A header is not simply a large piece of timber placed above an opening. It is part of a load transfer that includes its supports, connections and the members below. The entire path to foundation must remain credible. For structural alterations, site-specific assessment and qualified design are required; naming a header size from a recording would be a poor substitute.

### Rot and deformation tell histories

A sagging floor may reflect member bending, connection slip, foundation movement, decay, or a historic alteration. A crack in drywall may be cosmetic, may reflect seasonal wood movement, or may correlate with structural deformation. Observation alone rarely determines cause. We can map crack positions, measure levels over time, inspect accessible framing and document moisture conditions before choosing an explanation.

One of the most useful habits in carpentry is to ask what each member does, what loads it sees, what connections permit, and how moisture will affect it. A structurally sensible piece of work respects both force and time. Buildings do not stop moving when the carpenter puts away the tools.

## Wood Moisture, Grain, Species, and Grades

The load path is carried by a material whose dimensions and properties depend on grain and moisture.

Take two pieces of lumber that look alike. One stays straight through a season of indoor heating. The other cups, twists, and opens a seam. The difference is not necessarily that one board was cut accurately and the other badly. Wood carries its history into the building: the way a tree grew, how the log was sawn, which fibers were included, how it was dried, and what moisture conditions it encounters after installation.

Wood is an engineered material only in the sense that we select, grade, dry and shape a biological one. Its fundamental organization comes from the tree. Cells are mostly aligned along the stem. That alignment makes properties strongly directional. In many conditions a piece of wood behaves differently along the grain than across it. A species name tells us something useful, but not everything: grade, moisture condition, defects, treatment, dimensions and orientation matter too.

### What a growth ring tells us

Imagine a log as concentric growth rings around a central pith. Boards can be cut roughly tangential to those rings or so that rings meet the face more nearly at right angles. We often call their appearances flat grain and edge grain. These patterns influence how the board changes shape during drying.

Wood usually shrinks much more across the grain than along it when bound water leaves the cell walls. Shrinkage is also commonly greater in the tangential direction, around the growth rings, than in the radial direction, from pith to bark. Because the directions do not shrink equally, a flat-sawn board can cup. A board containing the pith or reaction wood may warp in additional ways. Grain direction changes local strength and splitting behavior, especially near knots or sloping grain.

Think of a shelf made from a broad solid-wood panel. In a humid season it can expand across its width. In a dry season it can contract. If the panel is rigidly fastened across its full width without allowing movement, it may crack, bow or damage its fasteners. A plywood panel often behaves differently because adjacent veneers are oriented in different directions. That cross-lamination is not magic, but it modifies dimensional response and structural properties.

### Moisture content uses a surprising denominator

Carpenters often say a board is at twelve percent moisture content. They do not mean twelve percent of its current weight is water. In the conventional oven-dry-basis definition, moisture content is the mass of water divided by the oven-dry mass of the wood, multiplied by one hundred percent.

Suppose a sample weighs one hundred and twelve grams and, after careful drying to the relevant test endpoint, its wood substance weighs one hundred grams. Its moisture content is twelve percent. If it weighs one hundred and thirty grams with the same dry mass, its moisture content is thirty percent. That last number is around the often-used conceptual fiber-saturation range for many woods, but the actual transition varies by wood and even within a piece. It is not a universal exact threshold.

Above that region, additional water can occupy cell cavities without proportionally causing further cell-wall swelling. Below it, changes in bound water within the cell walls tend to produce dimensional changes. A board drying from twenty percent toward eight percent can therefore shrink materially even though it may already look dry and feel dry. A surface finish slows moisture exchange; it generally does not abolish equilibrium with surrounding humidity.

### Equilibrium is a moving target

Leave a piece of wood in air at a particular temperature and relative humidity for long enough, and it tends toward an equilibrium moisture content. Change the indoor humidity, and that target changes. The wood responds over time, with the surface commonly changing before the core. The speed depends on dimensions, orientation, coating and conditions.

This is why millwork and flooring are often conditioned in the environment where they will be installed, and why storage matters. A stack of boards left on damp concrete or exposed to rain can develop moisture gradients even when originally kiln-dried. The goal is not to make all wood completely dry. It is to use wood at a suitable moisture state for the service it will experience, with room for expected movement.

### Species is not a grade

A species group describes botanical and material tendencies. A grade characterizes a particular piece of lumber under specified rules. Knots, checks, slope of grain, wane and other characteristics can reduce the capacity of a member or alter how it must be used. Structural grades are not cosmetic grades. A board with attractive clear faces could be the wrong structural product; a rough-looking but properly graded member may perform its intended task.

A structural stamp can include a grade, species or species group, grading agency identifier, mill information and moisture or treatment designations. Interpret a stamp according to the governing grading system, not by treating every letter as an intuitive adjective. Stamped dimensions and actual dimensions also differ: a nominal two-by-four is not literally two inches by four inches after surfacing. Plans and fittings must use actual sizes and specified tolerances.

### Direction of load changes the problem

Picture a beam that must carry a floor load. Its bending capacity depends on cross-section, span, wood properties, support conditions and defects. Turning a rectangular member on edge can greatly change its bending stiffness. Driving a screw parallel to grain may create a different splitting risk than fastening across grain. A connection has its own design limits that cannot be inferred from the grade of the surrounding wood alone.

In a structure, design values come from recognized grading and engineering methods with appropriate adjustment factors. A lecture on moisture and grade cannot supply a safe span chart for an arbitrary room. But it can teach us which properties and labels to gather before a structural decision.

### Read the board before the cut

When choosing stock, examine end grain and faces. Find knots and checks, sight along the length for bow and twist, look for cupping, and consider whether the board has reached a suitable moisture condition. Ask which face will be the reference and which faces will remain visible. If a plank will span a gap, the grade and structural use matter; if it will become decorative trim, movement and finish compatibility may dominate.

The biggest change in thinking is that a board is not a uniform plastic bar. It is a directional material whose water content and internal structure evolve. Wood's usefulness comes partly from accepting those realities and designing around them. The carpenter does not force wood to stop being wood; the carpenter gives its strength the right direction and its movement an acceptable place to go.

## Layout, Plumb, Level, and Square

A carpenter has framed the outline of a small shed floor. The four edges measure the planned lengths. The opposite sides appear parallel. Yet when the sheathing is brought over, its corners do not line up. A square piece will not fit a skewed opening merely because someone measured all four sides carefully.

Construction layout is the process of making intended geometry real. It begins with a reference and continually checks whether each new dimension remains related to that reference. The three familiar words, plumb, level, and square, each answer a different geometric question.

Level means horizontal with respect to gravity's local direction, as indicated by a properly adjusted level or equivalent reference. Plumb means vertical, aligned with gravity. Square means perpendicular: two lines or faces meet at a right angle. A surface can be level but out of square to the adjacent wall; a wall can be plumb yet placed at the wrong angle in plan. These are independent properties, not different words for 'looks straight.'

### Choose the geometry you trust

Imagine placing a board against an old wall. The wall has a slight bow, and the floor slopes. If you measure everything from whichever point is nearest, every new mark inherits a different distortion. It is better to establish a datum: an agreed baseline, reference face, or fixed elevation against which other measurements are made.

A datum is not necessarily a perfect piece of the existing building. It is a deliberate coordinate choice. You might choose a level line to establish cabinet height or a chalk line to align studs. The measured imperfections of the surrounding building then become facts to accommodate rather than hidden assumptions inserted into every cut.

This is why professionals measure existing openings at more than one location. An opening may be wider at the top than the bottom. A corner may not be ninety degrees. A wall may be bowed even if its two endpoints lie on the intended line. One measurement at eye height cannot capture a three-dimensional shape.

### The right triangle as a layout instrument

Suppose you need to erect a line perpendicular to a baseline on the ground. There is a geometric method that does not require a giant manufactured square. Along one line, mark a distance of three units from their common corner. Along the proposed perpendicular, mark four units. Adjust the second line until the distance between those marks is five units. Because three squared plus four squared equals five squared, the enclosed angle is a right angle in ideal Euclidean geometry.

The units can be feet, meters, or multiples. A larger triangle generally makes a small marking error matter less in angular terms, although long tapes introduce their own sag, alignment, and tension errors. A three-four-five check is a way to construct or test an angle. It is not proof that a full frame is straight, planar, or dimensionally correct.

### Why diagonals reveal racking

For a rectangle whose sides are equal in opposite pairs, the two corner-to-corner diagonals should be equal. If the frame is pushed sideways into a parallelogram, one diagonal grows and the other shrinks. Comparing them is an efficient way to detect racking. Adjust the frame, check the diagonals, and temporarily brace it so the geometry does not change while you fasten the structure.

But diagonal equality alone is not a universal certificate of rectangularity for every arbitrary quadrilateral. Nor does it prove that the members are straight or that the surface is flat. Combine diagonal checks with the known side dimensions, the intended right angles, and inspection of bowing or twist.

The same discipline applies to cabinets and door frames. A door opening can be nominally square when measured on one face but twisted through its depth. A door then may contact the stop unevenly because the four corners do not inhabit one plane. To diagnose it, check level, plumb, diagonal, and plane instead of immediately shaving the door edge.

### Build from references, not from accumulated guesses

Suppose you install a row of studs by measuring the spacing from one stud to the next. If each mark carries a small error, the deviations may accumulate. A more reliable method for many repetitive layouts is to mark all positions from one common reference or use a carefully set story pole, gauge, or template. The chosen reference becomes a shared origin, preventing independent guesses from drifting apart.

Another source of trouble is changing which side of the pencil line is the intended cut. A blade consumes material: its kerf has a measurable width. If you cut on the wrong side of every mark, a project with many cuts can become systematically undersized. The cure is to identify the finished side of the line and use a consistent cutting convention.

Wood also changes dimensions as its moisture condition changes, especially across the grain. Achieving square layout with wet or warped stock does not ensure the finished assembly stays square. Layout and material selection are linked. Some movement should be accommodated by detailing, not fought with more fasteners.

### Check before you commit

A useful sequence is to establish a reference, mark dimensions, verify them independently, assemble provisionally, check plumb and level, check squareness and plane, then lock the geometry with proper connections and bracing. At each stage, ask what changed since the last check. The answer might be fastener pull, a slightly warped member, or a temporary support that slipped.

This can sound slow. In practice, it is a way of avoiding expensive corrections. A frame that has been checked before sheathing is attached is much easier to square than one discovered to be racked after the finishes go on.

Return to the shed floor. Its four edges were measured, but its diagonal relationship was not secured. Now we know the missing operation. Geometry is not contained in a list of lengths. Geometry is a network of relationships among lengths, angles, planes, and reference directions. The job of careful layout is to preserve those relationships while real materials, real tools, and gravity do their imperfect work.

## Framing Walls and Openings as Load Paths

Accurate layout places members in relation to one another; the framing arrangement determines how their forces continue through the structure.

Walk into a house under construction and look at a framed wall. Most of it is an orderly repetition of vertical studs. Then there is a door opening where the studs seem to disappear. Above the opening is a thicker horizontal member, commonly called a header. Why is it there? Because a load cannot simply stop at the empty space occupied by the future door. It must find another route to the foundation.

A load path is the route through which forces pass from one structural element into another, and ultimately into the ground. Gravity loads include the weight of the structure itself and variable weights such as people, furnishings, snow, or stored material. Lateral loads can come from wind or earthquakes. Different members and connections participate in carrying different directions of force. Understanding the route is more useful than learning a vocabulary list without knowing what the words do.

### A normal stud is a segment of the route

In a conventional wood-framed bearing wall, a roof or floor system may deliver vertical forces to the wall's top plate. The plate distributes those forces into studs; studs carry them down toward the lower plate and floor or foundation framing. This sketch leaves out many details, but it lets us ask what changes at an opening. A typical header spans across the opening and transfers its load toward the studs that support its ends. Often these are called jack or trimmer studs. Adjacent full-height studs, often called king studs, help integrate the opening into the rest of the frame and provide fastening and load-transfer functions. The exact assembly varies with span, loads, wall arrangement, and governing construction details.

A header acts like a beam. Loads applied along its length produce bending and shear. The member's size, material, support conditions, and length matter. If the opening becomes wider while all else remains the same, bending effects generally increase. But this does not mean every doorway requires the same header or that every interior wall carries a roof load. Some interior partitions are nonbearing for gravity loads; some walls take significant lateral loads even if they are not the primary support for a floor above.

### Openings are also interruptions in lateral resistance

The wall is not just a row of vertical columns. A framed wall can help resist sideways forces when sheathing, framing, anchorage, and connections make it act as a braced panel or shear wall. A very large window opening removes area that might otherwise contribute to this resistance. The remaining narrow wall segments, their sheathing patterns, hold-downs, and connections become important. A wall that looks strong enough to hold up the roof under gravity is not automatically adequate against a design wind or earthquake.

A continuous load path for wind might begin at roof sheathing, pass through roof framing connections, move into wall framing and braced panels, travel through the floor system, and end at foundation anchorage. Each interface is a possible weak link. Strong lumber cannot compensate for an absent connector if the load must cross that missing connection. Conversely, adding an oversized header does not necessarily solve a lateral anchorage problem. Structural systems have multiple force directions and failure modes.

### The shape of the opening changes the details

Imagine the opening is in a tall wall. Its side studs must remain stable against buckling and out-of-plane loads. Imagine the wall is interrupted by a large garage door. A narrow strip beside the opening may be asked to resist substantial lateral forces, requiring special framing and hold-down details. Imagine a door in a partition that is definitely nonbearing for gravity. The assembly still needs a frame that resists use, finishes accurately, and stays aligned as materials move.

The temptation is to judge by visible lumber size alone. Instead trace what contacts what. Does the header actually bear on supporting members, or is it relying on fasteners in a way that wasn't designed? Do loads from above land near the supported area? Does the wall below continue down through the floor framing to a footing, beam, or another engineered load path? Are shear elements and straps present where the design expects them? A gap in continuity may be more consequential than a modest change in member dimension.

### Changing a wall changes more than a doorway

Someone wants a wider opening between rooms. They remove several studs, replace them with a long beam, and assume the problem is solved. But the beam's reactions have to go somewhere. New concentrated loads at the supports may require posts, bearing points below, or foundation evaluation. The lateral system may also change. Plumbing, electrical runs, and mechanical services affect where members can be placed and notched. The safest analysis begins with the entire load path, including hidden framing and connections, rather than just the visible wall face.

Reading a framing plan is like reading a map of possible forces. Top and bottom plates establish routes, headers bridge interruptions, studs carry compression and provide other support, and sheathing and connections bind components into a lateral system. No single piece is the house. Each piece is part of a network whose behavior depends on its neighbors.

The reason a doorway stays open is not that gravity kindly ignores it. The frame deliberately carries loads around it. Once you see that, framing becomes less like carpentry jargon and more like an understandable application of mechanics.

## Floor and Roof Systems

Walk across two wooden floors. One feels rigid. The other moves slightly with each step. Neither has necessarily reached a breaking load, but the second may already be unsuitable for its intended finish or use. To understand this distinction, imagine a joist as a beam supported at two ends. The beam has to resist bending stress, but it also has to limit deflection, the distance it bends under load. Strength and stiffness are related, yet they are not the same requirement.

A residential floor is not just a row of pieces of wood. It is a load-sharing assembly made of joists or engineered members, subfloor sheathing, connections, beams, walls, columns, and foundations. A roof has a related but different set of load cases: its own weight, snow, wind pressure and suction, plus the geometry that carries these forces into the building below.

### Load goes somewhere

Imagine a heavy bookcase standing midway across a room. Its weight first bears on the finished floor, then the subfloor, then the framing members that support it. A joist carries load toward its bearings. A beam below it collects reactions from several joists and sends them toward posts or walls. Eventually the foundation distributes the load to soil. If any connection in that path is absent, weak, or incorrectly configured, the problem may occur far from the furniture.

The subfloor helps distribute concentrated loads and braces framing members, but it does not make joists interchangeable. A floor framed with close-spaced modest joists may behave differently from one with wider-spaced deeper members. The member's span, stiffness, species or engineered design, grade, spacing, and how loads are shared all matter. A joist bearing on a plate must have adequate support. A hanger has to transfer the reaction through appropriate fasteners and compatible hardware. Decorative trim does not create a structural load path.

We can gain intuition from a simplified beam relationship. For a simply supported beam with a central point load, bending deflection grows roughly with the cube of span when all other properties are held fixed. Increase span substantially and the beam gets noticeably more flexible. Member depth is powerful because bending stiffness depends strongly on cross-sectional shape. This is why a deeper joist can outperform a wider but shallower member of similar material. However, adding depth alone does not validate a real floor; shear, bending strength, bearing, connections, lateral stability, vibration, and code-prescribed loads still need checking.

### Why span tables have so many columns

A span table is not just a list of how far each piece of lumber can reach. It embodies assumptions about grade, species, spacing, load, and deflection limit. The American Wood Council explains these assumptions in its span-table tutorials. A living-room floor might be checked under one live-load case and a bedroom under another; a ceiling with no storage is a different case again. Model-code examples illustrate common assumptions, but adopted requirements and actual building uses determine the design inputs.

Deflection limits are often expressed as a fraction of span. An L over 360 criterion, for example, would give a one-third-inch deflection limit on a ten-foot span under the load case to which the criterion applies. That is a serviceability criterion, not a declaration that the member becomes unsafe at the next tiny increment. A floor can meet a specified static limit and still feel lively because vibration depends on frequency, mass, damping, and how occupants excite the structure.

### Roofs have gravity and uplift

At roof level, snow and self-weight push downward. Wind can push inward on one face while lifting away from another. Rafters carry distributed load to their bearings, but also need restraint against lateral movement and connections that resist uplift. A collar tie, rafter tie, ceiling joist, ridge board, and structural ridge beam do different jobs. Treating these pieces as interchangeable can change whether the assembly resists outward wall thrust.

Think about an ordinary pair of rafters leaning together, with their feet on opposite exterior walls. Under downward roof load, the geometry can create outward forces at those feet unless the framing provides an appropriate tie or a ridge support system that carries the loads differently. A ridge board used for alignment is not automatically a structural beam capable of carrying the roof reaction. The correct configuration follows from the whole roof system.

Trusses are another engineered solution. Their slender webs, chords and connection plates work together to form a system designed for specific loads. Cutting a web to create storage space can destroy its intended force path. The fact that a piece is thin does not mean it is expendable.

### Openings, holes, and surprises

Floors often need ducts, plumbing, stair openings, and mechanical penetrations. Framing around an opening introduces headers, trimmers, hangers, and new reaction paths. Boring holes and making notches in solid sawn joists have constraints because they remove material from regions where forces differ. Engineered I-joists and trusses have manufacturer-specific rules; a practice acceptable in solid wood may be destructive to an engineered member.

When investigating a bouncy floor, look beyond the one joist you can see. Ask whether the span has changed because a support was removed, whether bearings have shifted, whether a connection has loosened, or whether wetting and decay have reduced material performance. A cracked finish might reveal differential movement rather than a defective finish product.

The point of structural reasoning is to read the building as a network of reactions. Every load needs a path to the ground. Every path needs adequate strength, stiffness, connections and stability. Carpentry becomes more systematic when each board is understood not only as material to cut, but as part of that network.

## Nails, Screws, Connectors, and Load Transfer

Imagine a roof holding snow. The rafters or trusses carry the weight toward supports; walls carry it toward floors or foundations; foundations pass it into the ground. We call this a load path, but the phrase can conceal the real engineering question. At every boundary between pieces of material, how does the force cross the joint?

A beam can be strong enough while its connection is too weak. A sheathed wall can resist a large lateral force while the floor-to-wall connection is poorly detailed. A roof truss can be carefully engineered while a missing hanger fastener leaves the intended support incomplete. The joint is therefore part of the load path: not only what holds wood in place during construction, but what carries force after the structure is loaded.

### Different loads ask different things of a connection

Gravity tends to load framing downward. Wind can push walls inward or outward, shear a wall sideways, and create uplift that tries to detach the roof from its supports. Earthquake loading reverses directions during a sequence of movements. Connections intended for one of these cases are not automatically suitable for the others.

A simple joist bearing on a beam transfers downward load through contact: wood presses on wood. A joist hanger creates a different path, usually from the joist into its fasteners, through the hanger metal, into more fasteners, and then into the support. Every part of that chain matters. If the specified nails are absent from the hanger's side holes, the installed connection may be a different device from the one whose capacity was published.

Likewise, a roof-to-wall tie intended to resist uplift must actually connect the structural members on both sides. Fastening a strap to sheathing alone may not provide the expected connection to the underlying framing. A connector can be mechanically sound but attached to a member that is itself inadequately supported. The load-path sketch should show where the force comes from, the connection, and where it goes next.

### Why nails remain important

People often assume screws are stronger because they are threaded and hard to pull out. Withdrawal resistance is one property; lateral load resistance and ductility are others. Certain structural nails can bend and redistribute force across a connection rather than fracture abruptly. Some common brittle screws may fail when forced to carry cyclic shear. Structural screws exist, but their approved uses depend on the actual product and installation—not merely on having threads.

Nail dimensions are not a minor naming detail. Diameter, length, head, shank, coating, and whether the fastener is fully driven influence the result. A clipped-head fastener may not substitute for a full-head nail where the head contributes to resistance. A pneumatic nailer can make work fast, yet an underdriven or overdriven nail can damage the intended connection. The number and placement of nails also determine how load is shared and whether the wood splits.

Think of a plywood wall panel used to resist sideways load. Its edges are attached to framing by many fasteners. Each fastener transmits force between panel and frame, and the collective pattern helps the assembly act as a shear wall. If some fasteners miss the framing or their heads tear through damaged panel edges, the total behavior changes. Extra nails placed too tightly together can split the framing or weaken the panel edge. Quantity without spacing discipline is not quality.

### Metal connectors are engineered systems

A metal connector can redirect force around a geometrically difficult joint, but its strength is not a property of the steel bracket alone. The manufacturer's capacity is tied to the connection as installed: substrate, specified fasteners, fastening pattern, orientation, corrosion environment, and the load direction. A galvanized hanger may have particular corrosion limitations in treated wood or wet exposure, so durability is also part of the design.

Consider a deck ledger. A line of bolts or approved screws is visible, but the joint connects an exterior structure to a house that may contain sheathing, flashing, rim joists, and finishes. If those materials are not what the fastener assumes, the load transfer may be wrong. The same detail needs control of water intrusion: a structurally adequate joint can still rot the load-bearing member through poor flashing. Structural design and moisture management do not occur in separate universes.

Reinforcement should be understood through the same model. Adding a metal plate to the face of a cracked member may not restore a lost load path unless the plate is sized and its fasteners are designed to transfer the relevant force. An obvious bolt pattern may be under-strength for a concentrated load while making the wood more vulnerable to splitting.

### Reading the marks of a stressed joint

A sound joint may show slight dimensional change as wood moisture varies. But gaps that widen under load, crushed fibers at bearing points, elongated holes, torn-out fasteners, and diagonal cracks can indicate a connection problem. A sloping floor can arise from member deflection, support settlement, or connection movement. Looking at the far end of a member may be as important as looking at the middle.

A useful inspection proceeds from force to evidence. Identify the member carrying the load. Trace its route to a support. At every change of material or direction, locate the joint and ask which component transfers the force. Look for evidence of the intended fastener and any movement or deterioration. Draw the result before deciding what replacement hardware might be appropriate.

This is why a carpentry education should not reduce to memorizing cuts and nail patterns. Those patterns are important, but their meaning comes from mechanics. A building frame is a network of members whose connections allow loads to move continuously into stable supports. The more clearly you see that network, the better you can understand both sound construction and a developing failure.

### How a Wooden House Carries Load: Members, Connections, and Moisture

**Track:** Carpentry and Construction. **Background:** Basic geometry. **Purpose:** introduce structural load paths and the physical nature of wood before teaching framing details.

#### Technical companion

For a simply supported ideal beam with constant section, small deflections and a central point load `P`, maximum elastic deflection is `δ = P L³ / (48 E I)`. For a rectangular section, `I = b h³ / 12`. These formulas explain qualitative behavior. Real framing selection must account for load combinations, bearing, stability, duration, grade, connections, deflection limits, and the governing design standard.

**Evidence map:** USDA Forest Products Laboratory, *Wood Handbook* (2021), Chapters 4–9 and 17, treats moisture, physical and mechanical properties, fastening, beam equations, and wood construction. A member's presence alone does not identify its structural role; no removal, drilling, notching, or replacement procedure is specified here.

**Desk exercise:** Draw a roof-to-foundation load path for a single-story wood-framed building. Draw a second path for wind uplift. Mark where a connection, rather than the lumber itself, could be the limiting element.

### Wood Moisture, Grain, Species, and Grades

**Track:** Carpentry and Construction. **Background:** Wood framing and load paths. **Purpose:** explain how cellular structure, moisture state and grading jointly determine useful wood behavior.

#### Technical companion: moisture and selection

**Dry-basis moisture content:** `MC (%) = 100*(m_wet - m_oven_dry)/m_oven_dry`. The example uses `112 g` and `100 g`, yielding `12%`. The fiber-saturation point is a variable concept, roughly 30% for many species; it is not a universal cut-off. Tangential, radial and longitudinal shrinkage differ and cannot be replaced with one coefficient for every board.

**Evidence:** USDA Forest Service *Wood Handbook* (2021), especially chapters 3, 4, 6, 7 and 13. Richard Bergman's chapter 13 describes equilibrium moisture and dimensional control. Structural grade and design should be traced to the relevant grading rules and engineering references, not assumed from appearance. Appropriate species, treatment and moisture specification are application-dependent.

**Observation:** Compare end-grain ring orientation on three boards and predict which face is more likely to cup under a drying change. Record grain, visible defects and stamp markings without claiming a structural grade where no stamp or documentation exists.

### Layout, Plumb, Level, and Square

**Track:** Carpentry and Construction. **Background:** Wood movement and reference faces. **Purpose:** teach how spatial references become accurate built assemblies.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Geometry checks

For an ideal rectangle with sides \(a\) and \(b\), each diagonal is \(d=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). In practice, diagonal equality is informative together with verified opposite-side dimensions, a planar frame, and straight members. A scaled `3–4–5` triangle gives a right-angle check; the tolerance depends on measurement uncertainty and project requirements.

##### Layout case study

For a nominal `2.4 m × 3.2 m` rectangular frame, the intended diagonal is `4.0 m`. Check both diagonals and member lengths before fastening; a single correct diagonal is insufficient. A 3-D opening also needs checks for twist and alignment at multiple depths.

##### Professional scope

### Framing Walls and Openings as Load Paths

**Track:** Carpentry and Construction. **Background:** Wood movement, measurement, plumb, level, and square. **Purpose:** follow gravity and lateral loads through walls and openings instead of memorizing component names in isolation.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Simplified beam model

For a simply supported header with a *uniformly distributed* vertical load \(w\) over a clear span \(L\), support reactions are \(R_A=R_B=wL/2\) and peak bending moment \(M_{max}=wL^2/8\), under the model's assumptions. Real framing may have concentrated loads, partial bearing, live-load patterns, different support conditions, and coupled lateral demands. **Do not size headers or modify bearing walls using this simplified model.**

##### Load-path tracing exercise

On a framing plan, use one color for gravity loads, another for wind uplift, and another for in-plane lateral loads. Trace each from roof/floor to supporting wall, connections, and foundation. Around an opening identify header, end bearing, adjacent studs, braced-wall/shear elements, and anchorage. Identify where concealed conditions prevent a reliable conclusion.

##### Scope

Member span tables, connector nailing, wall bracing, seismic/wind details, and alterations to existing walls are design- and jurisdiction-specific. Review adopted residential code, American Wood Council prescriptions, manufacturer fastening schedules, and engineering when outside prescriptive scope. The discussion is structural literacy, not a substitute for a load-bearing alteration plan.

### Floor and Roof Systems

**Track:** Carpentry and Construction. **Background:** wood moisture, layout, and framed wall load paths. **Purpose:** follow loads through horizontal framing without confusing strength with stiffness.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Beam reasoning, not a sizing prescription

For a simply supported, constant-section beam with center point load `P`, span `L`, modulus `E`, and second moment `I`, the idealized midspan deflection is `delta = P L^3/(48 E I)`. For a rectangle with width `b` and depth `h`, `I = b h^3/12`. Assumptions exclude composite action, bearing deformation, creep, vibration, and distributed/multiple loading. Actual design requires strength, shear, serviceability, stability, bearing, connections, and applicable load combinations.

A 120-inch span with `L/360` has a corresponding displacement criterion of `120/360 = 0.333 inch`; this criterion is load-case-specific and not a universal pass/fail test for safety.

##### Diagnostic exercise

On a floor plan, mark joist direction, spacing, clear span, bearings, beams, columns, and assumed dead/live load. Follow one reaction through the foundation. On a roof sketch, distinguish a ridge board with ties from a load-carrying ridge beam, and trace wind uplift connections. Verify adopted code, engineered design and manufacturer restrictions before cutting, notching, drilling or modifying load-bearing framing.
