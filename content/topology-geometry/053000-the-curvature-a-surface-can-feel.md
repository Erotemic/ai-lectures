# The Curvature a Surface Can Feel: From Small Circles to Gauss--Bonnet

There are two kinds of bending hiding in an ordinary sheet of paper. Roll the sheet into a cylinder, and it clearly bends when viewed from the room. But if you draw a small triangle on the paper before rolling it, its side lengths and interior angles do not change. Mold a sheet onto a sphere without stretching, on the other hand, and you quickly discover that something is wrong: the measurements will not all agree. A cylinder bends in three-dimensional space while remaining flat to its own inhabitants. A sphere is curved in a sense they can detect from within.

The distinction can sound philosophical until we calculate it. The central quantity is Gaussian curvature. We will compute it first from the way a surface sits in space, then explain why the answer can actually be recovered from internal measurements. Finally we will put together many local measurements and find an integer: the Euler characteristic of the entire surface.

## Two ways a surface can bend

Start with a point on a regular surface in Euclidean three-space. Choose a unit normal vector to the surface near that point. Move a little in a tangent direction. The normal vector usually changes. Different tangent directions may change it at different rates. This directional change is encoded in a linear map on the tangent plane, called the shape operator.

The shape operator has two real eigenvalues, called the principal curvatures. They are the maximum and minimum signed normal curvatures, with a sign convention determined by our choice of normal and shape operator. Their product is Gaussian curvature. Their average is mean curvature. These two combinations behave differently: reversing the surface normal changes the signs of both principal curvatures, so their product stays fixed while their average changes sign.

A sphere of radius R is a clean example. Its outward unit normal at a point is the radius vector divided by R. Move one unit in any tangent direction and the normal changes at a rate of one divided by R. With the common convention that the shape operator is minus the derivative of the normal, both principal curvatures are negative one over R. Their product is positive one over R squared. Every tangent direction bends equally, and Gaussian curvature is positive everywhere.

On a cylinder of radius R, move along the vertical direction. The surface normal does not change at all, because the cylinder is generated by straight vertical lines. One principal curvature is zero. Move around the circular direction; the normal turns at rate one over R. The other principal curvature is negative one over R under the same convention. Their product is zero. The cylinder has nonzero mean curvature, but zero Gaussian curvature.

This gives the first answer to our paper-and-cylinder puzzle. The outside world sees bending because one principal curvature is nonzero. But the product vanishes because there is a direction in which the surface has no normal curvature. The geometry can be unrolled locally without distorting lengths.

A saddle provides the opposite sign. Consider the graph z equals one half x squared minus one half y squared. At the origin the tangent plane is horizontal. In one coordinate direction the surface bends upward, and in the perpendicular direction it bends downward. The two principal curvatures there are one and negative one, so Gaussian curvature is negative one. A saddle is not flat merely because it goes both up and down. Its opposing bends multiply to a negative intrinsic curvature.

We now have three model geometries: positive curvature on the sphere, zero curvature on the plane or cylinder, and negative curvature at the saddle. But so far we computed them using a normal vector in the surrounding space. That seems to make Gaussian curvature an extrinsic measurement. Why should an inhabitant confined to the surface be able to detect it?

## What the ruler knows

The answer is Gauss's remarkable theorem, often called the Theorema Egregium: Gaussian curvature can be determined entirely from the metric and its derivatives. A local isometry, a smooth map preserving all infinitesimal lengths and angles, must preserve Gaussian curvature.

There are two layers in that assertion. First, the metric coefficients E, F, and G tell us how to measure tangent vectors. From those coefficients and their first derivatives we can construct the Levi-Civita connection, which tells us how to differentiate a vector field along the surface without leaving the tangent plane. Taking a suitable commutator of those covariant derivatives produces the curvature tensor. In two dimensions, the curvature tensor has only one independent sectional-curvature value, and that value is Gaussian curvature.

This is not a full proof of Gauss's theorem, but it identifies the mechanism. The ambient normal can help us calculate K, yet the final answer is already encoded in the changing internal ruler. If two surface patches are locally isometric, they have the same intrinsic K at corresponding points, no matter how differently they are positioned in space.

Our cylinder unrolls onto a plane and both have K equal to zero. A sphere has K positive, so no open region of the sphere can be mapped isometrically onto a flat region of the plane. A globe map can preserve selected properties, but it cannot preserve every infinitesimal length and angle throughout a two-dimensional region. The failure has a local mathematical cause.

## Small circles betray the curvature

An inhabitant need not calculate a curvature tensor to notice the effect. They can begin at a point p, walk a small fixed geodesic distance rho in every direction, and mark the resulting geodesic circle. On a flat plane, that circle has circumference two pi rho. On a positively curved sphere, the circumference is a little smaller than that. On a surface of negative curvature, it is a little larger, at least for sufficiently small circles near a point of negative K.

Let's derive the spherical case. Take a sphere of radius R and put p at the north pole. Points at geodesic distance rho have colatitude rho divided by R. They form an ordinary circle of latitude, whose radius in surrounding space is R times sine of rho divided by R. Therefore its circumference is two pi R times sine of rho divided by R.

When rho is small, sine of rho divided by R is approximately rho divided by R minus one sixth of rho cubed divided by R cubed. Multiply by two pi R. The circumference becomes two pi rho minus pi rho cubed divided by three R squared, plus terms of higher order. Because the sphere's Gaussian curvature is one over R squared, the leading departure from flat circumference is minus pi K rho cubed over three.

That same leading-order rule holds for a general smooth surface at the center point: the small geodesic circle's circumference is two pi rho minus pi K at p times rho cubed divided by three, plus higher-order corrections. Thus a patient inhabitant with sufficiently precise distance measurements can recover curvature by examining how circumference departs from the Euclidean prediction.

The corresponding small-disk area also reveals curvature. On the sphere, the geodesic disk about the north pole is a spherical cap, with area two pi R squared times one minus cosine of rho divided by R. Expand the cosine. Its area is pi rho squared minus pi rho to the fourth divided by twelve R squared, plus higher terms. Again, the correction involves the same K. These are intrinsic experiments: the traveler needs only surface distances and areas.

## The triangle that refuses to add up

There is a more dramatic experiment than a very small circle. Draw a triangle whose sides are geodesics, meaning locally straightest paths according to the surface metric. In an ordinary Euclidean triangle, the interior angles add to pi radians, or one hundred eighty degrees. On a sphere, the sum can be larger. On negatively curved surfaces, a small geodesic triangle has an angle sum smaller than pi.

Consider a sphere and choose three points. One is the north pole. The other two lie on the equator, separated by ninety degrees of longitude. Connect the north pole to each equatorial point along a meridian, then connect the equatorial points by the short equator arc. All three sides are great-circle arcs, hence geodesics. Each interior angle is a right angle. The triangle has three right angles: its sum is two hundred seventy degrees, exceeding the Euclidean sum by ninety degrees.

This triangle covers one eighth of the sphere. Its area is one eighth of four pi R squared, which is pi R squared over two. Multiply that area by the constant curvature one over R squared. The result is pi over two, exactly the angle excess measured in radians. That equality is not a coincidence. For a geodesic triangle in a region where the surface can be consistently oriented, the integral of Gaussian curvature over the triangle equals the sum of its interior angles minus pi.

If curvature is not constant, the integral averages the varying curvature across the triangle rather than multiplying one point value by its area. The same principle survives. Local turning, integrated over an area, governs how the geodesic triangle's angles depart from Euclidean geometry.

We can also explain this through parallel transport. Carry a tangent arrow along the three sides while keeping it parallel according to the surface connection. On a plane, bringing the arrow back to its starting point along a loop does not rotate it. On a sphere, the transported arrow can return rotated. The signed rotation records the enclosed curvature, up to complete turns. This is called holonomy. The triangle's angle excess is another way of measuring the same geometric obstruction.

## When triangles cover an entire surface

Now imagine cutting a smooth closed surface into many small geodesic triangles. Each triangle tells us its angle excess by integrating curvature. Add all those equalities. The curvature integrals combine into the integral of K over the entire surface. On the angle side, every triangle contributes its three angles minus pi.

The sum of the angles around every interior vertex is two pi, because the small triangles meet to fill a neighborhood without a gap. Each triangular face contributes three corners, and each edge is shared by two faces. If the triangulation has V vertices, E edges, and F triangular faces, the total angle sum is two pi V. Subtract pi for every face, leaving pi times two V minus F. Since three F equals two E, this becomes two pi times V minus E plus F.

The integer V minus E plus F is the Euler characteristic. It does not depend on the chosen triangulation, provided the usual surface triangulation hypotheses hold. Consequently, for a compact closed orientable surface, the integral of Gaussian curvature equals two pi times the Euler characteristic. This is the Gauss--Bonnet theorem.

We should appreciate both sides. The left side is constructed from local geometric measurements. Change the metric and K may change point by point. The right side is an integer determined by the topology. Smoothly deforming the shape or changing the metric can redistribute curvature, but the total integral remains fixed as long as the underlying closed surface is unchanged.

On a sphere, Euler characteristic is two, so total curvature is four pi. For the round sphere, we already know K equals one over R squared and area equals four pi R squared. The product is four pi, independent of its radius. As the sphere gets larger, its curvature becomes smaller while its area becomes larger by exactly the compensating factor.

On a torus, Euler characteristic is zero. The standard doughnut-shaped torus has positive Gaussian curvature on its outer bulging region and negative curvature on its inner region. The positive and negative contributions cancel, producing total curvature zero. It is not true that every point of a torus has zero curvature. The theorem concerns the integral.

For a closed orientable surface with g handles, Euler characteristic is two minus two g. A two-handled surface has total curvature negative four pi. It cannot admit a metric of everywhere strictly positive Gaussian curvature, since the integral would then be positive. This is an obstruction to a kind of geometry, not a ban on the surface existing.

## Boundaries add their own turning

Our sheet of paper usually has an edge, so a theorem only for closed surfaces would leave out an important case. With boundary, Gauss--Bonnet acquires a boundary-curvature term. Travel around the boundary with the region on the left. Geodesic curvature measures the component of the boundary's turning that occurs within the surface. For a piecewise smooth boundary, add also the exterior turning angles at the corners. The sum of interior curvature, boundary geodesic curvature, and corner turning equals two pi times the Euler characteristic.

Test this on a flat circular disk of radius R. The interior Gaussian curvature is zero. Its counterclockwise boundary has geodesic curvature one over R, and circumference two pi R. Multiply them and the boundary integral is two pi. The disk's Euler characteristic is one, so the theorem agrees perfectly. The disk is flat, yet its boundary turns all the way around.

For a flat annulus, the outer boundary contributes positive turning and the inner boundary contributes negative turning when both are oriented with the annular region on the left. They cancel. The annulus has Euler characteristic zero. Once again the curvature accounting tracks topology, but now the boundary participates.

This also explains why one must be cautious with a cut-open spherical triangle. Its three edges are geodesics, so they contribute no geodesic curvature, but the corners do contribute turning. The corner term reconstructs the angle-excess formula we derived directly.

## What is intrinsic, what is global, and what is not

We began with a sheet rolled into a cylinder. Its normal turns in three-dimensional space, but its intrinsic Gaussian curvature stays zero. We compared it with the sphere, where small circles have a shorter circumference than Euclid predicts. We then built a spherical triangle with three right angles, and that angle excess equaled the curvature integrated across the triangle. Finally, summing triangle contributions across a whole surface produced an integer describing its topology.

These conclusions have different scopes. The shape operator uses an embedding into three-dimensional space. Gaussian curvature, though initially calculated from that operator, is intrinsic to a two-dimensional Riemannian metric. Theorem Egregium is a local statement about what is preserved by a local isometry. Gauss--Bonnet is a global statement requiring suitable compactness, surface, and boundary hypotheses. A local theorem cannot simply be promoted to a global one without those additional ingredients.

There is also more geometry ahead. A metric determines curvature in any dimension, but in dimensions greater than two there is not just one curvature number at a point. We examine sectional curvatures of different tangent planes, the Ricci tensor, and scalar curvature. Connections and holonomy appear throughout differential geometry and gauge theory. Gauss--Bonnet and its generalizations connect curvature to topology and eventually to index theory. Those are paths into the later lectures of this track.

For now, we have the bridge we needed: from curves and their turning, to tangent planes, to a metric and geodesics, and finally to intrinsic curvature. The key object is not the picture of a surface in space. It is the network of measurements and derivatives that any inhabitant can perform. A curve can bend without changing its intrinsic one-dimensional metric. A surface can carry curvature that no unrolling can remove. And the total of that curvature can be constrained by the number of holes in the surface. Local calculus has become global geometry.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Curvature formulas

Let \(n\) be a smooth local unit normal and use \(S(v)=-D_vn\) as the shape operator. Its principal curvatures are \(k_1,k_2\). Then

\[
K=k_1k_2,\qquad H=\frac{k_1+k_2}{2}.
\]

For a graph \(X(x,y)=(x,y,f(x,y))\),

\[
K=\frac{f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2}{(1+f_x^2+f_y^2)^2}.
\]

For \(f=(x^2-y^2)/2\), \(K(0,0)=-1\). For a sphere of radius \(R\), \(K=1/R^2\); for a circular cylinder, \(K=0\).

### Intrinsic small-circle measurements

For geodesic radius \(\rho\) about a point p on a smooth surface,

\[
C(\rho)=2\pi\rho-\frac{\pi K(p)}3\rho^3+o(\rho^3),
\quad
A(\rho)=\pi\rho^2-\frac{\pi K(p)}{12}\rho^4+o(\rho^4).
\]

On the round sphere,

\[
C(\rho)=2\pi R\sin(\rho/R),
\quad
A(\rho)=2\pi R^2\left(1-\cos(\rho/R)\right)
\]

for sufficiently small \(\rho\) (and for the north-pole disk up to the antipode).

### Gauss--Bonnet statements

For a geodesic triangle in an oriented surface region,

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi=\int_{\triangle}K\,dA.
\]

For a compact, connected, oriented smooth surface M with piecewise smooth boundary and positive boundary orientation,

\[
\int_M K\,dA+\int_{\partial M}k_g\,ds+
\sum_j(\pi-\alpha_j)=2\pi\chi(M),
\]

where \(\alpha_j\) are interior corner angles and the boundary integral uses signed geodesic curvature. For a closed surface the boundary terms vanish.

The Gauss--Bonnet identity also has orientation-independent formulations for nonorientable surfaces, but the version above suffices for the calculations here.

### Exercises with answers

1. Give a surface with nonzero mean curvature and zero Gaussian curvature. **Answer:** A circular cylinder, with principal curvatures \(-1/R\) and \(0\) under the outward-normal convention.
2. For the spherical octant triangle, compute angle excess and curvature integral. **Answer:** Both are \(\pi/2\).
3. Could a compact closed orientable genus-two surface have \(K>0\) everywhere? **Answer:** No; its total curvature must be \(2\pi(2-4)=-4\pi\).
4. Does \(\int_M K\,dA=0\) imply \(K=0\) everywhere? **Answer:** No; the standard torus has positive and negative curvature regions.
5. For a planar circular disk of radius three, calculate the boundary term. **Answer:** \(k_g=1/3\), length \(6\pi\), product \(2\pi\).
6. At the origin of the saddle \(z=(x^2-y^2)/2\), find K. **Answer:** The graph formula yields \(-1\).
