# Chains, homology, cohomology, and concrete calculations

## Homology: Cycles, Boundaries, and Holes

Draw a circle on paper and then fill it with a disk. Both pictures have a loop around their outer edge. Yet in the first, the loop encloses a hole in the space; in the second, the loop bounds a region that actually belongs to the space. How can algebra tell these situations apart?

The central idea of homology is to distinguish cycles from boundaries. A cycle is a closed arrangement of oriented geometric pieces. A boundary is a cycle that comes from the edge of a piece one dimension higher. If every cycle were a boundary, there would be no hole for that dimension to detect. The quotient of cycles by boundaries records the obstruction.

This definition is astonishingly broad, but we can build it from triangles.

### Make geometry into formal sums

Take a finite triangle mesh. Choose an orientation for every edge. Instead of thinking of a geometric edge as only a drawn segment, regard it as a formal generator. A one-chain is an integer-weighted combination of these oriented edges. Weight two means traverse an edge twice in its chosen direction; weight negative one means traverse it once in the reverse direction.

Vertices similarly generate the group of zero-chains. Oriented triangles generate the group of two-chains. Each chain group is a free abelian group with the mesh's oriented pieces as generators.

The boundary of an oriented edge from vertex a to vertex b is b minus a. The boundary of an oriented triangle with vertices a, b, and c is the oriented edge from b to c minus the edge from a to c plus the edge from a to b. The signs come from deleting one vertex at a time and alternating signs.

Now apply the boundary operator twice to a triangle. Each vertex occurs twice with opposite signs, so everything cancels. The boundary of a boundary is zero. The same cancellation holds for higher-dimensional simplices: every codimension-two face appears twice, and the two orientations oppose each other.

That identity is not a slogan. It is the equation making homology possible.

### Three edges around an empty triangle

Suppose we have three vertices and three edges forming a loop, but no filled triangular face. The sum of the three oriented edges is a one-cycle: its boundary is zero because each vertex is entered once and left once. Is it also a boundary?

In this one-dimensional complex there are no two-simplices at all. Hence there are no nonzero two-chains that could have the loop as their boundary. The loop represents a nonzero class in first homology. It generates a copy of the integers. Traversing the loop twice represents twice that class; reversing direction represents its negative.

Now add a filled triangle as a two-simplex. The very same edge loop is exactly the boundary of that face. It becomes zero in first homology. One algebraic operation—the addition of a two-dimensional generator whose boundary is the loop—has filled the hole.

This is the simplest concrete illustration of the quotient called first homology: all closed one-chains, modulo those that arise as boundaries of two-chains.

### Dimension by dimension

At dimension zero, every zero-chain is a cycle, since there are no chains of dimension negative one. Taking the quotient by edge boundaries identifies vertices that are connected by paths. Thus zero-dimensional homology counts connected components, with a copy of the integers for each component.

At dimension one, homology detects independent loops modulo filled-in loops. In dimension two, it detects closed surfaces that do not bound a three-dimensional region within the space. The sphere has a nontrivial second homology class, reflecting its oriented surface. The solid ball does not: its spherical boundary actually bounds the three-dimensional ball.

These descriptions refer to homology with integer coefficients. Other coefficient groups can be useful, but they may discard information, especially torsion. Even with integers, homology is not a complete classification of spaces: two spaces may have identical homology groups while differing in their fundamental groups or other structures.

### Boundary matrices and a real calculation

A finite simplicial complex gives a sequence of integer matrices. The columns of the boundary matrix at dimension k describe the boundaries of k-simplices. The identity boundary squared equals zero becomes the matrix equation that consecutive boundary matrices multiply to zero.

To calculate first homology, find all integer one-chains whose boundary vanishes, then quotient by the integer subgroup generated by two-dimensional boundaries. When the complex is finite, Smith normal form gives a systematic route to this quotient. Over the rational numbers, ordinary linear algebra provides ranks and therefore Betti numbers, but it may miss torsion that the integer calculation detects.

For a triangulated circle with three vertices and three edges, the first boundary matrix has rank two. Since there are three one-chain generators, its kernel has rank one. There are no filled triangles, so the image of the second boundary map is zero. First homology has rank one. Fill the triangle, and the second boundary map sends its generator to precisely that remaining cycle. The quotient becomes zero.

This is homology as computation, not just an analogy about holes.

### The singular construction

Triangles are convenient when a space already comes triangulated. But many spaces have no chosen triangulation, and some are awkward to triangulate at all. Singular homology replaces the fixed mesh simplices with all continuous maps from standard simplices into the space. Each map is a singular simplex. Boundary maps come from restricting those maps to faces of the standard simplex.

The chain groups are enormous, but the formal definition works for every topological space. On a simplicial complex, the natural comparison between simplicial and singular homology is an isomorphism under the standard hypotheses. So the finite computation agrees with the more general topological invariant. The point of the singular construction is not computational efficiency; it is invariance without choosing a mesh.

Homotopic maps induce the same maps on homology. In particular, a homotopy equivalence induces isomorphisms on all homology groups. This is why deforming a circle into an ellipse does not change its homological hole. But adding the disk does: a circle is not homotopy equivalent to the filled disk.

### A place to stop and look back

The fundamental group remembers how loops compose, which can produce noncommutative information. First homology is abelian and loses some of that information. On a path-connected space, first homology with integer coefficients is the abelianization of the fundamental group. Thus homology often sacrifices distinctions in exchange for linearity, calculability, and access to higher dimensions.

It was a good trade. We can now assemble chain complexes from geometric pieces, compute groups using integer matrices, and detect features that survive continuous deformation. The boundary-of-boundary identity supplies the algebra, and the difference between a cycle and a boundary supplies the geometry. A hole becomes a precisely defined class of closed chains that nothing in the space can fill.

## Chain Complexes and Exact Sequences: How Topology Becomes Algebra

A circle has one independent loop. A filled disk does not. A sphere encloses a two-dimensional cycle, while a projective plane has a different kind of one-dimensional obstruction that disappears if counted twice. These statements sound geometric. To prove them systematically, we convert shapes into algebraic data.

The basic machine is a chain complex. Its ingredients are groups of formal pieces and maps that describe their boundaries. The same algebra will later appear in de Rham cohomology, elliptic complexes, Floer homology, and gauge-theoretic deformation theory. Learning to compute with it now pays off throughout advanced geometry.

### Build shapes from pieces

Imagine a triangle. Its vertices are zero-dimensional pieces, its edges are one-dimensional pieces, and its filled interior is a two-dimensional piece. Each edge has a signed boundary: the terminal vertex minus the initial vertex. The oriented triangular face has a signed sum of its three edges as boundary.

If we take the boundary of that boundary, the vertices cancel in pairs. This is not a coincidence peculiar to triangles. The boundary of an oriented boundary is always zero. In algebraic language, consecutive boundary maps compose to zero.

For a space made of many cells or simplices, form an integer combination of pieces of each dimension. The collection of such combinations is a chain group. The boundary operator tells us how those pieces meet. An expression with zero boundary is a cycle. A cycle that is itself the boundary of a higher-dimensional chain is called a boundary. Homology takes cycles and identifies those that differ by boundaries.

This quotient is the heart of the construction. It does not count every closed path separately. It counts the closed paths that cannot be filled using the pieces available in the space.

### Start with a circle

A circle can be built as a single vertex and a single edge whose two ends are attached to that vertex. The edge's boundary is the vertex minus itself, so the boundary map is zero. The edge is a cycle. No two-dimensional cell is present to make it a boundary.

Thus the first homology group is the integers. A loop can wrap once, twice, or negatively around the circle; those wrapping numbers do not disappear under continuous deformation. The zero-dimensional homology group is also the integers because the circle has one connected component.

Now fill the circle with a disk. A two-dimensional cell has appeared, with its boundary attached around the circle once. The original loop is now the boundary of the disk's face. Its class in first homology vanishes. Adding one cell has killed a hole.

The fact that these two spaces differ is now encoded in one boundary map, not in a visual judgment.

### A space with a cycle of finite order

The real projective plane is built by identifying opposite points of a disk's boundary. It has a cell structure with one vertex, one one-cell, and one two-cell. The crucial difference from the ordinary disk is that the attaching map of the two-cell travels twice around the one-cell, as seen in the cellular boundary map.

The resulting first homology is the integers modulo two. There is a nontrivial loop class, but taking it twice gives a boundary. This is a form of torsion. If we had used only ordinary real-valued differential forms and de Rham cohomology, this two-torsion information would disappear. Integer homology remembers something that real cohomology cannot.

That difference becomes important in bundle theory, spin structures, and characteristic classes. Not all topological data can be detected by integrating real differential forms.

### Exactness explains what one map cannot see

A sequence of groups and maps is called exact at a group when every element sent to zero by the next map comes from the previous group. The image of one map equals the kernel of the next.

It is useful to say this verbally. Exactness means that all failures of injectivity have already been explained by the preceding stage. A long exact sequence passes information between related topological spaces, keeping track of which cycles survive, which become boundaries, and which newly appear.

Suppose a subspace A lies inside X. Relative chains identify chains entirely inside A with zero. The relative homology of the pair measures what remains when A is treated as already filled in. The resulting long exact sequence connects the homology of A, the homology of X, and the relative homology of the pair.

Consider a disk and its boundary circle. The disk has no first-dimensional hole, but the pair consisting of the disk and its boundary has a relative two-dimensional class: the disk's interior, whose boundary lies in the designated subspace. The connecting map sends that relative class to the loop around the boundary circle. It is an isomorphism between two copies of the integers.

The map is not an abstract trick. It literally records the boundary of the relative two-dimensional object. Exactness then accounts for why this boundary loop is nontrivial on the circle but trivial when viewed inside the filled disk.

### Why the algebra generalizes

A chain complex need not be constructed from a triangulated picture. In de Rham theory, the objects are differential forms and the maps are exterior derivatives. In elliptic theory, a carefully chosen sequence of differential operators may form a complex. In Floer theory, the generators may be critical points or periodic trajectories, and the differential counts connecting solutions to differential equations.

In all these settings, the key proof obligation is the same: composing two consecutive differentials gives zero. Then cycles modulo boundaries make sense. Proving that a proposed differential really squares to zero can be one of the hardest parts of a new theory. But after that proof, familiar ideas such as exact sequences and maps induced by geometric constructions become available.

The geometry can be elaborate; the algebraic skeleton is stable. When an advanced lecture introduces a new cohomology group, ask four questions: what are its generators, what is its differential, why does that differential square to zero, and what maps between complexes preserve that structure? Those questions will keep the definitions connected to something calculable.

## Calculating Homology of Circles, Spheres, and Tori

Saying that homology detects holes is helpful for intuition, but not yet a method. How do we find the homology of a space that has no convenient global coordinates? We can cover it with simpler regions, or build it by attaching cells. Both techniques turn a complicated global question into a few maps between familiar groups.

We will calculate the first homology of a circle, the top-dimensional homology of a sphere, and the homology of a torus. The purpose is not to memorize three answers. It is to understand why those answers follow from exactness and boundary maps.

### Cutting a circle into two easy regions

Cover a circle with two open arcs, each slightly longer than half the circumference. Each individual arc can be continuously shrunk to a point. Their intersection, however, consists of two disconnected short arcs. This is the subtlety. The pieces are individually simple, but the overlap has two connected components.

The Mayer–Vietoris sequence relates the homology of the overlap, the two pieces, and their union. In the part dealing with first homology, the connecting map goes from loops in the whole circle to reduced zero-dimensional homology of the overlap. Reduced zero-dimensional homology records one independent difference between the overlap's two components.

A loop going all the way around the circle crosses from one overlapping component to the other in a way that cannot be resolved inside a single arc. The connecting homomorphism records that difference. Since both arcs have no first homology, the relevant exact sequence identifies the circle's first homology with one copy of the integers.

Notice how the global loop was detected by an overlap consisting of disconnected pieces. Local triviality does not force global triviality; the gluing carries information.

### Build a sphere out of two disks

Now take a two-dimensional sphere and cover it by a slightly enlarged northern disk and southern disk. Their intersection is a thick band around the equator, which retracts onto a circle. Each disk is contractible. Just as before, Mayer–Vietoris transfers a homology class from the overlap to the union, but now in one higher dimension.

The equatorial circle is the boundary between north and south. Think of the upper and lower hemispheres with opposite induced boundary orientations. Their common equator cancels when the hemispheres are added to form a closed sphere. That gives a two-cycle with no boundary.

Exactness tells us that its class generates the second homology group of the sphere. In integers, the answer is one independent two-dimensional class. This is the algebraic expression of the sphere's fundamental oriented surface.

There is a pattern here. The n-dimensional sphere can be cut into two contractible pieces whose overlap retracts onto the sphere of one dimension less. The same exact-sequence argument shifts the nontrivial reduced homology up by one degree. Inductively, the n-sphere has reduced homology equal to the integers in degree n and zero in other degrees.

### A torus by attaching a square

For the torus, a cell structure is the shorter calculation. Draw a square and identify opposite edges with matching directions. The four corners become a single vertex. There are two distinct one-cells: one horizontal loop and one vertical loop. The square's interior is a single two-cell.

The two one-cells have zero boundary, because each begins and ends at the same vertex. What is the boundary of the two-cell? Read around the square. The attaching word is: horizontal, vertical, reverse horizontal, reverse vertical. It is a commutator.

In a fundamental group, a commutator carries information. But the cellular boundary map counts signed occurrences of each one-cell in an abelian group. Horizontal appears once positively and once negatively. The same happens for vertical. Thus the cellular boundary of the two-cell is zero.

The first homology therefore has two integer generators: the horizontal and vertical loops. The second homology has one integer generator, coming from the oriented square whose edges cancel. Zero-dimensional homology is also one copy of the integers because the torus is connected.

The Betti numbers of a torus are consequently one, two, one, and its Euler characteristic is one minus two plus one, which is zero. That is consistent with Gauss–Bonnet for a flat torus, whose Gaussian curvature can vanish everywhere.

### When coefficients change the answer

The same cell structure may be analyzed with integer, rational, or finite-field coefficients. For the torus just considered, the free ranks behave predictably. For the projective plane, the coefficient choice is more dramatic. The two-cell attaches with degree two, so its boundary map is multiplication by two. Over the integers, the first homology is integers modulo two. Over the real numbers, multiplication by two is invertible and that first homology vanishes.

This explains why de Rham cohomology, which uses real-valued forms, cannot detect every integral torsion class. The smoothness of a space does not remove the distinction between integer and real invariants.

### The role of exactness and functoriality

Mayer–Vietoris is more than a collection of shortcuts. It describes how cycles in the overlap become cycles or boundaries in the union. The maps matter as much as the groups. Two gluing problems can use pieces with identical homology groups but attach them by different maps, producing different global spaces.

Likewise, a cellular calculation requires the degree of each attaching map. Counting how many cells exist is not enough. The relative signs and winding numbers determine the boundary maps, and the boundary maps determine homology.

Later we will pair these cycles with differential forms. A closed one-form integrated around either torus generator measures its cohomology class. A top-degree form integrated over the oriented surface measures the two-dimensional class. Those pairings connect the combinatorial calculation here to the analytic language of de Rham and Hodge theory.

The practical method is now clear: choose simple pieces, write the actual maps, compute kernels and images, and only then name the resulting groups. This is the difference between recognizing a hole in a drawing and proving what the topology contains.

## Integration over Cycles and the de Rham Map

Suppose you are given a smooth closed curve on a manifold. One question is whether the curve encloses a region inside that manifold. Another question is whether a closed one-form accumulates a nonzero integral when evaluated along the curve. These questions turn out to be linked by Stokes' theorem.

Homology studies cycles modulo boundaries. De Rham cohomology studies closed differential forms modulo exact forms. Integration provides a natural way for the two theories to speak to each other. We will build that relationship carefully, beginning with actual parametrized pieces rather than treating a cycle as a picture of a hole.

### Chains are formal sums of parametrized pieces

A smooth singular one-simplex is a smooth map from a standard interval into the manifold. It can trace an edge, a curved route, or a constant path. A one-chain is a finite formal linear combination of such maps with coefficients, often integers or real numbers. Its boundary is a signed sum of endpoints. When the signed endpoints cancel, the chain is a cycle.

A two-simplex is a map from a filled triangle. Its boundary is the alternating sum of its three oriented edges. A two-chain is a formal sum of these parametrized triangles. Its boundary is a one-chain. The algebraic rule that the boundary of a boundary is zero is a consequence of pairwise cancellation of oriented faces.

A closed path is a basic one-cycle, but not every cycle is presented as one simple loop. A cycle may have many pieces, self-intersections, and coefficients. This algebraic flexibility is useful: it lets homology behave well under subdivision and gluing.

### Integrating a form over a chain

Let omega be a one-form, and let sigma be a smooth parametrized one-simplex. Pull omega back along sigma to the interval and integrate there. For a chain consisting of several simplexes, multiply each integral by its coefficient and add them. A k-form can be integrated in the same way over a smooth k-chain, by pulling it back to each standard k-simplex.

This definition needs the form to have the same degree as the dimension of the chain. Integrating a two-form over a curve is not a standard differential-form integral: there are not enough independent tangent directions. To pair a two-form with a two-cycle, use a surface-like chain.

The result is linear in both the form and the chain. If you double a cycle with its coefficient, you double the integral. If you reverse orientation, you change the sign. The construction is a pairing, not yet a pairing on homology and cohomology classes. For it to descend to classes, we need to check what happens when we change a chain by a boundary or a form by an exact form.

### Why closed forms ignore boundaries

Let omega be a closed k-form, meaning d omega equals zero. Suppose a k-cycle z is the boundary of a k-plus-one-chain C. Integrating omega over z gives, by Stokes, the integral of d omega over C. Because d omega is zero, the result is zero. Thus closed forms have zero integral on boundaries, and their integrals depend only on the homology class of the cycle.

Now suppose the form is exact: omega equals d eta. Integrate over any k-cycle z. Stokes converts the integral to that of eta over the boundary of z, which is zero by the definition of cycle. So exact forms contribute zero to every cycle integral. The integral therefore depends only on the de Rham cohomology class of the form.

Together these statements show that integration gives a well-defined number from a cohomology class and a homology class of matching degree. This is the de Rham pairing.

### A calculation on the circle

Write the unit circle using an angle coordinate that increases once around the circle. There is a globally defined angular one-form, even though there is no global real-valued angle function. Normalize the form by dividing it by two pi. Its integral around one counterclockwise circuit is one. Around a path that winds twice it is two. Around the same circuit clockwise it is negative one.

This tells us something that neither a local derivative nor a single chart makes visible. The one-form measures the homology class of the loop, and indeed its integer winding number for loops of the circle. Although the form itself has real coefficients, these particular normalized periods on integral one-cycles are integers.

If we change the form by adding the differential of a globally defined smooth function, its integral around a closed loop is unchanged. And if we alter the cycle by adding the boundary of a two-chain, its integral is unchanged. The number belongs to the pair of classes, not to arbitrary representatives.

### Detecting a two-dimensional cycle

Consider the two-dimensional torus formed by identifying opposite edges of a square. There is a closed two-form representing oriented area. Normalize it so its integral over the torus is one. Because the torus is a closed oriented surface, it has a fundamental two-cycle. The integral of the normalized area form over that cycle is one.

The two-form cannot be exact. If it were the derivative of a one-form, Stokes on the entire torus, which has no boundary, would force its integral to vanish. This is a concrete way to exhibit a nonzero second de Rham cohomology class. The calculation depends on orientation and normalization, but the nonvanishing is a topological fact.

### What the de Rham map asserts

Integration is more than a source of examples. It assigns to a closed k-form a real-valued functional on k-dimensional singular homology. Under the standard comparison between smooth singular homology and ordinary singular homology, this produces a singular cohomology class with real coefficients. The de Rham theorem says this map from de Rham cohomology to singular cohomology over the real numbers is an isomorphism for smooth manifolds.

This is a strong result. It says that every real singular cohomology class comes from a closed differential form, and that a closed form whose class has zero integrals against all real homology classes represents zero de Rham cohomology. The statement does not say that every integer cohomology class is the de Rham class of an arbitrary differential form with some preferred integer coefficients. Real forms do not directly encode torsion classes, because torsion vanishes upon passage to real coefficients.

We have justified why integration descends to cohomology and homology. The full proof of the isomorphism needs more machinery, such as local exactness and a method for gluing local comparisons. The Poincare lemma and partitions of unity supply precisely those ingredients. It is valuable to distinguish constructing the map from proving that the map is an isomorphism.

### Why this bridge matters

A topological cycle may be difficult to see geometrically in a complicated manifold. A differential form can give a quantitative witness that the cycle is not a boundary: if a closed form integrates to a nonzero number over it, the cycle cannot bound. Conversely, differential forms allow us to describe classes using derivatives, coordinates and integrals rather than only combinatorial chains.

This is the deeper reason the theories fit together. The boundary operator on chains and the exterior derivative on forms are dual through integration. Stokes is the compatibility relation. Once we pass to classes, calculus and topology become two descriptions of the same real-valued invariants.

## Cohomology, Cup Products, and de Rham Classes

Homology organizes cycles and boundaries. There is a natural complementary question: instead of building cycles, could we assign measurements to them? Integrating a differential form around a loop is one such measurement. When the form is closed, that integral depends only on the homology class of the loop, under the appropriate smoothness and orientation conditions. A boundary contributes zero by Stokes' theorem.

Cohomology develops this dual viewpoint. But it does more than record the same information with arrows reversed. Cohomology classes can be multiplied, and that multiplication can distinguish spaces whose individual groups look alike.

### From chains to cochains

For a simplicial complex, an integer-valued cochain of degree k is a homomorphism from the group of k-chains into the integers. Think of it as a rule that assigns a compatible integer measurement to each oriented k-simplex and extends linearly to chains.

The boundary operator lowers dimension. Its dual, called the coboundary, raises degree. A cochain is a cocycle if its coboundary is zero. It is a coboundary if it is the coboundary of a lower-degree cochain. The identity that boundary applied twice vanishes implies that coboundary applied twice also vanishes.

Cohomology is cocycles modulo coboundaries. Notice the symmetry: homology divides closed chains by chains that bound; cohomology divides closed measuring rules by rules obtained from a lower-dimensional potential.

For the circle, first cohomology with integer coefficients is the integers. A generator can be understood as a rule measuring how many times an oriented loop winds around the circle. More precisely, cohomology pairs with homology: a cocycle evaluates on a cycle, and the value does not change when either representative is changed by an appropriate boundary or coboundary.

### Closed differential forms

Now work on a smooth manifold. Differential forms provide a second cochain complex. The exterior derivative takes k-forms to k-plus-one forms and satisfies d squared equals zero. A form is closed when its exterior derivative vanishes and exact when it is the derivative of a form one degree lower. De Rham cohomology is the quotient of closed forms by exact forms.

On the punctured plane, consider the one-form that measures angular change around the origin. In coordinates it is negative y times d x plus x times d y, divided by x squared plus y squared. This form is smooth away from the origin, and its exterior derivative is zero. But its integral around the unit circle is two pi. If it were exact globally, its integral around every closed loop would be zero. Therefore it represents a nontrivial first de Rham cohomology class.

The point here is not that a derivative fails locally. On sufficiently small simply connected neighborhoods avoiding the origin, one can choose a local angle coordinate and express the form as a derivative. The obstruction is global. A family of local potentials cannot be assembled into one globally defined potential around the missing point.

### What de Rham's theorem says

A foundational theorem identifies de Rham cohomology with singular cohomology using real coefficients for smooth manifolds. Integration provides the bridge: a closed differential form yields a cochain by integrating over smooth singular simplices, and Stokes guarantees compatibility with the differentials. Proving that the resulting map induces an isomorphism requires real work, including local exactness and a gluing argument.

The coefficient field is important. Real differential forms do not detect finite torsion in integral cohomology. An integer cohomology group may contain torsion that vanishes after passage to real coefficients. So de Rham cohomology gives a powerful geometric model of real cohomology, not a replacement for every integral calculation.

### Why a product appears

Cohomology contains more structure than a sequence of abelian groups. Given a degree p class and a degree q class, their cup product is a class of degree p plus q. On the cochain level, the product evaluates one factor on an initial face of a simplex and the other on a complementary final face, with choices made consistently with the combinatorial construction.

The cup product is associative on cohomology and has a unit in degree zero. With integer coefficients, it is graded commutative: exchanging a degree p class and a degree q class introduces a sign of negative one raised to p times q. This sign carries geometric information about the orientation of intersections.

A space's cohomology ring is the direct sum of its cohomology groups together with this product. Two spaces may have isomorphic groups in every degree but different cup products. The ring remembers how different families of classes intersect or combine, not just how many there are.

### The torus calculation

Consider the surface of a doughnut, a two-dimensional torus. It has two basic kinds of loops, one going around the long direction and one around the short. First integral cohomology is free abelian of rank two, generated by classes we may call a and b. Its second cohomology is free abelian of rank one, generated by the oriented surface class u.

The product of a with b is u after a consistent choice of orientations. Reversing their order changes sign. The product of a with itself is zero, and the same for b. This multiplication says that the two independent one-dimensional directions combine to detect the two-dimensional orientation.

Compare the torus with a wedge of two circles and a separate sphere attached at a single point. Its reduced cohomology groups have the same ranks in degrees one and two as the torus: two in degree one, one in degree two. But every product of positive-degree classes is zero in that wedge space. Thus the groups agree and the rings differ. The cup product distinguishes them.

This example is why cohomology should not be treated as just homology in reverse. The multiplication can carry geometric information absent from a bare list of groups.

### Pullbacks and naturality

A continuous map between spaces pulls cohomology classes backward. On manifolds, a smooth map pulls differential forms backward, and this operation commutes with the exterior derivative. In both settings, it is compatible with products. That gives a method for testing maps: a proposed map must respect the cohomology ring structure.

For example, if the image of a degree-one class under a pullback is specified, then products of those classes constrain what can happen in degree two. Sometimes these algebraic constraints prove that a certain continuous map cannot exist with prescribed behavior on cycles or orientations.

Homology measures cycles that fail to bound. Cohomology measures closed rules that cannot be reduced to potentials. De Rham theory turns those rules into calculus, while the cup product shows that independent measurements can interact. The result is a new language for global geometry: not just the existence of holes, but the algebra of how their dual classes fit together.

## Computing Cohomology of Circles and Tori

De Rham cohomology is defined as closed differential forms modulo exact ones. That definition sounds compact, but what does an actual computation look like? We will compute the cohomology of a circle and a two-dimensional torus. Both calculations have geometric meaning: they show precisely how integrals around fundamental cycles classify the obstruction to a global potential.

We begin with the circle because the entire calculation can be understood as a careful version of the fundamental theorem of calculus with periodic boundary conditions.

### A closed one-form on a circle

Imagine a point traveling around a circle, tracked by an angle theta. Theta increases continuously while we travel, but after one revolution we have returned to the same point even though theta has increased by two pi. The angle is not a globally defined real-valued function on the circle. Nevertheless the local differentials d theta agree where angle charts overlap, because two branches of angle differ by constant multiples of two pi. They define a global one-form.

Every one-form on a circle is closed: its exterior derivative would be a two-form, and there are no nonzero two-forms on a one-dimensional manifold. The question is which one-forms are exact. An exact form is d f for a smooth real function f defined all the way around the circle. Its integral over one full revolution must be zero, since the endpoint is the starting point.

Conversely, suppose a smooth one-form has zero integral over a full revolution. Lift it to the real line, where the angle becomes a genuine coordinate. On the real line we can integrate its coefficient to construct a potential. Because the integral over a period is zero, the potential takes the same value when theta is increased by two pi. It is periodic and therefore descends to a well-defined function on the circle. The original form is its differential.

This proves something precise. Two one-forms on the circle represent the same cohomology class exactly when they have the same integral around a positively oriented revolution. Every real number occurs as such an integral by taking a constant multiple of the angular one-form. The first de Rham cohomology of the circle is a one-dimensional real vector space.

The zeroth cohomology is also one-dimensional: a function whose differential is zero is constant on a connected circle, and no negative-degree differential makes a constant into a boundary. Cohomology in degrees two and higher vanishes because the circle has no nonzero forms in those degrees.

### From one loop to two independent loops

A two-dimensional torus can be thought of as a square with opposite edges identified. It has two basic types of loops: one circles around the first periodic coordinate, and the other around the second. Call the angular coordinates theta and phi. Both d theta and d phi are global closed one-forms.

Integrate d theta along the first basic loop. The result is two pi. Integrate it along the second basic loop. The result is zero. For d phi the roles are reversed. These two forms detect independent directions of winding.

Suppose omega is any closed one-form on the torus. Let its integral around the first basic loop be two pi times a, and its integral around the second be two pi times b. The coefficients a and b do not depend on which parallel representative of the basic loops we choose, because sliding the loop across a cylinder changes the integral by that of d omega over the intervening strip, and d omega is zero.

Subtract a times d theta plus b times d phi from omega. The remaining closed form integrates to zero around both generators of the torus's first homology. Consequently it has zero integral around every closed loop. A path integral from a fixed base point then defines a smooth global potential. The remaining form is exact.

We have classified every first cohomology class by the two real numbers a and b. The first de Rham cohomology of the two-torus has dimension two. It is not merely that the torus appears to have two holes in a picture. The classification is an explicit test using two integrals and the existence of a potential when both periods vanish.

### The top-degree class

A two-form on a two-dimensional torus is automatically closed, because its exterior derivative would have degree three. Every two-form can be written as a smooth periodic coefficient times d theta wedge d phi. Its integral over the torus is one real number.

If a two-form is exact, that integral vanishes by Stokes' theorem because the torus has no boundary. Conversely, a smooth two-form whose total integral is zero is exact. One way to see this is to average its coefficient in one angular direction, subtract off that average using an explicit antiderivative in the other direction, and then use the zero-total-integral condition to integrate the remaining one-dimensional function periodically. The resulting one-form has the original two-form as its derivative.

Thus the second de Rham cohomology of the torus is another copy of the real numbers. A normalized area form represents its generator, just as a normalized angular one-form represents the circle's first cohomology generator.

### Wedge products and the cohomology ring

There is more structure than a list of vector-space dimensions. Differential forms can be multiplied using the wedge product, and this multiplication descends to cohomology. For the torus, the product of the class of d theta with the class of d phi is the nonzero second-degree class represented by d theta wedge d phi. Swapping the factors reverses the sign. Wedge-multiplying either one-form by itself gives zero.

In other words, the cohomology ring of the two-torus is the exterior algebra on two degree-one generators. The word ring means we can add classes and multiply them, with the appropriate graded sign. This structure expresses how the two winding directions combine to give an oriented area class.

### A higher-dimensional pattern

An n-dimensional torus is the product of n circles. It has n angular one-forms, one for each circular direction. Their wedge products, taken over distinct directions, represent all cohomology classes. There is one degree-zero generator, n degree-one generators, a generator for each pair of directions in degree two, and so on. The number of independent degree-k classes is the number of ways to choose k directions out of n.

The full proof can be given by the de Rham Kunneth theorem or constructed directly from periodic forms. It is important not to present the pattern as a consequence of visual hole-counting alone. What makes it rigorous is the ability to reduce arbitrary closed forms to these constant-coefficient representatives, and the use of period integrals to prove their independence.

These computations explain what cohomology does. On a circle, one period detects every first cohomology class. On a two-torus, two loop periods detect first cohomology, and the total surface integral detects second cohomology. The objects are analytic differential forms, the tests are integrals, and the answers depend on the topology of the underlying space rather than on its choice of coordinates or metric.

## Mayer–Vietoris and Excision: Computing Homology by Gluing

Imagine trying to understand the topology of a sphere without calculating homology from the definition of singular chains. Cover it with two open regions, each slightly larger than a hemisphere. Each region is contractible, so its higher homology is trivial. The overlap is a band around the equator, homotopy equivalent to a circle. Somehow the homology of the sphere is hiding inside that overlap.

The Mayer–Vietoris sequence makes this intuition exact. It says that the homology of a union is related to the homology of its parts and their intersection by a long exact sequence. The word exact is doing real mathematical work: it tells us precisely which classes disappear, which survive, and which arise from gluing.

### A homology class can come from two sides

Let X be the union of open subspaces U and V. A cycle lying in U can be regarded as a cycle in X, and similarly for V. So there is a map from the direct sum of the homology groups of U and V into the homology of X.

But overlap creates redundancy. A cycle in U intersect V can be regarded as coming from either side. To account for that double counting, an earlier map sends a class in the intersection to its images in U and V, with opposite signs.

The Mayer–Vietoris sequence places these maps into a chain of groups connected by homomorphisms. For degree n, it runs from the n-th homology of the intersection to the direct sum of the n-th homologies of the parts, then to the n-th homology of the union, then back to the homology of the intersection one degree lower.

At each stop, the image of the preceding map equals the kernel of the next. This is what exactness means. The connecting map from a cycle in X to a class one degree lower in the overlap is especially important. It measures how pieces of a global cycle fail to be cycles separately when cut along their common region.

### A circle from two overlapping arcs

Cover a circle with two open arcs, each contractible, arranged so their intersection consists of two disjoint arcs. The intersection has two connected components. Each of U and V has one connected component.

Since the pieces and overlap have no positive-degree homology, the relevant portion of the long exact sequence says the first homology of the circle is the kernel of a map between zero-dimensional homology groups. The intersection contributes two integer generators, one for each component. Each of U and V contributes one.

The map sends a pair of integers from the two overlap components to their sum in U, and to its negative in V. Its kernel consists of pairs that sum to zero. That kernel is a copy of the integers. So we recover the first homology of the circle, entirely from information about connected components.

This example explains why Mayer–Vietoris is not just a formula for adding homology groups. The topology of the overlap and the signs in the maps matter. A circle's loop is visible as a disagreement between the two places where its arcs overlap.

### A sphere from two disks

Now return to the sphere. Cover it by U and V, two open sets each homotopy equivalent to a disk. Their intersection retracts to a circle. In degree two, the Mayer–Vietoris sequence reduces to a map from the second homology of the sphere into the first homology of the overlap.

The two disk-like regions have no second or first homology. Exactness forces that connecting map to be an isomorphism. Consequently the second homology of the sphere is the first homology of the circle: one copy of the integers.

We have turned a two-dimensional hole into a one-dimensional loop in the equatorial overlap. The dimension shift is not an accident. The connecting homomorphism detects the boundary created when a global cycle is split across the cover.

The same idea works for higher-dimensional spheres. Two overlapping contractible hemispherical regions have intersection homotopy equivalent to the sphere one dimension lower. An inductive Mayer–Vietoris calculation shows the top reduced homology of an n-sphere is the integers.

### Where the exact sequence comes from

The mechanism is chain-level bookkeeping. Singular chains supported in U or V form subcomplexes of chains in X. Their intersection is the chains supported in U intersect V. Adding the two subcomplexes produces a chain complex whose homology agrees with that of X, because singular simplices can be subdivided enough to lie in a member of the open cover.

This yields a short exact sequence of chain complexes. The resulting long exact sequence in homology is precisely Mayer–Vietoris. The connecting map can be constructed by lifting a global cycle to the sum of chains on the two sides, taking its boundary, and observing that the two boundaries cancel outside the overlap.

The proof explains both the sign convention and why the cover needs appropriate hypotheses. For an open cover, subdivision supplies the needed chain equivalence. For arbitrary subsets, one cannot apply the same statement without checking an excisive-cover condition.

### Excision: discard what does not matter

Excision is a related theorem with a different flavor. Suppose Z lies inside A and the closure of Z lies in the interior of A. Then removing Z from both X and A leaves the relative homology of the pair unchanged. The part removed was sufficiently deep inside the subspace A that it carried no new relative information.

Relative homology tracks cycles in X while treating chains inside A as negligible. Excision tells us we may often replace a large global pair with a small neighborhood of the feature we care about.

For example, local homology near a point of an n-dimensional manifold can be computed by replacing the manifold with a small coordinate ball and then using a ball relative to its punctured version. This ultimately records the dimension and local orientation behavior of the manifold. That is a different kind of locality from Mayer–Vietoris, but both depend on the fact that singular chains can be subdivided.

### The meaning of a gluing theorem

The advantage of exact sequences is compositionality. A complicated space can be decomposed into pieces, while the overlap carries the instructions for putting the homology back together. But a long exact sequence does not always determine every group without further information: we may need to calculate the maps, not just the ranks of the groups.

Mayer–Vietoris and excision are foundational because they make global topology computable from local data. Their ideas also reappear in sheaf theory, derived categories, and the geometry of manifolds. Gluing is not merely a picture. It is an algebraic process with kernels, images, connecting maps, and hypotheses that tell us when the process is valid.

### Homology: Cycles, Boundaries, and Holes

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** topological spaces, the fundamental group, oriented boundaries, elementary abelian groups. **Central question:** how can we measure holes in several dimensions with an algebra that is computable from finite pieces?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For an ordered simplicial \(k\)-simplex, the boundary is
\[
\partial_k[v_0,\ldots,v_k]
=\sum_{i=0}^k(-1)^i[v_0,\ldots,\widehat{v_i},\ldots,v_k],
\quad \partial_{k-1}\partial_k=0.
\]
Define \(Z_k=\ker\partial_k\) (cycles), \(B_k=\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}\) (boundaries), \(H_k=Z_k/B_k\). Over \(\mathbb Z\), each \(C_k\) is free abelian and finite complex boundary matrices can be analyzed with Smith normal form. Rank calculations over a field do not detect integral torsion.

**Circle versus disk.** On vertices \(v_0,v_1,v_2\), choose edges \(e_{01},e_{12},e_{20}\). The cycle \(z=e_{01}+e_{12}+e_{20}\) spans \(\ker\partial_1\cong\mathbb Z\). Without a two-simplex, \(H_1\cong\mathbb Z\). With the consistently oriented face \([v_0,v_1,v_2]\), \(\partial_2[v_0,v_1,v_2]=z\), and \(H_1=0\). In both connected spaces, \(H_0\cong\mathbb Z\).

The singular chain complex uses free abelian groups on continuous maps \(\Delta^k\to X\). Singular and simplicial homology agree for simplicial complexes. For path-connected \(X\), \(H_1(X;\mathbb Z)\cong\pi_1(X)_{\mathrm{ab}}\). Homotopy equivalences induce homology isomorphisms; the converse is false.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why must \(\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}\subseteq\ker\partial_k\)? **Answer:** \(\partial_k\partial_{k+1}=0\).
2. What is \(H_0\) of two disjoint contractible components? **Answer:** \(\mathbb Z^2\).
3. What is \(H_1\) of a filled triangular disk? **Answer:** zero, because its boundary cycle bounds its two-simplex.
4. Can rational homology distinguish \(\mathbb Z/2\) torsion from zero? **No:** tensoring with \(\mathbb Q\) kills finite torsion.

### Chain Complexes and Exact Sequences: How Topology Becomes Algebra

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** integers, kernels and images, the idea of homology. **Central question:** how can a drawing of holes produce calculations that can be checked line by line?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

A chain complex \((C_*,\partial)\) has homomorphisms \(\partial_n:C_n\to C_{n-1}\) with \(\partial_{n-1}\partial_n=0\). Its homology is \(H_n(C)=\ker\partial_n/\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}\). Exactness of \(A\overset f\longrightarrow B\overset g\longrightarrow C\) at \(B\) means \(\operatorname{im}f=\ker g\).

**Cellular models.** For \(S^1\), \(C_1=C_0=\mathbf Z\), \(\partial_1=0\), and \(C_2=0\), hence \(H_1(S^1)=\mathbf Z\). For \(D^2\), a CW structure with one zero-, one one-, and one two-cell attached by degree one has \(\partial_2=1\), so \(H_1(D^2)=0\). The usual minimal CW structure of \(\mathbf{RP}^2\) has \(C_0=C_1=C_2=\mathbf Z\), \(\partial_1=0\), \(\partial_2=2\); hence \(H_1(\mathbf{RP}^2;\mathbf Z)=\mathbf Z/2\), \(H_2=0\).

For a pair \((X,A)\), relative chains \(C_*(X,A)=C_*(X)/C_*(A)\) fit into a short exact sequence of complexes. Its connecting homomorphism gives the long exact sequence \(\cdots\to H_n(A)\to H_n(X)\to H_n(X,A)\overset\delta\to H_{n-1}(A)\to\cdots\). For \((D^2,S^1)\), \(H_2(D^2,S^1)=\mathbf Z\), and \(\delta:H_2(D^2,S^1)\to H_1(S^1)\) is an isomorphism, up to orientation sign.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Homology condition.** Why is \(\operatorname{im}\partial_{n+1}\subseteq\ker\partial_n\)? **Answer:** \(\partial_n\partial_{n+1}=0\).
2. **One missing face.** What happens to the class of a circular edge after a two-cell is attached with degree one? **Answer:** The edge becomes a boundary, so its first-homology class vanishes.
3. **Torsion.** Compute \(\ker(0:\mathbf Z\to\mathbf Z)/\operatorname{im}(2:\mathbf Z\to\mathbf Z)\). **Answer:** \(\mathbf Z/2\).
4. **Exact sequence.** Interpret the connecting map for a disk relative to its boundary. **Answer:** It takes the relative disk class to its oriented boundary loop.

### Calculating Homology of Circles, Spheres, and Tori

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** chain complexes, cycles and boundaries, exact sequences. **Central question:** when a theorem says we can calculate by gluing, what does a complete calculation look like?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(S^1=U\cup V\) with \(U,V\) contractible and \(U\cap V\) consisting of two contractible components, reduced Mayer–Vietoris yields \(\tilde H_1(S^1)\cong\tilde H_0(U\cap V)\cong\mathbf Z\). For \(S^n\), \(n\ge2\), hemispherical covers with \(U\cap V\simeq S^{n-1}\) yield \(\tilde H_k(S^n)\cong\tilde H_{k-1}(S^{n-1})\), so \(\tilde H_k(S^n;\mathbf Z)=\mathbf Z\) if \(k=n\) and zero otherwise.

For \(T^2\), a CW structure has \(C_2=\mathbf Z\), \(C_1=\mathbf Z a\oplus\mathbf Z b\), \(C_0=\mathbf Z\), \(\partial_1=0\), and \(\partial_2=0\), since the attaching word \(aba^{-1}b^{-1}\) has exponent sum zero in each generator. Consequently \(H_0=\mathbf Z\), \(H_1=\mathbf Z^2\), \(H_2=\mathbf Z\); \(\chi(T^2)=1-2+1=0\).

For \(\mathbf{RP}^2\), \(\partial_2:\mathbf Z\to\mathbf Z\) is multiplication by two, yielding \(H_1(\mathbf{RP}^2;\mathbf Z)=\mathbf Z/2\) but \(H_1(\mathbf{RP}^2;\mathbf R)=0\). These computations concern ordinary singular/cellular homology; de Rham cohomology is over \(\mathbf R\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Overlap.** Why is \(\tilde H_0(U\cap V)\) a single copy of \(\mathbf Z\) for the two-arc cover of \(S^1\)? **Answer:** Two components give ordinary \(H_0\cong\mathbf Z^2\), and reduced \(H_0\) removes one diagonal copy.
2. **Sphere.** Compute \(\tilde H_2(S^2)\) from the overlap of two disks. **Answer:** It is \(\tilde H_1(S^1)\cong\mathbf Z\).
3. **Torus.** What happens to the boundary of the square after edge identification? **Answer:** Each one-cell occurs with opposite signs, so the cellular boundary is zero.
4. **Coefficient test.** Compute \(\mathbf Z/2\otimes\mathbf R\). **Answer:** Zero, consistent with disappearance of first homology torsion over real coefficients.

### Integration over Cycles and the de Rham Map

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** smooth singular chains, boundaries, exterior derivatives, Stokes' theorem. **Question:** how does a differential form detect topological cycles?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a smooth singular \(k\)-simplex \(\sigma:\Delta^k\to M\), define \(I_\omega(\sigma)=\int_{\Delta^k}\sigma^*\omega\), and extend linearly over finite chains. Stokes gives
\[I_{d\eta}(c)=I_\eta(\partial c).\]
If \(d\omega=0\), then \(I_\omega(\partial b)=0\); if \(c\) is a cycle, then \(I_{d\eta}(c)=0\). Thus
\[H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\times H_k(M;\mathbb R)\longrightarrow\mathbb R,\quad([\omega],[c])\mapsto\int_c\omega.\]
The de Rham isomorphism is \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cong H^k_{\mathrm{sing}}(M;\mathbb R)\) for smooth manifolds; its construction uses the smooth-chain comparison. Over real coefficients, the universal coefficient theorem identifies singular cohomology with \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb R}(H_k(M;\mathbb R),\mathbb R)\).

On \(S^1\) with \(\eta=\frac1{2\pi}d\theta\) understood as a global one-form, \(\int_{S^1}\eta=1\). On \(T^2\) with angular coordinates modulo \(2\pi\), \(\omega=(2\pi)^{-2}d\theta\wedge d\phi\) integrates to one over the positively oriented fundamental cycle.

##### Check your understanding

2. If \(\omega=d\eta\) and \(\partial z=0\), what is \(\int_z\omega\)? **Answer:** zero.
3. Can an ordinary real-valued de Rham pairing detect torsion homology classes? **No:** torsion classes vanish after tensoring with \(\mathbb R\).

### Cohomology, Cup Products, and de Rham Classes

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** homology, differential forms, Stokes' theorem, basic linear algebra. **Central question:** how can functions on cycles detect topology, and why does multiplying cohomology classes reveal more than their group sizes?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a chain complex \((C_\bullet,\partial)\), cochains with coefficients in an abelian group \(G\) are \(C^k=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(C_k,G)\), with \(\delta\varphi=\varphi\circ\partial\). Then \(H^k(C;G)=\ker\delta^k/\operatorname{im}\delta^{k-1}\).

On a smooth manifold, \(H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)=\ker(d:\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k+1})/\operatorname{im}(d:\Omega^{k-1}\to\Omega^k)\). De Rham's theorem identifies this with \(H^k(M;\mathbb R)\). For \(M=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\), the form
\[
\omega=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2},\qquad d\omega=0,\qquad\int_{S^1}\omega=2\pi
\]
represents a nonzero class. No globally defined smooth function \(f\) satisfies \(df=\omega\).

The cup product gives \(H^p(X;R)\times H^q(X;R)\to H^{p+q}(X;R)\), graded commutative on cohomology:
\[
a\smile b=(-1)^{pq}b\smile a.
\]
For the oriented torus \(T^2\), with \(a,b\in H^1(T^2;\mathbb Z)\) and \(u\in H^2(T^2;\mathbb Z)\) suitably oriented, \(a\smile b=u\), \(b\smile a=-u\), and \(a^2=b^2=0\). The wedge \(S^1\vee S^1\vee S^2\) has the same cohomology groups in nonnegative degrees but all positive-degree cup products zero.

1. What is \(H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)\)? **Answer:** \(\mathbb R\).
2. Does a closed form always equal \(df\) globally? **No:** the angular form on the punctured plane is a counterexample.
3. What degree has the product of a class in \(H^1\) with one in \(H^2\)? **Answer:** degree three.
4. Does real de Rham cohomology detect integral torsion? **No:** torsion classes vanish when passing to real coefficients.
5. Why do identical cohomology group ranks not guarantee equivalent cohomology rings? **Answer:** the cup-product maps can differ, as for \(T^2\) and \(S^1\vee S^1\vee S^2\).

### Computing Cohomology of Circles and Tori

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** closed and exact forms, integration over cycles, Stokes' theorem. **Question:** can we compute de Rham cohomology rather than merely define it?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

On \(S^1=\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)\),
\[H^0_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)=\mathbb R,\quad H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)=\mathbb R[d\theta],\quad H^{k>1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)=0.\]
An arbitrary \(f(\theta)d\theta\) is exact iff \(\int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta)\,d\theta=0\), since a primitive on \(\mathbb R\) descends iff its period vanishes.

On \(T^2=(\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z)^2\),
\[
H^0=\mathbb R,\quad H^1=\mathbb R[d\theta]\oplus\mathbb R[d\phi],\quad H^2=\mathbb R[d\theta\wedge d\phi].
\]
For a closed \(\omega=P\,d\theta+Q\,d\phi\), set
\[a={1\over2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}P(\theta,\phi_0)\,d\theta,\qquad b={1\over2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}Q(\theta_0,\phi)\,d\phi.\]
Closedness \(\partial_\theta Q=\partial_\phi P\) makes these independent of \(\phi_0,\theta_0\). Then \(\omega-a\,d\theta-b\,d\phi\) has zero periods and is exact.

**Explicit two-form primitive when the average vanishes.** Let \(h(\theta,\phi)\) be smooth periodic with \(\int_{T^2}h\,d\theta d\phi=0\). Set
\[f(\theta)={1\over 2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}h(\theta,s)\,ds,\quad b(\theta)=\int_0^\theta f(t)\,dt,\quad k(\theta,\phi)=\int_0^\phi(h(\theta,s)-f(\theta))\,ds.\]
Both \(b\) and \(k\) are periodic in their respective angular variables. The one-form \(\beta=b(\theta)d\phi-k(\theta,\phi)d\theta\) satisfies \(d\beta=h\,d\theta\wedge d\phi\).

For \(T^n\), \(H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(T^n)\cong\Lambda^*(\mathbb R^n)^*\) and \(\dim H^k=\binom nk\).

##### Check your understanding

1. Does the form \(d\theta\) on \(S^1\) have a global real-valued primitive? **No:** its period is \(2\pi\).
2. What is \([d\phi]\wedge[d\theta]\) on the torus? **Answer:** \(-[d\theta\wedge d\phi]\).
3. How many independent degree-two classes does the three-torus have? **Answer:** three, corresponding to the three choices of pairs of coordinates.

### Mayer–Vietoris and Excision: Computing Homology by Gluing

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** singular homology, exact sequences, open covers, chain complexes. **Central question:** if a space is assembled from two simpler pieces, how does their overlap determine its holes?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For an excisive cover \(X=U\cup V\) (in particular, for an open cover), the Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence in singular homology has terms
\[\cdots\to H_n(U\cap V)\xrightarrow{(i_*,-j_*)}H_n(U)\oplus H_n(V)\xrightarrow{k_*+\ell_*}H_n(X)\xrightarrow{\partial}H_{n-1}(U\cap V)\to\cdots.\]

**Circle:** choose contractible arcs \(U,V\subset S^1\) with \(U\cap V=A\sqcup B\), two contractible components. Then \(H_1(S^1)\cong\ker(\mathbb Z^2\to\mathbb Z^2)\), where \((a,b)\mapsto(a+b,-a-b)\), hence \(H_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z\).

**Sphere:** choose contractible hemispherical neighborhoods \(U,V\subset S^2\) with \(U\cap V\simeq S^1\). Exactness gives \(H_2(S^2)\cong H_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z\).

**Excision:** if \(Z\subset A\subset X\) with \(\overline Z\subset\operatorname{int}_X A\), the inclusion \((X\setminus Z,A\setminus Z)\hookrightarrow(X,A)\) induces isomorphisms on all singular homology groups. More general excisive conditions exist.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why is the overlap of two arcs on a circle allowed to have two components? **Answer:** each arc covers almost all the circle but omits a different small closed interval; their shared region can split into two disjoint arcs.
2. Can we determine \(H_1(S^1)\) using only the number of components of \(U,V,U\cap V\)? **Here yes:** exactness and vanishing higher homology give the kernel \(\mathbb Z\).
3. For a sphere covered by two contractible open sets with annular overlap, what gives the top homology? **Answer:** the connecting homomorphism \(H_2(S^2)\to H_1(S^1)\) is an isomorphism.
4. What goes wrong if we remove a set without excision hypotheses? **Answer:** relative cycles near the removed boundary may change; excision does not apply automatically to arbitrary pairs.
