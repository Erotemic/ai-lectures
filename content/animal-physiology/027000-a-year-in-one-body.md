# A Year in One Body: The Elephant Seal's Physiological Budget

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At Año Nuevo, on the coast of California, a female northern elephant seal comes out of the Pacific in winter. She has spent most of the previous eight months at sea. She may have traveled thousands of kilometers. During those months she has been swimming, diving, capturing prey, making the chemical adjustments that sustain a pregnancy, and storing the fuel that will support her next visit to land. Now she comes ashore carrying a developing pup. Very soon, she will give birth. For the weeks that follow she will produce rich milk, and she will eat nothing.

That is an extraordinary way to make a living, but the interesting part is not a list of the seal's extraordinary properties. It is the way the properties depend on one another. A dive that costs too much energy can reduce the reserves available for lactation months later. A change in stored fat changes buoyancy, and buoyancy changes how much swimming is needed to move through the ocean. The same circulation that carries oxygen to the brain during a dive must deliver nutrients to a mammary gland during the fast. A hormone response that helps one animal survive a disturbance may have different consequences for this animal depending on whether she is foraging or has already spent weeks fasting. Physiological systems are not independent compartments. They share the same body, the same surroundings, and the same limited resources.

We will follow a representative female northern elephant seal through one annual cycle. She is a teaching example assembled from studies of many real individuals, not a documented seal to whom we can attribute an invented sequence of thoughts, dives, or pregnancies. Researchers have identified individual animals in long-term breeding records and recovered instruments after their long trips. The resulting data allow a much more rigorous story than one reconstructed from an imagined viewpoint. Our question is how a whole animal pays for the life it lives.

Begin with the annual calendar. Most adult females give birth during the northern winter, nurse a single pup for roughly four weeks, mate near the end of nursing, and depart for a relatively short foraging trip. They return to shore in spring to replace the coat and outer skin in a concentrated molt, then embark on a much longer ocean journey that lasts into the following winter. The timing varies among animals and colonies; these are approximate stages, not appointments kept to the day. Because elephant seals have delayed implantation, fertilization near the end of nursing does not immediately start the full period of fetal growth. The early embryo remains unattached for a time, and implantation occurs around the spring molt. Much of the subsequent long foraging trip is also a period of gestation.

This sequence already gives us a problem in accounting. The female has two intervals in which she can eat freely at sea and two intervals in which she is on land fasting. The shorter sea trip helps replenish reserves exhausted by lactation; the molt spends some of those reserves; the long trip must finance both her own maintenance and a fetus, then leave enough stored energy for another month of intensive milk production. It is not enough to ask whether she eats enough on one Tuesday. The meaning of a meal depends on which bills will come due later in the year.

How do we know this annual pattern? It is not inferred from one photograph of seals on the beach. At Año Nuevo, Burney Le Boeuf, Richard Condit, Joanne Reiter, and colleagues built decades of observations of marked females, following the timing and success of breeding and survival. Modern biologging adds different kinds of information. Satellite transmitters and location reconstructions follow travel; pressure sensors measure dive depth and duration; accelerometers track movement and swimming strokes; other measurements estimate body condition, oxygen capacity, and metabolism. A large open data set published in 2024 brought together roughly two decades of three-dimensional movement records for adult females. Each instrument measures a specific quantity, however. A dive trace is not automatically a direct measure of a meal, a hormone concentration, a heart rate, or an animal's intentions. The scientific work is to connect these observations without pretending they are interchangeable.

Now follow the animal back into the ocean after weaning. Elephant seals breathe air, but they hunt in water. Air breathing offers a high-oxygen environment when they reach the surface; prey lives under a different physical regime. With each descent the seal must leave the next breath behind. Diving is therefore not simply locomotion. It is locomotion under a time-dependent oxygen constraint.

Imagine a bank account with several places to store the same currency. Oxygen is held in the lungs, in the blood, and bound to myoglobin in the muscles. A diving mammal can carry large blood volumes and substantial quantities of oxygen-binding pigment compared with many terrestrial mammals. In measurements of adult female northern elephant seals, blood volume averaged around one fifth of body mass. Researchers also measured high muscle myoglobin concentrations. These features increase the available oxygen store, although the usable amount during a dive depends on what the animal is doing and how its circulation is controlled. The statement that elephant seals have large stores is not the same as saying every tissue has unlimited access to them.

Oxygen is consumed when cells use chemical energy. At a fixed oxygen store, higher aerobic expenditure generally means less time before that store is depleted. The seal can change both sides of this relationship. Swimming mechanics influence demand. Cardiovascular control influences where blood flows and at what rate. During submergence, the diving response can include a decrease in heart rate and constriction of some peripheral blood vessels, conserving oxygen for organs where interruption would be dangerous. This response is variable. It is not a single switch that forces every dive into an identical state. Measurements of seals diving voluntarily in a laboratory recorded reduced heart rates and oxygen consumption relative to surface conditions, but the authors warned that such measurements alone cannot fully describe a wild animal hunting at sea.

It is tempting to picture the seal simply turning off its muscles to save oxygen. That would make prey capture difficult. Instead, the animal coordinates costly activity, relatively economical swimming, and selective blood flow across the time course of a dive. The brain and heart still need support; working muscles may draw from their own myoglobin and local stores; some tissues can tolerate restricted oxygen delivery for limited periods. Researchers have documented that diving mammals can experience arterial oxygen levels that would be alarming in a human patient. That does not mean they are immune to hypoxia. It means their tissues, circulation, and behavior have evolved to operate in a different range of controlled exposures.

There is a further complication. Pressure rises rapidly with depth. Gas spaces compress, and the pattern of lung gas exchange changes as the seal descends. In marine mammals, collapse of gas-exchanging regions at depth is part of the discussion of nitrogen uptake and oxygen exchange, although exactly how gases behave depends on species, lung mechanics, and the dive. A deep dive has a different physiological history from a shallow one of the same duration. When we say the seal can stay down for a long time, we have not yet explained what happened during the descent, bottom phase, and ascent. Those are distinct phases in an oxygen budget.

An oxygen budget by itself is also incomplete. The seal does not earn anything merely by remaining underwater. To rebuild her fat stores she has to catch prey while spending less energy than the prey provides. Her foraging trips carry her into the open northeastern Pacific, where prey may be scattered vertically through the water column. Time-depth traces document repeated deep dives. More recent high-resolution sensors can register body movements associated with prey capture. These are useful estimates of feeding activity, not the same as visually identifying every fish or squid swallowed. In some monitored adult females, the estimated number of prey-capture events exceeds a thousand per day. A strategy of taking many relatively small prey items makes the cumulative efficiency of thousands of descents and ascents consequential.

The physics of swimming now enters our story. Moving an animal through water requires overcoming drag and changing its momentum. The streamlined shape of a seal reduces resistance. Flipper strokes require muscular work, but a seal can also glide. It is easy to say that gliding saves energy. More interestingly, the cost of moving can change as the animal becomes better fed.

Fat is less dense than many of the animal's lean tissues. A lean elephant seal at the start of a trip tends to be negatively buoyant, so with its lungs compressed at depth, it may sink when it stops swimming. As she adds lipid reserves, the same animal becomes less negatively buoyant; some individuals approach neutral buoyancy during the longer migration. The buoyancy shift changes the work needed to rise and the opportunities to use passive descent. Measurements that combine swimming strokes with characteristic drift phases can help estimate how body composition changes over time. Drift is informative, but not an infallible scale: pregnancy, changes in lean tissue, trapped gas, and water conditions can complicate how a given descent rate relates to fat gain.

Here is a feedback loop worth following carefully. A lean seal returning to sea may need to spend more work moving its body through the water. If she finds prey and builds fat, her buoyancy changes and economical gliding becomes easier in some phases of the dive. Lower movement costs leave more of the energy acquired from prey available for storage. The increasing reserves then change the costs of later movement. Physiology is altering behavior's mechanical price, while the behavior is altering the physiological state. This is more interesting than a statement that blubber is insulation or that muscle contracts by actin and myosin. Those facts matter because they change the net outcome of the seal's annual journey.

We can test whether the energetic effect is large enough to care about. In a 2015 study led by Jennifer Maresh and colleagues, researchers combined movement measurements and body-condition changes to construct energy budgets for adult female northern elephant seals. Estimated field metabolic rates during the short post-breeding trip averaged around ninety kilojoules per kilogram per day, while those during the long, pregnant foraging trip averaged about sixty-nine kilojoules per kilogram per day. These are measurements estimated for groups under particular conditions, not universal metabolic constants. Their significance is that an animal that remains active at sea and is also pregnant can show remarkably economical energy expenditure. Long-trip differences reflected both locomotor economy and apparent metabolic suppression, though partitioning the components depends on the estimation method.

The study included a particularly revealing contrast. Three additional seals carried large, less streamlined instruments that increased drag. In those animals a greater share of available energy went into locomotion, and their mass gain was substantially lower than in the normally instrumented group. This was a small comparison involving a physical intervention, not a license to predict a universal percentage loss from any obstacle. But it demonstrates a real possibility: changing the cost of swimming can change the resources available for future breeding, even when the animal continues to forage. A small extra cost repeated across many thousands of strokes need not remain a small yearly consequence.

There is a caution here about scientific measurements themselves. A tag is not an invisible observer. A sufficiently bulky device changes the flow of water around the animal. If the device changes the swimming cost we hope to measure, the measurement system has become part of the experiment. Modern bio-logging researchers therefore care deeply about instrument shape, placement, mass, and deployment duration. This is not a minor technical footnote. It is a case in which the same hydrodynamic principle being studied has to be applied to the scientist's apparatus.

Food acquisition connects digestion, circulation, and stored energy. A swallowed prey animal contains lipids, proteins, water, and other materials. Digestion breaks usable molecules down and absorbs them. Circulation distributes substrates to tissues; hormonal signals affect whether they are oxidized now, incorporated into tissues, or stored. An animal cannot put an intact fish directly into a fat cell. Its digestive and metabolic systems have to transform materials, and that transformation incurs costs. When researchers estimate the seal's energy budget, they distinguish energy ingested from energy lost in waste and from energy spent on digestion, maintenance, swimming, reproduction, and growth.

One simplified way to hear the equation is this. Change in stored body energy equals usable energy acquired from food, minus energy spent maintaining and operating the animal and minus energy transferred to offspring. In real field work, the categories have measurement error, and the body is not a sealed jar of pure fat. Mass gain may include water and lean tissue as well as lipid. The distinction matters: a kilogram of stored fat and a kilogram of water do not contain the same chemical energy. It is possible to gain mass without gaining equivalent future fasting capacity, and to lose mass in ways that matter differently for reproduction and diving.

The ocean creates other constraints. Water conducts heat far more effectively than air, and the cold water surrounding a seal imposes a continuous thermal problem. Blubber helps slow heat loss, while blood flow through the skin changes the delivery of core heat toward the surface. Yet extra fat is not free insulation. It alters body size, swimming mechanics, and the amount of reserve carried. Nor is the goal always to retain as much heat as possible: during strenuous activity, a warm-bodied animal may also need routes to release it. The useful concept is a controllable heat budget, not a blanket claim that thick animals are warm because they are thick.

Water balance also changes with the setting. At sea, a seal's diet contains water and metabolism produces some water as energy-rich molecules are oxidized. On land during the fast, she cannot solve dehydration by visiting a stream or eating another fish. Oxidizing stored fat produces metabolic water, while breathing, urine, milk, and evaporation remove it. Renal and respiratory conservation become integral to the fasting strategy. Studies of fasting northern elephant seals demonstrate low losses and protein-sparing metabolism. In experiments on fasting pups, extended breathing pauses and nasal structures that recover moisture from exhaled air were associated with reduced respiratory water loss. Those pup experiments should not be casually treated as a direct measurement of every lactating mother's respiratory budget, but they reveal the mechanisms available within the species.

We can see the interdependence again. Burning stored fat provides ATP, but it also produces water. Burning protein for energy increases the need to dispose of nitrogenous wastes. A mother who preserves protein and relies heavily on fat can conserve important tissue while reducing the water required to eliminate nitrogen. At the same time, she has to make milk containing both energy and water. Thus an energy-storage strategy also becomes a water-management strategy. The circulation that handles oxygen and glucose, the kidney that handles solutes, and the mammary gland that handles milk do not get separate copies of the body's resources.

The animal also needs to rest. But how can a mammal sleep while swimming and diving almost continuously for months? For years, researchers inferred rest from the shape of unusual dives, especially prolonged drifts. A drifting seal is not necessarily asleep, however. The decisive advance came when scientists recorded electrical brain activity in free-ranging northern elephant seals. In research published in 2023, Jessica Kendall-Bar and collaborators paired measurements of brain waves with depth and motion. They identified brief sleep periods on dives, including slow-wave sleep and rapid-eye-movement sleep. In some sequences the animal glided down, lost ordinary postural control, and entered a descending spiral before waking and returning toward the surface.

The distinction between behavior and brain evidence matters. A stationary depth trace cannot by itself demonstrate sleep, just as a swimming stroke cannot demonstrate a meal. Electrical activity provided a more direct criterion. By relating these detailed measurements to a much larger set of dive records, the researchers estimated that elephant seals in the open ocean averaged roughly two hours of sleep per day over months at sea. On land, their sleep pattern was dramatically different. The ocean is not just a feeding site that the seal happens to occupy. It also changes where sleep can occur, how long it can last, and how it fits around breathing and the risk of being attacked near the surface.

This does not mean the seal has defeated the need for sleep, or that every animal could safely reduce sleep by taking short naps. The functions and costs of sleep remain active research questions. The seal's behavior is a species-specific solution to constraints that include surfacing, predators, buoyancy, and the ability to remain submerged. It is a particularly good example of an organ system being organized around ecology: neural sleep states occur inside the timetable permitted by diving mechanics and oxygen storage.

Eventually the shorter foraging trip ends. The female returns to shore in spring and begins a concentrated molt. Elephant seals replace the outer coat and epidermal layer, and during this time they generally fast. The body that has spent months economizing on underwater movement now has a different task. It must support tissue replacement while receiving no new food. A molt is not simply hair falling out. Building new skin and coat requires material, cellular activity, and regulation; it occurs while the animal's other needs continue. Nor can the female ignore temperature. Returning to land changes the balance of heat loss and the ways she can control it.

Hormonal coordination is particularly revealing here. It is easy to teach the stress response as a sequence of hormone names: signals from the brain, pituitary, and adrenal glands eventually alter circulating glucocorticoids. The seal makes us ask what the response means in a body that is midway through a long fast. In a 2023 study, scientists administered the pituitary hormone ACTH to adult female northern elephant seals early and late in the molt, then followed hormone, metabolic, and immune measurements. The adrenal responses were associated with the animals' remaining fat reserves. After extended fasting, the responses were larger. Changes in thyroid hormones and an inflammatory signaling molecule were also detected after the challenge.

The interpretation needs care. An ACTH injection is a controlled physiological challenge, not proof that these animals experience the same hormone time course every time a boat passes. One measured cytokine is not the entire immune system, and a change in hormone concentration does not measure all future reproductive consequences. Still, the result has a powerful implication: the meaning of a chemical signal depends on the animal's current reserves and life-history stage. Stress physiology, metabolic regulation, and immune function are not three independent stories. The body condition left by the previous foraging trip can influence the response to the next disturbance.

Following the molt, the female returns to sea for the longer trip. By now, a fertilized embryo has been present for months, but pregnancy has not followed the timetable we might assume from human experience. Elephant seals exhibit delayed implantation: the early embryo does not implant immediately after fertilization, allowing the short feeding interval and the molt to occur before the main period of fetal development. The details of implantation timing are described in field and reproductive studies, but the delay is not evidence of conscious scheduling by the mother. It is a reproductive feature of the lineage, coordinated with its seasonal cycle.

Why might a delay of implantation matter? Without it, an early pregnancy might have to compete directly with the mother's depleted state immediately after lactation and again with the energy demands of the spring molt. Moving most fetal growth into the later foraging interval places an additional cost at a time when the female is again acquiring food. It also helps align winter births with the breeding season. This is an explanation of the allocation structure; to establish the evolutionary origin of the trait would require comparisons among species and evidence about reproductive success. A convenient outcome is not by itself proof of why a trait first evolved.

As the long migration proceeds, the mother is simultaneously maintaining tissues, swimming, diving, catching prey, storing fat, and sustaining a developing fetus. A fetus is a physical and physiological demand, not merely a label attached to her condition. It receives nutrients and oxygen through maternal exchange, and the mother has to accommodate growth while maintaining her own circulation. She needs sufficient reserves for the coming lactation fast. Notice how different the time scales are: a single descent lasts minutes, a feeding trip lasts months, gestation spans much of a year, and successful breeding extends across repeated annual cycles. Physiological coordination must work on all of them.

The long view changes our understanding of a good dive. A dive can be successful in the immediate sense if it obtains prey. It can be good for the current migration if the prey's usable energy exceeds the costs of capture. And it can contribute to reproductive success if the surplus ultimately reaches the conditions that permit birth and nursing. Those are nested tests, not interchangeable ones. A researcher might directly count probable prey strikes with a jaw or head sensor, estimate energy gain from mass and buoyancy changes, and later observe a weaned pup. Each measurement answers a different part of the chain. Inference becomes stronger when the chain is made explicit and weaker when all these outcomes are called simply 'foraging success.'

Now we come back to the winter rookery. The female hauls out, gives birth, and begins transferring stored resources at an astonishing rate. Northern elephant seal milk becomes exceptionally rich in lipid over the nursing period, reaching roughly half its mass in fat near the end. Physiological research has estimated that a mother can deliver more than a hundred liters of milk and on the order of two thousand megajoules of milk energy to her pup over several weeks. These are typical-scale estimates from studied mothers, not a fixed allotment for every pup. The energy is transferred from reserves accumulated in earlier months, and the mother has no food intake to make up the difference while she nurses.

This is where the opening scene acquires its real meaning. A mother arriving heavy with stored fat and leaving much lighter is not simply an animal that happened to go hungry. She is carrying out a scheduled transfer from her own tissues into another growing body. Milk production depends on mammary-cell activity, endocrine regulation, water balance, and circulation. The pup's rapid mass gain depends on those maternal systems. Every day of nursing spends some of the reserve needed to maintain the mother and to help her survive until she can forage again. The costs of the previous ocean migration arrive on the beach in the form of how much milk the female can produce.

There is no single organ that makes this possible. Fat tissue supplies substrates. The circulation carries them to the mammary gland. The kidneys and respiratory system help keep enough water in the body. Hormonal signals support lactation and reorganize fuel use. The immune system maintains tissue defenses and responds to the microbial conditions of the crowded rookery, but its performance must be measured rather than presumed to improve or worsen in a particular way. A fast can be compatible with normal function in one well-adapted stage without being harmless under every disturbance. When scientists measured protein use and kidney function during lactation, they found changes late in the fasting period consistent with constraints on how long maternal investment can be sustained.

The pup receives the result of this integrated physiology. At weaning it has substantial fat reserves, but it does not immediately enter the ocean as an accomplished deep diver. Weanlings spend weeks on land fasting while their respiratory pigments, cardiovascular responses, movement, and other capacities develop. A pup must eventually translate stored material into the ability to capture new food. The mother's allocation has bought time for that transition; it has not eliminated the dangers of the first independent foraging trips. Conditions and success later in life vary widely, and a heavy weanling is not guaranteed survival.

Long-term records reveal how consequential those early risks are. In the Año Nuevo study summarized by the National Park Service, about three quarters of monitored females died before reaching breeding age. Among those that did breed, lifetime output varied substantially; a small fraction produced many surviving pups across long lives, while many bred only a few times. This distribution is not a story about some animals trying harder. Survival, body size, rookery position, feeding success, and chance all influence what is possible. It is also a reminder that a spectacular physiology does not cancel ecological mortality. A seal can be capable of profound hypoxia tolerance and still be eaten by a predator or fail to find enough prey.

The biological question is therefore not 'Why has evolution made an ideal seal?' There is no ideal solution independent of the animal's constraints. A larger reserve improves the ability to fast but changes swimming dynamics. A longer dive offers additional time under water but requires oxygen management. A shorter time at the surface may reduce exposure, but less breathing time can limit recovery. A longer lactation might increase energy transferred to the pup, but the mother has limited reserves and must eventually return to sea. A stronger acute endocrine response might aid one challenge while affecting other systems during an extended fast. These are interacting tradeoffs, not flaws to be repaired by a clever design.

We can now state a useful whole-body model. Start with a state: stored fat and lean tissue, oxygen capacity, pregnancy stage, hydration, thermal condition, and information about the current environment. In any moment the animal acts—dives, swims, rests, hunts, breathes, nurses—and those actions alter its state. Feedback from sensors and hormones changes subsequent actions and allocation. Outside the body are changing prey fields, ocean temperature, pressure, predators, and breeding opportunities. The time evolution of the animal is the result of that coupled system. A diagram that ignores the outside world is incomplete, but a description of habitat that ignores the animal's physiology is also incomplete.

It is possible to write the outline as accounting identities without pretending that biology is simple. Energy gained must be transformed and divided between expenditure, stores, and offspring. Oxygen stored or gained at the surface is consumed at a rate shaped by activity and circulation. Body water is replenished from food and metabolism and lost through urine, evaporation, milk, and other routes. These budgets interact through the same molecules and tissues: lipids affect energy, water, and buoyancy; circulation affects oxygen delivery and heat exchange; endocrine responses affect multiple rates. The equations are not explanations on their own. They identify the quantities we need to measure before an explanation can be tested.

One final point concerns what a field study can and cannot tell us. A seal instrumented off the California coast does not represent every elephant seal in every ocean, and a result in a small group may depend on body size or season. A short experiment that increases drag can identify an energetic cost but cannot measure the whole evolutionary history of streamlined swimming. Hormone challenge data can identify physiological responsiveness but not reveal every effect of disturbance on future offspring. A lifetime census can quantify survival and breeding but may leave the animal's underwater experiences uncertain. Good integrative physiology does not erase these boundaries; it crosses them by connecting multiple measurements while marking where evidence stops.

Consider the winter mother again. What appears on the beach as a single animal nursing a single pup is also the delayed result of thousands of dives, countless strokes, many meals, months of energy conservation, a period of pregnancy, and a coordinated water and nutrient economy. When she leaves, the next cycle begins. Her condition has changed; the ocean has changed; the next pup, if there is one, will be supported by a new sequence of successes and costs. To understand an animal, we do not simply add up what its organs do. We follow how the work of one system changes the possibilities available to every other system over time. That is the task of integrative physiology, and it is why a year in one body can teach us what no inventory of organs can.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### The annual calendar is a population pattern

The lecture follows a *representative* adult female northern elephant seal (*Mirounga angustirostris*). It does not reconstruct the life of one documented tagged individual.

| Approximate phase | Immediate demands | Cross-system links |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Winter, breeding haul-out | Parturition, lactation and fasting | Stored fat, milk synthesis, water conservation, reproductive and stress endocrinology |
| Late winter to spring, post-breeding foraging | Recover body condition after lactation | Oxygen stores and diving response, prey capture, mechanical cost of locomotion, fat recovery |
| Spring, onshore molt | Skin and coat renewal while fasting | Tissue biosynthesis, thermal balance, endocrine response, immune state, stored reserves |
| Late spring through winter, long foraging trip | Long-distance foraging, gestation and reserve accumulation | Locomotor efficiency, fat-dependent buoyancy, fetal nutrient supply, diving, sleep |
| Next winter | Return and renewed investment, when breeding succeeds | Maternal state carries the history of the preceding year |

The timing varies among colonies and individuals. Short post-breeding trips typically last around 2–2.5 months; long trips roughly 7–8 months.

### Three linked conservation budgets

- **Energy:** Change in body energy stores = metabolizable energy acquired − maintenance and activity expenditure − energy allocated to production and offspring. This is a conceptual accounting identity, not a validated estimator for any single individual.
- **Oxygen:** An approximate aerobic dive limit depends on *usable oxygen stores / rate of oxygen consumption*. Neither factor is constant across dives or tissues. Lung collapse, circulation, changing activity, anaerobic contributions, and recovery modify the naive estimate.
- **Water:** Change in body water = dietary water + metabolic water − urinary, respiratory, evaporative and reproductive water losses. Fasting seals have low water loss and derive water from oxidizing stored energy; water balance cannot be inferred from body mass alone.

**Important couplings:** Body fat is energy storage, insulation, and a contributor to buoyancy. Reduced drag changes the energetic cost of feeding. Blood circulation transports oxygen, heat, nutrients and endocrine signals. Lactation spends reserves accumulated over preceding foraging trips; it is not fueled by contemporaneous eating on the beach.

### Measurement versus inference

| Method | Direct observation | Inference needing a model or other data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure logger | Depth versus time | Foraging success, oxygen expenditure, sleep |
| Accelerometer and stroke logger | Movement and stroke dynamics | Mechanical work and energy expenditure |
| Drift-rate analysis | Passive vertical movement in water | Body composition and nutritional condition |
| Brain-wave EEG | Electrical sleep-stage signatures | Population-level sleep patterns by extrapolation to broader dive records |
| Body composition and serial mass | Changes in mass and tissue proportions | Metabolizable energy acquisition and allocation |
| Hormone challenge | Measured response after ACTH injection | Responses to unobserved natural disturbances or long-term fitness effects |
| Individual mark–resight records | Observed reproduction and return to colonies | Detailed causes of mortality at sea |

The 2015 drag comparison used three seals with extra non-streamlined equipment. It indicates how extra drag can change locomotion costs and body gain, but it should not be generalized as a precise effect of all tagging procedures or disturbances.

### Questions to work through

1. How can acquiring fat simultaneously make the next swim less expensive and the next fast longer?
2. Why does the maximum duration of a dive not by itself determine its energetic efficiency?
3. What new evidence does an EEG add to a depth trace resembling a drift or sleep dive?
4. Which stage of the annual cycle is most vulnerable to a small persistent increase in locomotor drag, and what would you need to measure to answer?
5. Why is a high cortisol response after ACTH insufficient to conclude that immune defense has generally failed?
6. Why would an annual census of successful pups and a record of prey-capture events answer different parts of the same life-history question?
