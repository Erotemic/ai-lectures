# Who Feeds the Young?

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A female strawberry poison frog climbs into a small pool of water held inside a plant. A tadpole waits there, unable to travel to the forest floor to collect its own food. The mother deposits an unfertilized egg, which the tadpole consumes. This scene is not merely parental attention. It is a transfer of material from the body of one animal into the growth of another. The parent has paid the cost of making that egg without using it for another fertilization. The tadpole has received food that it could not easily obtain in its restricted nursery. Reproduction did not finish when the tadpole hatched. Its physiology still depended on an older animal.

Many animals provide no comparable care after releasing their eggs. Others defend nests, warm offspring, clean them, deliver food, carry them, or teach behaviors that improve survival. The relevant question is not which animal loves its young more. It is what a particular form of care accomplishes, what the parent gives up, and how its costs depend on the environment. A behavior that saves offspring under one condition may produce little additional benefit under another. The same parent may also face a conflict between raising the current brood and retaining resources for a future brood.

For an evolutionary analysis, parental investment is not simply anything an adult happens to do near offspring. Robert Trivers's 1972 formulation emphasized investment that increases the offspring's prospects at a cost to a parent's capacity to invest in other offspring. The cost may be energy, food, time, risk, or lost future reproduction. This definition describes consequences, not intentions. A bird guarding eggs may expend time it could have spent feeding; a mammal producing milk uses energy and water; a fish fanning eggs may increase ventilation while sacrificing body condition. The benefit must be measured in what happens to offspring, and the cost in what happens to the parent or its subsequent opportunities.

Consider a nest of fish eggs in water with little dissolved oxygen. Embryos cannot fan the water themselves, but a guarding parent can move its fins to refresh the water over the clutch. More flow reduces the thickness of the stagnant layer around the eggs and can help maintain the gradient that drives oxygen into embryonic tissue. That makes the behavior a direct extension of respiratory physiology. A parent is operating part of its offspring's gas-exchange system. But moving fins requires energy, and time spent fanning is time not spent doing something else.

In 1999 J. C. Jones and J. D. Reynolds reported an experiment on the common goby, a fish in which males provide care for eggs. They compared males under lower and normal dissolved-oxygen conditions across consecutive breeding cycles. Under low oxygen, males increased the time and tempo of egg fanning. They lost more body mass in the first breeding attempt and were more likely to abandon care during the second. Males that completed care could still achieve hatching success similar to that of controls, though hatching was delayed. This is the kind of evidence needed to establish an allocation cost: a useful response under environmental stress was followed by a measurable effect on the parent's later performance. Merely observing that a fish fans a nest would not have demonstrated that tradeoff.

The example also shows why it can be misleading to count only the number of eggs initially produced. A clutch may contain many eggs, but if local oxygen falls or predators reach the nest, fewer may survive. Parental behavior can raise survival without increasing the initial number. Conversely, if maintaining the clutch demands so much effort that a parent loses future breeding opportunities, the strategy has costs that the current clutch alone will not reveal. The appropriate currency is not always simply eggs laid or chicks hatched today; it can include survival to breeding age and the parent's remaining reproductive life.

The strawberry poison frog offers a more chemically elaborate example. Some mothers return to isolated nursery sites repeatedly to feed tadpoles unfertilized eggs. The eggs supply nutrients, but the story goes further. Poison frogs acquire defensive alkaloids from components of their diet. A field and laboratory literature has asked whether those defenses reach the tadpole through maternal food. In 2019 Ralph Saporito and colleagues supplied captive female frogs with a measurable synthetic alkaloid, then detected it in mothers, eggs, and developing tadpoles. This supported a direct route by which a parent provisions both nutrition and a chemical defense. A tadpole's immune or predator-defense state is not wholly its own achievement; it can be partly assembled through what its parent consumes and transfers.

This result does not mean that every poison frog uses identical parental care. Closely related species differ in whether tadpoles require eggs delivered by mothers, where the offspring develop, and how they acquire defensive compounds. Comparisons of obligate and facultative egg-feeding species help test whether the costly chemical transfer is tied to a particular care system. Biological categories should be connected to observed behavior and measured chemicals, not assumed from a bright color or a common name.

Birds give a familiar but still diverse set of examples. Some species divide incubation and feeding between two adults. Others have care provided mainly by one sex. In certain shorebirds, males incubate eggs and defend young while females may have additional mating opportunities. Emperor penguin males incubate during a lengthy fast, but that does not mean all penguins or all birds share the same division of labor. Care is a physiological and ecological pattern that has evolved under particular combinations of mating opportunities, chick needs, foraging conditions, and mortality. Gamete size alone does not tell us which adult will do which work after fertilization.

The timing of care can change which sex is limited by which task. A parent currently carrying embryos or defending a nest may have fewer opportunities to mate again, regardless of whether that parent produced the smaller gamete. Seahorses bring this point into focus: male gestation can occupy a substantial part of the reproductive cycle, while females allocate resources to producing eggs for transfer. Who is ready to reproduce again, who can afford another mating, and who benefits from guarding a partner can all shift with the duration and costs of incubation. That does not erase the difference between sperm and egg; it demonstrates why reproductive roles cannot be deduced from it alone.

Some forms of care persist even after offspring become physically independent of direct feeding. Adults may protect a territory, share information about food, or provide shelter from predators. In many social mammals, the movements and associations of older individuals influence which environments young animals encounter. But it is important not to label every adult–juvenile interaction as a benefit. Social relationships can include competition for food, disease transmission, interference, or conflict. To establish adaptive parental care, a researcher must specify the young animal's outcome and the parent's allocation, ideally comparing conditions in which care differs for reasons that can be analyzed.

Burying beetles allow such comparisons. Adults locate a small dead vertebrate, prepare it as a food source for larvae, and may remain to feed and defend the brood. In some species larvae can feed directly on the carcass and survive without post-hatching care; in others they depend more heavily on parental assistance. Researchers have manipulated the presence of parents and measured offspring survival, development, and adult traits. In a 2015 experiment with Nicrophorus vespilloides, removing parents before larval hatching while maintaining a comparable nutritional environment affected offspring morphology and later flight behavior. The result shows that care can influence more than immediate survival, and that parental presence should not be treated as interchangeable with simply placing enough food nearby.

Care can also create conflict within a family. Suppose a parent has enough resources to improve the survival of one additional offspring but could retain those same resources for another attempt later. Each offspring benefits from obtaining more, while the parent may benefit from spreading effort. Siblings may compete for deliveries, warmth, or preferred nest positions. Young animals signal hunger or demand care, and the parent must respond amid incomplete information. The resulting behavior can be studied as signaling and allocation, not as a moral dispute. A hungry chick's call may contain useful information about need, but loudness alone does not guarantee that feeding that chick would maximize the survival of the brood.

An experimental design can distinguish some of these explanations. If researchers enlarge a bird's brood, parents may increase the number of feeding trips. If the adults then have lower survival or produce fewer offspring the next year, that is evidence of a cost of raising the larger brood, provided food availability, parental quality, and other variables have been addressed. Without the experimental manipulation, parents with large broods might simply be the more capable individuals, making a straightforward correlation misleading. This is why classic questions about clutch size have remained important: the observed number of young is the outcome of allocation and selection, not a direct readout of the parent's maximum physiological capacity.

Sometimes caring for fewer offspring brings greater total success. Larger eggs can produce young with more reserves; fewer chicks can receive more food; a smaller brood may allow better defense. Christopher Smith and Stephen Fretwell developed a formal treatment of the size-versus-number problem in 1974. Their model asks how available reproductive resources should be divided when the future success of an offspring changes with investment per offspring. The resulting optimum depends on the shape of that relationship and on the environment. There is no universal magic clutch size. The model is useful precisely because it forces us to ask what happens when investment per offspring increases and what resources have to be given up.

The available strategies are also shaped by mortality. If eggs are exposed to intense predation but a guarded nest greatly improves survival, care may pay. If young disperse into a huge environment in which an adult cannot meaningfully track them, allocating more resources to each individual offspring might be less effective than producing many dispersed eggs. If disease or environmental unpredictability makes survival highly variable, distributing offspring across times or places may reduce the chance of losing an entire reproductive investment. The point is not that each species has consciously solved an optimization problem. Over generations, traits affecting successful reproduction encounter selection in particular physical and ecological circumstances.

We should be cautious about turning parental investment into a single explanation for every behavior associated with reproduction. Mating systems depend on operational sex ratios, ecology, dispersal, the possibility of monopolizing mates, and conflicts over genetic parentage. Parental care can be altered by those forces, and in turn can alter them. The immediate physiology may be as simple as fanning eggs to increase oxygen delivery, or as intricate as lactation and immune protection. A theory becomes useful when it helps organize hypotheses that can be measured, and limited when it becomes a slogan substituted for observation.

The tadpole in the little plant pool shows the combined result. Its mother supplies food, and sometimes a chemical defense, long after egg laying. A male goby supplies oxygen flow and protection to eggs it guards. A burying beetle reorganizes a food source and changes its offspring's developmental surroundings. These behaviors are diverse, yet each moves part of the cost of becoming a functioning animal onto an older body. Those costs cannot be understood fully within one breeding event. To finish the account, we need to ask how animals distribute growth and reproduction across an entire lifetime, and why so many lineages have evolved very different schedules for growing old.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### What counts as evidence for a care tradeoff?

| Measurement | Benefit side | Cost side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goby fanning under low oxygen | Eggs maintained and hatching outcome | Male mass loss and subsequent nest abandonment |
| Frog provisioning of nutritive eggs | Tadpole growth and transferred defensive alkaloids | Maternal egg production and repeated visits |
| Burying beetle parent removal | Changes in larval development and later traits | Removal experiment isolates contribution of parental presence; does not by itself estimate full lifetime adult cost |

**Definition:** Parental investment is investment that benefits a particular offspring at a cost to the parent's capacity to invest elsewhere. Time spent near young does not automatically meet the criterion without demonstrated benefit and an allocation cost. A measurable short-term cost is not always proof of reduced lifetime fitness.

**Comparative caution:** Care may be paternal, maternal, biparental, cooperative, or absent. Gamete type and incubating role should not be conflated.
