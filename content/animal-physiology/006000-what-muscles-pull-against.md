# What Muscles Pull Against

An earthworm moves through soil without a single bone. A grasshopper propels itself with long jointed legs made rigid by an external skeleton. A fish bends its whole body against water. A human takes a step, momentarily balancing on one foot, and a small fly changes direction before the eye has finished tracking it. We could describe all these actions as muscular movement. That would be true, but it would leave out the most important problem. A muscle produces force by changing its shape. For that force to move an animal, it must act against something, at the right place, at the right time, and for a sustainable expenditure of energy.

Consider the simplest picture of a muscle: a rope that gets shorter when activated. A rope can pull but it cannot push. If you attach it between two freely floating objects, pulling brings the objects toward one another; it does not by itself make their shared center of mass accelerate in a chosen direction. To move across a surface, the animal must push against the ground. To swim, it must transfer momentum to water. To fly, it must transfer momentum to air. The internal movement of its muscles and the external movement of the whole animal are different events. Locomotion joins molecular motors, body mechanics and the surrounding medium in a single physical system.

Inside many animal muscle cells, the basic force-generating molecules are actin and myosin. These are not tiny versions of a biceps. Myosin is a molecular motor: a protein whose shape changes as it interacts with a chemical fuel. Actin forms filaments that myosin motors can grip. In vertebrate skeletal muscle, thousands of repeating units called sarcomeres line up inside a fiber. Each sarcomere contains thin actin filaments and thick myosin-containing filaments. During contraction, the filaments slide past one another. They do not normally shrink like elastic bands. As overlapping regions change, the distance between the ends of a sarcomere can decrease, and a whole chain of sarcomeres can shorten.

How do we know that? In 1954, Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke reported interference-microscopy observations of living muscle fibers while they changed length. They measured patterns of light and dark bands that revealed changes in the arrangement of filaments during shortening. The broader sliding-filament explanation emerged from several lines of microscopy and physiology, including contemporaneous work by Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson. The vital conceptual change was from imagining that contractile filaments themselves grew shorter to recognizing that their relative positions changed. Microscopy alone did not yet provide a complete chemical movie of a working myosin head; later biochemical and structural work helped build that account.

Now follow one myosin motor. Bound to actin, it can generate force through a change in its configuration. A new molecule of ATP binding to the motor helps it detach from actin. Hydrolysis of that ATP, splitting it into ADP and phosphate, prepares the motor for another force-producing step. When it binds actin again and releases products of the reaction, it can make a power stroke. A muscle contains many motors cycling at different moments, so it can produce continuous tension even though each motor works through small discrete events. There is no mysterious conversion of chemical energy into intention here. The molecular motor changes which molecular configurations are accessible and how strongly they are favored; coordinated interactions generate mechanical work and heat.

ATP is needed not only to power the cycling motors. It is also needed to reset the conditions that make contraction possible. In vertebrate skeletal muscle, a motor neuron releases acetylcholine at a specialized contact with the muscle fiber. The muscle membrane becomes electrically active. That electrical change travels along the surface and through small membrane tunnels, helping trigger calcium release from internal stores. Calcium binds to a regulatory protein, troponin, which helps move tropomyosin away from myosin-binding regions on actin. The crossbridges can then cycle. When the nervous signal ends, pumps move calcium back into storage, and the contractile system can relax. Removing calcium from the cytoplasm is itself an energy-consuming job. A muscle does not simply spend fuel when it visibly shortens and become free to maintain when still.

Not every animal muscle is wired and regulated exactly like vertebrate skeletal muscle, and not every movement-producing cell uses this particular arrangement of sarcomeres and troponin. Smooth muscles and many invertebrate muscles regulate contraction differently. Cilia and flagella, which can propel cells or move fluid across a surface, commonly use another molecular motor, dynein, operating on microtubules. That broader comparison matters: actin and myosin are major tools in animal movement, not a universal mechanical blueprint for every living motion.

There is a subtle distinction between producing force and performing mechanical work. Push very hard against a wall that does not move. Your muscles consume energy, but the wall receives little mechanical work from your effort because its displacement is nearly zero. In a different situation, lift a weight through a distance: force acting over a displacement transfers mechanical energy. Yet biological muscle can also absorb work, as when your leg muscles control a descent. Muscles can develop tension while shortening, holding roughly constant length, or lengthening under load. The energetic cost and the mechanical function differ among these conditions. Measuring oxygen consumption or ATP use will not by itself tell us how much useful work went into moving an animal through its environment.

Return to the earthworm. It has no limbs to act as rigid levers. Its body is a set of fluid-filled segments surrounded by muscular walls. Because its internal fluid is hard to compress, tightening a ring of circular muscle can make a segment longer and thinner, while shortening longitudinal muscles tends to make it shorter and wider. These changes alter pressure and shape. Small bristles can grip the surrounding substrate while waves of contraction pass along the body. The worm produces directed motion because some regions anchor while others extend or contract. A hydrostatic skeleton is not an absence of a skeleton in the mechanical sense. It is a structure in which enclosed fluid resists compression and allows muscle force to change shape in useful ways.

A sea anemone also uses fluid pressure, although its movements serve different purposes. An octopus arm is more complicated still. It operates as a muscular hydrostat: tightly organized muscle in several orientations can bend, twist, extend and stiffen the arm without a chain of bones. The problem of controlling such a limb is formidable. A rigid hinge has a small number of obvious ways to move. A long, deformable arm has many degrees of freedom and can press along its length against a rock or prey. Its nervous system cannot afford to calculate one perfect set of muscle commands in the way a mechanical engineer might specify a rigid robot's joints. Local sensory feedback, tissue mechanics and distributed neural control all contribute. The absence of bones is not mechanical simplicity.

An insect takes a different route. Its cuticle forms a supportive exoskeleton. Muscles attached to interior surfaces pull on articulated elements. Joints constrain which movements are easy, and the animal can obtain leverage from long limbs. An external skeleton also protects tissues and reduces some water-loss problems, but it introduces constraints. Growth often requires molting; a rigid covering cannot expand smoothly in every direction. Mechanical strength, body mass, respiratory transport and ecological opportunity all influence the sizes and shapes in which this arrangement works. There is no rule that external skeletons are better for small animals and internal ones simply better for large animals. The comparative question is which constraints become dominant for a particular lineage in a particular environment.

Inside a vertebrate, muscle usually pulls on an internal skeleton through tendons. Joints act as pivots. Suppose a muscle attaches close to the elbow while the hand holds an object much farther away. The muscle has a short lever arm and may need to generate a force far greater than the weight held in the hand. That arrangement can sound badly engineered. Yet it allows a small shortening of the muscle to produce a larger movement at the hand, often with greater speed. The exchange is a mechanical tradeoff between force, motion and speed, not a failure of evolution to discover a simple pulley. Bones, tendons and muscles act together as levers and springs, and anatomical details determine the outcome.

A tendon contributes more than an attachment point. In some movements it stretches while muscle fibers produce force, then returns some stored elastic energy later in the cycle. Think of a running mammal's limb, which compresses and rebounds with each stride. If a muscle-tendon unit stores elastic energy during landing and returns it during push-off, the muscle fibers do not need to generate every joule of mechanical motion anew. Elastic recoil is not free energy. Some energy is lost in the material, and muscles must still control the limb, compensate for losses and support forces. But elastic structures can redistribute work in time, lower some metabolic costs and allow muscle fibers to operate in ranges favorable for force production.

Walking and running put different demands on this system. In an idealized walking gait, the body's center of mass moves over a relatively stiff supporting leg, resembling an inverted pendulum. Potential and kinetic energy can exchange during a step. In idealized running, the limb behaves more like a spring, storing energy during compression and releasing it during rebound. Real animals are neither perfect pendulums nor perfect springs. Their joints move, their muscles actively adjust force, and terrain changes the mechanics. These models are useful because they make predictions: measure the trajectory of the center of mass, the timing of ground forces and the exchange of energy. If the observations disagree with an ideal model, the disagreement helps identify where active control or tissue deformation matters.

Swimming replaces ground reaction forces with interactions between body and water. A fish sends bends along its trunk and tail, accelerating water backward and to the sides. The body receives a reaction force. Many swimmers arrange their shape and timing so that thrust exceeds drag long enough to accelerate, or balances drag while cruising. Some use undulatory waves; others use oscillating fins, paddles, or jet propulsion. Water provides a high-density medium for producing force, but also substantial resistance. A change that improves rapid acceleration may not minimize energy cost at cruising speed. The right comparison is not which animal has the most powerful muscle in isolation, but what motions its whole body can generate in water.

Flight raises a similar problem in air. A wing is not merely a panel that a muscle flaps up and down. It moves and redirects air, generating aerodynamic forces; its shape, speed, flexibility and angle matter. Birds use substantial chest musculature to power wingbeats, but wing structure and feather control influence how that power becomes lift and thrust. Insects employ several different mechanical arrangements. In many flying insects, wing motion is powered largely by muscles that deform the thorax, rather than by muscles directly pulling each wing like a handle. Some insects have synchronous flight muscles whose activation tracks individual wingbeats. Others use asynchronous flight muscles, in which stretch activation and mechanical feedback help sustain repeated wing oscillations with many wingbeats per neural activation cycle. This is one reason a tiny insect can beat its wings at rates that would be hard to manage by commanding every contraction separately.

A comparative study published in 2023 examined the relationship between synchronous and asynchronous flight modes using physiology, evolution and physical models. Its significance is not that all insects follow one mechanism, but that stretch-activated muscle and body mechanics can make intermediate arrangements possible. The categories describe mechanisms, not two disconnected inventions. Here, the animal's thorax is part of the motor system: passive elasticity and active muscle properties shape the rhythm. The nervous system may set the general conditions for oscillation and steer it, without delivering an independent instruction for each wing stroke.

Why can an animal sprint without sprinting indefinitely? Muscle fibers vary in contraction rate, force production, mitochondrial content, capillary supply and metabolic pathways. Many vertebrate muscles combine relatively slow, oxidative fibers with faster fibers that can generate high power for shorter intervals. Oxidative metabolism can supply ATP steadily when oxygen delivery and fuel availability keep up. Rapid glycolysis can contribute ATP quickly but cannot alone sustain high output forever. Fiber categories are simplifications of a broad biological range, and the pattern differs across species and tissues. A tuna cruising through the sea, a frog jumping suddenly and a bird maintaining long-distance flight need different combinations of force, endurance, heat management and recovery.

There is also a difference between force and power. A muscle that holds a heavy load without shortening can generate substantial force while doing little external mechanical work at that moment. A muscle that shortens rapidly under a moderate load may deliver high power, work per unit time. Force tends to change with muscle length and shortening velocity because the number and timing of productive molecular attachments change. The strongest instantaneous pull, the fastest possible movement and the greatest sustained power are not generally the same operating point. A skeleton or tendon arrangement changes which part of this muscle performance landscape an animal can use.

Body size changes the problem again. If you scaled an animal's shape uniformly, its mass would grow roughly with the cube of its length, while cross-sectional areas would grow with the square. Weight and forces linked to accelerating mass would grow faster than the area available to bear them, unless shape, posture, material and performance changed. Real lineages do change those things. Large land animals often have disproportionately substantial support structures; small animals can exploit movements and surfaces that do not work at larger scales. The simple cube-square reasoning is a starting constraint, not a prediction of each species' leg thickness or running speed. Elasticity, muscle architecture, safety margins and evolutionary history complicate the result.

It is tempting to rank movement strategies by efficiency. But efficiency of what? Mechanical work relative to chemical energy, distance traveled per joule, distance traveled per unit of fuel, or the probability of successfully catching a meal? A frog's explosive leap might be metabolically expensive compared with steady travel, but incredibly effective at escaping a predator. A hovering hummingbird pays for remaining in place rather than moving toward a distant destination. A migration can favor economical cruising, while a short chase favors acceleration. Biologists often measure the metabolic cost of transport, energy spent per unit body mass per unit distance, but even that measure depends on speed, gait, environment and how resting metabolism is treated. Different questions deserve different quantities.

We can test these ideas. Ground-force plates measure external forces during walking and running. High-speed video and motion tracking show which joints move and how quickly. Electromyography records electrical activation associated with muscle, although it is not a direct measurement of force. Measurements of muscle length, tendon stretch and joint torque can help reconstruct mechanical work. Respirometry estimates rates of energy expenditure through oxygen use and gas exchange, with assumptions about fuel and physiological state. In swimming, flow visualization tracks water pushed by fins or bodies. No one instrument tells the whole story. Force, energy and neural activation must be related rather than treated as interchangeable readings.

Return to the earthworm, grasshopper, fish and fly. All four solve the same outer problem: generating useful reaction forces in a world that resists or redirects their movement. But the crucial machinery is distributed across scales. Protein motors turn fuel into tension. Fluid compartments, cuticle, tendons or bones turn tension into changes of shape. Elastic tissue may store and release energy. Control systems match those movements to the surroundings. And the metabolic systems of the animal must ultimately pay the costs. To understand a step, a stroke or a wingbeat, we have to ask not merely which muscle contracted, but what it pulled against, what the environment pushed back, and what the animal gained for the energy it spent.

## Scientific companion — outside narration

### Technical distinctions

- **Force, work, and power:** force is measured in newtons; work transferred by force is the integral of force along displacement, measured in joules; power is rate of doing work, measured in watts. An isometric contraction can consume ATP with approximately zero external shortening work.
- **Scaling:** for geometrically similar bodies of characteristic length L, mass scales as L³ while a representative muscle or bone cross-sectional area scales as L². This is a dimensional starting point, not a sufficient whole-body biomechanical model.
- **Sliding filament:** shortening results primarily from relative translation of thin and thick filaments, not a reduction of filament lengths. Calcium regulation described for vertebrate skeletal muscle should not be blindly transferred to all smooth or invertebrate muscles.
- **ATP and myosin:** ATP binding facilitates detachment; hydrolysis and product release are coupled to the motor cycle. ATP also supports restoration of ionic and calcium gradients.
- **Hydrostatic support:** nearly incompressible internal fluid transmits pressure while muscles in different orientations change the body's shape. A muscular hydrostat, such as an octopus arm, and a segmented fluid-cavity skeleton, such as an earthworm, are related but structurally different designs.
- **Energetic measurement:** metabolic cost of transport, dimensionless energetic efficiency and instantaneous mechanical power are different quantities. Respirometry and mechanical motion capture measure different parts of the problem.

### Questions for the listener

Why can a tendon reduce muscle work without creating energy? Why does a short muscle lever arm require a high force but allow rapid joint movement? How can an insect make many wing strokes with relatively infrequent neural activation? Which measurements would distinguish efficient swimming from powerful acceleration?
