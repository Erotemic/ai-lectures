# More Than One Way to Defend a Body

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A fly larva is feeding inside a piece of fruit when a parasitoid wasp inserts an egg into its body. This is not the kind of invasion that can be solved by antibodies neutralizing a toxin. The egg is far too large for one small phagocytic cell to swallow, and it is already inside the insect. The larva's immune system has another possible response. Blood-like cells in its body cavity can gather at the foreign surface, spread over it, and form a capsule. Other cells and enzymes participate in depositing dark melanin around the trapped object. The objective is to isolate and damage a developing parasitoid before it consumes the host. A successful defense may allow the insect to complete development. A failed defense can turn the larva into food for the wasp.

This is one reason to study immunity comparatively. A list of human immune cell types is not a universal anatomy of defense. Different animals must recognize damage, contain infections, and survive parasites, but they do so using organs and molecular histories that differ. Insect blood, called hemolymph, circulates through an open body cavity rather than through a mammalian arrangement of arteries, capillaries, and veins. Insects have circulating immune cells called hemocytes. Some engulf small pathogens or debris. In fruit flies, larger foreign bodies such as parasitoid eggs recruit immune cells including specialized lamellocytes; melanization chemistry can help make the capsule hostile to the invader. This is a cellular response to the physical scale of the problem.

It is not necessarily a harmless response for the host. Melanization involves reactions in the phenoloxidase pathway and can produce reactive chemical intermediates. If such activity spread without constraint, the insect could injure itself. The capsule therefore does two jobs at once: it attacks something that should not be present and confines dangerous chemistry near the target. This connects immunity directly to the earlier physiology of metabolism and circulation. A larva invests material, cellular energy, and potentially time that might otherwise go into growing. Successful encapsulation is not guaranteed; parasitoid wasps have their own means of suppressing or escaping host defense.

An entirely different scale of threat appears when a virus infects a fly cell. The virus uses the cell to copy genetic material and manufacture its components. Some animal cells can exploit a characteristic of the invading virus: double-stranded RNA produced during infection. In fruit flies, a protein called Dicer two can help cut long double-stranded RNA into short interfering RNAs. These small sequences guide a protein complex toward matching viral RNA, allowing the cell to reduce viral replication. This is RNA interference operating as antiviral defense, not a little version of an antibody.

In 2006, Delphine Galiana-Arnoux and colleagues tested this role by studying flies lacking functional Dicer two. They found increased susceptibility to several RNA viruses, including viruses from different families. Another part of the story was viral counterattack: a virus could encode a protein that interfered with the host's RNA-silencing machinery. The experiments tied an identified molecular pathway to the ability of the whole insect to survive infection. It is important not to claim that RNA interference is the only antiviral defense in flies, or that every virus encounters it in the same way. Insects also use signaling pathways that change antiviral gene expression, and viruses vary enormously in how they evade or resist these mechanisms.

The famous Toll experiments offer a third kind of insect defense. Toll-mediated signaling contributes to responses to certain fungal and bacterial infections in the fruit fly. Other immune pathways, including one commonly named Imd, help regulate different sets of antimicrobial effectors. These are not simply different antibody classes. The animal is using inherited recognition and signaling components to change which defensive proteins its tissues produce. Many of these proteins can attack microbial surfaces or interfere with microbial physiology. The specificity of the response arises from the architecture of recognition and signaling, not from the same V, D, and J gene rearrangement used to make antibodies in jawed vertebrates.

Now leave the insect and consider a sea urchin. In 2006, comparative analysis of the purple sea urchin genome reported an expansive collection of genes related to innate immune recognition, including 222 Toll-like receptor genes and more than 200 members of a large group related to vertebrate nucleotide-binding immune receptors. These are gene counts inferred from a particular genome assembly and classification, not measurements of how many different pathogens an individual urchin can defeat. Still, the result changed the picture. An animal without a jawed-vertebrate antibody system can possess tremendous genetic complexity in inherited immune recognition. We cannot arrange all immune systems on a ladder with humans at the top and a progressively shorter list of parts in every more distant animal.

The sea urchin example also raises a subtle question: what counts as diversity? A population of sea urchins can inherit different variants of recognition genes. An individual may express different receptor families in different cells. Both are distinct from a lymphocyte rearranging its own DNA to create one of many antigen receptors, and all differ from an immune system changing its physiological state after exposure. These mechanisms can produce broad recognition or more tailored responses, but the word diversity hides their different origins if we do not name the process.

There is evidence that some invertebrates respond differently after an earlier encounter with a pathogen. In an experiment published in 2006, researchers studied the bumblebee Bombus terrestris. They exposed workers to bacteria and later challenged them again, varying whether the second bacterium matched the first. Protection after an earlier exposure could last for weeks and showed specificity to the infecting organism in the experimental comparisons. The authors called this specific immune priming. They were measuring an outcome: better survival after a relevant earlier exposure. They were not showing that the bee possessed B cells, T cells, or antibody gene rearrangements.

That distinction matters. A body can have a memory-like change without using the jawed-vertebrate mechanism of adaptive immunity. Some immune priming may involve changes in immune-cell populations, gene expression, metabolism, or epigenetic state. In some organisms the physiological causes remain unresolved or depend on the experimental system. Demonstrating higher survival after a second challenge does not by itself reveal where the memory is stored or how the first infection changed the second response. It gives a phenomenon for mechanistic investigation. The right question is not whether the bee has secretly acquired a miniature human immune system. It is which changes allow a second response to differ from the first.

A more dramatic evolutionary comparison lives in a jawless vertebrate. Sea lampreys are fishes, but they lack the conventional antibody and T-cell receptors of jawed vertebrates. For a long time, that absence made the origin of adaptive immunity difficult to interpret. In 2004, Zeev Pancer and colleagues reported a family of highly variable antigen receptors in lampreys. These proteins, called variable lymphocyte receptors, are assembled from repeated molecular building blocks known as leucine-rich repeats. Developing cells can build diverse receptors by copying segments from donor sequences into an incomplete receptor gene. The construction differs from the V, D, and J rearrangement of conventional antibodies, but its outcome is a large repertoire of distinct recognition structures.

Subsequent work revealed important functional distinctions among lamprey lymphocyte populations. Cells expressing the receptor family called VLRB can respond to antigen, multiply, and differentiate into cells that secrete antigen-binding VLRB proteins. Other cells expressing VLRA, and later identified additional receptor types, show properties that invite comparison with T-cell-like lineages. Those parallels are informative, not a claim that the lineages are anatomically or molecularly identical to mammalian B and T cells. Even the language of adaptive immunity has to be defined by mechanisms and functions rather than by the presence of a familiar-looking organ.

Notice the different kinds of experiments behind the story. Sequencing the sea urchin genome revealed a repertoire of inherited recognition genes; it did not tell us how often those genes are expressed during a real infection. Manipulating Dicer two in fruit flies directly tested an antiviral defense pathway, but it did not describe every antiviral response in those insects. Challenging bumblebees twice measured a change in survival, yet did not locate the underlying physiological memory. Sequencing lamprey receptors and studying the cells that express them connected molecular diversity to recognizable antigen responses. These studies answer different questions. Comparative biology becomes misleading when a genomic inventory, a whole-animal survival assay, and a mechanism-disrupting experiment are treated as interchangeable evidence.

The lamprey changes the evolutionary argument. Adaptive immunity did not require exactly one ancestral invention of an immunoglobulin receptor that all vertebrates then inherited unchanged. Jawed vertebrates and living jawless vertebrates have different molecular solutions for assembling diversified antigen receptors. Both have systems that can expand selected lymphocyte populations in response to antigen. Their shared ancestry and subsequent evolution matter, but the documented molecular architectures are not interchangeable. Explaining how they arose is a research question, not a license to tell a simple story in which evolution first made a crude immune system and then perfected it by inventing ours.

Nor should we imagine the vertebrate and invertebrate distinction as the boundary between animal defenses that learn and animal defenses that cannot. The specific immune priming found in bumblebees, the repertoire of an urchin, the RNA interference of a fly, and the diversified receptors of a lamprey show four different kinds of sophistication. They operate at different levels: changed state after experience, inherited recognition-gene families, sequence-guided destruction of viral RNA, and diversification of lymphocyte receptors during development. If we collapse them into a single word, we lose the actual biology. If we insist they are wholly unrelated, we lose the common problems the systems solve.

The parasitoid egg, the virus, the bacterium, and the migrating fish also force different decisions about resources. A capsule around a wasp egg can be metabolically expensive; a broad antiviral response can interfere with cellular activity; an immune system that activates too widely can damage epithelial surfaces needed for gas exchange and feeding. The defensive solution that protects a young larva may not resemble the solution used by an adult animal, even within the same species. Development, nutritional state, and environmental conditions determine which defenses are available and affordable.

We can now see immunity as a family of physiological designs. The mechanisms differ because bodies differ in size, circulation, developmental pattern, evolutionary history, and the kinds of pathogens they encounter. There is no single molecular definition that captures every form of recognition, memory, and elimination. Comparative physiology works best when we ask what was measured, which mechanism was tested, and how that mechanism changes survival. The fly larva surrounding a wasp egg is not an incomplete version of a mammal fighting a virus. Both are living systems solving the problem of keeping something dangerous from taking over the body, with tools their own evolutionary histories have made available.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Do not confuse four meanings of immune specificity

| Case | Mechanism or observation | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drosophila lamellocyte capsule | Hemocytes enclose a large parasitoid egg; melanization contributes to containment | That the egg can be phagocytosed by one cell |
| Drosophila Dicer-2 antiviral response | Viral double-stranded RNA guides sequence-sensitive silencing | An antibody-like antigen receptor or the only antiviral pathway |
| Purple sea urchin receptor families | Genome encodes many inherited innate-recognition receptors | Hundreds of independent adaptive lymphocyte clones |
| Bumblebee specific immune priming | Improved second-challenge survival in a pathogen-dependent manner | Vertebrate B/T cells or a fully mapped memory mechanism |
| Lamprey variable lymphocyte receptors | Somatic assembly of diverse leucine-rich-repeat receptors in lymphocyte-like lineages | Immunoglobulin V(D)J rearrangement |

**Updated evolutionary caution.** Living jawless vertebrates have independent antigen-receptor architecture from the Ig/TCR receptors of jawed vertebrates. Invertebrate immune priming should not be renamed classical lymphocyte memory, but neither should prior exposure effects be erased by defining all invertebrate immunity as uniformly nonspecific.
