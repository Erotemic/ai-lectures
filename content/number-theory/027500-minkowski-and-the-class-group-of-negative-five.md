# Minkowski's Lattice Argument and a Class Group of Order Two

The ring of integers of the square root of negative five has prime ideals which are not generated by single elements. We have seen one of them: the ideal p generated by two and one plus root negative five. We know p is not principal, but p squared is principal. This makes p behave like an obstruction that disappears when used twice. Is it the only kind of obstruction? Could there be infinitely many different sorts of nonprincipal ideals, or another hidden class with some other multiplication rule?

The answer, in this particular field, is remarkably small. There are exactly two ideal classes. One is the class of principal ideals. The other is represented by p, and multiplying that class by itself returns to the principal class. We will earn the calculation with a geometric theorem about lattice points in a large disk. It turns an apparently infinite classification problem into checking only ideals of norm one or two.

The proof rests on the general arithmetic theorem that nonzero ideals in a number ring are invertible and factor uniquely into prime ideals. We stated that theorem with its proof route in the ideals lecture but did not prove every algebraic lemma. We will continue to identify that dependency. Apart from it, the geometric bound and the resulting two-class computation will be developed here.

## When are two ideals the same kind?

Think of a nonzero ideal as a lattice inside the complex plane, but with an extra rule: multiplying one of its points by any algebraic integer in the ring produces another point of the same lattice. Multiplying an ideal by a nonzero field element rotates or scales that lattice in a structured way. We say two nonzero fractional ideals are equivalent if one is a nonzero field multiple of the other.

A fractional ideal is allowed to have denominators so long as multiplying it by one fixed nonzero algebraic integer clears all of them. These ideals multiply. In a Dedekind domain every nonzero fractional ideal has a multiplicative inverse among fractional ideals, so their equivalence classes form a group. This is the ideal class group. The identity is the class of the whole ring, equivalently the class of any nonzero principal fractional ideal.

In the root-negative-five ring, p times p is the ideal generated by two. Therefore the class of p squared is the identity. But p itself is not principal: a principal ideal of index two would require an element with norm two, and no integer pair satisfies a squared plus five b squared equal two. Thus its class is not the identity and has order exactly two.

This shows the class group has at least two elements. It does not yet show there are only two. The geometry of numbers will give the missing upper bound.

## The fundamental region of a lattice

A lattice in the plane is formed by all integer combinations of two independent vectors. Draw the parallelogram spanned by the two basis vectors. Copies translated by the lattice tile the plane, apart from shared edges. Its area is called the covolume of the lattice. Changing the integer basis does not change this area, because the transition matrix has determinant plus or minus one.

For the ring R equal to Z adjoin w, with w the square root of negative five, one basis vector is the real number one and the other is the imaginary number i times root five. Its fundamental parallelogram has area root five. It is a rectangle in this example. A sublattice I of finite index N has fundamental area N times as large, because N copies of a fundamental cell for R form one fundamental cell for I. Thus an integral ideal I, whose norm is the size of R modulo I, has area root five times its norm.

It is helpful to have an image in mind. If an ideal has large norm, it is a sparser lattice. If its norm is small, it is dense. The task of finding a small-norm ideal in every class will be turned into finding a relatively short nonzero point in each lattice.

## A surprisingly powerful pigeonhole argument

Let L be any lattice in the plane, with fundamental area A. Suppose we draw a convex region B symmetric about the origin, with area greater than four times A. Symmetric means that if a point lies in B, its negative lies there too. Convex means that every straight segment between two points in B stays within B.

Minkowski's convex body theorem in two dimensions asserts that B contains a nonzero lattice point. We can prove this version using only area and the pigeonhole principle.

Shrink B by a factor of two about the origin. Its area becomes a quarter as large, so the shrunken region has area greater than A. Now imagine reducing every point of that region modulo the lattice: move it by a lattice vector into one fixed fundamental parallelogram. If the reduced points were all distinct, we would have fitted an area greater than A into a region of area A without overlap. That is impossible. More formally, cutting the shrunken region into pieces along lattice translates of the fundamental parallelogram and placing all the pieces in one cell forces positive-area overlap.

Therefore two distinct points u and v of the shrunken region differ by a nonzero lattice vector. Twice u and twice v both belong to B. Symmetry puts negative twice v inside B, and convexity puts the midpoint of twice u and negative twice v inside B. That midpoint is u minus v. So the difference is a nonzero lattice point lying in B. The theorem is proved.

There is no number theory in the statement. It concerns any two-dimensional lattice. Yet its conclusion is exactly what we need: a shape large enough in comparison with the lattice cell is forced to contain a nonzero lattice vector.

## Turn area into a bound on algebraic norm

Take B to be a disk centered at zero with radius r. Its area is pi times r squared, and it is both convex and symmetric. For an ideal I of index N in our ring, its lattice cell has area root five times N. The Minkowski theorem gives a nonzero element alpha of I whenever pi r squared exceeds four times root five times N.

The complex absolute value of alpha squared equals its algebraic norm, because alpha times its complex conjugate is a squared plus five b squared. Thus we have found a nonzero alpha belonging to I with norm at most r squared. We can choose r squared as close as we like to four root five over pi times N. Numerically that constant is about two point eight four seven.

The decisive observation is that two point eight four seven is less than three. We can choose a disk whose squared radius lies between two point eight four seven times N and three times N. It is large enough for Minkowski's theorem, and small enough to guarantee that our nonzero alpha satisfies norm strictly less than three times N.

At first glance, this is only a bound on an element inside an ideal. How does it give a small-norm ideal representing the class we care about? This is where the algebraic invertibility of ideals enters.

## Divide by the ideal

Let C be any ideal class. Choose a nonzero integral ideal I whose class is the inverse of C. Every fractional-ideal class has such an integral representative: multiply a fractional representative by a common denominator. Apply our lattice bound to I. We obtain a nonzero alpha inside I whose norm is less than three times the norm of I.

Since alpha belongs to I, the principal ideal generated by alpha is contained in I. Multiply this containment by the inverse fractional ideal I inverse. The result is that the fractional ideal J formed by the principal ideal of alpha times I inverse is actually contained in R. Thus J is an ordinary integral ideal.

Its class is the class of I inverse, because multiplication by a principal ideal does not change the ideal class. By choice of I, that class is precisely C. So J is an integral ideal representing the class we wanted.

What is the norm of J? Ideal norms are multiplicative in the ring of integers. The norm of the principal ideal generated by alpha is the algebraic norm of alpha. Hence the norm of J is the norm of alpha divided by the norm of I. Our disk bound makes this quotient strictly less than three. But the norm of a nonzero integral ideal is a positive integer. Therefore J has norm one or two.

We have established that every ideal class in this field contains an integral ideal of norm at most two. Notice how much information has been compressed: we have not classified all the ideals, but we need no longer inspect any ideal of norm three, four, or a million to determine the possible classes.

## There is only one ideal of norm two

An ideal of norm one is the whole ring, hence principal. What about norm two? If R modulo J has exactly two elements, it must be the field with two elements. The element w, whose square is negative five, must map to a root of X squared plus five in that field. Modulo two, that polynomial becomes X squared plus one, which equals X plus one squared. There is only one root, namely one.

Thus any quotient map from R to the field with two elements sends w to one. We already know the kernel of precisely that map: the ideal p generated by two and one plus w. Therefore p is the only ideal of norm two.

It follows that every ideal class is either the principal class or the class of p. Since p is not principal, the two classes are distinct. Since p squared equals the principal ideal of two, the nontrivial class multiplied by itself is the identity. The class group is a cyclic group of order two.

This is a complete calculation of the class group of the field Q of root negative five, conditional only on the previously identified standard theorem that ideals in its ring of integers are invertible and have multiplicative norms. We have supplied the lattice theorem, the norm bound, the finite list of candidate ideals, and the class relation explicitly.

## The general shape of the theorem

The method scales. In any imaginary quadratic field, the embedded ring of integers is a lattice with fundamental area one half the square root of the absolute value of its discriminant. Applying the same disk argument gives a bound: each ideal class has an integral representative of norm at most two over pi times that square root. In the root-negative-five case the discriminant is negative twenty, making the bound two point eight four seven.

A class-number calculation therefore becomes a finite arithmetic problem: factor the ordinary primes that can occur as the norms of ideals beneath the bound, determine which prime ideals are principal, and work out relations among their classes. The geometric bound is what proves that the finite list is complete. Without that bound, checking many small ideals would only be evidence, never a classification.

For real quadratic fields, the geometry uses two real embeddings and a different convex body; there are also infinitely many units, as Pell equations already showed us. For higher-degree number fields the geometry lives in more dimensions, with real and complex coordinates. The full Minkowski bound uses the field's discriminant and the number of its complex embeddings. Our two-dimensional proof is the simplest instance of a much wider theory.

There is a philosophical lesson, but it is also computational. Unique factorization of elements failed because some prime ideals were not principal. The ideal class group measures that failure. Geometry proves the possible failures are finite in number, and a handful of quotient and norm calculations tells us which ones actually occur. The obstruction for the square root of negative five is not chaotic. It is one nontrivial class, appearing twice when squared. That is the arithmetic structure hidden inside the two factorizations of six.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Two-dimensional Minkowski theorem, with proof

Let \(L\subset\mathbb R^2\) be a lattice of covolume \(A\). If \(B\subset\mathbb R^2\) is bounded, measurable, convex and centrally symmetric, and \(\operatorname{area}(B)>4A\), then \(B\cap(L\setminus\{0\})\ne\varnothing\). Indeed \(B/2\) has area \(>A\). Translation modulo \(L\) into one fundamental cell must identify two distinct points \(u,v\in B/2\) (Blichfeldt's elementary cut-and-paste argument). Then \(u-v\in L\setminus\{0\}\), and \(u-v\in B\) since it is the midpoint of \(2u\in B\) and \(-2v\in B\). The strict area inequality avoids delicate boundary cases.

### Norm bound for imaginary quadratic ideal classes

For an imaginary quadratic field \(K\) with discriminant \(\Delta<0\), \(\mathcal O_K\) embeds as a lattice in \(\mathbb C\) of covolume \(\sqrt{|\Delta|}/2\). An integral ideal \(I\) has index \(N(I)\), so its covolume is \(\sqrt{|\Delta|}N(I)/2\). A disk of squared radius
\[
r^2>\frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{|\Delta|}}2N(I)=\frac2\pi\sqrt{|\Delta|}N(I)
\]
contains \(0\ne\alpha\in I\) by Minkowski, yielding \(|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)|<r^2\). Taking \(I\) in the inverse of a desired class, \(J=(\alpha)I^{-1}\subseteq\mathcal O_K\) is integral, lies in the desired class, and has
\[
N(J)=\frac{|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)|}{N(I)}\le\frac2\pi\sqrt{|\Delta|}
\]
(the weak inequality follows by approximation; for the present example a strict bound by 3 is enough). The step \(I I^{-1}=\mathcal O_K\) and multiplicativity of ideal norm uses the **Dedekind ideal theorem**, stated but not fully proved in 027250.

### The class group of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})\)

With \(\Delta=-20\),
\[
\frac2\pi\sqrt{20}=\frac{4\sqrt5}{\pi}\approx2.847<3.
\]
Every ideal class has an integral representative \(J\) with \(N(J)\in\{1,2\}\). Norm 1 gives \(J=\mathcal O_K\). Norm 2 means \(\mathcal O_K/J\cong\mathbb F_2\), and \(w^2+5=0\) forces \(w\mapsto1\) modulo 2, so \(J=\mathfrak p=(2,1+w)\) is unique. It is nonprincipal (no \(a^2+5b^2=2\)); but \(\mathfrak p^2=(2)\). Consequently
\[
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5}))=\{[\mathcal O_K],[\mathfrak p]\}\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.}
\]
The notation \(\operatorname{Cl}(K)\) denotes the ideal class group of \(\mathcal O_K\), not the group of units and not an assertion that all elements factor uniquely.
