# Which Primes Can a Quadratic Form Represent?

A rational prime may split inside a quadratic number field, but there is more than one way it can split. A split prime is the product of two prime ideals. Those ideals have norm p, yet neither need be generated by an algebraic integer. If they are principal, an element of norm p exists. If they are not, the arithmetic remembers the failure through a nontrivial ideal class.

This distinction turns quadratic forms into a classification tool. We can now ask not merely whether an integer p is prime or whether a congruence has a square root, but which quadratic expression with integer coefficients can equal p.

Our main example will be the field generated by the square root of negative five. We already know its discriminant is negative twenty, and we proved that its ideal class group has two elements. The corresponding reduced forms are x squared plus five y squared, and twice x squared plus twice x y plus three y squared. We are going to prove a complete statement about which odd primes each form represents, with just a few congruence classes modulo twenty.

Before using the specific forms, let us understand the general relation. Fix an imaginary quadratic field with fundamental discriminant D. A primitive positive definite form of this discriminant comes from the norm function of an integral ideal I. If some integer pair makes the form equal to a rational prime p, then the corresponding element alpha of I has field norm p times the ideal norm of I.

Form the fractional-ideal quotient: the principal ideal generated by alpha, multiplied by the inverse of I. Because alpha belongs to I, this product is actually an integral ideal. Its ideal norm is p. An integral ideal of prime norm must itself be a prime ideal: its quotient ring has p elements and is therefore the p-element field. Thus there is a prime ideal of norm p lying over the rational prime p.

What does this rule out? If p were inert, the only prime ideal over p would have norm p squared. There could be no ideal of norm p. So an inert prime cannot be represented by any primitive positive definite form of the field's fundamental discriminant.

Conversely, suppose p has a prime ideal P of norm p above it. This happens when p splits, and also when p ramifies. The ideal P itself contains the rational integer p. Take the norm form associated with P. The element p has field norm p squared, and the ideal has norm p. Therefore evaluating the norm form at the integer coordinates of p gives p. We have proved that whenever a prime ideal of norm p exists, some form class represents p.

Putting both directions together: a rational prime is represented by at least one primitive positive definite quadratic form of fundamental discriminant D exactly when there is a prime ideal of residue degree one above p. In a quadratic field this means p splits or ramifies. To say which *class* represents p is a more refined problem, governed by the class of a prime ideal over p.

Now return to discriminant negative twenty and first exclude the ramified primes two and five. For an odd prime p different from five, the splitting test is the Legendre symbol of negative five modulo p. Our earlier reciprocity theorem lets us rewrite that symbol as the product of the sign symbol for negative one and the symbol of p modulo five. The two factors depend only on p modulo four and p modulo five. The Chinese remainder theorem combines these into a test modulo twenty.

Work through the eight residue classes relatively prime to twenty. The split classes are one, three, seven, and nine modulo twenty. The inert classes are eleven, thirteen, seventeen, and nineteen modulo twenty. We could establish this by a short table of square residues, and the companion provides that table. But the real question is: among the four split classes, which produce an element of norm p, and which produce only nonprincipal ideals?

First examine x squared plus five y squared. Suppose p is odd and equals this expression. Since squares are zero or one modulo four, and five is one modulo four, we obtain the following constraint. If both x and y are even, the expression is divisible by four. If both are odd, it is two modulo four. Neither can give an odd prime. So exactly one of x and y is odd, and the expression is one modulo four. Therefore every odd prime represented by the principal form is one modulo four.

Now inspect the nonprincipal form, twice x squared plus twice x y plus three y squared. If y is even, the whole expression is even. For an odd result, y must be odd. Reduce modulo four. If x is even, both terms involving x vanish modulo four, and the remaining term is three. If x is odd, the first term is two, the second is two, and the last is three; the total is seven, also three modulo four. Therefore every odd prime represented by this form is three modulo four.

These restrictions are mutually exclusive and exhaust the odd possibilities. We have exactly two form classes, and every split odd prime is represented by one of them. So the parity tests tell us which class is responsible. For p in a split residue class which is one modulo four, the principal form must represent it. For p in a split class which is three modulo four, the nonprincipal form must represent it.

Our complete classification follows. An odd prime different from five is a square plus five times a square if and only if it is one or nine modulo twenty. An odd prime different from five is twice a square plus twice the product plus three times a square if and only if it is three or seven modulo twenty.

Notice the logical structure. The easy direction comes from a modular obstruction. The difficult direction is an existence theorem: a congruence class should guarantee an integer representation. We obtained that direction by combining splitting, ideal classes, and the exact enumeration of reduced forms. A congruence calculation alone would not have supplied it.

Let's test the result. Three is in the residue class three modulo twenty, so it belongs to the second form. Indeed, three equals the second form at x equal to zero and y equal to one. Seven is in the residue class seven, and seven equals the second form at x and y both equal to one. Both primes split in the field of negative five, but neither is a square plus five times a square.

Twenty-nine is in the residue class nine. It belongs to the principal form: twenty-nine equals three squared plus five times two squared. Forty-one is one modulo twenty and likewise equals six squared plus five times one squared.

The field provides a nice second way to understand twenty-nine. The congruence t squared equals negative five modulo twenty-nine has solutions. One solution is thirteen, because thirteen squared leaves remainder twenty-four, which is negative five modulo twenty-nine. Hence the ideal generated by twenty-nine and the square root of negative five minus thirteen is a prime ideal of norm twenty-nine. The element three plus twice the square root of negative five also belongs to this ideal, because three plus twice thirteen equals twenty-nine modulo twenty-nine. Its norm is twenty-nine. Thus the ideal is principal: it is generated by that element. We have witnessed both the splitting and the extra assertion of principality.

Contrast this with the prime three. The congruence t squared equals negative five has roots one and minus one modulo three, so three splits. But the norm equation x squared plus five y squared equals three has no integer solution. The prime ideals over three are nonprincipal. The form class of those ideals, not the failure of a square-root congruence, is the obstruction.

The exceptional ramified primes fit the geometry too. Five equals zero squared plus five times one squared, so it is represented by the principal form. Two equals the nonprincipal form at x equal to one and y equal to zero. Those are genuine representations, but the congruence classification above deliberately excluded them because they divide the field discriminant. Ramified primes are not tested by the same split-versus-inert residue table.

What if we change fields? In the field of discriminant negative twenty-three, there are three ideal classes. The prime two splits, but neither prime above it is principal. The two nonprincipal reduced forms have middle coefficients with opposite signs. Those classes are inverses and have order three. The splitting test still tells us that an appropriate form represents two, but it does not say that the principal norm form does.

That is a glimpse of a larger subject. Splitting tests are governed by quadratic reciprocity; determining the *ideal class* of a prime above p is a more subtle refinement. General theorems about how these classes are distributed belong to class field theory and the study of class-group characters. We do not need those theories to complete our example with negative twenty, and we will not pretend our elementary argument proves them.

There is also a computational lesson. A square root modulo p gives us a prime ideal above p. Lattice reduction or a quadratic-form algorithm can then search for a short vector in that ideal. If the ideal is principal, a vector of norm p gives the desired representation. If it is not, our class-group computation tells us why searching forever for such a vector would fail. Instead we work with the form belonging to its class.

We have traveled from a simple question about an expression in two squares to a complete algebraic description. Congruences decide which primes split. Ideals distinguish splitting from element factorization. Quadratic forms turn each ideal class into an explicit equation. Reduction makes the classes countable by a finite search. And together these steps transform necessary modular conditions into sufficient conditions for representations. That is the power of building these foundations in sequence.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a fundamental imaginary quadratic discriminant \(D\), a rational prime \(p\) is represented by some primitive positive definite integral form of discriminant \(D\) iff there exists \(\mathfrak p\subset\mathcal O_K\) with \(N\mathfrak p=p\). Proof:

- If \(p=Q_I(x,y)=N(\alpha)/N(I)\), then \(J=(\alpha)I^{-1}\) is integral of norm \(p\), so \(J\) is prime over \(p\).
- If \(N\mathfrak p=p\), then \(p\in\mathfrak p\) and \(Q_{\mathfrak p}(p)=N(p)/N\mathfrak p=p\) (write the element \(p\) in any oriented integral basis of \(\mathfrak p\)).

In degree two this criterion is equivalent to **split or ramified**, rather than inert.

For \(D=-20\), reciprocity gives \((D/p)=(-5/p)=(-1/p)(p/5)\) for \(p\) odd, \(p\ne5\). Hence
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccccccc}
p\bmod20&1&3&7&9&11&13&17&19\\\hline
(D/p)&+1&+1&+1&+1&-1&-1&-1&-1
\end{array}
\]
The two reduced forms are \(Q_0=x^2+5y^2\), \(Q_1=2x^2+2xy+3y^2\). For odd values, \(Q_0\equiv1\pmod4\) and \(Q_1\equiv3\pmod4\). Since every split prime is represented by some class, and there are only two classes,
\[
\boxed{p=x^2+5y^2\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p\equiv1,9\pmod{20}}\quad(p\text{ odd prime},\ p\ne5),
\]
\[
\boxed{p=2x^2+2xy+3y^2\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p\equiv3,7\pmod{20}}\quad(p\text{ odd prime},\ p\ne5).
\]
Examples: \(3=Q_1(0,1)\), \(7=Q_1(1,1)\), \(29=Q_0(3,2)\), \(41=Q_0(6,1)\). The ramified primes are \(2=Q_1(1,0)\) and \(5=Q_0(0,1)\).

**A prime ideal of norm 29:** with \(w=\sqrt{-5}\), \(13^2\equiv-5\pmod{29}\) and \(\mathfrak p=(29,w-13)\). Then \(3+2w\in\mathfrak p\) and \(N(3+2w)=29\), so \(\mathfrak p=(3+2w)\) is principal. For \(p=3\), the analogous ideals of norm 3 are nonprincipal because no element has norm 3.
