# Where a Rational Prime Goes in a Quadratic Field

Consider the ordinary prime number three. In the integers it is indivisible, except by one and itself. We now move to a different ring, the integers of a number field. Does three remain prime there? Does it break into two different prime ideals? Or does something else happen, in which a single prime ideal occurs twice?

These are three different questions with three distinct answers. Their names are inertia, splitting, and ramification. We will work them out, not from a classification table handed to us in advance, but by reducing a polynomial modulo the prime and examining a finite ring.

Start with a squarefree integer d other than one and let K be the field obtained by adjoining its square root to the rational numbers. Its ring of integers has the form Z adjoin omega, where omega is the square root of d when d is two or three modulo four, and omega is one plus the square root of d, divided by two, when d is one modulo four.

Here is the first important detail. We must use a generator of the *full ring of integers*. The square root itself is not always enough. If d is five, for example, one plus square root of five over two is integral, and leaving it out changes what happens at the prime two. We will return to that trap.

Call the monic quadratic polynomial satisfied by omega f. If omega is the square root of d, then f is X squared minus d. In the other case, f is X squared minus X plus one minus d, divided by four. The apparent fraction is an integer because d is one modulo four.

Why does the polynomial contain information about prime ideals? Because the entire integer ring is Z of omega. Every element can be expressed as a plus b omega with ordinary integer coefficients. We may divide by an ordinary prime p, meaning that two elements are considered the same when their difference is p times an algebraic integer. The result is a finite ring with p squared elements, since there are p choices for each coefficient. And the rule governing multiplication in this finite ring is exactly f of omega equals zero, now interpreted modulo p.

In symbols, the quotient by the ideal generated by p is the polynomial ring over the p-element field, divided by the reduced polynomial f. This conversion is our principal tool.

First imagine that the reduction of f has no root. For a quadratic polynomial, that means it is irreducible. The quotient is then a field with p squared elements. Its zero ideal is prime. Translating back, the ideal generated by p is already a prime ideal in the larger ring. We call p *inert*. Its residue field has p squared elements, so its residue degree is two.

Now imagine that the polynomial splits into two *different* linear factors. For example, suppose its roots are r and s modulo p, with r not equal to s. The Chinese remainder theorem says the quotient ring is a product of two copies of the p-element field. A product of fields is not itself a field: it has two different ways for a product to be zero. Correspondingly, the ideal generated by p is the product of two different prime ideals. They are the ideals generated by p together with omega minus r, and by p together with omega minus s. Each has norm p. We say the rational prime *splits*.

The third case is what makes the story richer. The reduced polynomial may have one repeated root r, so it is the square of X minus r. The quotient is then like a field with an infinitesimal element epsilon whose square is zero. It is neither a field nor a product of two fields. The unique prime ideal over p is generated by p and omega minus r, but that prime appears with multiplicity two in the factorization of the ideal generated by p. We say that p *ramifies*.

Let us justify that last multiplicity instead of simply reading it from a repeated polynomial factor. In the quotient modulo p, the square of omega minus r is zero, so the square of the ideal P generated by p and omega minus r is contained in the ideal generated by p. We already proved that ideal norms multiply. P has norm p, so P squared has norm p squared. The ideal generated by p also has norm p squared. Inclusion and equal finite indices force equality. Thus the repeated factor really means p becomes P squared.

We have obtained the entire trichotomy: one field of size p squared; two fields of size p; or a ring with a repeated root and one prime ideal squared. These three structures account for all p squared residue classes.

How can we predict the case without factoring the polynomial afresh each time? The discriminant answers that question. The discriminant of the integer basis one and omega is D, equal to d when d is one modulo four, and to four d otherwise. The polynomial f has exactly the same discriminant D. For an odd prime p, its discriminant modulo p tells us whether the two roots can be distinguished. If p divides D, the roots coincide, and p ramifies. If p does not divide D, the roots are distinct over a quadratic extension of the p-element field. They are both in the p-element field precisely when D is a quadratic residue modulo p. If D is a nonresidue, the polynomial is irreducible.

We can therefore say: for odd p, a zero Legendre symbol for D means ramification, plus one means splitting, and minus one means inertia. Because D differs from d by at most a factor of four, the Legendre symbol of D is the same as that of d at odd primes.

The prime two must be treated by the actual integral polynomial, rather than by a Legendre symbol defined only for odd primes. If d is two or three modulo four, the discriminant is divisible by four and two ramifies. The reductions are X squared when d is even, or X squared plus one, which is the square of X plus one in characteristic two, when d is three modulo four.

If d is one modulo four, use omega equal to one plus square root of d over two. Its polynomial is X squared minus X plus one minus d over four. When d is one modulo eight, that final constant is even, and modulo two the polynomial becomes X times X plus one. Two splits. When d is five modulo eight, the constant is odd and the reduction becomes X squared plus X plus one. That polynomial has no root in the two-element field. Two is inert.

There is no arbitrary exception here. The prime two simply notices the distinction between the two integral bases, and characteristic two does not permit the same square-root test we use at odd primes.

Let us carry out a complete example. Take K to be the rational numbers with the square root of negative five. Because negative five is three modulo four, its integer ring is Z adjoin the square root of negative five. The polynomial is X squared plus five and the field discriminant is negative twenty.

Modulo two, the polynomial becomes X squared plus one, the square of X plus one. Thus two ramifies. The ideal generated by two is the square of the ideal generated by two and one plus square root of negative five. That is exactly the prime ideal that appeared in our earlier class-group calculation.

Modulo three, X squared plus five becomes X squared minus one, which has the distinct roots one and minus one. Therefore three splits into two distinct prime ideals of norm three. One is generated by three and the square root of negative five minus one, the other by three and the square root of negative five plus one.

Modulo eleven, the equation X squared equals negative five would mean a square congruent to six. The nonzero squares modulo eleven are one, three, four, five, and nine. Six is missing, so eleven is inert. The ideal generated by eleven remains prime, and the quotient has a hundred and twenty-one elements.

Now take the different field formed by adjoining the square root of five. Its ring of integers has omega equal to one plus square root of five over two, with polynomial X squared minus X minus one and discriminant five. Modulo two, this becomes X squared plus X plus one, an irreducible polynomial. So two is inert here. Modulo five the discriminant vanishes and five ramifies. Modulo eleven the polynomial has two roots, four and eight, and eleven splits.

What have we accomplished? We can now translate a congruence calculation into a statement about prime ideals in an actual number field. Quadratic reciprocity, which we proved much earlier, is no longer only a rule for comparing Legendre symbols. It predicts how ordinary primes behave inside quadratic integer rings.

But the classification says nothing yet about whether those prime ideals are generated by individual elements. That is a second arithmetic question. We know from the field of negative five that splitting can occur even when no element has the desired prime norm. To understand that distinction, we will need quadratic forms and ideal classes.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For squarefree \(d\ne1\), set \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt d)\) and \(R=\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\omega]\), where
\[
\omega=\begin{cases}\sqrt d&d\equiv2,3\pmod4,\\(1+\sqrt d)/2&d\equiv1\pmod4.\end{cases}
\quad
f(X)=\begin{cases}X^2-d&d\equiv2,3\pmod4,\\X^2-X+(1-d)/4&d\equiv1\pmod4.\end{cases}
\]
The fundamental discriminant is \(D=d\) or \(4d\) respectively, and \(\operatorname{disc}(f)=D\). For any prime \(p\),
\[
R/pR\cong\mathbb F_p[X]/(\overline{f}).
\]
If \(\bar f=(X-r)(X-s)\) with \(r\ne s\), then \(pR=(p,\omega-r)(p,\omega-s)\), both prime factors have norm \(p\). If \(\bar f\) is irreducible, \(pR\) is prime with norm \(p^2\). If \(\bar f=(X-r)^2\), then \(pR=(p,\omega-r)^2\). The last equality follows from inclusion \((p,\omega-r)^2\subseteq pR\) and equal ideal norms \(p^2\).

For odd \(p\), \((D/p)=1,-1,0\) correspond respectively to split, inert, and ramified. For \(p=2\), split exactly when \(d\equiv1\pmod8\), inert exactly when \(d\equiv5\pmod8\), and ramified when \(d\equiv2,3\pmod4\).

Worked examples:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
K&D&p&\text{factorization type}\\\hline
\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})&-20&2&\text{ramified}\ (2)=\mathfrak p^2\\
&&3&\text{split}\ (3)=\mathfrak p_1\mathfrak p_2\\
&&11&\text{inert}\ (11)\text{ prime}\\
\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)&5&2&\text{inert}\\
&&5&\text{ramified}\\
&&11&\text{split, roots }4,8\text{ of }X^2-X-1
\end{array}
\]
