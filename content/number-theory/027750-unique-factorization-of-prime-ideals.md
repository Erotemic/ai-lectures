# Why Every Nonzero Ideal Factors Uniquely

In the integers built from the square root of negative five, the number six gave us two different decompositions into irreducible elements. That did not make either calculation wrong. It showed that irreducible elements no longer carried all the divisibility information. We repaired the example by forming ideals, actual sets that could be multiplied and compared. The ideal generated by two became the square of a prime ideal, and the ideal generated by three became a product of two prime ideals. Both element factorizations of six then produced the same ideal product.

But a successful example is not a theorem. We still have to show that this repair works for every nonzero ideal, in every ring of integers of a number field, and that no competing ideal factorization can give a different answer. We also need to explain why dividing by a nonprincipal ideal makes sense. These are the central claims of Dedekind ideal theory, and today we will prove them.

From the previous lecture we know three structural properties of our ring R. It is Noetherian, so every ideal is finitely generated and ascending chains stabilize. It is integrally closed in its fraction field. And every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. We will use each of these properties at an identifiable moment.

## What is a fractional ideal?

An ordinary ideal stays inside R. A fractional ideal is an R-module sitting inside the fraction field of R that becomes an ordinary ideal after we multiply all of its elements by some nonzero denominator from R. For the integers, fractions with denominator three and integer numerator form the fractional ideal one third times the integers. It is not contained in the integers, but multiplying it by three produces the integer ideal itself.

For a nonzero ordinary ideal I define its candidate inverse I inverse as all field elements x with the property that x times every element of I lies in R. This is an actual fractional ideal: choose a nonzero element a of I. Then a times every element of I inverse lies in R, so its denominators are controlled. The ordinary ring R is contained in I inverse, since multiplication by an ordinary ring element never takes I outside R.

Multiply ideals by taking finite sums of pairwise products of their elements. By the definition of I inverse, I times I inverse is always contained in R. But that containment alone is not enough. A true inverse must make their product equal all of R, which means the product must contain the number one.

For a principal ideal generated by a nonzero element a, we already know the inverse: it is the principal fractional ideal generated by one divided by a. The challenge is a nonprincipal ideal, which has no single element we can divide by.

The proof begins with a smaller lemma. We need to know that every nonzero proper ideal contains some finite product of nonzero prime ideals. Note the direction of containment: the product is inside our ideal, not yet equal to it. The lemma will supply just enough prime factors to start working.

## Find a product of primes somewhere inside

Suppose, for contradiction, that some nonzero proper ideal I does not contain any finite product of nonzero prime ideals. Among all such ideals containing I, choose one that is maximal by inclusion. We can choose it because our ring is Noetherian: any repeated strict enlargement would create a forbidden ascending chain.

Call the maximal bad ideal B. It cannot itself be prime. If it were a nonzero prime ideal, it would contain the one-factor product consisting of itself. That would make it good, contrary to the definition.

Since B is not prime, there are two elements x and y not in B whose product does lie in B. Enlarge B by adding x, and enlarge it separately by adding y. Both enlargements are strictly larger than B. Therefore neither enlargement is bad: by maximality, each contains a finite product of nonzero prime ideals. If one of the enlargements is the whole ring, interpret its product as the empty product, which is R itself.

Now multiply the two prime products that lie in those enlargements. Their product lies in the product of the two enlarged ideals. But that entire ideal product lies in B. Every term in its expansion contains something from B, except the product x times y, and that exceptional term lies in B by our choice of x and y. So B contains a finite product of nonzero prime ideals after all. Contradiction.

The first lemma is proved. It relied on Noetherianity to choose a maximal bad ideal and on the definition of a prime ideal to find x and y. We did not use unique factorization, which is precisely what we are trying to establish.

## Invert one prime ideal

Now take a nonzero prime ideal P. Choose a nonzero element a inside P. The principal ideal generated by a is nonzero, so our lemma says it contains some product of nonzero prime ideals. Choose such a product with the fewest factors possible.

Because the product is contained in the ideal of a, and the ideal of a is contained in P, the primality of P forces at least one of the prime factors to be contained in P. But every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. Therefore that factor must be exactly P. Reorder the factors so P occurs last.

Remove that last factor and call the product of all preceding factors J. Minimality says J is not contained in the ideal generated by a. So we can choose an element b of J that is not a multiple of a by an element of R. The fraction z equal to b divided by a is therefore not in R.

Nevertheless, multiplying z by any element of P lands in R. Why? Because b belongs to J and J times P was contained in the ideal of a. Dividing the elements of that product by a gives elements of R. In other words, z belongs to P inverse, but not to R. We have found a genuinely new fractional multiplier that preserves the integrality of P.

Now inspect P times P inverse. This product is an ordinary ideal, and it contains P, since R is contained in P inverse. Could the product still equal P? If so, multiplying P by our special fraction z would take P back into P.

That would violate integral closure. Here is the missing reasoning. A finitely generated ideal P has generators p one through p r. If z times P is contained in P, multiplication by z expresses each z p i as an R-linear combination of those generators. Put the coefficients into an r by r matrix C. The adjugate identity applied to z times the identity matrix minus C produces a monic polynomial in z that annihilates every generator of P. Since the ring has no zero divisors and P contains a nonzero generator, the polynomial itself must vanish at z. So z is integral over R.

But R is integrally closed, so an element of its fraction field integral over R must already belong to R. We constructed z specifically not to belong to R. Contradiction.

Therefore P times P inverse is strictly larger than P. It is still contained in R. Since P is maximal, no proper ideal can lie strictly between P and R. Hence P times P inverse equals R.

We have proved that every nonzero prime ideal is invertible. This step is the hinge of the entire theory. Noetherianity supplied a minimal prime product and a finite set of generators; integral closure forbade the special fraction from stabilizing P; maximality promoted a strict enlargement to the whole ring.

## Factor an arbitrary ideal

Take a nonzero ordinary ideal I. Use the first lemma to find a product of m nonzero prime ideals contained in I. Choose m as small as possible. We prove by induction on m that I is itself a product of primes.

If m is zero, the empty product is the whole ring. Containment of the whole ring in I means I equals R, so its factorization is empty. This is the correct base case.

For m positive, I is proper. Choose a maximal ideal P containing I. Since I contains the chosen product, P contains that product. By primality P contains one of its factors, and by maximality that factor equals P. Move it to the end of the list.

Form I times P inverse. Because I is contained in P, and P times P inverse is R, the new product is an ordinary ideal rather than merely a fractional ideal. More importantly, it contains the product of the remaining m minus one prime factors. To see this, multiply the containment of the original prime product in I by P inverse, and cancel P against its inverse.

The induction hypothesis now applies: I times P inverse is a product of prime ideals. Multiply that factorization by P. Since P inverse times P equals R, the result is a prime-ideal factorization of I itself.

This procedure cannot run forever. The induction measures the number of prime factors in a product known to be contained in the current ideal, and that number decreases at each stage. Notice again that we did not presume factorization when constructing the inverse. The inverse was proved independently.

## Why the answer is unique

Suppose an ideal has two products of nonzero prime ideals. Choose one prime factor P on the first side. The whole product is contained in P, so the equal product on the other side is also contained in P. Since P is prime, at least one factor Q on the second side must be contained in P. Q is a nonzero prime ideal, hence maximal. Therefore Q equals P.

We have found a matching prime on both sides. Multiply both sides by P inverse and cancel it. Repeat. Eventually the products are exhausted together. Their factors and their multiplicities are identical, apart from their order.

This is the same pattern as Euclid's proof of uniqueness of ordinary prime factorization: a prime dividing a product must divide one factor. But here a prime is an ideal, not an element, and cancellation works because we have proved every prime ideal invertible.

Existence and uniqueness are now established for every nonzero ordinary ideal. In particular, no two genuinely different lists of prime ideals can multiply to the same ideal. The earlier reconciliation of six was an example of a completely general theorem, not an accidental coincidence.

## Let negative exponents enter

What about fractional ideals? Choose a nonzero denominator d such that d times a given fractional ideal F is an ordinary ideal. Both d F and the principal ideal generated by d factor into prime ideals. Since each prime ideal is invertible, we can divide the two products by canceling prime factors.

Thus every nonzero fractional ideal has a unique representation as a finite product of prime ideals with integer exponents. Positive exponents indicate factors occurring in an ordinary ideal; negative exponents indicate denominators. An ordinary ideal has only nonnegative exponents. The whole ring has every exponent zero.

The nonzero fractional ideals consequently form a group under multiplication. Their identity is R, and their inverses are obtained by reversing all exponents. The principal fractional ideals form a subgroup. The quotient is the ideal class group we used in the preceding Minkowski lecture. We have now supplied the algebraic theorem on which that discussion depended.

For our negative-five example, the prime ideal P above two obeys P squared equals the principal ideal generated by two. Its class is therefore nontrivial but has order two. Two other primes above three multiply to the principal ideal generated by three. These are not special exceptions to the general proof; they illustrate how ideal arithmetic repairs the loss of unique factorization of elements.

## A theorem with a visible proof dependency

The proof is complete, but it is important to see exactly what it does and does not say. It proves that ideals factor uniquely as products of prime ideals. It does not say that irreducible elements in R regain unique factorization; the negative-five example still disproves that. It also does not automatically tell us the number of residue classes modulo a product of ideals. That additional quantitative fact, the multiplicativity of ideal norms, requires a short local argument and is our next topic.

For now, the structural accomplishment is enough. In any ring of integers of a number field, nonzero fractional ideals behave multiplicatively like rational numbers built from prime powers. Their exponents are the missing divisibility coordinates. And the class group measures precisely the remaining obstruction to expressing every ideal using a single field element.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Definitions

Let \(R\) be a Noetherian integrally closed domain in which every nonzero prime is maximal, with fraction field \(K\). A nonzero fractional ideal is a finitely generated \(R\)-submodule \(F\subseteq K\) for which \(dF\subseteq R\) for some \(0\ne d\in R\). For nonzero integral ideal \(I\),
\[
I^{-1}=\{x\in K:xI\subseteq R\},\qquad II^{-1}\subseteq R.
\]

### Lemma A: an internal product of primes

Every nonzero proper ideal \(I\) contains a finite product of nonzero prime ideals. Otherwise choose a maximal bad ideal \(B\supseteq I\) by ACC. \(B\) is not prime, so \(x,y\notin B\) with \(xy\in B\). Then \(B+(x)\) and \(B+(y)\) contain prime products; their product is contained in
\[
(B+(x))(B+(y))\subseteq B+(xy)=B,
\]
contradicting badness. The empty prime product is \(R\).

### Lemma B: each nonzero prime is invertible

For \(\mathfrak p\ne0\) prime choose \(0\ne a\in\mathfrak p\). Let \(\mathfrak p_1\cdots\mathfrak p_m\subseteq(a)\) be a prime product with \(m\) minimal. Since \(\mathfrak p\) is prime and contains the product, some \(\mathfrak p_i\subseteq\mathfrak p\); by maximality it equals \(\mathfrak p\). Reorder as \(\mathfrak p_m=\mathfrak p\). Put \(J=\mathfrak p_1\cdots\mathfrak p_{m-1}\). By minimality choose \(b\in J\setminus(a)\), so \(z=b/a\notin R\) but \(z\mathfrak p\subseteq R\).

If \(\mathfrak p\mathfrak p^{-1}=\mathfrak p\), then \(z\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak p\). If \(p_1,\dots,p_r\) generate \(\mathfrak p\), choose \(C=(c_{ij})\) with \(zp_i=\sum_j c_{ij}p_j\). The adjugate relation yields
\[
\det(zI-C)\,p_i=0\quad\forall i.
\]
At least one \(p_i\ne0\); as \(R\) is a domain, \(\det(zI-C)=0\). This monic polynomial shows \(z\) integral over \(R\), contradiction. Thus
\[
\mathfrak p\subsetneq\mathfrak p\mathfrak p^{-1}\subseteq R.
\]
Maximality proves \(\boxed{\mathfrak p\mathfrak p^{-1}=R}\).

### Theorem: existence, uniqueness, and fractional ideals

For a nonzero integral ideal \(I\), induct on the least \(m\) such that some nonzero prime product \(\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_m\subseteq I\). If \(m=0\) then \(I=R\). Otherwise choose a maximal \(\mathfrak p\supseteq I\). It contains the product, so by primality and maximality some \(\mathfrak q_i=\mathfrak p\). Reorder as \(\mathfrak q_m=\mathfrak p\). Then \(I\mathfrak p^{-1}\) is integral and contains \(\mathfrak q_1\cdots\mathfrak q_{m-1}\). By induction it factors, and \(I=(I\mathfrak p^{-1})\mathfrak p\) factors.

If \(\prod_i\mathfrak p_i=\prod_j\mathfrak q_j\), any \(\mathfrak p_i\) contains some \(\mathfrak q_j\) by primeness. Their maximality forces equality. Cancel the matching prime using its inverse; induction gives identical multisets.

If \(F\) is fractional and \(dF\) integral, factor both \(dF\) and \((d)\), and divide. Uniqueness after clearing negative exponents gives
\[
\boxed{F=\prod_{\mathfrak p}\mathfrak p^{v_{\mathfrak p}(F)}},
\quad v_{\mathfrak p}(F)\in\mathbb Z,
\]
with only finitely many nonzero exponents. For integral \(F\), all exponents are nonnegative. Therefore fractional ideals form a group, and \(\mathrm{Cl}(R)=\mathrm{FracIdeal}(R)/\mathrm{PrincipalFracIdeal}(R)\).

### Concrete check

For \(R=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\), let \(w^2=-5\), \(\mathfrak p=(2,1+w)\), \(\mathfrak q=(3,1+w)\), \(\overline{\mathfrak q}=(3,1-w)\). Direct generator multiplication yields
\[
\mathfrak p^2=(2),\qquad\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q}=(3),
\qquad\mathfrak p\mathfrak q=(1+w),
\]
so \((6)=\mathfrak p^2\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q}\) regardless of the chosen factorization of the **element** six.
