# Prime Ideals and How They Repair Factorization

The number six has two incompatible decompositions into irreducible elements in the ring Z adjoin the square root of negative five. That failure is not fixed by declaring one decomposition more fundamental. Each is a genuine product of irreducibles. The solution is to factor a different sort of object: the collection of all multiples of an element, and more generally collections closed under addition and multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. These collections are ideals.

We will not begin with an abstract theorem and ask you to accept that ideals somehow restore uniqueness. We will build the relevant ideals, count how many residue classes they leave behind, multiply them, and see six acquire exactly the same prime-ideal factors from either element factorization. Then we will distinguish what these calculations prove from the broader unique-ideal-factorization theorem.

## An ideal is a structured set of multiples and sums

Work in the ring R of all a plus b w, where a and b are integers and w squared is negative five. For one element alpha, the principal ideal generated by alpha is the set of alpha times every element of R. We write this as parentheses around alpha. Two generators, a and b, give the set of all combinations r times a plus s times b with r and s in R. We write that as parentheses around a comma b.

The defining properties are that an ideal is an additive subgroup and that multiplying any ideal element by any ring element keeps the result inside the ideal. Why insist on both? Additive closure lets us combine divisibility relations, and multiplication closure means membership still makes sense after every legitimate arithmetic operation in the ring.

A principal ideal remembers divisibility in reverse order. For ordinary elements alpha and beta, alpha divides beta exactly when the principal ideal generated by beta is contained in the principal ideal generated by alpha. Containment reverses the naive size relation: the multiples of six form a smaller set than the multiples of two.

We can also multiply ideals. The product of ideals I and J is the set of finite sums of pairwise products of elements from I and J. For finitely generated ideals, it suffices to take all pairwise products of generators, then allow their R-linear combinations. If I is generated by a and b, and J by c and d, their product is generated by a c, a d, b c, and b d. The product of principal ideals agrees with ordinary multiplication: the ideal of alpha times the ideal of beta is the ideal of alpha beta.

## Make a prime ideal of norm two

Consider the ideal p generated by two and one plus w. We want to understand the quotient R modulo p: two elements of R count as the same residue if their difference lies in p. The generators tell us that, in this quotient, two becomes zero and one plus w becomes zero. Thus w acts like negative one, which in characteristic two is the same as plus one.

Every a plus b w therefore reduces to a plus b modulo two. That is a homomorphism from R onto the field with two elements. Its kernel is precisely p, because the relations two equal zero and one plus w equal zero account for every element whose reduced value is zero. The quotient has two elements, and it is a field. So p is a maximal ideal, hence a prime ideal. Its norm, defined as the number of residue classes in R modulo p, is two.

The word prime for an ideal means something slightly different from irreducible for an element. An ideal p is prime if, whenever a product of two ring elements belongs to p, at least one factor belongs to p. This is equivalent to the quotient having no zero divisors. A field certainly has no zero divisors, so our quotient computation has proved primality.

Is p generated by a single element? If it were generated by alpha, its norm would be the absolute value of the field norm of alpha. Here that would require a squared plus five b squared equal two. We already proved there is no solution. Thus p is not principal. It is a prime ideal that cannot be represented by a single prime element. This distinction is the whole reason ideals can do more than element factorization.

## Two more prime ideals of norm three

Now construct q from three and one plus w. Modulo q, the number three becomes zero and w becomes negative one, which is two in the field with three elements. Substituting w equal to negative one is consistent with w squared equal to negative five, since one is congruent to negative five modulo three. Every element reduces to an element of the field with three elements, and the kernel is q. So q is prime and has norm three.

There is a second possibility. Let q bar be the ideal generated by three and one minus w. The quotient sets w equal to positive one modulo three, again satisfying w squared equal to negative five. This gives another field with three elements, so q bar is also prime with norm three. These ideals are distinct. For instance, one plus w is in q but maps to two in the quotient by q bar, so it is not in q bar.

Why did the ordinary prime three split into two ideals? Modulo three, the polynomial X squared plus five becomes X squared minus one, the product of X minus one and X plus one. Its two roots produce two distinct quotient maps to the field with three elements. This simple calculation is the beginning of the splitting-of-primes problem in algebraic number theory.

For the prime two, the reduction of X squared plus five is X squared plus one, which in characteristic two is the square of X plus one. Its repeated root corresponds to the different behavior we will shortly discover: the ideal p occurs twice in the factorization of the principal ideal generated by two. The words split and ramify are names for these two patterns, but they describe calculations we can already perform.

## Multiply the ideals, not just their labels

We now check the essential equalities by expanding generators. Start with p squared. Since p is generated by two and one plus w, its square is generated by four, twice one plus w, and one plus w squared. The last expression is negative four plus two w. Every generator is divisible by two in R, so p squared is contained in the ideal generated by two.

For the reverse inclusion, notice that four is in p squared. Subtract twice one plus w from negative four plus two w; the result is negative six. Adding four gives negative two. Therefore two lies in p squared. We have both containments, and hence p squared equals the principal ideal generated by two.

Next multiply q and q bar. Their generators include nine, three times one plus w, three times one minus w, and the product one plus w times one minus w, which is six. All four are multiples of three, so their ideal product is contained in the ideal generated by three. But nine minus six equals three. Thus three itself is in the product, and q times q bar equals the principal ideal generated by three.

One more calculation connects the different factorizations of six. Multiply p by q. Its generators include six, twice one plus w, three times one plus w, and one plus w squared. Every generator is a multiple of one plus w, including six, since six equals one plus w times one minus w. Conversely, subtract twice one plus w from three times one plus w, and we recover one plus w. Thus p times q is exactly the principal ideal generated by one plus w. Conjugating the calculation shows p times q bar equals the principal ideal generated by one minus w.

Now read the two factorizations of six as ideal equalities. From six equals two times three, we get the ideal of six equals p squared times q times q bar. From six equals one plus w times one minus w, we get p q times p q bar, again p squared times q times q bar. The conflict has disappeared. We did not discard either element factorization. We refined both into the same prime-ideal data.

## What does unique ideal factorization really require?

We have established these specific identities from generator arithmetic, and the individual ideals are prime because their quotients are fields. That is already a complete explanation of how the six example is repaired. But it is not yet a proof that every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals.

The general theorem is a foundational result about Dedekind domains. A Dedekind domain is a ring with three relevant properties: it is Noetherian, meaning ideals do not form endlessly increasing chains; it is integrally closed in its fraction field; and every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. In such a domain every nonzero ideal is a product of nonzero prime ideals in an essentially unique way. The ring of algebraic integers of any number field satisfies these hypotheses.

There is a conceptual proof route. To produce prime factors, start with a proper ideal and find a maximal ideal containing it, then enlarge the ideal by multiplying with an inverse fractional ideal. Repeating cannot continue indefinitely because the ring is Noetherian. To prove uniqueness, use the prime-ideal property to match one prime factor in two products and use ideal invertibility to cancel it. That is analogous to the familiar proof for ordinary integers, but the difficult ingredient is proving that the relevant ideals have inverses.

Our explicit calculations already exhibit some inverses. Since p squared equals the ideal of two, multiplying p by one half of p gives the whole ring. So one half of p is a fractional-ideal inverse for p. Likewise q times q bar equals the ideal of three, so one third of q bar is the inverse of q. A fractional ideal is simply an R-submodule of the field that becomes an ordinary ideal after multiplication by some nonzero ring element; its elements may have controlled denominators.

The full Dedekind proof establishes these inverses for every nonzero ideal, not just our three. We are recording that theorem as an external proved result here, together with a proof strategy, rather than pretending that checking six establishes it. The next geometric step will rely on ideal invertibility and the uniqueness theorem. Those hypotheses should stay visible.

## A measure of the old failure

The nonprincipal ideal p has a peculiar property: it is not generated by one element, but its square is. If we consider ideals equivalent whenever they differ by multiplication by a nonzero field element, p represents a nontrivial class whose square is the trivial class. That is the first glimpse of the ideal class group.

Where ordinary integers make every ideal principal, a ring like ours can possess several different kinds of ideals. Multiplying by an element changes the size and position of an ideal lattice, but not its ideal class. Unique ideal factorization tracks the primes inside each ideal. The class group tracks why those prime ideals cannot always be bundled into one generator.

We began with an apparent contradiction in element arithmetic. We now have a noncontradictory product of actual sets, checked by elementary generators, and a precise statement about the general theorem that extends it. Ideals have earned their place: they retain the divisibility information that elements alone lose.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Ideal arithmetic and quotient maps

Set \(R=\mathbb Z[w]\) where \(w^2=-5\). For generators \(a,b,c,d\in R\),
\[
(a,b)=Ra+Rb,\quad (a,b)(c,d)=(ac,ad,bc,bd).
\]
Define
\[
\mathfrak p=(2,1+w),\quad\mathfrak q=(3,1+w),\quad
\overline{\mathfrak q}=(3,1-w).
\]
The quotient maps \(R\to\mathbb F_2\) via \(a+bw\mapsto a+b\bmod2\), \(R\to\mathbb F_3\) via \(a+bw\mapsto a-b\bmod3\), and \(R\to\mathbb F_3\) via \(a+bw\mapsto a+b\bmod3\) have kernels \(\mathfrak p,\mathfrak q,\overline{\mathfrak q}\), respectively. Thus the three ideals are maximal/prime and have norms \(2,3,3\).

### Explicit factorization identities

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak p^2 &=(4,2+2w,(1+w)^2)=(4,2+2w,-4+2w)=(2),\\
\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q}&=(9,3-3w,3+3w,6)=(3),\\
\mathfrak p\mathfrak q&=(6,2+2w,3+3w,-4+2w)=(1+w),\\
\mathfrak p\overline{\mathfrak q}&=(1-w).
\end{aligned}
\]
For \(\mathfrak p^2\supseteq(2)\): \((-4+2w)-(2+2w)=-6\) and \(-6+4=-2\). For \(\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q}\supseteq(3)\): \(9-6=3\). For \(\mathfrak p\mathfrak q\supseteq(1+w)\): \((3+3w)-(2+2w)=1+w\). Conjugation proves the fourth equality. Therefore
\[
\boxed{(6)=(2)(3)=\mathfrak p^2\mathfrak q\overline{\mathfrak q}=(1+w)(1-w).}
\]
In this displayed final equality the outer parentheses denote principal **ideals**; they are not reasserting equality of the constituent elements after removing parentheses.

### Principal norm and nonprincipal prime

For \(\alpha=a+bw\ne0\), multiplication by \(\alpha\) in the basis \((1,w)\) has matrix
\[
M_\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}a&-5b\\b&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad
[R:(\alpha)]=|\det M_\alpha|=a^2+5b^2.
\]
Since \([R:\mathfrak p]=2\) but no \(a^2+5b^2=2\), \(\mathfrak p\) is nonprincipal. Likewise \(\mathfrak q\) and \(\overline{\mathfrak q}\) have index three and are nonprincipal.

### General theorem, with proof status

**Used as a cited theorem, not proved in full here:** the ring \(\mathcal O_K\) of integers of a number field is Dedekind, and every nonzero ideal of \(\mathcal O_K\) factors uniquely into nonzero prime ideals. Every nonzero ideal is invertible as a fractional ideal. The companion computations verify the theorem's conclusion for the special ideals involved in \((6)\); the general existence/invertibility proof is not supplied. A dedicated proof of Dedekind factorization remains worthwhile.
