# The Two Layers of a Local Field: Frobenius and Eisenstein Polynomials

A finite extension of a p-adic field has a degree that is the product of two integers: the ramification index and the residue degree. That is a numerical statement. We can ask for something stronger. Can we actually take the extension apart? Can we identify a field where the residues grow but the valuation does not, and then obtain the rest of the extension by adjoining an element of smaller valuation?

The answer is yes. Every finite extension of a p-adic field contains a uniquely determined maximal unramified subextension. Beyond it, the extension is totally ramified. The first layer is governed by the familiar finite fields and their Frobenius maps. The second layer is governed by Eisenstein polynomials, whose constant terms are divisible by the uniformizer exactly once.

To understand the construction, let K be a finite extension of the p-adic numbers, A its ring of integers, pi a uniformizer, and k its residue field with q elements. We have proved that each finite extension L has one valuation, a ring B of integers, and integers e and f satisfying the degree formula.

## Build a field by enlarging its residues

Pick any positive integer f. Finite-field theory tells us that there exists an irreducible monic polynomial over k of degree f. Call it g-bar. Lift its coefficients arbitrarily to A to obtain a monic polynomial g. Let alpha be a root of g in an algebraic closure of K, and set E equal to K adjoin alpha.

Because g-bar is irreducible, g itself is irreducible over K. If it factored over K, the monic factors could be chosen integral over A, and their reductions would factor g-bar. Consequently E has degree f over K.

The element alpha is integral, and its reduction in the residue field of E satisfies g-bar. That residue has degree at least f over k, because an irreducible degree-f polynomial has acquired a root. But we already know that the residue degree cannot exceed the full extension degree f. Therefore the residue degree is exactly f, and the ramification index is one. We have constructed an unramified extension with precisely q to the f residue elements.

Even the integral basis is explicit. One, alpha, through alpha to the power f minus one form an A-basis for the entire ring of integers of E, not just for a subring. The reason is that these elements already give all q to the f residue classes. Lift the residue of an integral element as a polynomial in alpha, subtract, divide by pi, and repeat. The coefficients converge in A. Completeness turns the successive approximations into an exact expression in our basis.

The unramified extension is unique up to a K-isomorphism. If E-prime is any other unramified degree-f extension, its residue field is another copy of the unique field with q to the f elements. Choose a residue element whose minimal polynomial is g-bar. Hensel's lemma lifts it to a root of g in E-prime because an irreducible polynomial over a finite field is separable. That root produces a K-embedding of E in E-prime. Since the degrees agree, the embedding is an isomorphism.

Here is the crucial distinction: this uniqueness is *up to an isomorphism over K*. It is not a claim that all roots of all defining polynomials are literally the same elements. Inside a fixed algebraic closure, the unramified field of each degree can be chosen canonically.

## The Frobenius map does not disappear

In the residue field of E, raising an element to the q-th power is an automorphism. This is Frobenius, familiar from finite-field theory. It has order f. Hensel lifting lets us lift each of these residue-field automorphisms to an automorphism of E fixing K. There are exactly f of them, and they form a cyclic group generated by the lifted Frobenius.

There is a particularly concrete way to see the lift. Every element of the residue field solves the equation T to the q to the f, minus T equals zero. The derivative of this polynomial is negative one modulo pi, since q is a multiple of p. Thus each residue class has a unique integral lift solving the same equation. These are called Teichmuller representatives. Zero lifts to zero; every nonzero residue class lifts to a root of unity whose order divides q to the f minus one.

The lifts multiply correctly. The product of the lifts of two residue units again satisfies the same root-of-unity equation and has the correct residue. By uniqueness it is the lift of the product. So residue-field multiplication survives exactly, not merely approximately. Addition is more subtle: adding two Teichmuller representatives usually requires a pi-adic correction.

For example, over Q-three take the unramified quadratic extension with nine residue elements. The eight nonzero residues form a cyclic group, and the extension contains their eight Teichmuller lifts as eighth roots of unity. Frobenius raises residues to their third powers. Its lift is the nontrivial automorphism of this quadratic extension.

## Find the unramified layer inside an arbitrary extension

Now let L over K be any finite extension, with residue degree f. Its residue field is the field with q to the f elements. Choose a residue generator there and lift a root of its separable minimal polynomial by Hensel's lemma. The root generates an unramified subfield E of degree f over K, by the same argument as before.

This is the maximal unramified subfield. Any unramified subextension of L has a residue field sitting inside the residue field of L. Finite-field theory says that its degree divides f; the uniqueness of lifted residue-field embeddings puts that subextension inside E. There is no room for a larger unramified field.

Since L over K has degree e times f, and E over K has degree f, the degree of L over E is e. Both L and E have the same residue field. Thus the relative residue degree of L over E is one, and its relative ramification index is e. The remaining layer is totally ramified.

We have transformed the equation degree equals e times f into a tower of fields. The bottom extension is unramified and understood by finite fields. The top extension is totally ramified and controlled by a uniformizer.

## Why Eisenstein polynomials govern total ramification

Suppose L over E is totally ramified of degree e. Choose a uniformizer varpi of L. Its valuation is one, whereas every nonzero element of E has valuation divisible by e. Look at one, varpi, varpi squared, up to varpi to the e minus one. Any nontrivial linear combination with coefficients in E has summands with different valuations modulo e. The smallest valuation cannot cancel. Therefore these e elements are linearly independent over E, and since the extension degree is e, they form an E-basis of L.

The minimal polynomial of varpi has degree e. Because varpi is integral, its coefficients lie in the ring of integers of E. We claim all coefficients except the leading coefficient are divisible by pi, and its constant coefficient is divisible by pi but not by pi squared.

One way to see this is to examine multiplication by varpi on the ring of integers B. The powers of varpi give an integral basis as well. To construct it, repeatedly remove the residue of an integral element using the common residue field of E and L. Every stage removes a multiple of a higher power of varpi. Regroup the convergent expansion by exponents modulo e. This expresses every integral element as a sum of the first e powers of varpi with coefficients in the ring of integers of E.

Modulo pi, multiplication by varpi is nilpotent: varpi to the e is a unit times pi. Therefore its characteristic polynomial reduces to T to the e. The characteristic polynomial equals its minimal polynomial because those e powers are a basis. That shows all lower coefficients vanish modulo pi. Its constant coefficient is, up to sign, the norm of varpi, and our norm formula shows that this has pi-adic valuation one. So the polynomial is Eisenstein.

Conversely, suppose a monic polynomial of degree e has all its nonleading coefficients divisible by pi, but its constant coefficient divisible by pi exactly once. An elementary Newton-polygon or valuation argument shows it is irreducible: if alpha is a root, the equality between alpha to the e and the remaining terms forces alpha to have valuation one over e. No polynomial of smaller degree over E can have such a root, because the denominator of that valuation would force the ramification index to be at least e. Thus adjoining alpha produces an extension of degree e, ramification index e, and residue degree one. Eisenstein polynomials describe exactly the totally ramified finite extensions when their generators are chosen to be uniformizers.

## A tower you can calculate

Start with Q-three. First adjoin the square root of two, giving an unramified quadratic extension E with nine residue elements. Next adjoin the square root of three. The polynomial T squared minus three is Eisenstein over E, because three is still a uniformizer there. The resulting field L has degree four over Q-three. Its residue field contains nine elements, so f is two; the valuation of the square root of three is one-half, so e is two. Their product is four.

The field is neither wholly ramified nor wholly unramified. It has both layers, and we constructed each by a different kind of polynomial. That is the power of the structure theorem: the complicated finite extensions are assembled from two elementary behaviors, residue growth and finer valuation steps.

We have not classified all Eisenstein polynomials up to isomorphism. Many different Eisenstein polynomials can produce isomorphic fields, and understanding their possible ramification depths requires the different, discriminants, and ultimately ramification groups. But we now have a constructive route into every finite p-adic extension, with finite fields controlling the unramified part and a uniformizer controlling the totally ramified part.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let K be a local field of characteristic zero, O_K=A, uniformizer \(\pi\), residue field \(k=\mathbb F_q\).

### Unramified construction and uniqueness

Choose monic irreducible \(\bar g\in\mathbb F_q[T]\) of degree f, and lift to monic \(g\in A[T]\). Then
\[
E=K[T]/(g),\quad [E:K]=f,\quad \mathcal O_E=A[\alpha],\quad k_E\cong\mathbb F_{q^f},\quad e(E/K)=1.
\]
Irreducibility follows by monic Gauss factorization over the DVR. The residue class of \(\alpha\) generates degree f, so \(f(E/K)\ge f\); \([E:K]=ef\) forces equality. The basis \(1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{f-1}\) follows by successive division by \(\pi\) and completeness. A residue-field isomorphism between unramified degree-f fields lifts by simple-root Hensel to a K-isomorphism.

The Teichmuller section \([\cdot]:\mathbb F_{q^f}\to\mathcal O_E\) is defined by
\[
[a]^{q^f}=[a],\quad [a]\bmod\mathfrak m_E=a,\quad [ab]=[a][b].
\]
Frobenius \(a\mapsto a^q\) has a unique lift \(\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(E/K)\) satisfying \(\sigma([a])=[a^q]\). The group is cyclic of order f.

### Maximal unramified subfield and Eisenstein generator

For L/K of degree n=ef, choose a primitive element \(\bar\alpha\in k_L\), lift its separable minimal polynomial by Hensel, and set \(E=K(\alpha)\subset L\). Then
\[
[E:K]=f,\quad e(E/K)=1,\quad [L:E]=e,\quad f(L/E)=1.
\]
For a uniformizer \(\varpi\) of L, \(w_L(\varpi)=1\), while \(w_L(E^\times)=e\mathbb Z\). The elements \(1,\varpi,\ldots,\varpi^{e-1}\) have distinct valuations modulo e, hence are an E-basis. Since the residue fields of E and L agree, successive residue lifting shows they are also an O_E-basis of O_L. Modulo \(\pi\), multiplication by \(\varpi\) is nilpotent, hence its characteristic/minimal polynomial reduces to \(T^e\). The constant term has valuation \(v_E(N_{L/E}(\varpi))=1\). Thus the polynomial is Eisenstein.

**Caveat on the converse:** For an Eisenstein polynomial g of degree e, a root has normalized valuation \(1/e\). To avoid circularity, apply the already proved finite-extension theorem to E(\alpha) and deduce that the denominator e forces \([E(\alpha):E]\ge e\); the polynomial gives the opposite inequality.
