# Residue Degrees, Polynomial Factors, and the Index Trap

The polynomial method for quadratic fields was wonderfully direct. Factor a quadratic polynomial modulo a prime, and read off what happens to that prime in the integer ring. One irreducible factor of degree two meant inertia. Two distinct linear factors meant splitting. One repeated linear factor meant ramification.

What survives if the number field has degree three, four, or twenty? Most of the method survives. A polynomial may have several irreducible factors, possibly with different degrees and multiplicities. Each such factor will correspond to a prime ideal, provided that the polynomial really describes the ring of integers at the prime we are studying.

That proviso matters enough to deserve a lecture of its own. We will establish the factorization rule, prove how all the residue degrees and ramification indices add up, and examine an example where applying the rule carelessly gives a false conclusion.

Let K be a number field of degree n over the rationals and let R be its ring of integers. Choose an algebraic integer alpha whose rational powers generate K. Let f be its monic irreducible polynomial over the integers. Then Z of alpha is a subring of R, but it need not be the full ring. As an additive group it is a lattice with basis one, alpha, and successive powers through alpha to the n minus one. It has a finite index m in R.

Suppose p is a rational prime which does not divide this index m. Multiplication by m annihilates the finite quotient group R divided by Z of alpha. Because m has an inverse modulo p, every element of R becomes, modulo p, equal to an element of Z of alpha. More explicitly, there are integers u and v with u m plus v p equal to one. For any beta in R, m beta belongs to Z of alpha; hence beta differs from u m beta by a multiple of p in R. This proves surjectivity of the map from Z of alpha modulo p to R modulo p.

For injectivity, suppose an element of Z of alpha equals p beta for a beta in R. Then its class modulo p in the larger ring is zero. Since p does not divide the lattice index, the inclusion of the smaller lattice induces an isomorphism on reduction modulo p. One way to see this last point is to choose integer bases and write their inclusion as an integer matrix of determinant plus or minus m. Its determinant is a unit modulo p, so that matrix is invertible there. We have therefore obtained an isomorphism of rings after reducing modulo p.

Now use the polynomial relation. The smaller ring modulo p is the polynomial ring over the p-element field, divided by the reduction of f. Consequently the same description holds for the full ring of integers modulo p.

Factor the reduced polynomial as a product of pairwise distinct monic irreducible polynomials phi one through phi r, with exponents e one through e r. Write f one through f r for their degrees. The word f is unfortunately now doing two jobs: it was the name of our original polynomial and also labels residue degrees. The objects are different. In the companion we will use separate symbols for them.

By the Chinese remainder theorem, the quotient R modulo p is the product of the rings over the finite field obtained by dividing by phi i to the e i. Each component is local: it has one maximal ideal, generated by phi i, and its residue field has p to the f i elements. Pulling that maximal ideal back to R gives a prime ideal P i containing p. Concretely, P i is generated by p together with phi i evaluated at alpha, using any integer-coefficient lift of phi i.

What remains is to establish the exponent of P i in the ideal factorization of p. The simple fact that the polynomial factor occurs e i times is suggestive but not by itself a proof. We have already proved unique prime-ideal factorization in R. The quotient ring has exactly r maximal ideals, so the only prime ideals over p are P one through P r. Write pR as their product with initially unknown positive exponents a i.

Localize the quotient at one of those primes. Because all the other polynomial factors become invertible, the local component is the quotient by phi i to its e i power. Its chain of successive quotients by powers of the maximal ideal has exactly e i nonzero layers, and each layer is a one-dimensional vector space over the residue field of size p to the f i. The corresponding layers in the Dedekind localization, which we studied in the ideal-norm lecture, number exactly a i. Therefore a i must equal e i.

We have proved the Dedekind–Kummer polynomial factorization rule in the form we need: when p does not divide the index of Z of alpha in R, distinct irreducible factors of the reduced minimal polynomial give the primes over p; their degrees are the residue degrees, and their multiplicities are the ramification indices.

The same counting leads to an identity that holds even without a convenient generator. The ideal pR has norm p to the n, because R has a free integer basis of rank n. By unique prime-ideal factorization and multiplicativity of ideal norms, its norm is also the product over the primes P i of p raised to e i times f i. Equate the powers of p. The sum of e i times f i is n.

This identity is not a statement that the number of prime ideals over p always equals n. Sometimes there is only one prime with residue degree n. Sometimes there is one prime with ramification index n. Sometimes several primes have varying degrees and multiplicities. What always adds to the field degree is the sum of their weighted residue degrees.

For a concrete cubic pattern, suppose a valid reduction yields one distinct linear factor and one irreducible quadratic factor, with neither repeated. Then there are two primes, one having a residue field with p elements and the other having a residue field with p squared elements. Both are unramified. The degree sum is one plus two equals three. If instead the cubic reduction is a repeated linear factor squared times another distinct linear factor, the sum is two times one plus one times one, again three. The ring, not the simple number of factors, tells us how the degree is distributed.

Here is the promised counterexample to a careless computation. Take the quadratic field obtained by adjoining the square root of five. Choose alpha equal to the square root of five. Its polynomial is X squared minus five. Modulo two, this becomes X squared plus one, or X plus one squared. If we blindly read that repeated root as ramification, we conclude that two ramifies.

That conclusion is false. The full integer ring is Z of omega, where omega is one plus square root of five over two. The smaller ring Z of square root of five has index two in the full integer ring. We have chosen the very prime which divides the index and violates the hypothesis of the theorem.

The correct generator omega satisfies X squared minus X minus one. Modulo two this becomes X squared plus X plus one, an irreducible polynomial. Thus the prime two is inert. The apparent repeated root in the nonmaximal subring was an artifact of using the wrong lattice.

This is not an isolated warning about the integer two. In higher-degree fields it can be difficult to find an alpha for which the entire ring of integers equals Z of alpha. A field need not possess such a generator at all. The weaker theorem with the prime-to-index hypothesis remains valuable: an alpha whose index is divisible by only a few primes is enough to factor almost every rational prime. At the excluded primes one must use another generator, an integral basis, or more general ideal arithmetic.

What about the discriminant? The discriminant of the power basis one, alpha, and its powers equals the field discriminant times the square of the index. Thus a prime dividing the index can create a repeated factor in the reduced polynomial even when it does not ramify in the full field. This is exactly what happened for square root of five: its polynomial discriminant was twenty, whereas the field discriminant is five. The spurious factor of four came from the squared index.

In characteristic zero, the field discriminant truly identifies the rational primes which ramify. The proof of that general discriminant–ramification equivalence uses the trace pairing and the local different. We will not pretend to have proved it here. In the quadratic case we have already proved the result directly from the integral polynomial, and the general theorem is a useful guide for future study.

This distinction between the field, its full ring of integers, and a convenient subring is broadly important. A rational prime is not intrinsically split or ramified just because a polynomial has a certain pattern modulo p. It has that behavior in a particular extension, and we need an accurate model of the arithmetic at p to compute it. The index condition tells us exactly when a polynomial model is accurate enough.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let \(K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\), \([K:\mathbb Q]=n\), with \(\alpha\in\mathcal O_K\). Let \(F\in\mathbb Z[X]\) be the monic minimal polynomial and \(m=[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\alpha]]\). If \(p\nmid m\), then
\[
\mathcal O_K/p\mathcal O_K\simeq \mathbb Z[\alpha]/p\mathbb Z[\alpha]\simeq \mathbb F_p[X]/(\overline F).
\]
If \(\overline F=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\phi_i^{e_i}\), with pairwise distinct monic irreducibles \(\phi_i\) of degree \(f_i\), then
\[
p\mathcal O_K=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathfrak P_i^{e_i},\qquad
\mathfrak P_i=(p,\widetilde\phi_i(\alpha)),\qquad
\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P_i\cong\mathbb F_{p^{f_i}}.
\]
Proof of the exponents: localizing \(\mathbb F_p[X]/(\phi_i^{e_i})\) at its maximal ideal yields a length-\(e_i\) chain whose simple factors are the \(f_i\)-dimensional residue field; compare with the prime-power filtration in a DVR. The quotient \(\mathcal O_K/p\mathcal O_K\) has cardinality \(p^n\); more generally, without the index hypothesis,
\[
N(p\mathcal O_K)=p^n=\prod_i N(\mathfrak P_i)^{e_i}
=p^{\sum_i e_i f_i},\qquad \boxed{\sum_i e_i f_i=n}.
\]

**Index trap:** \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\), \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt5]\subset\mathcal O_K\) of index 2. With \(\alpha=\sqrt5\), \(X^2-5\equiv(X+1)^2\pmod2\) falsely suggests ramification. With the integral generator \(\omega=(1+\sqrt5)/2\), \(X^2-X-1\equiv X^2+X+1\) is irreducible modulo 2, so 2 is inert. Discriminants satisfy \(\operatorname{disc}(1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{n-1})=m^2 D_K\): here \(20=2^2\cdot5\).

### Exercises

1. Prove \([\mathcal O_K/p\mathcal O_K:\mathbb F_p]=n\) by reducing an integral basis.
2. Why is a degree-four polynomial unable to reduce to \(\phi_1^2\phi_2\) when \(\deg\phi_1=1\) and \(\deg\phi_2=3\)? Give two genuine reduction patterns of total degree four.
3. Derive the identity \(\sum_i e_if_i=n\) without referring to a polynomial generator.
4. Let \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-11})\) and \(\alpha=\sqrt{-11}\), whose power-basis index is two. Explain why \(\alpha\) may still be used at every odd prime.
5. Explain what an equality \(\overline F=\phi^e\) says at a prime dividing \([\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\alpha]]\), and what it does *not* say.
