# Two Quadratic Extensions of Q-three: Ramification in a Complete Field

We have met three ways a rational prime can behave in a quadratic number field. It may split into two distinct prime ideals. It may remain prime, or inert. Or it may ramify, meaning that one prime ideal appears twice. Earlier we read this behavior from polynomial factorization modulo the prime and from the ideal structure of an algebraic number ring.

There is a second way to see the same phenomenon, one that brings together valuations and completion. Start with Q-three, the three-adic numbers, and adjoin a square root that does not already belong to that field. The resulting quadratic extension is a local field, but the valuation may behave in one of two different ways. Either the residue field grows while the value group stays the same, or the value group becomes finer while the residue field stays the same.

Those are the basic unramified and ramified patterns. We can see each one with elementary calculations. The fields Q-three with the square root of two, and Q-three with the square root of three, are enough to display the distinction. We will construct their valuations rather than just assigning them names.

## The extension where residues grow

First take the polynomial X squared minus two. Modulo three it becomes X squared plus one. The only squares modulo three are zero and one, so this polynomial has no root in the field with three elements. Therefore two is not a square in Q-three, by our local square criterion. Adjoining a symbol alpha whose square equals two produces a genuine quadratic field extension of Q-three.

Every element in this field has a unique expression a plus b alpha, with a and b in Q-three. Addition is coordinatewise, and multiplication uses alpha squared equals two. Conjugation sends a plus b alpha to a minus b alpha. Their product is a squared minus two b squared, an element of Q-three called the field norm.

Take the ordinary three-adic valuation of that norm. What can it tell us about a plus b alpha? Let m be the smaller of the valuations of a and b. If one is strictly smaller than the other, the term with smaller valuation dominates the norm, and the norm has valuation twice m. If the valuations are equal, factor out three to the m and reduce the remaining expression a squared minus two b squared modulo three. Because two is not a square modulo three, the expression cannot vanish unless both reduced coefficients vanish. But at least one is a unit by our choice of m. So cancellation does not occur.

In every case, the valuation of the norm is exactly twice the smaller of the valuations of a and b. Half that number is an integer. Define the valuation of a plus b alpha to be this smaller valuation. This is a genuine valuation: the norm is multiplicative, and we have just shown that its valuation is twice our chosen quantity. The strong triangle inequality follows from taking the minimum of the coordinate valuations.

Notice that the valuation of a rational element a is unchanged. The valuation of three is one, just as it was in Q-three. The element alpha is a unit, because alpha squared equals two, a three-adic unit. We have not introduced a new fractional valuation step.

What is the ring of integers in this extension, meaning the elements whose extended valuation is nonnegative? The formula says it is precisely the expressions a plus b alpha with a and b in Z-three. Its maximal ideal is generated by three. Reduce both coefficients modulo three. Since alpha satisfies alpha squared equals two, the residue ring is the polynomial ring over the field of three elements, modulo X squared minus two.

Because that polynomial is irreducible, the residue ring is a field with nine elements. We started with three possible residues and ended with nine. The valuation did not become finer, but the residue field doubled in dimension over the original field of three elements. This is the unramified quadratic extension pattern: ramification index one and residue degree two.

A simple check makes it concrete. The element three plus alpha has valuation zero: its coefficient of alpha is a unit, and its norm is nine minus two, equal to seven, another three-adic unit. It reduces to the nonzero element alpha in the field with nine elements. On the other hand, three times this element has valuation one, exactly as multiplication by three should.

## The extension where the value group grows

Now use X squared minus three. This polynomial has no root in Q-three, but for a different reason. The valuation of three is odd and valuations of squares are even. So let beta squared equal three, and form the quadratic field Q-three with beta.

Again, every element is uniquely a plus b beta, and its field norm is a squared minus three b squared. This time the two terms have valuations of opposite parity: the valuation of a squared is even, and the valuation of three b squared is odd. As long as both are nonzero, they cannot have the same valuation, so their sum cannot cancel at the lowest level.

Therefore the three-adic valuation of the norm is the smaller of twice the valuation of a and one plus twice the valuation of b. Call this integer the *normalized* valuation on the extension. Its value at beta is one, since beta has norm negative three. Its value at three is two, since the norm of the rational element three is nine.

That is the crucial difference. In Q-three, three has valuation one. In this extension, with a normalization that gives integral values and assigns value one to the smallest positive valuation, three has valuation two. The new uniformizer, the element generating the maximal ideal, is beta, and three equals beta squared. We have gained a finer valuation step halfway between successive old ones.

We should be careful about normalizations. If we insist that the extended valuation equal the *old* valuation on the rationals, divide this normalized valuation by two. Then three still has value one, while beta has value one half. Both conventions describe the same absolute value topology. The normalized convention is useful for describing the local ring and its ideals; the unscaled convention makes comparison with Q-three immediate.

What are the integral elements? Our formula says an element a plus b beta has nonnegative normalized valuation precisely when both a and b are in Z-three. The maximal ideal is generated by beta. When we reduce modulo beta, the coefficient b beta disappears, and three also becomes zero because three equals beta squared. The residue field is simply the field with three elements. There was no increase in residue degree. Instead the ramification index is two.

This is the totally ramified quadratic pattern. The extension has degree two, and all of that degree appears in the valuation group rather than in a larger residue field.

For example, three plus beta equals beta times beta plus one. The factor beta plus one is a unit, so three plus beta has valuation one. Its norm is nine minus three, equal to six, and the ordinary three-adic valuation of six is one. This is consistent with the normalized valuation formula. Contrast it with three plus alpha, which was a unit in the unramified extension.

## Why these really are complete local fields

We should not assume that adjoining a root somehow preserves completeness without an argument. In the unramified extension, the absolute value of a plus b alpha is the larger of the three-adic absolute values of a and b. Therefore a sequence of extension elements is Cauchy exactly when both coordinate sequences are Cauchy in Q-three. Since Q-three is complete, both coordinates converge, and the extension sequence converges.

In the ramified extension, the absolute value of a plus b beta is the larger of the absolute value of a and the absolute value of b multiplied by the square root of one third. The same coordinatewise completeness argument applies. The factor involving the square root of one third is a positive real scale for the metric; it is not an element we need to adjoin to Q-three.

Their rings of integers are also compact. In both cases, the integral elements are a plus b times the chosen square root with a and b in the compact ring Z-three. That ring of integers is therefore the continuous image of the compact product Z-three times Z-three. Each extension is complete and has a compact neighborhood of zero. In other words, it is a local field in the topological sense as well as a field with a local valuation ring.

## Read global splitting through local fields

Now return briefly to number fields over the rationals. In Q with the square root of two, the prime three is inert: the defining polynomial is irreducible modulo three. Completing at the prime above three produces the unramified quadratic extension we just constructed, with residue field of nine elements.

In Q with the square root of three, the prime three ramifies: the discriminant is divisible by three and three is the square of the uniformizer beta in the local ring. Completing at its prime above three produces the totally ramified extension, with the same three-element residue field as Q-three but a doubled valuation index.

What if a prime splits instead? We examined the field Q with the square root of negative five and found that three splits globally. Locally, negative five is one modulo three, so it has a square root in Q-three by Hensel's lemma. The polynomial X squared plus five has two distinct roots already in Q-three. Therefore adjoining one of them does not make a degree-two field extension; the two roots correspond to *two different primes* above three. Algebraically, the polynomial quotient over Q-three splits as a product of two copies of Q-three, one for each choice of local root.

Thus the three global behaviors have concrete local counterparts. An inert prime gives an extension of the residue field. A ramified prime gives a smaller uniformizer and a finer valuation. A split prime gives more than one local component, each of smaller degree. The simple degree identity behind these cases is that ramification index times residue degree accounts for the degree of each local field extension. In our quadratic examples, those products are one times two, two times one, and one times one in each of two components.

## What is proved, and what comes later

Our arguments for these two explicit quadratic extensions were self-contained once we knew Q-three, field norms, irreducible polynomials and finite fields. We calculated the valuation from the norm, checked completeness through coordinates, identified the integral ring, and counted residues. This is a model for much more general local-field theory.

For an arbitrary finite extension of Q-p, the general theory proves that the valuation extends uniquely, that the ring of integers is a complete discrete valuation ring with finite residue field, and that its extension degree equals ramification index times residue degree. We have *illustrated* this general theorem and proved its ingredients in our two quadratic examples; we have not proved its full generality here. Different ideals, discriminants, and higher-degree splitting laws will require those broader results.

The main achievement is conceptual. When a prime factors in a number field, we can understand the factorization by looking closely at that prime rather than at all the primes at once. A p-adic completion isolates one arithmetic direction, and an extension may enlarge its residue field, refine its scale of valuation, or separate into several components. These are not competing descriptions. They are the local geometric meaning of the global ideal factorizations we already know.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let K=Q_3 with valuation v_3, ring O_K=Z_3, residue field F_3.

### Unramified example L=K(α), α²=2

Since \(X^2-2\) is irreducible over F_3, it is irreducible over K. Every z=a+bα has

\[
N_{L/K}(z)=a^2-2b^2,\qquad
v_3(N(z))=2\min\{v_3(a),v_3(b)\}.
\]

For equal coefficient valuations, the leading residues cannot cancel because \(a_0^2=2b_0^2\) has no nonzero solution in F_3. Define \(v_L(z)=\frac12v_3(N(z))\). Its additivity under multiplication follows from multiplicative norm; the ultrametric inequality follows by taking the minimum of coefficient valuations. Then

\[
O_L=Z_3[\alpha],\quad \mathfrak m_L=3O_L,\quad
O_L/\mathfrak m_L\cong F_3[T]/(T^2-2)=F_9,\quad e=1,\ f=2.
\]

The absolute value restricted to K is |·|_3: \(|a+bα|_L=\max(|a|_3,|b|_3)\).

### Ramified example M=K(β), β²=3

Valuation parity shows X²-3 has no root in K. For z=a+bβ,

\[
N_{M/K}(z)=a^2-3b^2,\quad
w_M(z)=v_3(N(z))=\min\{2v_3(a),1+2v_3(b)\}.
\]

The terms have opposite-parity valuations; hence no leading cancellation. Norm multiplicativity makes w_M additive under multiplication, and the formula gives the ultrametric inequality. Here w_M(β)=1 and w_M(3)=2. With the normalized integer valuation w_M:

\[
O_M=Z_3[\beta],\quad \mathfrak m_M=\beta O_M,\quad
O_M/\mathfrak m_M\cong F_3,\quad e=2,\ f=1.
\]

To extend the original absolute value from K, take \(|z|_M=3^{-w_M(z)/2}\). Then \(|a+bβ|_M=\max(|a|_3,3^{-1/2}|b|_3)\); completeness follows coordinatewise.

### Relation with global splitting

| Global Q-field and p=3 | Polynomial modulo 3 | Local structure | e and f |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q(√2) | X²−2 irreducible | Q_3(√2), unramified | e=1, f=2 |
| Q(√3) | X²−3=X² | Q_3(√3), ramified | e=2, f=1 |
| Q(√−5) | X²+5=X²−1=(X−1)(X+1) | Q_3×Q_3, split | e=f=1 at each of two primes |

**Scope:** The general theorem about unique valuation extension and ef=[L:K] for arbitrary finite extensions of Q_p is stated and referenced, not proved here. The computations above prove these identities in the chosen quadratic cases.
