# Local Valuations and Why Ideal Norms Multiply

We have shown that nonzero ideals in a number field factor uniquely into prime ideals. This gives us an algebraic version of prime factorization, but there is a numerical question that our proof did not settle. How many residue classes are there modulo a given ideal? And if we multiply two ideals, does their number of residue classes multiply as well?

For ordinary integers, the answer is familiar. There are six residue classes modulo six. If two ordinary integers are coprime, the Chinese remainder theorem explains why their residue counts multiply. But in number fields, ideals need not be principal, and two ideals need not be coprime. A factorization into prime ideals gives us a promising description, but a missing local statement prevents us from deducing a numerical formula immediately.

The precise answer is that the norm of a nonzero ideal I is the number of elements in the quotient ring R modulo I. This norm is finite in a number field, and for all nonzero ideals I and J we have the exact identity: the norm of their product equals the norm of I times the norm of J. We will prove this, including the case where the two ideals share prime factors.

The most instructive route is to look at one prime ideal at a time. There the whole ideal theory becomes a theory of powers of a single element. These localized rings are called discrete valuation rings, and we will build the relevant structure instead of merely naming it.

## Why localization focuses on one prime

Choose a nonzero prime ideal P in a Dedekind ring R, and write K for its fraction field. Form a new ring by allowing denominators from R that do not belong to P. An element looks like a divided by s, where a is in R and s is not in P. The resulting ring is called R localized at P.

This choice of denominators is deliberate. In the quotient R modulo P, every element outside P is a nonzero member of a field, because P is maximal. Localizing declares those elements invertible before taking the quotient. It removes arithmetic associated with other prime ideals and leaves the behavior at P in focus.

The localized ring has a unique maximal ideal: all its fractions whose numerators belong to P. It is a local ring. Localization preserves the three Dedekind properties we established. It remains Noetherian because denominators can be cleared from any finitely generated ideal. It remains integrally closed: if a fraction satisfies a monic equation with localized coefficients, clearing a suitable common denominator makes a multiple of that fraction integral over R, and integral closure puts that multiple in R. And its only nonzero prime ideal is the localized P, since prime ideals of the localization correspond to prime ideals of R contained in P, and every nonzero prime of R is maximal.

So we have reached a Noetherian, integrally closed local domain of dimension one. The crucial fact is that its unique nonzero maximal ideal is generated by a single element. Let us prove that fact.

## Extract a generator from integral closure

Call the local ring A and its maximal ideal M. Choose a nonzero element a of M. Every prime ideal containing a must be nonzero, and in this one-dimensional local ring the only such prime is M. Therefore the radical of the principal ideal generated by a is M: every element of M has some power that belongs to the ideal of a.

Because M is finitely generated, we can make this statement uniform. Choose finitely many generators, and take a sufficiently large power of M. Every monomial in those generators then contains a high enough power of one generator to lie in the ideal of a. Hence some power M to the N is contained in the ideal of a.

Choose the smallest such positive N. The previous power is not contained in the ideal of a, so select b in M to the N minus one that does not belong to the ideal of a. The fraction z equal to b divided by a is outside A. But multiplying z by any element of M lands in A, because b times M lies in M to the N and therefore in the ideal of a.

Exactly as in our prime-ideal invertibility proof, if z multiplied M back into M, the determinant trick would make z integral over A. Integral closure would then force z into A, a contradiction. Thus z M is an ideal contained in A but not contained in the unique maximal ideal M. It must be all of A. There is some element of M whose product with z equals one, and in fact M equals the principal ideal generated by z inverse.

We have proved that the maximal ideal is principal. Denote one generator by pi, traditionally called a uniformizer. This result was not true for an arbitrary local ring; it came from the specific Noetherian, integrally closed, one-dimensional hypotheses.

## An integer measuring divisibility

Since M is generated by pi, every nonzero element of A can be written as pi to a nonnegative integer power times a unit. To justify that, we must rule out an element divisible by every power of pi. Suppose x were such an element. Then x divided by pi, x divided by pi squared, and so on would all belong to A. The principal ideals they generate would form a strictly increasing infinite chain, since pi is not a unit. That contradicts Noetherianity.

Thus there is a largest exponent v for which x is divisible by pi to the v. Divide by that power. The remaining factor is not in M, and any element outside the unique maximal ideal of a local ring is a unit. So x equals a unit times pi to the v.

Extend the exponent to nonzero fractions by subtracting exponents of numerator and denominator. This gives the P-adic valuation: an integer recording how many copies of the local prime divide the fraction. The valuation of a product is the sum of the valuations, and the valuation of a sum is at least the smaller of the two valuations. We have arrived at a discrete valuation ring. Every nonzero ideal of it is a power of the maximal ideal: choose an element having the smallest valuation in the ideal, and that element generates the ideal.

It is important to distinguish this local valuation from the p-adic real-number-like completion that one may study later. We have only inverted denominators outside P and measured an integer exponent. No infinite p-adic expansions or topological completeness have been assumed.

## What a prime-power quotient looks like

Now return to the original ring R. The quotient by P is a finite field; call its number of elements q. Consider the chain of ideals R, P, P squared, P cubed, and so on. The quotient R modulo P squared contains more residue classes than R modulo P, but exactly how many more?

Look at the thin layer P to the j divided by P to the j plus one. Multiplication by elements of P sends this layer to zero, so it is naturally a vector space over the field R modulo P.

Localizing at P does not change this vector space. Any denominator outside P already acts as a nonzero field element modulo P, and hence acts invertibly. But after localization our ideal P is generated by the uniformizer pi. The thin layer becomes pi to the j times the local ring, modulo pi to the j plus one. Multiplication by pi to the j identifies this quotient with the field of residues modulo pi. It is a one-dimensional vector space.

Therefore each thin layer contains exactly q elements. The sequence of successive quotients from R down to P to the e multiplies the number of residue classes by q at every step. It follows that the norm of P to the e equals q to the e.

This answers the shared-prime-factor case. Multiplying by another copy of P adds one more layer, and its norm multiplies by q even though the factors are certainly not coprime.

## Put different primes back together

Suppose an arbitrary nonzero ideal I factors as the product of distinct prime ideals P one through P r raised to positive exponents e one through e r. Two distinct nonzero prime ideals are maximal and therefore comaximal: their sum is the whole ring. Powers of comaximal ideals remain comaximal, as follows by expanding a sufficiently high power of a sum equal to one.

The Chinese remainder theorem now says reduction modulo I is the same as recording independently a residue class modulo each prime power. At the level of quotient rings, R modulo I is a direct product of the R modulo P i to the e i. Counting the elements of those finite sets gives the product of their cardinalities.

We have just proved the exact norm formula. The norm of I equals the product, over its prime factors, of the norm of each prime raised to that prime's exponent.

The prime-ideal factorization theorem tells us that the exponents in a product I times J are the sums of the exponents for I and J. The norm formula therefore proves immediately that the norm of I times J equals the norm of I times the norm of J, with no coprimality assumption.

This is the proof that our class-group argument in the previous installment needed. We can now use ideal norms multiplicatively without appealing to an unproved general statement.

## Two numerical meanings of norm

For a nonzero algebraic integer alpha, the ideal generated by alpha has finite index. Multiplication by alpha is a linear map on an integral basis of the ring of integers. Its determinant is the field norm of alpha, and the absolute value of that integer determinant is the index of the sublattice alpha times R.

Consequently the norm of the principal ideal generated by alpha equals the absolute value of the field norm of alpha. There are two different objects here: the field norm sends an element to a rational integer, possibly negative; the ideal norm counts a finite set, so it is always positive. The absolute value reconciles them.

For the ring of integers generated by the square root of negative five, the field norm of a plus b times that root is a squared plus five b squared. The prime ideal P above two has norm two, although there is no element of norm two generating it. Its square is the principal ideal of two, whose norm is four. Multiplicativity gives two times two equals four, exactly as our explicit ideal calculation predicted.

Likewise the two prime ideals above three each have norm three; their product is the principal ideal of three, which has norm nine. These small checks are not a substitute for the proof, but they now illustrate the theorem without relying on accidental numerical behavior.

## What has been completed

We began with a qualitative structure theorem: ideals are uniquely assembled from primes. Now we have also shown how many residue classes live in every ideal quotient, by counting one local prime-power layer at a time. Localization reduces the ring to a single divisibility scale. Integral closure and Noetherianity force that scale to be discrete. The finite residue field measures the number of choices at each step. The Chinese remainder theorem combines the local data globally.

Both the inverse-ideal theorem and the multiplicativity of ideal norms are now proved, rather than deferred. The class group calculation for the negative-five field can be read with its prerequisites supplied. And the same machinery prepares us for deeper questions: how rational primes split into prime ideals, how norms constrain class groups, and how local behavior can illuminate global arithmetic.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Local Dedekind rings are DVRs

Let \(R\) be Dedekind and \(\mathfrak p\ne0\) prime. The localization \(A=R_{\mathfrak p}\) is Noetherian, integrally closed, and local with unique nonzero prime \(\mathfrak m=\mathfrak pA\). The integrally closed property localizes: if \(z\) satisfies a monic equation over \(R_{\mathfrak p}\), multiply \(z\) by a common denominator \(t\notin\mathfrak p\) to make \(tz\) integral over \(R\); then \(tz\in R\), so \(z\in R_{\mathfrak p}\).

For \(0\ne a\in\mathfrak m\), the radical \(\sqrt{(a)}=\mathfrak m\). If \(\mathfrak m=(x_1,\ldots,x_r)\), choose \(d_i\) with \(x_i^{d_i}\in(a)\); then \(\mathfrak m^N\subseteq(a)\) for \(N=1+\sum(d_i-1)\). Choose the minimal such \(N\), and \(b\in\mathfrak m^{N-1}\setminus(a)\). Then \(z=b/a\notin A\) but \(z\mathfrak m\subseteq A\). If \(z\mathfrak m\subseteq\mathfrak m\), the determinant trick makes \(z\) integral, contradiction. Therefore \(z\mathfrak m=A\), so \(\mathfrak m=(z^{-1})=(\pi)\).

No nonzero \(x\in A\) belongs to \(\bigcap_{n\ge0}(\pi^n)\): otherwise the ideals \((x/\pi^n)\) are an infinite strict ascending chain. Hence \(x=u\pi^{v(x)}\) for a unit \(u\), with \(v(x)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). Fractions have integer valuation and every nonzero ideal of \(A\) is \((\pi^e)\). Thus \(A\) is a discrete valuation ring.

### Prime powers have exact residue counts

Let \(k=R/\mathfrak p\), \(q=|k|\). For \(j\ge0\), the module \(\mathfrak p^j/\mathfrak p^{j+1}\) is annihilated by \(\mathfrak p\), hence is a \(k\)-vector space. Localization at \(\mathfrak p\) changes nothing because each \(s\notin\mathfrak p\) acts as an invertible scalar on it. After localization,
\[
\frac{\mathfrak p^j}{\mathfrak p^{j+1}}
\cong \frac{\pi^j A}{\pi^{j+1}A}\cong\frac A{\pi A}=k,
\]
so \(|\mathfrak p^j/\mathfrak p^{j+1}|=q\). Multiplication of indices in
\[
R\supset\mathfrak p\supset\cdots\supset\mathfrak p^e
\]
gives \(\boxed{N(\mathfrak p^e)=q^e}\).

If \(I=\prod_i\mathfrak p_i^{e_i}\), distinct maximal ideals have comaximal powers. CRT gives
\[
R/I\cong\prod_i R/\mathfrak p_i^{e_i},\qquad
N(I):=|R/I|=\prod_i N(\mathfrak p_i)^{e_i}.
\]
Using unique factorization and addition of exponents:
\[
\boxed{N(IJ)=N(I)N(J)}
\qquad (I,J\subset R\text{ nonzero integral ideals}).
\]
The local layer argument, not CRT by itself, is what handles shared prime factors.

For principal ideals,
\[
N((\alpha))=[R:\alpha R]
=|\det(m_\alpha)|=|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)|.
\]

### Quadratic check and exercises

For \(R=\mathbb Z[w]\), \(w^2=-5\), and \(\mathfrak p=(2,1+w)\),
\[
N(\mathfrak p)=2,\quad\mathfrak p^2=(2),\quad
N((2))=|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(2)|=4.
\]
The two ideals above three have norm three and multiply to \((3)\), of norm nine.

1. Explain in detail why \(\mathfrak p^j/\mathfrak p^{j+1}\) is unchanged by localizing at \(\mathfrak p\).
2. If \(I+J=R\), prove \(I^e+J^f=R\) using the binomial theorem, then derive the usual CRT isomorphism.
3. Compare the local valuation \(v_{\mathfrak p}(x)\) with the exponent of \(\mathfrak p\) in the principal fractional ideal \((x)\).
4. Suppose a rational prime \(p\) splits into \(\mathfrak p_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak p_r^{e_r}\). Use \(N((p))=p^n\) to prove \(\sum_i e_if_i=n\), where \(N(\mathfrak p_i)=p^{f_i}\).
5. Explain why the proposed rule \(N(IJ)=N(I)N(J)\) cannot be deduced from the CRT for \(I,J\) unless you first handle common prime factors.
