# Ramification Exponents: Tame Primes, Wild Primes, and the Different

The square root of three and the square root of two both generate quadratic extensions of p-adic fields. Near the prime three, the square root of three is ramified, and its different has exponent one. Near the prime two, the square root of two is ramified, but its different has exponent three. Both extensions have ramification index two. Why does one pay a cost of one, while the other pays three?

The difference is not an accidental feature of these equations. It is the distinction between tame and wild ramification. An extension of ramification index e is tame when the residue characteristic does not divide e. At a wild prime, the residue characteristic does divide e. The different measures the gap between these cases.

## First separate residue growth from ramification

Let K be a finite extension of the p-adic numbers, with ring of integers A and normalized valuation v-K. Let L be a finite extension, with ring of integers B, normalized valuation v-L, ramification index e, and residue degree f. We have already proved that the extension degree is e times f.

The residue degree counts how many new residue-field elements appear. The ramification index counts how much the unit of valuation changes. We should not confuse these. An unramified extension can have large degree while still having e equal to one. In that case the residue field grows, but the local uniformizer does not need a finer division.

Our finite-extension theorem constructs a maximal unramified intermediate field K-zero between K and L. It has degree f over K. Its ring of integers is generated by a lift of a primitive residue-field element, whose minimal polynomial has separable reduction. The derivative of that polynomial is a unit, because it remains nonzero modulo the residue characteristic. By our derivative formula, its different is the unit ideal. So no different exponent is contributed by the unramified part.

The remaining extension L over K-zero is totally ramified of degree e, with the same residue field on both sides. It is generated by a uniformizer pi of L, whose minimal polynomial over K-zero is Eisenstein. We have previously proved that its integer ring is the integer ring of K-zero adjoin pi. The derivative formula is therefore available with no index ambiguity.

This reduces the question to a single explicit calculation. Let g of X be X to the e plus c-e-minus-one times X to e-minus-one, down through c-one times X plus c-zero. Eisenstein means every coefficient except the leading one is divisible by a uniformizer of K-zero, while the constant coefficient has exactly one such factor. The root pi is a uniformizer of L.

## Differentiate an Eisenstein polynomial

The derivative g prime at pi has first term e times pi to the power e minus one. Each other term has the form j times c-j times pi to the power j minus one, for j between one and e minus one.

Normalize v-L so that pi has valuation one. Because L over K-zero has ramification index e, the uniformizer of K-zero has valuation e when measured in L. Thus every coefficient c-j has valuation at least e. The j-th lower derivative term has valuation at least e plus j minus one. In particular, it has valuation at least e.

What about the leading term? It has valuation e minus one, plus v-L of the integer e. If p does not divide e, then e is a unit in the ground local field. Its v-L valuation is zero. So the leading term has valuation exactly e minus one, strictly smaller than all the other derivative terms. No cancellation can destroy a uniquely lowest-valuation term. Therefore the valuation of g prime at pi is exactly e minus one.

We have proved the tame different exponent formula: for a finite extension of p-adic fields with ramification index e prime to p, the exponent of the different, measured in the normalized valuation of L, is e minus one.

Now suppose p divides e. The integer e is divisible by the ground-field uniformizer, so its v-L valuation is positive, in fact at least e. The leading derivative term now has valuation at least two e minus one. Every other term has valuation at least e. Therefore the entire derivative has valuation at least e. We have proved a sharp qualitative distinction: wild ramification forces the different exponent strictly above e minus one.

There is no universal formula depending only on e and f in the wild case. The coefficients of the Eisenstein polynomial matter. Two degree-two totally ramified extensions of the two-adics may have different exponents, even though both have e equal to two and f equal to one.

## A tame quadratic calculation

Take K equal to the three-adics and L obtained by adjoining the square root of three. The polynomial X squared minus three is Eisenstein at three. Its derivative at the root is twice the square root of three. Let pi be that square root. In L, pi has valuation one, while three has valuation two. The number two is a three-adic unit, so two pi has valuation one.

Thus the different is the first power of the maximal ideal. Here e is two, p is three, and e minus one is one. The formula agrees.

Contrast this with the square root of two over the two-adics. X squared minus two is Eisenstein, and the derivative is again two times the square root, but two is no longer a unit. In L, the square root of two has valuation one and two has valuation two. Their product has valuation three. Thus the different is the third power of the maximal ideal: not the first.

The same ramification index has produced a larger different exponent, because the derivative of the leading term has an additional factor divisible by the residue characteristic.

## Another wild quadratic, a different answer

The extension of the two-adics obtained by adjoining i is also totally ramified quadratic. If you use i itself as an integral generator, its minimal polynomial is X squared plus one and its derivative is two i. The valuation of i is zero, while the valuation of two in the extension is two. So the different exponent is two.

If we want a uniformizer, we can take one plus i. Its square is two i, so it has normalized valuation one. Its minimal polynomial is X squared minus two X plus two, which is Eisenstein at two. The derivative at one plus i is two i, once again of valuation two.

Thus one quadratic wild extension has different exponent three, and another has exponent two. They are not distinguished by e and f. The distinction is encoded in how their integral generators and conjugates differ.

Now consider the unramified quadratic extension of the two-adics generated by a root of X squared minus X minus one. Modulo two this polynomial becomes X squared plus X plus one, irreducible over the two-element field. Its derivative is two X minus one, which reduces to one modulo two and is therefore a unit. This extension has different exponent zero. The three examples give exponents zero, two, and three, all in degree two, depending on how the local field is built.

## Why the discriminant detects exactly the ramified primes

For a complete discrete valuation ring A, choose an A-basis of the ring of integers B of L. Reduce its trace Gram matrix modulo the maximal ideal of A. The determinant reduces to the discriminant of the finite algebra B modulo the ground-field uniformizer.

We can describe that finite algebra. If pi-K is a uniformizer in A and P the maximal ideal of B, then pi-K times B equals P to the e. So B modulo pi-K is B modulo P to the e. If e is greater than one, it contains nonzero nilpotents: the image of a uniformizer of L is nilpotent. A nonzero nilpotent lies in the radical of the trace pairing, because multiplication by it is nilpotent after multiplying by any other element. Hence the reduced trace pairing is degenerate and its discriminant vanishes.

If e equals one, our residue field extension is finite and separable, because the residue fields of finite p-adic extensions are finite fields. Then B modulo pi-K is just the residue field of L. For a separable field extension, the trace pairing is nondegenerate. Its determinant does not vanish. So the local discriminant is a unit.

This proves that a prime divides the discriminant precisely when the extension ramifies there. It also explains why in an extension of global number fields only finitely many primes ramify: the global discriminant is a nonzero ideal with finitely many prime divisors. Completing at any prime transfers the question to the local calculation just made.

## Measuring the defect

There are now two separate statements. The discriminant detects whether ramification occurs at all. The different exponent measures how strongly it occurs. For tame ramification, that exponent is determined entirely by the ramification index: e minus one. For wild ramification it is larger, and detailed arithmetic of the extension is required to know exactly how much larger.

This is why the prime two repeatedly receives special attention in quadratic number fields. In a degree-two extension, the residue characteristic two divides e whenever that extension ramifies. Every ramified quadratic extension at two is wild. At an odd prime, a ramified quadratic extension is tame.

The derivative calculation has turned a broad distinction into an exact one. Tame ramification means a uniquely smallest derivative term survives. Wild ramification means that leading coefficient is itself divisible by the residue prime, and the derivative's valuation rises. The different remembers that rise. Later, higher ramification groups will measure it by tracking how strongly field automorphisms fix residue classes. For now we already have a precise and usable theory, together with examples where a small numerical difference carries substantial arithmetic meaning.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let `L/K` be a finite extension of nonarchimedean local fields with finite residue fields. Write `e=e(L/K)` and let `v_L(pi_L)=1`; let the different be `D_{L/K}=P_L^d`.

For the maximal unramified `K_0/K`, `D_{K_0/K}=O_{K_0}`. For the totally ramified remainder `L/K_0`, choose an Eisenstein polynomial
\[
g(X)=X^e+\sum_{j=0}^{e-1}c_jX^j,\qquad O_L=O_{K_0}[\pi_L].
\]

Then
\[
 d=v_L(g'(\pi_L)),\qquad
 g'(\pi_L)=e\pi_L^{e-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{e-1}jc_j\pi_L^{j-1}.
\]

The lower terms have valuation at least `e` and the leading term has valuation `e-1+v_L(e)`. Thus
\[
\boxed{d=e-1\quad\text{if }p\nmid e,\qquad d\ge e\quad\text{if }p\mid e.}
\]

Examples, with `v_L` normalized to give a uniformizer value 1:

| Local field | `(e,f)` | different generator | `d` |
|---|---|---|---:|
| `Q_3(sqrt 3)` | `(2,1)` | `2sqrt 3` | 1 |
| `Q_2(sqrt 2)` | `(2,1)` | `2sqrt 2` | 3 |
| `Q_2(i)` | `(2,1)` | `2i` | 2 |
| unramified `Q_2`-quadratic | `(1,2)` | unit | 0 |

For the ramification test, the trace pairing on `O_L/pi_K O_L` is nondegenerate iff `e=1` (finite residue fields are perfect); hence the local discriminant is a unit iff the extension is unramified.
