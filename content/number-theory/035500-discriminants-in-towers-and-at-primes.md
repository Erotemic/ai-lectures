# Discriminants in Towers: Local Exponents and Global Norms

Imagine that we build a number field in stages. First adjoin i to the rationals. Then adjoin a square root of i. We arrive at the field generated by an eighth root of unity. Its field discriminant is two hundred fifty-six. The first stage already has discriminant minus four. What part of the final discriminant comes from the first extension, and what part comes from the second?

There must be a consistent way to answer, because a number field does not remember which sequence of intermediate fields we happened to use to describe it. The different and discriminant have tower formulas that make such calculations compatible. We will derive them from traces, and then learn how local exponents assemble into global ones.

## Traces compose

Let K be contained in L, and L contained in M, with all extensions finite and separable. The first fact is a linear-algebra identity: the trace from M to K of an element x equals the trace from L to K of the trace from M to L of x.

This is plausible from the embedding viewpoint. The embeddings of M over K can be grouped according to their restrictions to L; summing conjugates first within each group and then over the groups gives the total sum. Equivalently, multiplication by x on M can be represented in blocks after selecting an L-basis and a K-basis. Taking the ordinary matrix trace in two stages produces the same result.

Trace transitivity is the central tool. A condition that the trace all the way to K be integral can be divided into two conditions: first trace down to L, then pair that result against the integer ring of L.

Let A, B, and C be the rings of integers of K, L, and M. We defined the codifferent of B over A to be the set of elements y of L whose trace of yB lies in A. For C over B we have the analogous fractional ideal in M. For C over A, the condition is that the trace from M to K of x times every element of C lies in A.

Use trace transitivity. The latter condition says that, for every c in C, the relative trace from M to L of xc lies in the codifferent of B over A. The codifferent of B over A is a fractional ideal J of B. Requiring a trace pairing to land in J rather than in B simply multiplies the relative codifferent of C over B by the extended ideal J times C. This is because J is invertible: locally it is generated by a single element, and we may divide the condition by that generator.

Thus the codifferent for C over A is the codifferent for C over B times the extension to C of the codifferent for B over A. Invert the fractional ideals. We get the tower law for the different: the different of M over K equals the different of M over L times the different of L over K extended to the integer ring of M.

This algebraic formula tells us how ramification defects accumulate in towers. It is not usually ordinary addition of two integer exponents, because an ideal from L changes its valuation when extended to M. That scaling factor is a ramification index.

## The local exponent law

Suppose we work in completed local fields. Let e be the ramification index of M over L. Write d-one for the different exponent of L over K, and d-two for the different exponent of M over L, with each exponent measured using the normalized valuation in its own upper field.

Extending an ideal with d-one powers of the uniformizer of L into M multiplies its valuation by e. The tower law therefore becomes: the different exponent of M over K is d-two plus e times d-one.

For example, an unramified stage has exponent zero; it contributes nothing of its own. A tame, totally ramified stage of index e contributes e minus one, but an earlier different exponent gets multiplied by e as it passes through that stage. You can see how a small amount of ramification can become amplified as the tower grows.

This formula is especially useful when we know a field through successive adjoining of roots. Computing the minimal polynomial of one enormous primitive element may be awkward. Computing two or three modest different ideals and combining their exponents can be much easier.

## The discriminant tower law

The discriminant ideal is the ideal norm of the different. We proved that by comparing the index of the trace dual lattice with the determinant of the trace Gram matrix. Apply the ideal norm from M down to K to the different tower law.

Norms of ideals compose. For an ideal of B extended into C, the norm from M down to L is the original ideal raised to the degree of M over L. This is the ideal-theoretic counterpart of multiplying the determinant of a scalar map once for each basis vector.

Putting these pieces together gives the discriminant tower law. The discriminant ideal of M over K equals the discriminant ideal of L over K, raised to the degree of M over L, multiplied by the norm from L to K of the discriminant ideal of M over L.

Notice that the two factors live in the same base ring, A. The first repeats the old discriminant once for each dimension of the new stage. The second brings down the new relative discriminant by taking an ideal norm.

This formula is the precise version of saying that a tower contains old ramification and new ramification. It also gives a strong consistency check on calculations done by several methods.

## Recovering the rational prime exponent

Now specialize to a number field L over the rationals. Let p be an ordinary prime, and suppose its ideal in the ring of integers factors into prime ideals P-one through P-r, with ramification indices e-i and residue degrees f-i.

Write the different as a product of powers of prime ideals. At P-i its exponent is d-i. When we take its ideal norm to the ordinary integers, P-i contributes p to the f-i, because the quotient of the ring of integers by P-i has p to the f-i elements. Hence its d-i-th power contributes p to the product f-i times d-i.

So the exponent of p in the absolute value of the field discriminant is the sum over the primes above p of f-i times d-i.

The formula explains a common misreading. A degree-two unramified extension of the p-adics has residue degree two but different exponent zero, so it contributes nothing to the discriminant. A totally ramified quadratic extension has residue degree one, and the exponent in the discriminant is exactly its different exponent. In a number field with several primes above p, the global exponent combines contributions from each local field.

We can now state the ramification criterion precisely. A rational prime divides the field discriminant if and only if at least one prime above it ramifies. The implication works in both directions because every local different exponent is nonnegative and is positive exactly for ramified primes. For a general number-field extension, replace rational primes by prime ideals of the base ring.

## A tower containing the Gaussian integers

Return to the example in the opening. Put L equal to the rationals with i, and M equal to the rationals with a primitive eighth root of unity, call it zeta. Since zeta squared is i, M over L is quadratic. Its relative minimal polynomial is X squared minus i. In fact the full integer ring of M is the integer ring of L adjoin zeta; this is a special case of the cyclotomic integral-basis result we will prove shortly.

The relative derivative is two zeta. Its relative norm from M to L is minus four i, which generates the ideal four in the Gaussian integers. Therefore the relative discriminant ideal of M over L is the ideal generated by four.

The base field L has discriminant minus four. The degree of M over L is two, so the first factor of the discriminant tower law is minus four squared, or sixteen. The ideal norm of the ideal four from the Gaussian integers down to the ordinary integers is sixteen. Multiply the two factors. The absolute discriminant of M is two hundred fifty-six.

At first this might look like numerology. But the arithmetic matches the degrees and norms exactly: the original discriminant contributes sixteen after being repeated twice, and the new relative discriminant contributes another sixteen.

At the local prime two, the corresponding different exponents obey the local tower formula as well. The field L over the two-adics is ramified of index two with different exponent two. The extension M over L is ramified of index two; the derivative two zeta has valuation four in M because zeta is a unit and two has valuation four. The total exponent is four plus two times two, or eight. That matches the exponent of two in two hundred fifty-six.

## A bookkeeping method for real computations

When you calculate a discriminant through a tower, record four things at each step: the field, an integral basis or justified maximal order, the different or relative discriminant, and the ramification index at every relevant prime. Only after verifying the integral structure should you apply a derivative formula. Otherwise an index error at an early stage propagates through every later stage.

The central relationships are now visible without having to compute every basis from scratch. Traces compose, so codifferents multiply. Differents multiply, but valuations of extended ideals scale by ramification index. Their ideal norms produce discriminants. Finally, discriminant exponents are weighted sums of local different exponents, weighted by residue degrees.

This is an unusually useful chain of implications because each step answers a different kind of question. Trace duality describes a lattice. The different describes the extension ring. A local different exponent measures one prime of that ring. The discriminant returns the information to the base field, where we can compare different number fields and locate the ramified primes. We can move up and down the chain depending on which calculation is easiest.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a tower of finite separable number fields `K ⊂ L ⊂ M`, with integer rings `A ⊂ B ⊂ C`:
\[
\operatorname{Tr}_{M/K}=\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}\circ\operatorname{Tr}_{M/L},
\]
\[
\boxed{\mathfrak D_{M/K}=\mathfrak D_{M/L}\,(\mathfrak D_{L/K}\,C).}
\]
\[
\boxed{\mathfrak d_{M/K}=\mathfrak d_{L/K}^{[M:L]}\,N_{L/K}(\mathfrak d_{M/L}).}
\]

For local fields, with `e=e(M/L)` and `d=different exponent`,
\[
d(M/K)=d(M/L)+e(M/L)\,d(L/K).
\]

For a number field `L/Q` and rational prime `p`, if `pO_L=prod_{P|p} P^{e_P}` and `different=prod_{P}P^{d_P}`, then
\[
\boxed{v_p(|\Delta_L|)=\sum_{P\mid p} f_Pd_P.}
\]

Worked tower `Q ⊂ Q(i) ⊂ Q(zeta_8)`:
\[
\mathfrak d_{Q(i)/Q}=(4),\quad
\mathfrak d_{Q(\zeta_8)/Q(i)}=(4)\subseteq\mathbf Z[i],
\]
\[
|\Delta_{Q(\zeta_8)}|=4^2\cdot N_{\mathbf Q(i)/\mathbf Q}((4))=16\cdot16=256.
\]

Local at 2: `d(Q_2(i)/Q_2)=2`, `d(Q_2(zeta_8)/Q_2(i))=4`, `e(Q_2(zeta_8)/Q_2(i))=2`, so `d_total=4+2*2=8`.
