# Galois Groups: Symmetries of the Roots

The polynomial x squared minus two has two roots. We can exchange them without breaking their equation: send the square root of two to its negative while leaving every rational number unchanged. But a field contains more than a list of roots. It also contains their sums, products, ratios, and all the polynomial relations among them. If we propose to exchange roots, we must check that this exchange respects *every* such relation.

The correct object is a field automorphism. For an extension L over K, a K-automorphism of L is a bijection from L to itself preserving addition, multiplication, and the multiplicative identity, and fixing each element of K. These automorphisms form a group under composition. The group is written Aut of L over K. When L is a suitable splitting field, this becomes a Galois group.

## Why the image of a root is constrained

Suppose alpha satisfies a polynomial equation with coefficients in K. Apply an automorphism sigma that fixes K to that equation. Because sigma respects the field operations, the resulting equation says sigma of alpha satisfies the *same* polynomial. An automorphism therefore sends each root to another root of its minimal polynomial over K. When the extension is generated by the roots, the automorphism is completely determined by where it sends those generators.

This gives a faithful permutation action on the roots of a separable polynomial whose splitting field is L. Faithful means that if an automorphism fixes every root, it fixes all of L and is the identity. But the converse is not automatic: not every permutation of the roots extends to an automorphism. The chosen permutation must preserve all algebraic relations among the generators, not merely the one equation they visibly share.

For the square root of two, there are exactly two possibilities. If sigma fixes the rationals, it must send square root of two to either positive or negative square root of two. Each choice extends uniquely to a field automorphism, because every element has the form a plus b times square root of two. The resulting group has two elements. It records the one available flip.

## Three roots can support six symmetries

Now return to x cubed minus two. Let alpha be its positive real cube root and omega a primitive complex cube root of unity. The splitting field L is generated over the rationals by alpha and omega. Its degree is six. How many rational-fixing automorphisms does it have?

Any such automorphism must send alpha to one of the three cube roots: alpha, omega alpha, or omega squared alpha. It must send omega to a primitive cube root of unity: omega or omega squared. That suggests three times two, or six, candidate transformations. Are all six genuine? Yes. The minimal polynomial of alpha is irreducible, so sending it to another root defines a rational-field embedding of the degree-three field it generates. Once that image has been chosen, the quadratic polynomial for omega remains irreducible over the image field, which is still a real field generated by a real or nonreal cube root? Here we must be more precise: although the image of the real field need not lie in the real numbers, the extension to the full splitting field can be constructed by sending omega to either of its two roots, because x squared plus x plus one remains the minimal polynomial over the image of the degree-three subfield. The six embeddings all land in L and, because L is finite over the rationals, each is an automorphism.

There is a cleaner symmetry description. Define r by sending alpha to omega alpha and fixing omega. Applying r three times returns alpha, so r has order three. Define s to be complex conjugation: it fixes alpha and exchanges omega with omega squared, and has order two. Their actions do not commute. Conjugating r by s reverses its direction, giving the relation s r s equals r inverse.

Those are the same relations as the rotations and reflections of an equilateral triangle. The Galois group has six elements and is isomorphic to the permutation group on three objects, also called S three. This group is not merely an answer about cardinality. Its multiplication remembers how the two kinds of root symmetry interact.

The preceding extension-of-embeddings argument is a standard theorem for finite separable normal extensions. In this concrete case one can check the six formulas directly using the defining relations alpha cubed equals two, omega squared plus omega plus one equals zero, and the fact that alpha and omega generate the field. The group structure then follows without relying on a guess about permutations.

## An example where the group is not the full symmetric group

Consider L obtained by adjoining square root of two and square root of three to the rationals. Every element has a unique form a plus b square root of two plus c square root of three plus d square root of six. An automorphism fixing the rationals must send each square root independently to its positive or negative. All four sign choices extend to automorphisms, because the multiplication relations are preserved.

The group has four elements. Flipping the first radical twice is the identity, as is flipping the second twice, and the two flips commute. This is the Klein four-group, sometimes written C two times C two. It is not the cyclic group of order four: no element has order four. In contrast to the cubic example, the splitting field here has a commutative group of symmetries.

A useful test is the polynomial with roots square root of two, negative square root of two, square root of three, and negative square root of three. A random permutation among four roots will usually fail to preserve squares. For example, an automorphism cannot send both square root of two and negative square root of two to roots whose sum is nonzero. And once it sends square root of two to a choice of sign, it must send its negative to the opposite sign. So even though we see four roots, the automorphism group is a four-element subgroup of the much larger group of all permutations of four objects.

## What changes in an inseparable extension

There is one more reason not to equate degree with number of automorphisms blindly. Over the rational-function field in characteristic p, adjoin alpha with alpha to the p-th power equal to t. The extension has degree p, but x to the p-th power minus t has only one distinct root in any extension field. Every automorphism fixing the base must send alpha to another root; there is no other. Its automorphism group is trivial. Degree p, symmetry group of size one.

For a finite extension that is both normal and separable, the automorphism group has order equal to the extension degree. Splitting fields of separable polynomials meet those conditions. The cubic and biquadratic examples illustrate the equality, while the purely inseparable example explains why the hypotheses cannot simply be discarded.

## What the group is telling us

The coefficients of a polynomial define what is held fixed. Roots and combinations of roots constitute the enlarged field. Automorphisms describe exactly which changes of those new quantities leave the original arithmetic untouched. Instead of trying to calculate every root by radicals at once, we can study how the roots are related. This is what makes Galois theory powerful: it turns an equation into a group, and later turns subgroups into intermediate fields. The equation's complexity becomes a question about the possible patterns of symmetry.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let \(L/K\) be a field extension. Define
\[
\operatorname{Aut}_K(L)=\{\sigma:L\to L\mid\sigma\text{ is a field automorphism and }\sigma|_K=\operatorname{id}\}.
\]
If \(L\) is the splitting field of a separable \(f\in K[x]\), then \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\operatorname{Aut}_K(L)\), and the action on the roots of \(f\) is faithful. **Not every root permutation is induced by an automorphism.**

**Cubic.** Let \(\alpha^3=2\), \(\omega^3=1\), \(\omega\ne1\). Then \(\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\alpha,\omega)/\mathbb Q)\cong S_3\). Choose \(r(\alpha)=\omega\alpha\), \(r(\omega)=\omega\) and \(s(\alpha)=\alpha\), \(s(\omega)=\omega^2\). They satisfy
\[
r^3=s^2=1,\qquad srs=r^{-1}.
\]
To construct all six field automorphisms, use normality and separability, or extend embeddings along the tower \(\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb Q(\alpha)\subset L\). The quadratic relation \(x^2+x+1\) remains irreducible over each \(\mathbb Q(\omega^j\alpha)\), since a degree-three extension cannot contain the quadratic field \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\).

**Biquadratic.** For \(E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\), the four sign-change maps give \(\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)\cong C_2\times C_2\).

### Exercises with answers

1. What is the Galois group of \(x^2-7\) over \(\mathbb Q\)? **Answer:** \(C_2\), generated by the sign flip of \(\sqrt7\).
2. Why is the map \(\sqrt2\mapsto\sqrt3\) not a \(\mathbb Q\)-automorphism of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\)? **Answer:** it changes the equation \(x^2=2\) into \(x^2=3\).
3. What is the automorphism group of the purely inseparable extension \(\mathbb F_p(t^{1/p})/\mathbb F_p(t)\)? **Answer:** the trivial group.
