# Tensor Products: Encoding Bilinear Structure in a Linear Map

A linear map takes one vector as input and respects addition and scalar multiplication. But mathematics constantly asks about constructions involving two inputs: the dot product of two vectors, the product of two polynomials, or the operation that multiplies one function by another. Such a rule is often linear in each input separately, a property called bilinearity. If we fix one argument, the rule is linear in the other. It need not be linear in the pair considered as a single vector in the ordinary direct sum.

Tensor products are a way of organizing all bilinear rules into one universal linear construction. They also allow us to combine modules and representations in ways that direct sums cannot. The word universal has an exact meaning: a single object represents an entire family of compatible maps.

## Why pairs of vectors are not enough

Take two vector spaces V and W over the same field. A naïve attempt is to form pairs of vectors. But a bilinear rule treats a pair differently from an element of a direct sum. For a bilinear function B, if you double the first input, you double the output; if you double the second input, you also double the output. If you double both inputs, you multiply the output by four. A linear map on the direct sum would multiply its output only by two when both components were doubled.

Instead, introduce a formal symbol, spoken as v tensor w, for every pair of vectors. Impose the relations demanded by bilinearity. The symbol for v plus v-prime tensor w is the sum of the symbols for v tensor w and v-prime tensor w. Similarly in the second argument. And a scalar may pass from either input to the coefficient of the resulting symbol.

The space generated by these symbols, modulo those relations, is the tensor product, written V tensor W. Most of its elements are finite sums of elementary tensors. An arbitrary element need not be a single v tensor w. That distinction matters: restricting attention to elementary tensors discards most of the tensor product.

Now any bilinear map B from V times W to a vector space U extends uniquely to a linear map from V tensor W to U. The linear map sends each elementary tensor v tensor w to B of v and w. The imposed relations guarantee that this assignment is well-defined; uniqueness follows because elementary tensors generate the tensor product.

We could have started with a different construction and tried to prove it has the same property. The universal property tells us the result would be canonically isomorphic. This is what makes the tensor product a mathematical object rather than a particular collection of formal symbols.

## Count the dimensions

Suppose V has basis e-one through e-m and W has basis f-one through f-n. The elementary tensors e-i tensor f-j span the tensor product. Every elementary tensor is expanded by expressing its two factors in their bases and distributing bilinearly. The coefficient on e-i tensor f-j is the product of the corresponding coordinates.

Why are these m times n tensors independent? For each pair of basis indices, take the bilinear rule that multiplies the i-th coordinate of the first input by the j-th coordinate of the second. The universal property turns this into a linear functional on V tensor W. Applied to our list of basis tensors, it selects exactly one item. Hence no nontrivial linear relation among them exists.

So, in finite dimension, the dimension of V tensor W is the dimension of V times the dimension of W. For example, a two-dimensional vector space tensored with a three-dimensional one has dimension six, not five. The direct sum has dimension five; the tensor product has dimension six because it accounts for all pairwise interactions of coordinates.

Matrices offer a concrete realization. A two-vector tensored with a three-vector corresponds to an outer-product matrix of size two by three. A general tensor corresponds to a sum of outer products, which may have matrix rank greater than one. This is another reason not every tensor is elementary.

## Tensor products of representations

If a group G acts linearly on V and W, it acts on V tensor W by sending v tensor w to the image of v tensored with the image of w. This defines another representation because group composition respects both factors and the bilinear relations.

A one-dimensional sign representation of the triangle group tensored with itself is trivial: each group element acts by the square of positive or negative one. Thus tensor products can combine representations and produce new decomposition problems. In a fixed basis, the corresponding matrices are Kronecker products of the original representation matrices.

The character of a tensor-product representation is the pointwise product of the two characters. This follows from the matrix identity that the trace of a Kronecker product equals the product of the traces. Together with character orthogonality, this gives a practical method to decompose tensor products into irreducible representations.

This construction is not the same as taking a direct sum. Direct sum collects alternative degrees of freedom. Tensor product collects pairwise interactions, and often makes symmetry constraints on those interactions visible.

## Modules over the integers behave differently

Over a field, tensor products are well behaved with respect to dimensions. Over the integers, tensor products encode divisibility information that cannot be seen by multiplying ranks alone.

Consider the module of integers modulo two. It has an element of order two: twice that element is zero. Consider integers modulo three, with an element of order three. What is the tensor product of these two modules over the integers?

Take any elementary tensor a tensor b. The first factor is annihilated by two, so two times this tensor is zero. The second factor is annihilated by three, so three times the same tensor is zero. But two and three have integer coefficients whose combination equals one. Consequently the tensor itself must be zero. All elementary tensors vanish, so the whole tensor product is the zero module.

More generally, tensoring the integers modulo m with the integers modulo n yields the integers modulo their greatest common divisor. When m and n are relatively prime, there is no common torsion to retain.

Tensoring with integers modulo two has a simple effect on an integer module M: it identifies elements that differ by an even multiple, giving M modulo two M. This is sometimes called reduction modulo two. It is a change of coefficients; it can reveal arithmetic structures while erasing others.

## When tensoring loses exactness

Consider the injective map from the integers to the integers given by multiplication by two. If we tensor this map with integers modulo two, it becomes multiplication by two on the integers modulo two, which is the zero map. The original injection no longer remains injective.

This is the first sign of a broader principle. Tensor product over a ring preserves quotients and right exact sequences, but it need not preserve injections. Over a field tensoring by a fixed vector space is exact; over a general ring it may fail to be exact. The modules for which tensoring preserves injections are called flat modules.

The failure is not a technical inconvenience. It is a measurable phenomenon. Homological algebra introduces derived constructions, especially the functor Tor, to measure exactly what tensoring fails to preserve. The next part of the course will make that claim precise by using projective resolutions.

The tensor product therefore accomplishes three jobs: it turns bilinear rules into linear rules, it combines representations, and it exposes arithmetic torsion. Each job is a different face of the same universal object.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a commutative ring \(R\), modules \(M,N\), and an \(R\)-module \(P\), the tensor product \(M\otimes_R N\) is characterized by
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_R(M\otimes_R N,P)\cong \operatorname{Bilin}_R(M\times N,P).
\]
This is natural in \(P\). For a noncommutative ring, use a right \(R\)-module for the first factor and a left \(R\)-module for the second, with the balancing relation \((mr)\otimes n=m\otimes(rn)\).

**Construction.** Take the free \(R\)-module on pairs \((m,n)\) and quotient by the additive and balanced-scalar relations. Finite sums of pure tensors generate the quotient. A universal bilinear map descends uniquely through those relations.

**Bases.** For \(k\)-spaces with bases \((e_i)_{i=1}^m\), \((f_j)_{j=1}^n\), the elements \(e_i\otimes f_j\) form a basis for \(V\otimes_k W\). Coordinate product bilinear maps distinguish the basis elements.

**Cyclic abelian groups.** \(\mathbb Z/m\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z/n\cong\mathbb Z/\gcd(m,n)\). One derivation: \((\mathbb Z/m)\otimes N\cong N/mN\) with \(N=\mathbb Z/n\).

**Right exact but not left exact.** Tensoring \(0\to\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2\to0\) with \(\mathbb Z/2\) yields \(\mathbb Z/2\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb Z/2\to\mathbb Z/2\to0\), which remains right exact but the first arrow is not injective.

**Tensor characters.** For finite-dimensional complex representations, \(\chi_{V\otimes W}(g)=\chi_V(g)\chi_W(g)\).

### Exercises with answers

1. Find \(\dim(\mathbb R^2\otimes\mathbb R^4)\). **Answer:** eight.
2. Is every element of \(\mathbb R^2\otimes\mathbb R^2\) a pure tensor? **No.** Under the matrix identification, the identity matrix has rank two, whereas every pure tensor has rank at most one.
3. Compute \(\mathbb Z/6\otimes\mathbb Z/10\). **Answer:** \(\mathbb Z/2\).
4. Compute sign \(\otimes\) sign for \(S_3\). **Answer:** the trivial one-dimensional representation.
