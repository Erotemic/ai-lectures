# Number fields, class groups, and arithmetic heights

## Algebraic Number Fields: Discriminants and Ramification

The integers have a familiar property: every positive integer factors uniquely into primes. Earlier we learned that this statement has a productive generalization. In a Dedekind domain, elements may fail to factor uniquely, but nonzero ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals. Today we use that fact to understand an algebraic number field, a finite extension of the rational numbers.

The guiding question is surprisingly concrete. Choose an ordinary rational prime, such as two, five, or eleven. When we allow algebraic numbers, does that prime remain indivisible? Does it split into several primes? Or does a prime factor appear with multiplicity? These alternatives encode arithmetic information about the extension. They are called inertness, splitting, and ramification.

### Which integers belong to a number field?

Consider the field obtained by adjoining the square root of five to the rationals. Any element can be written as a rational number a plus a rational number b times the square root of five. But which of these elements count as integers? The correct definition is not simply to require a and b themselves to be integers. An algebraic integer is a root of some monic polynomial with integer coefficients.

The number one plus the square root of five, divided by two, is an algebraic integer: it satisfies the polynomial t squared minus t minus one. Let us call it omega. The complete ring of integers of this field consists of all a plus b omega, where a and b are ordinary integers. It is larger than the ring generated by the square root of five alone.

That distinction will matter when we calculate discriminants. A basis for the smaller ring is not necessarily an integral basis for the whole field. If we forget the difference, a prime can appear to ramify when the polynomial merely records a nonmaximal order.

### Norm and trace remember conjugates

There are two embeddings of our quadratic field into the complex numbers that fix the rationals. One sends the square root of five to itself; the other sends it to its negative. Apply both embeddings to an algebraic number. Their sum is its trace and their product its norm.

For omega, the two conjugates are omega and one minus omega. Their sum is one, and their product is negative one. These are exactly the signed coefficients of its defining quadratic polynomial. The norm of a product is the product of the norms, a fact that gives a bridge between multiplication of elements and arithmetic of ideals.

For an integral basis, we can construct a matrix of traces of pairwise basis products. Its determinant is the field discriminant. For the basis one and omega, the matrix has first row two and one, and second row one and three. Its determinant is five. So the field discriminant here is five.

This one integer carries striking information: a rational prime ramifies in a number field precisely when it divides the field discriminant. In this example the sole ramified rational prime is five. Be careful: that criterion uses the discriminant of the full ring of integers, not an arbitrary polynomial presentation.

### Splitting, inertia, and ramification

Let K be a number field, and let its ring of integers be O. An ordinary prime p defines an ideal p times O. Unique ideal factorization writes it as a product of prime ideals P one through P g, each raised to a positive exponent e. That exponent is its ramification index. For each prime ideal P, the quotient O modulo P is a finite field, containing the field with p elements. Its degree over that smaller field is called the residue degree f.

A central relation states that the sum of e times f over all primes P lying above p equals the degree of K over the rationals. Thus an extension of degree two has only a few possibilities. The prime may remain prime, with residue degree two. It may split into two different prime ideals, each with residue degree one and ramification index one. Or it may become the square of a single prime ideal, with ramification index two and residue degree one.

In our field, consider p equal to two. Reduce the polynomial for omega modulo two. It becomes t squared plus t plus one, which has no roots in the field with two elements. Thus it remains irreducible, and two stays prime in the ring of integers; it is inert.

For p equal to five, reduction gives a repeated root. Indeed the ideal generated by five is the square of the prime ideal generated by the square root of five. That is ramification: one prime ideal occurs twice. For p equal to eleven, the polynomial has distinct linear factors modulo eleven, so eleven splits into two different prime ideals.

### Why the polynomial test needs a hypothesis

This easy test by reducing a minimal polynomial is related to the Dedekind–Kummer theorem. When the ring of integers equals the ring generated by the chosen algebraic integer, or more generally when p does not divide the index of that subring in the full ring of integers, factorization modulo p correctly describes prime-ideal factorization above p.

Here omega generates the full ring, so the test works at every prime. But if we use the square root of five instead, the smaller order has index two. Its polynomial t squared minus five reduces modulo two to a repeated factor. It would misleadingly suggest ramification at two, although two is inert in the actual number field.

A discriminant computation makes the discrepancy visible. The basis one and the square root of five has discriminant twenty, whereas the integral basis one and omega has discriminant five. The ratio is the square of the index, namely four. It is not a new ramified prime of the field. It is an artifact of a deficient lattice of algebraic integers.

### Why ramification matters beyond examples

Ramification is a local failure of a prime to remain simple under extension. At unramified primes, reduction behaves cleanly, and Frobenius automorphisms help describe how primes split in Galois extensions. At ramified primes, repeated factors and nontrivial powers of prime ideals appear, and more delicate invariants such as the different arise.

Discriminants also control arithmetic estimates, arithmetic geometry, and finiteness theorems. They quantify how far the integer lattice in an extension is from behaving like independent copies of the original integers. But a discriminant is not a complete fingerprint of the field: different number fields can have the same discriminant.

The key lesson is that a rational prime does not have a single fate in a number field. It can persist, separate, or ramify. The distinction is encoded in residue degrees, ramification indices, and the integral structure of the extension. The ideal-theoretic viewpoint is what makes that arithmetic stable under changes of generators.

## Units, Norms, and Class Groups: Measuring Failure of Unique Factorization

Consider the equation six equals two times three. In the ring of integers, these are the beginnings of a familiar, essentially unique prime factorization. Now enlarge the ring to include the square root of negative five. Six is still two times three, but it is also the product of one plus that square root and one minus it. All four factors are irreducible in the larger ring, and the two factorizations cannot be turned into one another by reordering or multiplying factors by invertible elements.

This is not merely an inconvenience for arithmetic. It tells us something systematic about which rings allow prime factorization of elements and which do not. In the preceding study of Dedekind domains, we learned that nonzero ideals retain unique factorization into prime ideals. The class group measures exactly the discrepancy between those two facts.

### The first diagnostic: norms

Take the ring made of numbers a plus b times the square root of negative five, with a and b integers. Conjugation changes the sign of the square root. Multiply an element by its conjugate, and the result is a squared plus five b squared, a nonnegative integer. This is its field norm.

The norm is multiplicative: the norm of a product is the product of the norms. In particular, the only units in this ring have norm one. Solving a squared plus five b squared equals one shows that the units are just plus one and minus one.

Now ask whether two, three, or one plus the square root of negative five could factor nontrivially. Their norms are respectively four, nine, and six. A nontrivial factorization would force norms of nonunits to multiply to those numbers. But the ring contains no element with norm two or three, because a squared plus five b squared cannot equal either. Thus the displayed factors are irreducible. They are not associates: their norms and, where necessary, direct comparison rule that out. The two factorizations of six really are different.

The norm does two jobs here. It gives a finite arithmetic test for a particular assertion, and it warns us that the behavior of elements cannot be rescued by a clever choice of irreducibles.

### Replace elements by ideals

There is another way to package a factorization. To an element alpha we associate the principal ideal consisting of all multiples of alpha. A principal ideal forgets the choice of generator up to a unit, which is exactly the ambiguity we already regard as harmless.

In a Dedekind domain, every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. Yet not every ideal needs to be principal. This is where the class group enters. We allow nonzero fractional ideals, so that ideals can be multiplied and inverted. Two fractional ideals represent the same class when one is a nonzero field-element multiple of the other. The set of classes forms an abelian group under ideal multiplication. Its identity is the class of principal ideals.

If the class group is trivial, every nonzero ideal is principal. In a Dedekind domain that is equivalent to being a principal ideal domain, hence a unique factorization domain. Conversely, when the class group contains a nonidentity element, an ideal cannot be generated by one element. The obstruction is recorded in a group that can often be computed.

Return to our example. Consider the ideal generated by two and one plus the square root of negative five. The quotient of the ring by this ideal has two elements, so its ideal norm is two. It cannot be principal: a generator would have absolute norm two, and no such element exists. Thus we have found a nontrivial class without needing to enumerate all elements.

There is also a pleasant surprise. The square of this ideal is the principal ideal generated by two. In the class group, its class therefore has order two. It is a genuine failure of unique element factorization, but a tightly controlled one.

### Why the class group is finite

A group of fractional ideal classes might sound enormous. For the ring of integers of a number field, a central theorem says the class group is finite. The proof is not a purely symbolic manipulation of ideals; geometry enters.

Embed a number field into a real vector space using all its real and complex embeddings. An ideal becomes a lattice there. Minkowski's geometry-of-numbers theorem finds a suitably short, nonzero lattice point in a large symmetric convex body. Applied to the inverse of an ideal, the point produces an integral ideal in the desired class whose norm is bounded in terms of the field's degree and discriminant.

Only finitely many integral ideals have norm below any fixed bound. Thus every ideal class has a representative in one finite list. That gives finiteness and, in favorable examples, a practical algorithm: factor only the prime ideals whose norms lie below the Minkowski bound, and find the relations between their classes.

For the field with the square root of negative five, the discriminant is negative twenty, and the degree-two Minkowski bound is less than three. Every class can therefore be represented by an integral ideal of norm one or two. Up to the principal class, the only candidate is the prime ideal over two that we just constructed. We have already seen that its square is principal and that it is not principal. Consequently the class group has precisely two elements.

### Units are a different kind of arithmetic information

A norm also detects units, but the set of units is not the class group. The units form a multiplicative group inside the ring, while the class group measures ideals modulo principal ideals.

In an imaginary quadratic field like our example, the unit group is finite. Real quadratic fields behave differently. In the integers of the field generated by the square root of two, one plus that square root is a unit because its norm is negative one. Its positive and negative powers supply infinitely many units.

Dirichlet's unit theorem describes the general pattern. If a number field has r real embeddings and s pairs of complex embeddings, its unit group is a finite group of roots of unity times a free abelian group of rank r plus s minus one. The rank is explained by taking logarithms of the absolute values of the embeddings. Multiplicative norm constraints become a linear equation, leaving a lattice in a hyperplane.

Two invariants, then, answer different questions. Units describe ambiguity within the elements that generate a principal ideal. The ideal class group describes the ideals for which no such generator exists at all.

### The broader lesson

Unique factorization did not simply disappear when we enlarged the integers. It migrated from elements to ideals, and the discrepancy became an object of its own: the class group. This point of view reaches beyond quadratic examples. Class groups shape questions about integer solutions of equations, the behavior of primes in extensions, and ultimately the geometry of algebraic curves.

A useful mathematical habit is on display. When an expected theorem fails, do not merely catalogue counterexamples. Ask whether the failure can be measured. Here the measurement is finite, computable in many cases, and rich enough to distinguish number fields whose elements look deceptively similar.

## Arithmetic Intersection Theory: Heights and the Size of Rational Points

A rational point on a projective line can be written as a pair of integers, not both zero. But multiplying both entries by the same nonzero number does not change the point. So if we want to measure how complicated a rational point is, we need a size that does not depend on an arbitrary choice of coordinates.

Take a point represented by two relatively prime integers a and b. A natural answer is the logarithm of the larger absolute value. The point one over three has height log three. So does the projective point represented by two and six, because after dividing by their common factor it represents one and three. The height measures the arithmetic cost of specifying the point, not its distance from a chosen location in Euclidean space.

This elementary measure becomes a bridge to geometry. Divisors and line bundles let us define height functions on general projective varieties. Arithmetic intersection theory provides a way of understanding how those heights transform and how they behave in families.

### Why every absolute value matters

At first, rational numbers seem to have one absolute value: their usual magnitude. But number theory also has p-adic absolute values, one for each prime p. A rational number can be small in the ordinary sense and large with respect to a p-adic valuation, or conversely.

The product formula says that when we multiply the appropriately normalized absolute values of a nonzero rational number over all places, the answer is one. In logarithms, the sum is zero. This is exactly what makes a properly normalized projective height independent of multiplying the coordinates by a common rational scalar.

For a projective point with rational coordinates, we sum the logarithm of the largest coordinate absolute value at each place. For primitive integer coordinates, all the non-Archimedean contributions vanish, leaving our familiar logarithm of the largest integer magnitude. For a point over a number field, the same idea works with local weights and a normalization by field degree.

This definition may look like bookkeeping until we ask an arithmetic question: how many algebraic points can have bounded height? Northcott's finiteness theorem says that once we bound both height and degree over the rational numbers, only finitely many algebraic points remain. The degree restriction matters. Infinitely many roots of unity have bounded height, but their degrees can grow.

### From heights to line bundles

On an ordinary projective curve, a divisor records zeros and poles, with multiplicity. A line bundle turns this local information into a geometric object that can be pulled back and tensored. The degree of a line bundle measures the net divisor contribution.

In arithmetic geometry, a line bundle alone is not quite enough. We must also specify how to measure sections at the complex embeddings. This additional measurement is a metric. A line bundle equipped with suitable metrics is called a metrized line bundle. Its arithmetic degree combines contributions from finite primes and from the Archimedean places.

The need for both sorts of contributions is not an aesthetic choice. If we change a trivialization by multiplying by a rational function, its valuations shift at finite places and its ordinary absolute values shift at infinity. The product formula is what makes the total arithmetic degree invariant.

Consider a rational point on the projective line. It defines a section of the projection from the arithmetic projective line over the integers to its base. Pulling back the metrized hyperplane bundle gives an arithmetic degree. With standard normalizations, this degree recovers the logarithmic height of the point. The familiar quantity log three is therefore an arithmetic degree of a geometric line bundle.

### Intersection over an arithmetic base

On a surface over a field, intersecting two divisors produces a number. Over the spectrum of the integers, the geometric situation has an extra dimension. Intersections of horizontal and vertical divisors at finite primes contribute discrete terms, while complex embeddings contribute analytic corrections involving metrics and Green functions.

The full Arakelov intersection pairing combines these pieces. We should not picture it merely as counting where two curves meet. At infinity, an intersection has an analytic component. This allows arithmetic intersections to remain compatible with changes of rational sections and with the global product formula.

A good use of the theory is to compare arithmetic and geometric positivity. A line bundle may be ample in the geometric sense, but the heights it produces also depend on the chosen metrics, up to bounded changes in many common constructions. This gives a quantitative language for studying rational and integral points.

### A small calculation and a large principle

Compute the height of the rational projective point represented by twelve and eighteen. Divide by the greatest common divisor, six. We obtain two and three, so the logarithmic height is log three. The original coordinates had larger numbers, but their redundancy should not count. Now consider the squaring map on the projective line. It sends the point two to three to four to nine, so the height doubles: log nine equals twice log three. More generally, the standard height on the projective line scales approximately, and for this monomial example exactly, by the degree of the map.

That scaling is one reason heights are useful in arithmetic dynamics. If a rational map repeatedly raises arithmetic complexity, the height lets us quantify the growth. Arithmetic intersection theory gives the geometric infrastructure behind this apparently elementary observation.

The deeper lesson is that number theory has a geometry spanning all its places. A rational point has ordinary magnitude, p-adic information, and a projective equivalence relation. Height gathers those ingredients into one invariant. Arithmetic intersections explain why the invariant behaves like a geometric degree and why it can constrain the distribution of rational points.

### Algebraic Number Fields: Discriminants and Ramification

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** field extensions, ideals in Dedekind domains, norms and traces, polynomial factorization. **Central question:** what changes when a rational prime enters a larger number field?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a number field \(K/\mathbb Q\) of degree \(n\), let \(\mathcal O_K\) be the integral closure of \(\mathbb Z\) in \(K\). For any rational prime \(p\),
\[
p\mathcal O_K=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\mathfrak P_i^{e_i},\qquad
f_i=[\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P_i:\mathbb F_p],\qquad
\sum_i e_if_i=n.
\]
For an integral basis \(b_1,\ldots,b_n\), the field discriminant is
\[D_K=\det(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(b_i b_j))\in\mathbb Z.\]
For a number field, \(p\mid D_K\) iff some prime of \(K\) over \(p\) ramifies. If \(\alpha\) is integral with \(\mathbb Z[\alpha]\subseteq\mathcal O_K\) finite index \(m\), then
\[\operatorname{disc}(\mathbb Z[\alpha])=m^2D_K.\]
The simple factorization-mod-\(p\) test is valid under the Dedekind–Kummer hypothesis \(p\nmid[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\alpha]]\).

**Worked quadratic example:** Let \(\omega=(1+\sqrt5)/2\) and \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\), so \(\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\omega]\) and \(f(t)=t^2-t-1\). Since \(\operatorname{Tr}(1)=2\), \(\operatorname{Tr}(\omega)=1\), \(\omega^2=\omega+1\), we have \(\operatorname{Tr}(\omega^2)=3\), giving \(D_K=\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}=5\). Modulo \(2\), \(f=t^2+t+1\) is irreducible (inert). Modulo \(5\), \(f=(t-3)^2\) (ramified); indeed \((5)=(\sqrt5)^2\) as ideals. Modulo \(11\), \(f=(t-4)(t-8)\) (split: \(4+8=12\equiv1\), \(4\cdot8=32\equiv-1\)). For \(\alpha=\sqrt5\), the suborder \(\mathbb Z[\alpha]\) has index \(2\) and discriminant \(20\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. Compute the discriminant of \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt5]\) in its displayed basis. **Answer:** \(\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&10\end{pmatrix}=20\), which is not the field discriminant.
2. In a quadratic field, a prime has two factors with \(e_1=e_2=1\). What are their residue degrees? **Answer:** Both are one, since their weighted sum is two.
3. Why is a repeated factor of an arbitrary generator's polynomial modulo \(p\) insufficient to prove field ramification? **Answer:** \(p\) may divide the index of its generated order; the factorization theorem then does not apply.
4. What is the norm of \(\omega\)? **Answer:** \(-1\), the product of its conjugates.

### Units, Norms, and Class Groups: Measuring Failure of Unique Factorization

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** algebraic number fields, Dedekind domains, prime-ideal factorization, field norm. **Central question:** when elements fail to factor uniquely, what invariant records the obstruction?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a number field \(K\) with ring of integers \(\mathcal O_K\), let \(I_K\) be the group of nonzero fractional ideals and \(P_K=\{(\alpha):\alpha\in K^\times\}\). Then
\[\mathrm{Cl}(K)=I_K/P_K.\]
Every nonzero integral ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. Thus \(\mathrm{Cl}(K)=0\iff\mathcal O_K\) is a PID \(\iff\mathcal O_K\) is a UFD (for Dedekind \(\mathcal O_K\)).

**Explicit example:** \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})\), \(\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\), discriminant \(D_K=-20\), element norm \(N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2\). Let \(\mathfrak p=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})\). Then \(\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak p\cong\mathbb F_2\), so \(N(\mathfrak p)=2\). If \(\mathfrak p=(\alpha)\) then \(|N(\alpha)|=2\), impossible. The square satisfies \(\mathfrak p^2=(2)\): inclusion follows from the generators and both sides have norm four. Therefore \([\mathfrak p]\) is nontrivial of order two. The quadratic-field Minkowski bound is \((2/\pi)\sqrt{20}<3\); all classes have a representative of norm at most two. Hence \(\mathrm{Cl}(K)\cong\mathbb Z/2\).

**Units:** \(\mathcal O_K^\times\cong\mu(K)\times\mathbb Z^{r+s-1}\) abstractly (Dirichlet), with \(r+2s=[K:\mathbb Q]\). Here \(\mu(K)\) is the finite group of roots of unity in \(K\). The class-group finiteness theorem and unit theorem are distinct global results.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Why does norm three fail for \(a+b\sqrt{-5}\)? **Answer:** if \(b\ne0\), the norm is at least five; otherwise it is a square, never three.
2. Why does \(\mathfrak p\) have index two? **Answer:** set \(2=0\) and \(\sqrt{-5}=-1=1\) mod two, yielding \(\mathbb F_2\).
3. Why does \(\mathfrak p^2=(2)\) not imply \(\mathfrak p=(\sqrt2)\)? **Answer:** the square root of two is not an element of this number field's ring of integers; ideals can be invertible without being principal.
4. If a Dedekind ring has trivial class group, what happens to elements? **Answer:** every nonzero ideal is principal, so it is a PID and therefore a UFD.

### Arithmetic Intersection Theory: Heights and the Size of Rational Points

**Track:** Abstract Algebra. **Background:** number fields, divisors, line bundles, algebraic curves, intersection theory. **Central question:** what replaces a geometric intersection number when rational points have arithmetic size?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For primitive `(a,b) in Z^2 \ {(0,0)}` define `h([a:b])=log max(|a|,|b|)`. Then `h([12:18])=h([2:3])=log 3`. Under `[a:b] -> [a^d:b^d]`, primitiveness is preserved and `h` becomes `d h` for positive integer `d`.

The absolute logarithmic Weil height on `P^n(K)` is
\[
h([x_0:\cdots:x_n])=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb Q]}\sum_{v\in M_K} n_v\log\max_i |x_i|_v,
\]
for normalized local absolute values, local degrees `n_v`, and the product formula `sum_v n_v log|lambda|_v=0` for `lambda != 0`. The height does not depend on the choice of homogeneous coordinates or of field of definition. There are infinitely many degree-unbounded roots of unity of height zero, so Northcott finiteness requires a bound on field degree.

A metrized line bundle on an arithmetic curve has an arithmetic degree combining valuations of rational sections at finite places with negative logarithms of their metric norms at infinite places. For the standard metrized `O(1)` on `P^1_Z`, the arithmetic degree along a rational section agrees with the Weil height, subject to the stated metric convention.

The arithmetic intersection product on arithmetic surfaces also needs Green-current or metric contributions at Archimedean places. Do not conflate a literal number of intersection points with the full arithmetic pairing, and do not claim all heights on a fixed variety coincide exactly: changes of metrics and models can alter them by bounded terms under standard hypotheses.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Find `h([35:14])`. **Answer:** `log 5`, since the primitive pair is `[5:2]`.
2. What is the standard height of `[2^4:3^4]`? **Answer:** `4 log 3`.
3. Why is degree boundedness required in Northcott's theorem? **Answer:** infinitely many roots of unity have height zero but increasing algebraic degrees.
4. Which principle cancels the effect of rescaling projective coordinates across places? **Answer:** the product formula.
