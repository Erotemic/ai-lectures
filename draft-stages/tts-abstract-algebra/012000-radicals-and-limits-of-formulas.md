# Solvability by Radicals and the Limits of Algebraic Formulas

The quadratic formula looks like an elementary trick. We complete the square, take a square root, and obtain two answers. Cubic equations have their own formulas involving cube roots; quartics have more elaborate ones. But the general quintic resisted comparable formulas for centuries. There are infinitely many quintic equations one can solve exactly, so the obstruction cannot simply be that degree five is too large for arithmetic.

The meaningful question is narrower. Is there a single method using a finite sequence of additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and extraction of integer roots that expresses the roots of *every* polynomial of a given degree from its coefficients? Galois theory answers this by translating the question into a property of groups.

## Radical towers

A radical extension can be built by starting with a field K, adjoining an element whose n-th power lies in the field we already have, and repeating finitely many times. We allow the intermediate fields to grow at each step. An algebraic equation is solvable by radicals over K if all its roots lie in some such tower, possibly after adjoining suitable roots of unity in the course of the construction.

For x squared minus two, one adjoining step suffices: add a square root of two. For x cubed minus two, adjoining a cube root of two obtains a single root, but its splitting field also needs a primitive cube root of unity. That additional root can itself be constructed by radicals; for example, it belongs to the field obtained by adjoining a square root of minus three. So the cubic remains solvable by radicals even though its complete splitting field has a noncommutative symmetry group.

Notice that solvability by radicals does not mean the polynomial factors over the rationals or that a single radical gives every root. It permits an entire sequence of extensions, and the answer may pass through complex quantities even when all the desired roots are real.

## The cubic calculation

A general cubic can be transformed, by shifting the unknown, into a depressed cubic of the form z cubed plus p z plus q equals zero. Now write z as u plus v. Expanding the cube produces u cubed plus v cubed plus three u v times u plus v. If we choose three u v to equal negative p, the terms involving u plus v cancel the linear term. We then require u cubed plus v cubed to equal negative q.

The two unknown cubes have known sum negative q and known product negative p cubed divided by twenty-seven. They are therefore roots of a quadratic equation. Solving that quadratic gives u cubed and v cubed. We take cube roots, choosing them compatibly so that their product remains negative p divided by three, and add u and v.

This is the heart of Cardano's formula. It replaces one cubic equation with a quadratic equation in the cubes of the auxiliary quantities. There is a subtle branch choice: arbitrary independent cube roots of the two expressions need not satisfy the product condition, so simply writing two radical signs without qualification can produce the wrong answer. The method works because the root symmetries can be decomposed into simpler steps that correspond to radical extensions.

There are cubics with three real roots for which the standard formula passes through complex numbers. This is often called the irreducible case of Cardano's method in the historical literature, though one should distinguish that term from the technical irreducibility of a polynomial over its coefficient field. The appearance of a complex intermediate expression does not mean the final roots are nonreal. It tells us something about the limitations of a chosen expression and its branches.

## Derived groups and solvable groups

A group is solvable if it has a finite chain of normal subgroups whose successive quotient groups are abelian. One way to detect this is to repeatedly form the commutator subgroup, generated by products of the form g h g inverse h inverse. Commutators measure failure to commute: if every commutator is the identity, the group is abelian. A group is solvable when repeating this commutator operation eventually produces the trivial group.

The symmetry group S three of a cubic splitting field is not abelian, but it is solvable. Its commutator subgroup is A three, which is cyclic of order three; the commutator subgroup of that cyclic group is trivial. So noncommutativity alone is not an obstruction to radicals. What matters is whether noncommutativity can be resolved in finitely many abelian layers.

For polynomials over a field of characteristic zero, the fundamental criterion is that a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if the Galois group of its splitting field is solvable. Roots of unity and normal closures account for technical details in the passage between radical towers and the group chain. We should not mistake this as saying every radical step is itself a Galois extension with an abelian group. Instead, after adjoining sufficient roots of unity and taking suitable normal extensions, the total tower can be organized so its Galois groups have the required abelian layers.

The criterion tells us *why* the cubic formula exists. It also explains why not every polynomial of degree five has such a formula, without requiring us to search through every conceivable arrangement of radicals.

## Why the generic quintic is different

The generic polynomial of degree n, whose coefficients are algebraically independent variables over the rationals, has the full symmetric group S n as its Galois group. For n at least five, that group is not solvable. The obstruction begins with the alternating group A five, which is nonabelian and simple: it has no nontrivial proper normal subgroups. It cannot be decomposed into a chain of normal subgroups with abelian quotients. Because A five is the commutator subgroup of S five, the derived-series process does not eventually reach the identity.

This yields the Abel–Ruffini theorem: there is no radical formula for the roots of the *general* polynomial of degree five. It does not say every quintic is unsolvable. For example, x to the fifth minus two is immediately solvable using a fifth root of two and fifth roots of unity. Other quintics have smaller, solvable Galois groups. The theorem excludes a uniform radical expression valid for an arbitrary collection of coefficients, not exact solutions to every individual equation.

The sharp distinction is between individual equations and the generic family. A polynomial's degree gives an upper limit on how many roots there are, but its Galois group records how the roots can be permuted subject to all their algebraic relations. Two degree-five polynomials can therefore have radically different solvability properties.

## What counts as a solution?

The impossibility theorem is tied to a particular language of formulas. It does not prohibit numerical approximation to any desired accuracy. It does not forbid defining new special functions whose values solve equations, or using convergent series, implicit descriptions, or geometric constructions under different rules. Nor does it imply that a quintic is conceptually harder than every quartic for all purposes.

The real achievement is a change of viewpoint. We begin with operations on symbols and ask which root expressions are possible. We end with automorphism groups and their normal-subgroup structure. Theorems about groups become theorems about the limits of formulas. Galois theory does not merely explain existing quadratic and cubic tricks; it supplies a method for proving that certain kinds of tricks cannot work in general.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

**Radical tower:** \(K=K_0\subset K_1\subset\cdots\subset K_m\), with \(K_i=K_{i-1}(\alpha_i)\), \(\alpha_i^{n_i}\in K_{i-1}\). A polynomial is solvable by radicals if its splitting field embeds into a radical tower (over characteristic zero).

**Depressed cubic:** for \(z^3+pz+q=0\), set \(z=u+v\), \(uv=-p/3\). Then
\[
u^3+v^3=-q,\qquad u^3v^3=-p^3/27,
\]
so \(u^3,v^3\) solve \(w^2+q w-p^3/27=0\), giving
\[
u^3,v^3=-\frac q2\pm \sqrt{\left(\frac q2\right)^2+\left(\frac p3\right)^3}.
\]
Cube-root branches must obey \(uv=-p/3\).

**Solvability:** a finite group \(G\) is solvable iff its derived series \(G\trianglerighteq G'\trianglerighteq G''\trianglerighteq\cdots\) ends at \(1\). Over a characteristic-zero field, a polynomial is solvable by radicals iff the Galois group of its splitting field is solvable. \(S_3' = A_3 \cong C_3\), so \(S_3\) is solvable; \(S_5'=A_5\), and \(A_5\) is nonabelian simple, so \(S_5\) is not.

### Exercises with answers

1. Is \(x^5-2\) solvable by radicals? **Answer:** yes: adjoin \(\sqrt[5]2\) and the fifth roots of unity.
2. Why is the cubic Galois group \(S_3\) no obstacle to radicals? **Answer:** its derived series is \(S_3\supset A_3\supset1\).
3. What does Abel–Ruffini actually rule out? **Answer:** a radical formula for the general quintic (or higher generic polynomial), not solutions to every special quintic.
4. Does the theorem forbid numerical approximation? **Answer:** no; numerical methods use a different notion of solving.
