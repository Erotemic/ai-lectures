# Algebraic Number Fields: Discriminants and Ramification

The integers have a familiar property: every positive integer factors uniquely into primes. Earlier we learned that this statement has a productive generalization. In a Dedekind domain, elements may fail to factor uniquely, but nonzero ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals. Today we use that fact to understand an algebraic number field, a finite extension of the rational numbers.

The guiding question is surprisingly concrete. Choose an ordinary rational prime, such as two, five, or eleven. When we allow algebraic numbers, does that prime remain indivisible? Does it split into several primes? Or does a prime factor appear with multiplicity? These alternatives encode arithmetic information about the extension. They are called inertness, splitting, and ramification.

## Which integers belong to a number field?

Consider the field obtained by adjoining the square root of five to the rationals. Any element can be written as a rational number a plus a rational number b times the square root of five. But which of these elements count as integers? The correct definition is not simply to require a and b themselves to be integers. An algebraic integer is a root of some monic polynomial with integer coefficients.

The number one plus the square root of five, divided by two, is an algebraic integer: it satisfies the polynomial t squared minus t minus one. Let us call it omega. The complete ring of integers of this field consists of all a plus b omega, where a and b are ordinary integers. It is larger than the ring generated by the square root of five alone.

That distinction will matter when we calculate discriminants. A basis for the smaller ring is not necessarily an integral basis for the whole field. If we forget the difference, a prime can appear to ramify when the polynomial merely records a nonmaximal order.

## Norm and trace remember conjugates

There are two embeddings of our quadratic field into the complex numbers that fix the rationals. One sends the square root of five to itself; the other sends it to its negative. Apply both embeddings to an algebraic number. Their sum is its trace and their product its norm.

For omega, the two conjugates are omega and one minus omega. Their sum is one, and their product is negative one. These are exactly the signed coefficients of its defining quadratic polynomial. The norm of a product is the product of the norms, a fact that gives a bridge between multiplication of elements and arithmetic of ideals.

For an integral basis, we can construct a matrix of traces of pairwise basis products. Its determinant is the field discriminant. For the basis one and omega, the matrix has first row two and one, and second row one and three. Its determinant is five. So the field discriminant here is five.

This one integer carries striking information: a rational prime ramifies in a number field precisely when it divides the field discriminant. In this example the sole ramified rational prime is five. Be careful: that criterion uses the discriminant of the full ring of integers, not an arbitrary polynomial presentation.

## Splitting, inertia, and ramification

Let K be a number field, and let its ring of integers be O. An ordinary prime p defines an ideal p times O. Unique ideal factorization writes it as a product of prime ideals P one through P g, each raised to a positive exponent e. That exponent is its ramification index. For each prime ideal P, the quotient O modulo P is a finite field, containing the field with p elements. Its degree over that smaller field is called the residue degree f.

A central relation states that the sum of e times f over all primes P lying above p equals the degree of K over the rationals. Thus an extension of degree two has only a few possibilities. The prime may remain prime, with residue degree two. It may split into two different prime ideals, each with residue degree one and ramification index one. Or it may become the square of a single prime ideal, with ramification index two and residue degree one.

In our field, consider p equal to two. Reduce the polynomial for omega modulo two. It becomes t squared plus t plus one, which has no roots in the field with two elements. Thus it remains irreducible, and two stays prime in the ring of integers; it is inert.

For p equal to five, reduction gives a repeated root. Indeed the ideal generated by five is the square of the prime ideal generated by the square root of five. That is ramification: one prime ideal occurs twice. For p equal to eleven, the polynomial has distinct linear factors modulo eleven, so eleven splits into two different prime ideals.

## Why the polynomial test needs a hypothesis

This easy test by reducing a minimal polynomial is related to the Dedekind–Kummer theorem. When the ring of integers equals the ring generated by the chosen algebraic integer, or more generally when p does not divide the index of that subring in the full ring of integers, factorization modulo p correctly describes prime-ideal factorization above p.

Here omega generates the full ring, so the test works at every prime. But if we use the square root of five instead, the smaller order has index two. Its polynomial t squared minus five reduces modulo two to a repeated factor. It would misleadingly suggest ramification at two, although two is inert in the actual number field.

A discriminant computation makes the discrepancy visible. The basis one and the square root of five has discriminant twenty, whereas the integral basis one and omega has discriminant five. The ratio is the square of the index, namely four. It is not a new ramified prime of the field. It is an artifact of a deficient lattice of algebraic integers.

## Why ramification matters beyond examples

Ramification is a local failure of a prime to remain simple under extension. At unramified primes, reduction behaves cleanly, and Frobenius automorphisms help describe how primes split in Galois extensions. At ramified primes, repeated factors and nontrivial powers of prime ideals appear, and more delicate invariants such as the different arise.

Discriminants also control arithmetic estimates, arithmetic geometry, and finiteness theorems. They quantify how far the integer lattice in an extension is from behaving like independent copies of the original integers. But a discriminant is not a complete fingerprint of the field: different number fields can have the same discriminant.

The key lesson is that a rational prime does not have a single fate in a number field. It can persist, separate, or ramify. The distinction is encoded in residue degrees, ramification indices, and the integral structure of the extension. The ideal-theoretic viewpoint is what makes that arithmetic stable under changes of generators.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a number field \(K/\mathbb Q\) of degree \(n\), let \(\mathcal O_K\) be the integral closure of \(\mathbb Z\) in \(K\). For any rational prime \(p\),
\[
p\mathcal O_K=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\mathfrak P_i^{e_i},\qquad
f_i=[\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P_i:\mathbb F_p],\qquad
\sum_i e_if_i=n.
\]
For an integral basis \(b_1,\ldots,b_n\), the field discriminant is
\[D_K=\det(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(b_i b_j))\in\mathbb Z.\]
For a number field, \(p\mid D_K\) iff some prime of \(K\) over \(p\) ramifies. If \(\alpha\) is integral with \(\mathbb Z[\alpha]\subseteq\mathcal O_K\) finite index \(m\), then
\[\operatorname{disc}(\mathbb Z[\alpha])=m^2D_K.\]
The simple factorization-mod-\(p\) test is valid under the Dedekind–Kummer hypothesis \(p\nmid[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\alpha]]\).

**Worked quadratic example:** Let \(\omega=(1+\sqrt5)/2\) and \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\), so \(\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\omega]\) and \(f(t)=t^2-t-1\). Since \(\operatorname{Tr}(1)=2\), \(\operatorname{Tr}(\omega)=1\), \(\omega^2=\omega+1\), we have \(\operatorname{Tr}(\omega^2)=3\), giving \(D_K=\det\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}=5\). Modulo \(2\), \(f=t^2+t+1\) is irreducible (inert). Modulo \(5\), \(f=(t-3)^2\) (ramified); indeed \((5)=(\sqrt5)^2\) as ideals. Modulo \(11\), \(f=(t-4)(t-8)\) (split: \(4+8=12\equiv1\), \(4\cdot8=32\equiv-1\)). For \(\alpha=\sqrt5\), the suborder \(\mathbb Z[\alpha]\) has index \(2\) and discriminant \(20\).

### Exercises with answers

1. Compute the discriminant of \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt5]\) in its displayed basis. **Answer:** \(\det\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&10\end{pmatrix}=20\), which is not the field discriminant.
2. In a quadratic field, a prime has two factors with \(e_1=e_2=1\). What are their residue degrees? **Answer:** Both are one, since their weighted sum is two.
3. Why is a repeated factor of an arbitrary generator's polynomial modulo \(p\) insufficient to prove field ramification? **Answer:** \(p\) may divide the index of its generated order; the factorization theorem then does not apply.
4. What is the norm of \(\omega\)? **Answer:** \(-1\), the product of its conjugates.
