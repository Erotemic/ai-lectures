# Completion, Discrete Valuations, and Hensel's Lemma

In ordinary numerical analysis, we regard two real numbers as close when their difference is small in absolute value. In number theory, there is another useful notion of closeness. Two integers may be called close when their difference is divisible by a very large power of a prime. The numbers one and one plus a million need not be close in the ordinary metric. But if the million is replaced by a sufficiently high power of seven, they can be extremely close in the seven-adic metric.

This is not just a change of vocabulary. It produces a new complete number system, new forms of Newton iteration, and a systematic method for lifting algebraic solutions through the powers of a prime.

## Measuring divisibility rather than magnitude

Fix a prime p. Every nonzero rational number can be written uniquely as p to an integer power times a fraction whose numerator and denominator are both not divisible by p. The integer exponent is the p-adic valuation. A high valuation means a large power of p divides the number. The p-adic absolute value assigns p raised to minus that valuation. A rational with a high power of p dividing it has small p-adic absolute value, even if its ordinary numerical magnitude is huge.

The defining inequality is stronger than the familiar triangle inequality. The p-adic absolute value of a sum is at most the maximum of the absolute values of its summands. As a result, every triangle is isosceles in a certain metric sense; distances behave differently from those on the real line. A sequence is Cauchy if its differences eventually become divisible by arbitrarily large powers of p.

The rational numbers are not complete in that metric. Completing them produces the p-adic field, conventionally written Q sub p. The closure of the ordinary integers is the ring Z sub p of p-adic integers. Its elements can be represented by compatible residue classes modulo p, p squared, p cubed, and so on. Compatibility means that each residue reduces to the preceding one. This inverse-limit description makes the ring tangible without requiring us to picture a mysterious infinite decimal.

## Localizing is not completing

There are two operations worth keeping separate. Localize the integers at the prime p: allow denominators not divisible by p. This gives rational fractions whose denominators are p-adic units. It is a local ring, with a unique maximal ideal generated by p, but it is not complete. Complete that local ring in its p-adic topology and you obtain Z sub p.

The distinction matters whenever an argument repeatedly improves an approximation. Localization permits division by the appropriate denominators. Completion guarantees that a compatible sequence of increasingly accurate approximations converges to an actual element of the ring.

For a particularly transparent example, consider the geometric series one plus p plus p squared and so on. Its ordinary real series diverges when p is greater than one. But in the p-adic metric, the powers of p approach zero, so the series converges. Its sum is one divided by one minus p, an ordinary rational number whose denominator is not divisible by p. A single expression is divergent in one metric and convergent in the other.

## A polynomial root modulo seven

Consider the equation x squared equals two. Modulo seven, x equals three is a solution because nine differs from two by seven. Can we find an integer solution modulo forty-nine that reduces to three modulo seven?

Write x as three plus seven times t. Expanding the square and subtracting two gives seven plus forty-two times t plus forty-nine times t squared. For the expression to vanish modulo forty-nine, we need one plus six times t to vanish modulo seven. Choosing t equal to one works. Thus x equal to ten is a solution modulo forty-nine. Ten squared is one hundred, and one hundred differs from two by ninety-eight, which is twice forty-nine.

That is one step of Hensel lifting. A root modulo p has become a root modulo p squared. If the appropriate derivative is invertible modulo p, the process can be repeated to all higher powers of p. The compatible sequence defines an actual root in the p-adic integers.

## Why the derivative appears

The calculation resembles Newton's method. Suppose f of a is zero modulo p to the power k. Try correcting a by a multiple of p to the power k. To first order, f at the corrected point equals f of a plus the correction times f prime of a, while the remaining terms are divisible by at least p to the power two k. To cancel the residual modulo the next prime power, we must solve a linear congruence involving f prime of a.

If the derivative is not divisible by p, it has a multiplicative inverse modulo p, so there is a unique correction digit at each stage. This gives the simplest form of Hensel's lemma: a simple root modulo p of a polynomial with p-adic integer coefficients lifts uniquely to a root in Z sub p with the prescribed residue class.

The adjective simple is doing work. If the derivative vanishes modulo p, the lifting argument no longer guarantees existence or uniqueness. Some multiple roots do lift, some do not, and multiple lifts can occur. One should not silently apply the simple-root theorem to every congruence solution.

## A useful failure

Take the equation x squared equals p, and work modulo p. The residue x equals zero solves it, but the derivative two x is also zero modulo p. Over the p-adic integers there is no solution: the valuation of a square is even, while the valuation of p is one. Thus the modular solution does not lift to a p-adic root. The obstacle is arithmetic, not an artifact of a poor choice of approximation.

Here a valuation gives a stronger obstruction than the failed linear correction alone. It shows why some congruences have solutions at the first level that disappear at higher precision.

## Beyond isolated roots

Hensel's lemma is useful in local factorization, algebraic number theory, and computational algebra. A polynomial factorization modulo p can sometimes be lifted to a factorization modulo higher powers, provided suitable coprimality hypotheses hold. One can then combine local information with global arithmetic questions, while remaining careful that a solution in every local completion need not always give a solution over the rationals. Local consistency and global solvability are distinct questions.

Completion adds limits to arithmetic. Valuations measure how much divisibility a number contains. Hensel's lemma shows how a sufficiently nondegenerate local answer can be refined indefinitely. The result is a version of analysis in which divisibility, rather than Euclidean distance, determines what it means to approach the truth.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(x\in\mathbb Q^\times\), write \(x=p^{v_p(x)}u\) with \(v_p(u)=0\). Define \(|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}\) and \(|0|_p=0\). Then
\[
|x+y|_p\le \max(|x|_p,|y|_p),\qquad
\mathbb Z_p\simeq\varprojlim_n\mathbb Z/p^n\mathbb Z.
\]
The localization \(\mathbb Z_{(p)}=\{a/b\in\mathbb Q:p\nmid b\}\) is not complete; its \(p\)-adic completion is \(\mathbb Z_p\). Completing \(\mathbb Q\) gives \(\mathbb Q_p\).

**Simple-root Hensel lemma.** If \(f\in\mathbb Z_p[x]\) and \(a_0\in\mathbb Z_p\) satisfy \(f(a_0)\equiv0\pmod p\) and \(f'(a_0)\not\equiv0\pmod p\), there is a unique \(a\in\mathbb Z_p\) with \(a\equiv a_0\pmod p\) and \(f(a)=0\). A digit correction \(a_{k+1}=a_k+p^k t\) with \(t\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\) is chosen to solve
\[
f(a_k)/p^k+t f'(a_k)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]
For \(f(x)=x^2-2\), \(p=7\), \(a_1=3\), the correction satisfies \(1+6t\equiv0\pmod7\), so \(t=1\) and \(a_2=10\) mod \(49\). Indeed \(10^2-2=98\).

**Failure case.** \(x^2=p\) has solution \(x=0\) modulo \(p\), but none in \(\mathbb Z_p\), since \(v_p(x^2)=2v_p(x)\) cannot equal \(1\).

### Exercises with answers

1. Evaluate \(|49|_7\). **Answer:** \(7^{-2}\).
2. Does \(1+p+p^2+\cdots\) converge \(p\)-adically? **Yes**, to \((1-p)^{-1}\).
3. Lift the other root \(x\equiv4\pmod7\) of \(x^2-2\) to mod \(49\). **Answer:** \(x=39\): \(39^2-2=1519=31\cdot49\).
4. Why is \(f'(a_0)\not\equiv0\pmod p\) central? **Answer:** it makes the linear correction uniquely solvable modulo \(p\).
