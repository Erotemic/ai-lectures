# The Galois Correspondence: Intermediate Fields and Subgroups

Between the rationals and the field generated by square root of two and square root of three lie several smaller fields. We can see three immediately: the rationals with square root of two; the rationals with square root of three; and the rationals with square root of six. Are these all the proper intermediate fields? Trying to inspect every expression a plus b square root of two plus c square root of three plus d square root of six is not an appealing way to decide.

Galois theory offers another method. Instead of examining every number, study every symmetry, then ask which numbers each collection of symmetries leaves fixed. A correspondence between subgroups and subfields emerges. The remarkable feature is that it reverses inclusion: the more symmetries you insist on preserving, the fewer elements can remain fixed.

## Fixed fields

Let L over K be a finite Galois extension, which means a finite extension that is both normal and separable. Normal says, roughly, that irreducible polynomials over K having one root in L have all their roots in L. Separable says those polynomial roots are distinct. Write G for its Galois group.

Take any subgroup H of G. Define the fixed field of H to consist of all elements of L that every automorphism in H fixes. It really is a field: if all automorphisms fix a and b, they fix a plus b, their product, and the inverse of a when a is nonzero. The fixed field contains K, because every automorphism in the Galois group fixes K by definition.

Go in the other direction. If E is any field lying between K and L, look at all automorphisms of L that fix E pointwise. They form a subgroup of G. These two constructions send intermediate fields to subgroups and subgroups to intermediate fields.

Why does inclusion reverse? If E is enlarged, automorphisms must fix more elements, so fewer qualify. If H is enlarged, an element must survive more possible transformations, so fewer elements remain fixed. The correspondence swaps a large intermediate field for a small subgroup, and vice versa.

## The fundamental theorem

For a finite Galois extension L over K, these two assignments are inverse bijections. If H is a subgroup of G, the elements fixed by H have exactly H as their group of fixing automorphisms. If E is an intermediate field, the elements fixed by every automorphism fixing E are precisely E. Moreover, the degree of L over the fixed field of H equals the order of H, and the degree of the fixed field over K equals the index of H in G.

Why should group size equal a field degree? There is a key finite-group theorem, often called the fixed-field theorem: if a finite group H acts by distinct field automorphisms on a field L, then L has degree exactly the number of elements of H over its fixed field. We can see the main proof mechanism, rather than merely accept the count.

Let H have m elements. Suppose, for contradiction, that we have m plus one elements of L that are linearly independent over the fixed field. Introduce m plus one unknown coefficients in L. For each automorphism in H, form the homogeneous equation obtained by applying it to our chosen elements and taking their weighted sum. There are m equations and m plus one unknowns, so there is a nonzero solution. Choose a solution with as few nonzero coefficients as possible and rescale so one of them is one.

Apply any automorphism of H to every equation. Because composing with that automorphism merely permutes the equations, the transformed coefficient vector is another solution. Subtract the original solution. The chosen coefficient one disappears, so minimality forces all coefficients to remain unchanged. They are therefore fixed by every automorphism, hence belong to the fixed field. But the equation associated with the identity automorphism is now a nontrivial linear relation over the fixed field among the original m plus one elements. Contradiction. Thus the extension degree is at most m.

The reverse inequality uses a companion fact: distinct field automorphisms are linearly independent as functions when coefficients are allowed from L. If there were a shortest nontrivial linear relation between different automorphisms, we could evaluate it at an element multiplied by a carefully chosen scalar, then subtract a suitable multiple of the original relation to eliminate one term but not all others. This contradicts minimality. Once we know the extension degree is finite, more automorphisms than its degree would give a linear dependence by evaluating them on a basis. Hence m is at most the extension degree as well. The two inequalities give equality.

Applied to a subgroup of order two, this theorem says that subgroup fixes a field over which L has degree two. The numerical result has been proved from field operations, linear algebra, and the group action rather than guessed from examples.

For an intermediate E, the extension L over E is itself finite Galois. This follows because L was a splitting field of separable equations over K, and it is still a splitting field of those equations over E. Its automorphism group therefore has order equal to its degree. Those facts complete the inverse construction: a fixed field cannot be silently larger or smaller than E because their degrees agree.

The complete proof of the fixed-field theorem takes further work, but the logic of the correspondence is now visible. It is not a coincidence that group and field quantities match. The theorem gives a reason that the match must occur for every intermediate field and every subgroup.

## Listing every intermediate field in a biquadratic extension

Let L be the rationals with square root of two and square root of three adjoined. Its group G has four elements: identity, flip of square root of two, flip of square root of three, and the simultaneous flip. The group is C two times C two. It has exactly three proper nontrivial subgroups, each of order two. What does each fix?

The subgroup generated by flipping square root of two fixes square root of three, so its fixed field is Q of square root of three. The subgroup generated by flipping square root of three fixes Q of square root of two. The subgroup generated by flipping *both* radicals fixes their product, square root of six, and its fixed field is Q of square root of six. Each fixed field has degree two over Q, since the subgroup has index two in the four-element group.

No other proper intermediate field exists, because the four-element group has no other proper nontrivial subgroup. The classification of fields has become a classification of subgroups, and that classification takes only a few lines.

Notice what happens at the extremes. The trivial subgroup fixes the whole field L; it imposes no conditions. The full Galois group fixes exactly Q. Every other subgroup lies between them, so its fixed field lies in the opposite direction between Q and L.

## The cubic splitting field reveals noncommutativity

Now consider the splitting field of x cubed minus two over Q. Its Galois group is S three. This group has a normal cyclic subgroup of order three, together with three different subgroups of order two. The order-three subgroup fixes the quadratic field Q of omega, where omega is a primitive cube root of unity. Each order-two subgroup fixes a cubic subfield: for example, complex conjugation fixes the real field Q of alpha, where alpha is the real cube root of two.

The three order-two subgroups correspond to three distinct cubic subfields generated by the three different cube roots of two. They have equal degree, but need not be the same subfield inside the splitting field. This is why a group structure does more than announce the extension degree. It distinguishes how several subfields fit together.

There is also a normality test. An intermediate field E over K is a Galois extension exactly when its corresponding subgroup is normal in G. When that happens, the Galois group of E over K is isomorphic to G modulo the subgroup. In the cubic example the order-three subgroup is normal, so the quadratic field Q of omega is Galois over Q. The order-two subgroup fixing Q of alpha is not normal, reflecting the fact that Q of alpha does not contain all the roots of x cubed minus two.

## What the correspondence does not say

The hypotheses matter. A finite extension that is not normal or not separable can have too few automorphisms to detect its intermediate structure. The real cubic field Q of alpha has degree three over Q, but every rational-fixing automorphism of that real field must send alpha to a real cube root of two. Only alpha qualifies, so the automorphism group is trivial. Looking only at that group would give no information about the extension's degree.

The cure is not to ignore the field and trust an abstract group. It is to choose the right extension, check normality and separability, and understand what the automorphisms fix. Once these conditions are satisfied, a field question becomes a subgroup question. The correspondence turns the abstract symmetry introduced by roots into a precise instrument for finding every intermediate layer of arithmetic.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For finite Galois \(L/K\) with \(G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\), define
\[
L^H=\{x\in L: \sigma(x)=x\ \forall\sigma\in H\},\qquad
\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)=\{\sigma\in G: \sigma|_E=\mathrm{id}\}.
\]
The inclusion-reversing bijection is \(H\leftrightarrow L^H\), with
\[
[L:L^H]=|H|,\qquad [L^H:K]=[G:H].
\]
An intermediate \(E/K\) is Galois iff \(H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)\) is normal in \(G\), in which case \(\operatorname{Gal}(E/K)\cong G/H\).

**Biquadratic field.** For \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\), the three order-two subgroups fix \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\), \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)\), and \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)\), respectively. This list is exhaustive because \(C_2\times C_2\) has exactly three subgroups of order two.

**Cubic splitting field.** For \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2,\omega)\) with \(\omega^2+\omega+1=0\), the normal \(A_3\cong C_3\) fixes \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\). A reflection subgroup fixes a cubic subfield such as \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2)\).

### Proof details and roadmap

Choose a nonzero solution \(a\in L^{m+1}\) to \(\sum_i \sigma(x_i)a_i=0\) for every \(\sigma\in H\). Normalize a minimal-support solution so some \(a_j=1\). ### Exercises with answers

1. What subgroup corresponds to the entire extension field \(L\)? **Answer:** the identity subgroup.
2. What field corresponds to the full group \(G\)? **Answer:** the base field \(K\).
3. If \(|G|=12\) and \(|H|=3\), what are the two degrees? **Answer:** \([L:L^H]=3\) and \([L^H:K]=4\).
4. Are the three cubic subfields of the splitting field of \(x^3-2\) normal over \(\mathbb Q\)? **Answer:** no; their order-two subgroups are not normal in \(S_3\).
