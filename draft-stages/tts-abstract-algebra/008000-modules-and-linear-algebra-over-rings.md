# Modules: Linear Algebra without Division

In linear algebra, it is tempting to treat every scalar like a nonzero real number: divide by it, cancel it, or pivot on it. That works over a field. But many structures we want to study have integer scalars, polynomial scalars, or matrix scalars. An equation such as two times x equals one need not have a solution, and cancellation can fail when a scalar is a zero divisor.

A module is what remains of a vector space when the scalars belong to a ring instead of a field. It captures systems as varied as abelian groups, integer lattices, polynomial recurrences, and linear operators. But the price of this generality is real: bases, dimensions, and complements are no longer automatic.

## The definition and first examples

Take a commutative ring R with identity. An R-module M is an abelian group under addition, with a scalar multiplication by elements of R. Scalar multiplication distributes over vector addition and scalar addition, multiplication of scalars is compatible with successive actions, and the ring's one acts as the identity.

A vector space over a field is a module, but there are many others. The integers themselves form a module over the integer ring. Integer pairs form another, as do integer triples and integer matrices. Any abelian group is a module over the integers: a positive integer acts by repeated addition, zero acts by zero, and a negative integer acts using additive inverses. Conversely, the axioms of an integer module force precisely that action. So integer modules and abelian groups describe the same structures.

The cyclic group of remainders modulo six is an integer module. The scalar two acts by doubling a residue. Notice that two times the residue three is zero even though three is not zero. Over a field, a nonzero scalar cannot kill a nonzero vector; here it can. Such elements are called torsion elements. Torsion is not a technical nuisance. It records information that ordinary vector spaces cannot possess.

## Submodules and quotient modules

A submodule is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by every scalar in R. Quotients are formed exactly as in groups: two module elements become equivalent when their difference belongs to the submodule. Scalar multiplication descends to equivalence classes because multiplying a submodule element by any scalar stays inside the submodule.

In the integer module Z, the subgroup of even integers is a submodule. The quotient Z modulo two Z is the two-element cyclic group. We can now describe the short exact sequence: zero maps into Z, multiplication by two maps Z into Z, reduction modulo two maps Z onto Z modulo two Z, and then we map to zero.

The adjective exact means that at each position, the elements produced by the preceding map are exactly those killed by the following map. Multiplication by two is injective on the integers. Its image is exactly the even integers, which are the kernel of reduction modulo two. Reduction is surjective.

Why is this sequence interesting? Because it says a quotient need not be a direct summand. If there were a homomorphism from Z modulo two Z back into Z that reversed reduction, it would have to send the nonzero element of order two to an integer of order two. No such integer exists. So this sequence does not split. By contrast, every short exact sequence of vector spaces over a field splits: choose a basis of the image, extend to a basis, and construct a complement. Over a general ring we cannot simply assume a complement exists.

## The failure of automatic bases

A free module has a basis in the familiar sense: each element has a unique finite linear combination of basis elements. Integer pairs form a free module of rank two, with the two coordinate vectors as a basis. But Z modulo two Z is not a free integer module of any positive rank. Every nonzero free integer module contains elements of infinite order, while all elements of Z modulo two Z have finite order.

This gives a precise distinction between generating sets and bases. The group Z modulo two Z is generated by the class of one, but this generator satisfies the nontrivial relation two times one equals zero. In a free module, basis vectors cannot satisfy nontrivial linear relations.

Over more general rings, even submodules of free modules need not be free. Over the integers, submodules of finite-rank free modules *are* free, a special and powerful theorem connected to the structure of finitely generated abelian groups. The fact that it requires a theorem is itself a warning against importing field-based intuition without checking hypotheses.

## Homomorphisms and their kernels

An R-linear map preserves addition and scalar multiplication. The kernel is a submodule, and the image is a submodule. The first isomorphism theorem works again: M modulo the kernel is isomorphic to the image. The proof is exactly the same representative argument we used for groups and rings, now checking compatibility with scalar multiplication.

Consider multiplication by six on the integers modulo fifteen. Its kernel consists of residues x for which six x is divisible by fifteen. This occurs exactly for the multiples of five modulo fifteen: zero, five, and ten. The kernel has three elements. The image consists of residues zero, three, six, nine, and twelve, and has five elements. The quotient by the three-element kernel has five elements, matching the image.

This is a finite example of a general principle. A homomorphism organizes a module by what it loses and what it preserves. The role of exact sequences is to make such information available across several maps at once.

## Linear operators as polynomial actions

There is a less obvious module hiding inside any linear transformation. Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field F, and let T be a linear operator from V to itself. We can let the polynomial ring F of x act on V by defining the action of a polynomial p to be the operator p of T.

For example, x acting on a vector means apply T. The polynomial x squared minus three x plus two acting on a vector means apply T twice, subtract three applications of T, and add twice the original vector. The polynomial-ring identities make the module axioms hold automatically.

Now the kernel of the action map from F polynomials to operators consists of polynomials that annihilate T. In finite dimension, that kernel contains a nonzero polynomial, because the list of powers of T must eventually become linearly dependent as operators. There is then a unique monic polynomial of least degree in the kernel: the minimal polynomial of the operator. A polynomial annihilates T exactly when the minimal polynomial divides it. Compare this with an algebraic element of a field extension: a number alpha and an operator T can both be studied through the polynomial relations they satisfy.

If T is represented by a matrix, the familiar questions of invariant subspaces, diagonalization, and Jordan form become questions about submodules and decompositions of an F of x module. This module viewpoint helps explain why a minimal polynomial is a structural invariant rather than merely another coefficient list to compute.

## What survives when division disappears

Some of the most reliable ideas in linear algebra require little or no division: kernels, images, compositions, quotients, exactness, and universal mapping properties. Others—automatic bases, complements, and a single numerical dimension—require a field or additional hypotheses.

Modules make that boundary visible. They are flexible enough to describe integer congruences, algebraic extensions, and linear operators in one language, while still distinguishing between relations that can be solved by division and relations that cannot. They are also the natural setting for the exact sequences and resolutions that appear in homological algebra. Their value is not that everything becomes a vector space. It is that we learn precisely which parts of vector-space reasoning are genuinely structural.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

An \(R\)-module \(M\) is an abelian group with scalar action \(R\times M\to M\) satisfying distributive, associative, and unit axioms. A module is **free** if it is isomorphic to a direct sum of copies of \(R\).

**Non-split exact sequence over \(\mathbb Z\):**
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb Z
\xrightarrow{\bmod2}\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\longrightarrow0.
\]
No section of reduction exists: a section would inject an element of additive order two into \(\mathbb Z\).

**Map on a finite module:**
\[
\varphi:\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/15\mathbb Z,\quad [x]\mapsto[6x],
\]
\[
\ker\varphi=\{[0],[5],[10]\},\quad
\operatorname{im}\varphi=\{[0],[3],[6],[9],[12]\}.
\]

**Operator action:** for finite-dimensional \(V\), \(p(x)\cdot v:=p(T)v\) makes \(V\) into an \(F[x]\)-module. The annihilator ideal \(\{p:p(T)=0\}\) is generated by the minimal polynomial of \(T\).

### Exercises with answers

1. Why is every abelian group a \(\mathbb Z\)-module? **Answer:** integer multiplication is repeated addition and additive inversion; this is forced by the axioms.
2. Is \(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\) free as a \(\mathbb Z\)-module? **Answer:** no; it has torsion, while a nonzero free integer module is torsion-free.
3. What is the kernel of multiplication by four on \(\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z\)? **Answer:** classes \(0,3,6,9\).
4. If \(T^2=I\), what polynomial annihilates \(T\)? **Answer:** \(x^2-1\), though the minimal polynomial may be a proper divisor.
