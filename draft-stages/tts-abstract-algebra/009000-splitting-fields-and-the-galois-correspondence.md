# Splitting fields and the Galois correspondence

## Splitting Fields, Finite Fields, and Separability

Suppose we start with the rational numbers and ask for a square root of two. The field obtained by adjoining that root also contains its negative, so both roots of the equation x squared minus two equals zero are available. But adjoining one real cube root of two does not do the same thing for x cubed minus two. The other two roots are complex. A field containing just the real cube root is not yet large enough to express all the answers to its own defining equation.

This distinction motivates a splitting field. A polynomial splits over a field when it factors there as a product of linear factors, allowing repeated factors. A splitting field of a polynomial over a base field K is an extension in which the polynomial splits and which is generated over K by its roots. The last condition matters. Without it we could always attach unrelated numbers, concealing the size and structure of the extension the polynomial actually needs.

### Constructing the field one root at a time

Why should such a field exist? Suppose the polynomial has positive degree. Choose an irreducible factor over K. Form the quotient of the polynomial ring over K by the ideal generated by that factor. The quotient is a field, and the residue class of the polynomial variable is a root of the factor. We have built a field containing one root without assuming we already know a larger universe containing it.

In this new field we can divide the original polynomial by the corresponding linear factor. If factors of degree greater than one remain, repeat the construction. Each step reduces the remaining degree. After finitely many steps, the polynomial becomes a product of linear factors. Finally keep only the subfield generated by the roots. That is a splitting field.

For x cubed minus two over the rationals, call the positive real cube root alpha. Its minimal polynomial has degree three. Let omega be a primitive complex cube root of unity: omega cubed equals one, but omega is not one. The three roots are alpha, omega alpha, and omega squared alpha. Once we adjoin alpha and omega, we have all three. The field L equals the rationals with alpha and omega adjoined.

The real field generated by alpha cannot contain the nonreal omega. More precisely, omega satisfies a quadratic polynomial x squared plus x plus one, irreducible over the real subfield generated by alpha. The tower law therefore gives degree six for L over the rationals: first a degree-three extension, then a degree-two extension. Six is the size of the vector space basis over the rationals, not the number of elements in L; L is infinite.

### Distinct roots versus repeated roots

Splitting is not the same as having distinct roots. Over the real numbers, x squared splits into two linear factors, but both are x; the only root is zero, with multiplicity two. When a polynomial has no repeated roots over a suitable extension, it is called separable.

There is a computational test that avoids finding the roots. A repeated root of a polynomial f is also a root of its formal derivative f prime. To see the forward direction, write f as x minus alpha squared times another factor; differentiation leaves at least one x minus alpha factor. Conversely, if alpha is a common root of f and f prime, then after factoring out one x minus alpha from f, differentiation shows the remaining factor must vanish at alpha as well. Thus f has no repeated root in a splitting field exactly when the greatest common divisor of f and f prime is one.

In characteristic zero, an irreducible polynomial is automatically separable. Its derivative cannot be the zero polynomial, because differentiation lowers degree and a nonconstant polynomial in characteristic zero has some nonzero differentiated coefficient. An irreducible polynomial and its nonzero derivative cannot share a nonconstant factor. This is the reason field theory over the rationals can often discuss conjugate roots without constantly adding separability hypotheses.

Characteristic p behaves differently. Derivative of x to the p-th power is p times x to the p minus one, which equals zero in characteristic p. In the rational-function field over the p-element field, consider x to the p-th power minus t, where t is a transcendental parameter. This polynomial is irreducible, but its derivative is zero. Adjoin a p-th root alpha of t; then x to the p-th power minus t becomes x minus alpha, raised to the p-th power. There is one distinct root, not p distinct roots. Such an extension is inseparable.

This example is a warning: having extension degree p need not provide p ways to permute the roots. A later theory of symmetries must distinguish algebraic degree from the number of field automorphisms.

### A finite field is a root set

The same positive-characteristic arithmetic has a striking constructive side. In any field of characteristic p, the map sending a to its p-th power preserves multiplication and addition, since all intermediate binomial coefficients are divisible by p. It is called the Frobenius map.

Take any prime p and positive integer n. Inside an algebraic closure of the p-element field, look at all solutions of x raised to p to the n, minus x equals zero. This set is closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and the inverse of every nonzero element. For example, if a and b satisfy the equation, their sum does too, because raising a sum to that huge p-power is the same as separately raising its two terms. The polynomial has derivative minus one, so it has no repeated roots. It therefore has exactly p to the n distinct roots. Those roots form a field containing p to the n elements.

Any finite field has characteristic p for some prime p. Its prime subfield is the p-element field, and the entire finite field is a finite-dimensional vector space over that subfield. Thus its size must be a power of p. Conversely, our root construction produces a field of every prime-power size. Up to isomorphism, it is the only field of that size: any field with p to the n elements satisfies a raised to p to the n equals a for every element, since nonzero elements form a finite multiplicative group whose order is p to the n minus one. Consequently every element is a root of that polynomial, so the field is its splitting field.

It is tempting to call the Frobenius map the identity, but that is true only on the prime subfield and sometimes on smaller subfields. On the field with p to the n elements, the n-th iterate of Frobenius is the identity. This distinction becomes central when we study automorphism groups of finite fields.

### The point of separability

We now have three questions about an equation. Can all its roots be put into a single field? That is the splitting-field question. Are they distinct, so an equation of degree d yields d distinguishable roots? That is separability. And once all the roots have been gathered, what transformations of the field may permute them while leaving the original coefficients unchanged? That is the symmetry question.

The first two questions prepare the third. In characteristic zero, the splitting field of a polynomial without repeated irreducible factors is a finite extension with enough conjugate roots to support a rich group of symmetries. In characteristic p, the same conclusion requires care about inseparability. The field itself can be large even when the supply of symmetries is small. Keeping those two measurements separate is the beginning of Galois theory.

## Galois Groups: Symmetries of the Roots

The polynomial x squared minus two has two roots. We can exchange them without breaking their equation: send the square root of two to its negative while leaving every rational number unchanged. But a field contains more than a list of roots. It also contains their sums, products, ratios, and all the polynomial relations among them. If we propose to exchange roots, we must check that this exchange respects *every* such relation.

The correct object is a field automorphism. For an extension L over K, a K-automorphism of L is a bijection from L to itself preserving addition, multiplication, and the multiplicative identity, and fixing each element of K. These automorphisms form a group under composition. The group is written Aut of L over K. When L is a suitable splitting field, this becomes a Galois group.

### Why the image of a root is constrained

Suppose alpha satisfies a polynomial equation with coefficients in K. Apply an automorphism sigma that fixes K to that equation. Because sigma respects the field operations, the resulting equation says sigma of alpha satisfies the *same* polynomial. An automorphism therefore sends each root to another root of its minimal polynomial over K. When the extension is generated by the roots, the automorphism is completely determined by where it sends those generators.

This gives a faithful permutation action on the roots of a separable polynomial whose splitting field is L. Faithful means that if an automorphism fixes every root, it fixes all of L and is the identity. But the converse is not automatic: not every permutation of the roots extends to an automorphism. The chosen permutation must preserve all algebraic relations among the generators, not merely the one equation they visibly share.

For the square root of two, there are exactly two possibilities. If sigma fixes the rationals, it must send square root of two to either positive or negative square root of two. Each choice extends uniquely to a field automorphism, because every element has the form a plus b times square root of two. The resulting group has two elements. It records the one available flip.

### Three roots can support six symmetries

Now return to x cubed minus two. Let alpha be its positive real cube root and omega a primitive complex cube root of unity. The splitting field L is generated over the rationals by alpha and omega. Its degree is six. How many rational-fixing automorphisms does it have?

Any such automorphism must send alpha to one of the three cube roots: alpha, omega alpha, or omega squared alpha. It must send omega to a primitive cube root of unity: omega or omega squared. That suggests three times two, or six, candidate transformations. Are all six genuine? Yes. The minimal polynomial of alpha is irreducible, so sending it to another root defines a rational-field embedding of the degree-three field it generates. Once that image has been chosen, the quadratic polynomial for omega remains irreducible over the image field, which is still a real field generated by a real or nonreal cube root? Here we must be more precise: although the image of the real field need not lie in the real numbers, the extension to the full splitting field can be constructed by sending omega to either of its two roots, because x squared plus x plus one remains the minimal polynomial over the image of the degree-three subfield. The six embeddings all land in L and, because L is finite over the rationals, each is an automorphism.

There is a cleaner symmetry description. Define r by sending alpha to omega alpha and fixing omega. Applying r three times returns alpha, so r has order three. Define s to be complex conjugation: it fixes alpha and exchanges omega with omega squared, and has order two. Their actions do not commute. Conjugating r by s reverses its direction, giving the relation s r s equals r inverse.

Those are the same relations as the rotations and reflections of an equilateral triangle. The Galois group has six elements and is isomorphic to the permutation group on three objects, also called S three. This group is not merely an answer about cardinality. Its multiplication remembers how the two kinds of root symmetry interact.

The preceding extension-of-embeddings argument is a standard theorem for finite separable normal extensions. In this concrete case one can check the six formulas directly using the defining relations alpha cubed equals two, omega squared plus omega plus one equals zero, and the fact that alpha and omega generate the field. The group structure then follows without relying on a guess about permutations.

### An example where the group is not the full symmetric group

Consider L obtained by adjoining square root of two and square root of three to the rationals. Every element has a unique form a plus b square root of two plus c square root of three plus d square root of six. An automorphism fixing the rationals must send each square root independently to its positive or negative. All four sign choices extend to automorphisms, because the multiplication relations are preserved.

The group has four elements. Flipping the first radical twice is the identity, as is flipping the second twice, and the two flips commute. This is the Klein four-group, sometimes written C two times C two. It is not the cyclic group of order four: no element has order four. In contrast to the cubic example, the splitting field here has a commutative group of symmetries.

A useful test is the polynomial with roots square root of two, negative square root of two, square root of three, and negative square root of three. A random permutation among four roots will usually fail to preserve squares. For example, an automorphism cannot send both square root of two and negative square root of two to roots whose sum is nonzero. And once it sends square root of two to a choice of sign, it must send its negative to the opposite sign. So even though we see four roots, the automorphism group is a four-element subgroup of the much larger group of all permutations of four objects.

### What changes in an inseparable extension

There is one more reason not to equate degree with number of automorphisms blindly. Over the rational-function field in characteristic p, adjoin alpha with alpha to the p-th power equal to t. The extension has degree p, but x to the p-th power minus t has only one distinct root in any extension field. Every automorphism fixing the base must send alpha to another root; there is no other. Its automorphism group is trivial. Degree p, symmetry group of size one.

For a finite extension that is both normal and separable, the automorphism group has order equal to the extension degree. Splitting fields of separable polynomials meet those conditions. The cubic and biquadratic examples illustrate the equality, while the purely inseparable example explains why the hypotheses cannot simply be discarded.

### What the group is telling us

The coefficients of a polynomial define what is held fixed. Roots and combinations of roots constitute the enlarged field. Automorphisms describe exactly which changes of those new quantities leave the original arithmetic untouched. Instead of trying to calculate every root by radicals at once, we can study how the roots are related. This is what makes Galois theory powerful: it turns an equation into a group, and later turns subgroups into intermediate fields. The equation's complexity becomes a question about the possible patterns of symmetry.

## The Galois Correspondence: Intermediate Fields and Subgroups

Between the rationals and the field generated by square root of two and square root of three lie several smaller fields. We can see three immediately: the rationals with square root of two; the rationals with square root of three; and the rationals with square root of six. Are these all the proper intermediate fields? Trying to inspect every expression a plus b square root of two plus c square root of three plus d square root of six is not an appealing way to decide.

Galois theory offers another method. Instead of examining every number, study every symmetry, then ask which numbers each collection of symmetries leaves fixed. A correspondence between subgroups and subfields emerges. The remarkable feature is that it reverses inclusion: the more symmetries you insist on preserving, the fewer elements can remain fixed.

### Fixed fields

Let L over K be a finite Galois extension, which means a finite extension that is both normal and separable. Normal says, roughly, that irreducible polynomials over K having one root in L have all their roots in L. Separable says those polynomial roots are distinct. Write G for its Galois group.

Take any subgroup H of G. Define the fixed field of H to consist of all elements of L that every automorphism in H fixes. It really is a field: if all automorphisms fix a and b, they fix a plus b, their product, and the inverse of a when a is nonzero. The fixed field contains K, because every automorphism in the Galois group fixes K by definition.

Go in the other direction. If E is any field lying between K and L, look at all automorphisms of L that fix E pointwise. They form a subgroup of G. These two constructions send intermediate fields to subgroups and subgroups to intermediate fields.

Why does inclusion reverse? If E is enlarged, automorphisms must fix more elements, so fewer qualify. If H is enlarged, an element must survive more possible transformations, so fewer elements remain fixed. The correspondence swaps a large intermediate field for a small subgroup, and vice versa.

### The fundamental theorem

For a finite Galois extension L over K, these two assignments are inverse bijections. If H is a subgroup of G, the elements fixed by H have exactly H as their group of fixing automorphisms. If E is an intermediate field, the elements fixed by every automorphism fixing E are precisely E. Moreover, the degree of L over the fixed field of H equals the order of H, and the degree of the fixed field over K equals the index of H in G.

Why should group size equal a field degree? There is a key finite-group theorem, often called the fixed-field theorem: if a finite group H acts by distinct field automorphisms on a field L, then L has degree exactly the number of elements of H over its fixed field. We can see the main proof mechanism, rather than merely accept the count.

Let H have m elements. Suppose, for contradiction, that we have m plus one elements of L that are linearly independent over the fixed field. Introduce m plus one unknown coefficients in L. For each automorphism in H, form the homogeneous equation obtained by applying it to our chosen elements and taking their weighted sum. There are m equations and m plus one unknowns, so there is a nonzero solution. Choose a solution with as few nonzero coefficients as possible and rescale so one of them is one.

Apply any automorphism of H to every equation. Because composing with that automorphism merely permutes the equations, the transformed coefficient vector is another solution. Subtract the original solution. The chosen coefficient one disappears, so minimality forces all coefficients to remain unchanged. They are therefore fixed by every automorphism, hence belong to the fixed field. But the equation associated with the identity automorphism is now a nontrivial linear relation over the fixed field among the original m plus one elements. Contradiction. Thus the extension degree is at most m.

The reverse inequality uses a companion fact: distinct field automorphisms are linearly independent as functions when coefficients are allowed from L. If there were a shortest nontrivial linear relation between different automorphisms, we could evaluate it at an element multiplied by a carefully chosen scalar, then subtract a suitable multiple of the original relation to eliminate one term but not all others. This contradicts minimality. Once we know the extension degree is finite, more automorphisms than its degree would give a linear dependence by evaluating them on a basis. Hence m is at most the extension degree as well. The two inequalities give equality.

Applied to a subgroup of order two, this theorem says that subgroup fixes a field over which L has degree two. The numerical result has been proved from field operations, linear algebra, and the group action rather than guessed from examples.

For an intermediate E, the extension L over E is itself finite Galois. This follows because L was a splitting field of separable equations over K, and it is still a splitting field of those equations over E. Its automorphism group therefore has order equal to its degree. Those facts complete the inverse construction: a fixed field cannot be silently larger or smaller than E because their degrees agree.

The complete proof of the fixed-field theorem takes further work, but the logic of the correspondence is now visible. It is not a coincidence that group and field quantities match. The theorem gives a reason that the match must occur for every intermediate field and every subgroup.

### Listing every intermediate field in a biquadratic extension

Let L be the rationals with square root of two and square root of three adjoined. Its group G has four elements: identity, flip of square root of two, flip of square root of three, and the simultaneous flip. The group is C two times C two. It has exactly three proper nontrivial subgroups, each of order two. What does each fix?

The subgroup generated by flipping square root of two fixes square root of three, so its fixed field is Q of square root of three. The subgroup generated by flipping square root of three fixes Q of square root of two. The subgroup generated by flipping *both* radicals fixes their product, square root of six, and its fixed field is Q of square root of six. Each fixed field has degree two over Q, since the subgroup has index two in the four-element group.

No other proper intermediate field exists, because the four-element group has no other proper nontrivial subgroup. The classification of fields has become a classification of subgroups, and that classification takes only a few lines.

Notice what happens at the extremes. The trivial subgroup fixes the whole field L; it imposes no conditions. The full Galois group fixes exactly Q. Every other subgroup lies between them, so its fixed field lies in the opposite direction between Q and L.

### The cubic splitting field reveals noncommutativity

Now consider the splitting field of x cubed minus two over Q. Its Galois group is S three. This group has a normal cyclic subgroup of order three, together with three different subgroups of order two. The order-three subgroup fixes the quadratic field Q of omega, where omega is a primitive cube root of unity. Each order-two subgroup fixes a cubic subfield: for example, complex conjugation fixes the real field Q of alpha, where alpha is the real cube root of two.

The three order-two subgroups correspond to three distinct cubic subfields generated by the three different cube roots of two. They have equal degree, but need not be the same subfield inside the splitting field. This is why a group structure does more than announce the extension degree. It distinguishes how several subfields fit together.

There is also a normality test. An intermediate field E over K is a Galois extension exactly when its corresponding subgroup is normal in G. When that happens, the Galois group of E over K is isomorphic to G modulo the subgroup. In the cubic example the order-three subgroup is normal, so the quadratic field Q of omega is Galois over Q. The order-two subgroup fixing Q of alpha is not normal, reflecting the fact that Q of alpha does not contain all the roots of x cubed minus two.

### What the correspondence does not say

The hypotheses matter. A finite extension that is not normal or not separable can have too few automorphisms to detect its intermediate structure. The real cubic field Q of alpha has degree three over Q, but every rational-fixing automorphism of that real field must send alpha to a real cube root of two. Only alpha qualifies, so the automorphism group is trivial. Looking only at that group would give no information about the extension's degree.

The cure is not to ignore the field and trust an abstract group. It is to choose the right extension, check normality and separability, and understand what the automorphisms fix. Once these conditions are satisfied, a field question becomes a subgroup question. The correspondence turns the abstract symmetry introduced by roots into a precise instrument for finding every intermediate layer of arithmetic.

## Solvability by Radicals and the Limits of Algebraic Formulas

The quadratic formula looks like an elementary trick. We complete the square, take a square root, and obtain two answers. Cubic equations have their own formulas involving cube roots; quartics have more elaborate ones. But the general quintic resisted comparable formulas for centuries. There are infinitely many quintic equations one can solve exactly, so the obstruction cannot simply be that degree five is too large for arithmetic.

The meaningful question is narrower. Is there a single method using a finite sequence of additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and extraction of integer roots that expresses the roots of *every* polynomial of a given degree from its coefficients? Galois theory answers this by translating the question into a property of groups.

### Radical towers

A radical extension can be built by starting with a field K, adjoining an element whose n-th power lies in the field we already have, and repeating finitely many times. We allow the intermediate fields to grow at each step. An algebraic equation is solvable by radicals over K if all its roots lie in some such tower, possibly after adjoining suitable roots of unity in the course of the construction.

For x squared minus two, one adjoining step suffices: add a square root of two. For x cubed minus two, adjoining a cube root of two obtains a single root, but its splitting field also needs a primitive cube root of unity. That additional root can itself be constructed by radicals; for example, it belongs to the field obtained by adjoining a square root of minus three. So the cubic remains solvable by radicals even though its complete splitting field has a noncommutative symmetry group.

Notice that solvability by radicals does not mean the polynomial factors over the rationals or that a single radical gives every root. It permits an entire sequence of extensions, and the answer may pass through complex quantities even when all the desired roots are real.

### The cubic calculation

A general cubic can be transformed, by shifting the unknown, into a depressed cubic of the form z cubed plus p z plus q equals zero. Now write z as u plus v. Expanding the cube produces u cubed plus v cubed plus three u v times u plus v. If we choose three u v to equal negative p, the terms involving u plus v cancel the linear term. We then require u cubed plus v cubed to equal negative q.

The two unknown cubes have known sum negative q and known product negative p cubed divided by twenty-seven. They are therefore roots of a quadratic equation. Solving that quadratic gives u cubed and v cubed. We take cube roots, choosing them compatibly so that their product remains negative p divided by three, and add u and v.

This is the heart of Cardano's formula. It replaces one cubic equation with a quadratic equation in the cubes of the auxiliary quantities. There is a subtle branch choice: arbitrary independent cube roots of the two expressions need not satisfy the product condition, so simply writing two radical signs without qualification can produce the wrong answer. The method works because the root symmetries can be decomposed into simpler steps that correspond to radical extensions.

There are cubics with three real roots for which the standard formula passes through complex numbers. This is often called the irreducible case of Cardano's method in the historical literature, though one should distinguish that term from the technical irreducibility of a polynomial over its coefficient field. The appearance of a complex intermediate expression does not mean the final roots are nonreal. It tells us something about the limitations of a chosen expression and its branches.

### Derived groups and solvable groups

A group is solvable if it has a finite chain of normal subgroups whose successive quotient groups are abelian. One way to detect this is to repeatedly form the commutator subgroup, generated by products of the form g h g inverse h inverse. Commutators measure failure to commute: if every commutator is the identity, the group is abelian. A group is solvable when repeating this commutator operation eventually produces the trivial group.

The symmetry group S three of a cubic splitting field is not abelian, but it is solvable. Its commutator subgroup is A three, which is cyclic of order three; the commutator subgroup of that cyclic group is trivial. So noncommutativity alone is not an obstruction to radicals. What matters is whether noncommutativity can be resolved in finitely many abelian layers.

For polynomials over a field of characteristic zero, the fundamental criterion is that a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if the Galois group of its splitting field is solvable. Roots of unity and normal closures account for technical details in the passage between radical towers and the group chain. We should not mistake this as saying every radical step is itself a Galois extension with an abelian group. Instead, after adjoining sufficient roots of unity and taking suitable normal extensions, the total tower can be organized so its Galois groups have the required abelian layers.

The criterion tells us *why* the cubic formula exists. It also explains why not every polynomial of degree five has such a formula, without requiring us to search through every conceivable arrangement of radicals.

### Why the generic quintic is different

The generic polynomial of degree n, whose coefficients are algebraically independent variables over the rationals, has the full symmetric group S n as its Galois group. For n at least five, that group is not solvable. The obstruction begins with the alternating group A five, which is nonabelian and simple: it has no nontrivial proper normal subgroups. It cannot be decomposed into a chain of normal subgroups with abelian quotients. Because A five is the commutator subgroup of S five, the derived-series process does not eventually reach the identity.

This yields the Abel–Ruffini theorem: there is no radical formula for the roots of the *general* polynomial of degree five. It does not say every quintic is unsolvable. For example, x to the fifth minus two is immediately solvable using a fifth root of two and fifth roots of unity. Other quintics have smaller, solvable Galois groups. The theorem excludes a uniform radical expression valid for an arbitrary collection of coefficients, not exact solutions to every individual equation.

The sharp distinction is between individual equations and the generic family. A polynomial's degree gives an upper limit on how many roots there are, but its Galois group records how the roots can be permuted subject to all their algebraic relations. Two degree-five polynomials can therefore have radically different solvability properties.

### What counts as a solution?

The impossibility theorem is tied to a particular language of formulas. It does not prohibit numerical approximation to any desired accuracy. It does not forbid defining new special functions whose values solve equations, or using convergent series, implicit descriptions, or geometric constructions under different rules. Nor does it imply that a quintic is conceptually harder than every quartic for all purposes.

The real achievement is a change of viewpoint. We begin with operations on symbols and ask which root expressions are possible. We end with automorphism groups and their normal-subgroup structure. Theorems about groups become theorems about the limits of formulas. Galois theory does not merely explain existing quadratic and cubic tricks; it supplies a method for proving that certain kinds of tricks cannot work in general.

### Splitting Fields, Finite Fields, and Separability

**Track:** Abstract Algebra: Structure, Symmetry, and Arithmetic. **Background:** irreducible polynomials, quotient fields, and extension degrees. **Question:** what does it mean to adjoin *all* the roots, and when are those roots distinguishable?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(f\in K[x]\), a **splitting field** is an extension \(L/K\) such that \(f=c\prod_i(x-\alpha_i)\) in \(L[x]\), and \(L=K(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m)\). It exists and is unique up to a \(K\)-isomorphism (not a unique distinguished embedding).

**Cubic example.** With \(\alpha=\sqrt[3]{2}\) and \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}\),
\[
L=\mathbb Q(\alpha,\omega),\quad [\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=3,\quad [L:\mathbb Q(\alpha)]=2,\quad [L:\mathbb Q]=6.
\]
The second degree is two because \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\subset \mathbb R\) while \(\omega\notin\mathbb R\), and \(\omega\) satisfies \(x^2+x+1\).

**Separable criterion.** \(f\) is square-free in an algebraic closure precisely when \(\gcd(f,f')=1\). For irreducible \(f\), this means \(f'\neq0\). In characteristic \(p\), \((x^p-t)'=0\) over \(\mathbb F_p(t)\).

**Finite fields.** The roots of \(x^{p^n}-x\) in an algebraic closure of \(\mathbb F_p\) form \(\mathbb F_{p^n}\), unique up to isomorphism, with \(p^n\) elements. The derivative is \(-1\), ensuring all roots are simple. Frobenius \(F(a)=a^p\) satisfies \(F^n=\mathrm{id}\) on \(\mathbb F_{p^n}\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. What is the splitting field of \(x^2-5\) over \(\mathbb Q\)? **Answer:** \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\), of degree two.
2. Why is \(x^4-1\) separable over \(\mathbb Q\)? **Answer:** its derivative \(4x^3\) has gcd one with \(x^4-1\).
3. Does a field with six elements exist? **Answer:** no; six is not a prime power.
4. What is the derivative of \(x^p-a\) in characteristic \(p\)? **Answer:** zero; separability therefore needs separate examination.

### Galois Groups: Symmetries of the Roots

**Track:** Abstract Algebra: Structure, Symmetry, and Arithmetic. **Background:** splitting fields, field homomorphisms, and group actions. **Question:** which permutations of roots actually preserve every algebraic relation?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let \(L/K\) be a field extension. Define
\[
\operatorname{Aut}_K(L)=\{\sigma:L\to L\mid\sigma\text{ is a field automorphism and }\sigma|_K=\operatorname{id}\}.
\]
If \(L\) is the splitting field of a separable \(f\in K[x]\), then \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\operatorname{Aut}_K(L)\), and the action on the roots of \(f\) is faithful. **Not every root permutation is induced by an automorphism.**

**Cubic.** Let \(\alpha^3=2\), \(\omega^3=1\), \(\omega\ne1\). Then \(\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\alpha,\omega)/\mathbb Q)\cong S_3\). Choose \(r(\alpha)=\omega\alpha\), \(r(\omega)=\omega\) and \(s(\alpha)=\alpha\), \(s(\omega)=\omega^2\). They satisfy
\[
r^3=s^2=1,\qquad srs=r^{-1}.
\]
To construct all six field automorphisms, use normality and separability, or extend embeddings along the tower \(\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb Q(\alpha)\subset L\). The quadratic relation \(x^2+x+1\) remains irreducible over each \(\mathbb Q(\omega^j\alpha)\), since a degree-three extension cannot contain the quadratic field \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\).

**Biquadratic.** For \(E=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\), the four sign-change maps give \(\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)\cong C_2\times C_2\).

##### Exercises with answers

1. What is the Galois group of \(x^2-7\) over \(\mathbb Q\)? **Answer:** \(C_2\), generated by the sign flip of \(\sqrt7\).
2. Why is the map \(\sqrt2\mapsto\sqrt3\) not a \(\mathbb Q\)-automorphism of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\)? **Answer:** it changes the equation \(x^2=2\) into \(x^2=3\).
3. What is the automorphism group of the purely inseparable extension \(\mathbb F_p(t^{1/p})/\mathbb F_p(t)\)? **Answer:** the trivial group.

### The Galois Correspondence: Intermediate Fields and Subgroups

**Track:** Abstract Algebra: Structure, Symmetry, and Arithmetic. **Background:** finite extension degrees, Galois groups, fixed elements. **Question:** how can knowledge of a group list the fields hidden between two given fields?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For finite Galois \(L/K\) with \(G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\), define
\[
L^H=\{x\in L: \sigma(x)=x\ \forall\sigma\in H\},\qquad
\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)=\{\sigma\in G: \sigma|_E=\mathrm{id}\}.
\]
The inclusion-reversing bijection is \(H\leftrightarrow L^H\), with
\[
[L:L^H]=|H|,\qquad [L^H:K]=[G:H].
\]
An intermediate \(E/K\) is Galois iff \(H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)\) is normal in \(G\), in which case \(\operatorname{Gal}(E/K)\cong G/H\).

**Biquadratic field.** For \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)\), the three order-two subgroups fix \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\), \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)\), and \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt6)\), respectively. This list is exhaustive because \(C_2\times C_2\) has exactly three subgroups of order two.

**Cubic splitting field.** For \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2,\omega)\) with \(\omega^2+\omega+1=0\), the normal \(A_3\cong C_3\) fixes \(\mathbb Q(\omega)\). A reflection subgroup fixes a cubic subfield such as \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2)\).

##### Proof details and roadmap

For \(m=|H|\), the upper bound \([L:L^H]\le m\) follows from the minimal-support homogeneous-system argument in the narration. Let \(x_1,\dots,x_{m+1}\) be independent over \(F=L^H\). Choose a nonzero solution \(a\in L^{m+1}\) to \(\sum_i \sigma(x_i)a_i=0\) for every \(\sigma\in H\). Normalize a minimal-support solution so some \(a_j=1\). For any \(	au\in H\), the coefficients \(	au(a_i)\) also solve every equation, by reindexing \(	au^{-1}\sigma\). Minimal support forces \(a_i=	au(a_i)\), hence \(a_i\in F\), contradicting independence of the \(x_i\).

For the lower bound, distinct homomorphisms \(\sigma_i:L	o L\) are linearly independent over \(L\) as functions. In a shortest nontrivial relation \(\sum_i c_i\sigma_i(x)=0\) for all \(x\), choose \(b\) with \(\sigma_1(b)
e\sigma_2(b)\). Subtract \(\sigma_1(b)\) times the original relation from the relation evaluated at \(bx\); this removes the first term but not the second, a contradiction. Since \([L:F]\) is finite by the first part, \(m>[L:F]\) would make the \(m\) evaluation vectors of automorphisms on an \(F\)-basis linearly dependent in \(L^{[L:F]}\). Thus \(m\le[L:F]\).

1. Show \(L^H\) is a subfield and the two assignments reverse inclusion.
2. Prove the fixed-field theorem for a finite group of field automorphisms: \([L:L^H]=|H|\).
3. Show that any intermediate \(L/E\) of a finite Galois \(L/K\) is Galois and has \(|\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)|=[L:E]\).
4. Compare degrees using the tower law to obtain the inverse bijection and normal-subgroup quotient statement.

##### Exercises with answers

1. What subgroup corresponds to the entire extension field \(L\)? **Answer:** the identity subgroup.
2. What field corresponds to the full group \(G\)? **Answer:** the base field \(K\).
3. If \(|G|=12\) and \(|H|=3\), what are the two degrees? **Answer:** \([L:L^H]=3\) and \([L^H:K]=4\).
4. Are the three cubic subfields of the splitting field of \(x^3-2\) normal over \(\mathbb Q\)? **Answer:** no; their order-two subgroups are not normal in \(S_3\).

### Solvability by Radicals and the Limits of Algebraic Formulas

**Track:** Abstract Algebra: Structure, Symmetry, and Arithmetic. **Background:** Galois groups, subgroup chains, field extensions. **Question:** why do quadratics, cubics and quartics have general radical formulas, while generic quintics do not?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

**Radical tower:** \(K=K_0\subset K_1\subset\cdots\subset K_m\), with \(K_i=K_{i-1}(\alpha_i)\), \(\alpha_i^{n_i}\in K_{i-1}\). A polynomial is solvable by radicals if its splitting field embeds into a radical tower (over characteristic zero).

**Depressed cubic:** for \(z^3+pz+q=0\), set \(z=u+v\), \(uv=-p/3\). Then
\[
u^3+v^3=-q,\qquad u^3v^3=-p^3/27,
\]
so \(u^3,v^3\) solve \(w^2+q w-p^3/27=0\), giving
\[
u^3,v^3=-\frac q2\pm \sqrt{\left(\frac q2\right)^2+\left(\frac p3\right)^3}.
\]
Cube-root branches must obey \(uv=-p/3\).

**Solvability:** a finite group \(G\) is solvable iff its derived series \(G\trianglerighteq G'\trianglerighteq G''\trianglerighteq\cdots\) ends at \(1\). Over a characteristic-zero field, a polynomial is solvable by radicals iff the Galois group of its splitting field is solvable. \(S_3' = A_3 \cong C_3\), so \(S_3\) is solvable; \(S_5'=A_5\), and \(A_5\) is nonabelian simple, so \(S_5\) is not.

##### Exercises with answers

1. Is \(x^5-2\) solvable by radicals? **Answer:** yes: adjoin \(\sqrt[5]2\) and the fifth roots of unity.
2. Why is the cubic Galois group \(S_3\) no obstacle to radicals? **Answer:** its derived series is \(S_3\supset A_3\supset1\).
3. What does Abel–Ruffini actually rule out? **Answer:** a radical formula for the general quintic (or higher generic polynomial), not solutions to every special quintic.
4. Does the theorem forbid numerical approximation? **Answer:** no; numerical methods use a different notion of solving.
