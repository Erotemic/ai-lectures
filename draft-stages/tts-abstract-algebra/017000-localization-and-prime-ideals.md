# Localization and Prime Ideals: Arithmetic Near One Prime

An integer is divisible by five, or it is not. In ordinary arithmetic that distinction is one fact among many. We also care about divisibility by seven, eleven, and the other primes. But imagine investigating only the behavior associated with five. We would like to divide by seven with no hesitation, divide by eleven, and divide by every integer not divisible by five, while continuing to treat five as a nonunit.

The resulting ring exists. It consists of rational numbers that can be written as a fraction with denominator not divisible by five. It contains the integers and has a remarkable property: all its nonunits lie in one maximal ideal, the multiples of five in this new ring. This is the first example of localization, a construction that lets algebra attend to one part of a larger structure.

The distinction between localization and quotienting is important. Reducing the integers modulo five throws away all multiples of five. Localizing at five does not make five zero. It keeps five, five squared, and their divisibility relations; it merely declares all integers relatively prime to five invertible. We have changed which operations are allowed, not identified all differences divisible by five with zero.

## Construct the fractions

Take a commutative ring R with an identity, and a collection S of its elements that contains one and is closed under multiplication. Suppose first that R has no zero divisors and S contains no zero. We can form fractions whose numerators come from R and denominators come from S. Two fractions represent the same element when cross-multiplication gives the same answer. Addition and multiplication work as for ordinary fractions.

The ring obtained is called S inverse R, or the localization of R at S. Every element of S becomes a unit because one divided by s is an inverse for s divided by one. The construction is precisely suited to the decision that those elements should now be invertible.

For a general ring with zero divisors, the elementary cross-multiplication rule is not adequate. We identify a over s with b over t when some additional element of S annihilates the difference between a times t and b times s. That qualification allows us to localize even when some nonzero elements disappear: if s times a is zero and s becomes invertible, then a must become zero in the localization. The canonical map from R into its localization therefore need not be injective.

What makes the construction fundamental is not merely the fraction recipe. It is its universal property. If a ring homomorphism sends every element of S to a unit of another ring, there is exactly one map from the localization to that other ring compatible with the original map. Any subsequent calculation that requires denominators from S must pass through this construction. Localization is the most economical way to make those divisions legal.

## Work near a prime

An ideal p of R is prime if it is proper and whenever a product lies in p, at least one factor lies in p. Equivalently, R modulo p has no zero divisors. The complement of a prime ideal is multiplicatively closed: the product of two elements outside p cannot land inside p. So we can localize using precisely the elements outside p.

We write the result as R localized at p. In this ring, a fraction is invertible exactly when its numerator is outside p, provided we interpret membership using the natural prime ideal of the localization. The fractions with numerators in p form an ideal, and every nonunit is in that ideal. It is therefore the unique maximal ideal. A ring with exactly one maximal ideal is called a local ring.

Return to the integers and the prime ideal generated by five. Three divided by seven is a valid element, since seven is invertible. Ten divided by seven lies in the maximal ideal, since its numerator is divisible by five. Ten divided by seven is not zero, however. Its difference from zero is still visible in the localized arithmetic. And the residue field of this local ring, obtained by quotienting by its maximal ideal, is the field with five elements.

This gives us three related but different objects: the original integers, the integers localized at five, and the field of integers modulo five. The localized ring lies between the other two in the sense that it retains information about powers of five while allowing all other prime factors in denominators.

## Which ideals survive?

Localization transforms ideals. If I is an ideal of R, its extension to S inverse R is the collection of finite sums of fractions whose numerators lie in I. For ordinary commutative localization, these can be represented as fractions with numerator in I. If I contains an element of S, the extended ideal becomes the whole localization: we have made one of its elements invertible, so the ideal must contain one.

The surviving prime ideals have an exact description. Prime ideals of the localized ring correspond to prime ideals of R that do not meet S. Under the correspondence, one extends the original prime ideal or contracts the prime ideal upstairs. In a localization at p, the corresponding prime ideals downstairs are exactly those contained in p.

For the integers localized at five, only two prime ideals occur: the zero ideal and the ideal generated by five. The ring has one nonzero prime, and that prime is maximal. But in more complicated rings, a local ring may contain several prime ideals nested below its unique maximal ideal. Being local does not mean having only one prime ideal; it means having only one maximal ideal.

## A local test for zero

Localization also works for modules, not just rings. We may form fractions m over s, and the result is a module over the localized ring. An element m of an R-module becomes zero after localization exactly when some allowed denominator annihilates m.

This leads to a principle with a proof worth understanding: a module element is zero if it becomes zero after localization at every maximal ideal. Suppose m is not zero. Consider the set of ring elements that annihilate m. This is a proper ideal, so it is contained in some maximal ideal q. If m became zero after localizing at q, an element outside q would annihilate it. That element would belong to the annihilator and hence to q, a contradiction. Thus a nonzero module element remains visible at at least one maximal localization.

A global equality of module elements can therefore be checked locally. Similar statements about the injectivity or surjectivity of maps become local tests after we apply them to kernels or cokernels. In commutative algebra, this lets a complicated global question break into a family of smaller questions focused at primes.

## What localization does not do

It is tempting to imagine localization as zooming into a tiny neighborhood until all global structure disappears. That picture is incomplete. Localization does not automatically complete a ring in any metric; the integers localized at five are not the five-adic integers. It also does not make every difficult module free, and a property true near one prime need not be true everywhere.

The geometric interpretation is nevertheless powerful. In the geometry of polynomial equations, prime ideals act like points of a spectrum, and localization at a prime gathers algebraic functions that make sense near that point. The elements outside the prime are precisely the denominators we allow. This is why the algebra of local rings appears so naturally in algebraic geometry: the same equations can be studied both globally and through what survives when we focus on one prime.

The essential lesson is a choice of what to invert. Quotients forget distinctions; localizations make selected divisions possible. When combined, these two operations provide much of the working vocabulary of modern commutative algebra.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

All rings here are commutative with identity. Let \(S\subseteq R\) be multiplicatively closed with \(1\in S\), \(0\notin S\). General localization is formed by
\[
(a,s)\sim(b,t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exists u\in S:\ u(at-bs)=0.
\]
Write \(a/s\) for the equivalence class. The canonical map \(R\to S^{-1}R\) may have a kernel. Its universal property: for any ring map \(f:R\to B\) with \(f(S)\subseteq B^\times\), a unique \(\tilde f:S^{-1}R\to B\) satisfies \(\tilde f(a/s)=f(a)f(s)^{-1}\).

For a prime \(\mathfrak p\), the set \(S=R\setminus\mathfrak p\) is multiplicative, and \(R_{\mathfrak p}=S^{-1}R\) has unique maximal ideal \(\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p}\). More generally, \(\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)\) is naturally identified with \(\{\mathfrak q\in\operatorname{Spec}R:\mathfrak q\cap S=\varnothing\}\).

**Example.** \(\mathbb Z_{(5)}=\{a/b\in\mathbb Q:5\nmid b\}\), maximal ideal \(5\mathbb Z_{(5)}\), residue field \(\mathbb F_5\). The fraction \(10/7\) is nonzero in \(\mathbb Z_{(5)}\), but its image in \(\mathbb F_5\) is zero. The notation \(\mathbb Z_{(5)}\) must not be confused with the completion \(\mathbb Z_5\).

**Local detection lemma.** For an \(R\)-module \(M\) and \(m\in M\), if \(m/1=0\) in \(M_{\mathfrak m}\) for every maximal \(\mathfrak m\), then \(m=0\). If \(m\ne0\), the proper ideal \(\operatorname{Ann}(m)\) lies in some maximal \(\mathfrak m\); no element of \(R\setminus\mathfrak m\) annihilates \(m\).

### Exercises with answers

1. In \(\mathbb Z_{(7)}\), is \(3/14\) an element? **No:** in lowest terms its denominator is divisible by seven.
2. Is \(\mathbb Z_{(7)}\) a field? **No:** seven is a nonunit and is nonzero.
3. Which primes of \(\mathbb Z\) survive when \(S=\{2^n:n\ge0\}\)? **Answer:** \((0)\) and \((p)\) for every odd prime \(p\); the prime \((2)\) meets \(S\) and disappears.
4. Show that a localization map need not be injective. **Answer:** localize \(R=\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\) at \(S=\{1,3\}\) only after noticing that \(3^2=3\) and \(3\cdot2=0\); the class of \(2\) maps to zero. (\(0\notin S\).)
