# Recognize trauma and coordinate the handoff

## Fractures, Sprains, and Immobilization Principles

A person slips on an icy step and lands awkwardly on one arm. The wrist becomes painful and swollen. Is it a sprain, a fracture, or a dislocation? A bystander has no X-ray, and the swelling might look similar in all three conditions. The immediate question is therefore not how to make the perfect diagnosis. It is how to recognize danger, avoid making the injury worse, and get the person appropriate care.

### Different tissues, overlapping signs

A fracture is a break in bone. A sprain injures a ligament, the connective tissue linking bones around a joint. A strain affects muscle or tendon. A dislocation occurs when joint surfaces move out of their normal alignment. These injuries can coexist. The same fall may damage a ligament and fracture a nearby bone. Pain, swelling, bruising, and reduced function overlap, so a dramatic bruise or the ability to move a joint does not reliably rule out a fracture.

Look for the overall pattern: how the injury occurred, where it hurts, whether an extremity is deformed, whether the person can use it, and whether there is an open wound. Severe pain, obvious deformity, exposed bone, major bleeding, or a limb that is pale, blue, or otherwise poorly perfused requires urgent assessment. Numbness, weakness, or loss of normal movement also raises concern for nerve or vascular involvement. A suspected hip, pelvis, spine, or major long-bone injury can have hazards not visible at the skin.

The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidelines specifically identify a blue, purple, or pale fractured extremity as a reason to activate emergency response immediately. That is because circulation matters as much as the apparent shape of the bone. An injury can threaten the limb even when bleeding is not obvious outside the body.

### Stabilize what is injured, not what you imagine it should look like

For a painful suspected fracture, unnecessary movement can increase pain and damage surrounding tissue. Supporting the limb in a comfortable position can be helpful while arranging further care. An improvised splint can reduce movement and ease transport, but it should not become a reason to delay emergency help or force an extremity into a straight position.

A splint works by limiting relative movement across an injured region. That is a mechanical concept: it distributes forces away from the injury and provides external support. A rigid board alone can create pressure points or cut off circulation if tied tightly. Padding, broad attachment, and attention to the patient's comfort matter. Immobilization often includes supporting the joints adjacent to a suspected long-bone fracture when this can be done without additional pain or manipulation. The exact method depends on injury location and available training.

A visibly deformed limb should generally be supported in the position found by an ordinary first-aid provider rather than forcibly straightened. Professional management of fractures and dislocations sometimes includes realignment, but that is a separate clinical intervention with its own risks and indications. The first-aid task is protection, not reconstruction.

If an injury has broken the skin, the open wound introduces contamination risk. Covering it with a clean dressing may help protect it. A protruding bone should not be pushed back beneath the skin by an untrained helper. If there is severe bleeding, controlling that bleeding and obtaining emergency response take priority over constructing an elaborate splint. The same person may need several kinds of aid; they are ordered by urgency rather than by neat categories.

### Check the part beyond the injury

Before and after supporting or wrapping an extremity, observe the fingers or toes farther from the body than the injury. Consider color, warmth, sensation, and ability to move them within comfort. A very tight wrap or a swelling limb can compromise circulation or compress nerves. If color, sensation, pain, or circulation appears to worsen after support is applied, the arrangement needs prompt reassessment and medical help as appropriate.

A common ankle injury illustrates the point. Someone rolls an ankle while walking and can still put a little weight on it. It may be a sprain, but a fracture remains possible. Avoiding painful activity and seeking assessment when function is limited is more reliable than making a definitive diagnosis from whether the person can take a few steps. The criteria clinicians use to decide when imaging is indicated are specific clinical decision tools, not a casual test that a bystander should improvise from memory.

### Cold and compression have limited, real purposes

For a likely acute sprain or strain, cold applied through a suitable protective cloth can reduce pain and swelling. The 2024 first-aid guidelines describe limited applications, typically no longer than twenty to thirty minutes per application, and emphasize avoiding direct ice-to-skin contact that can cause cold injury. Cold has not been established as a universal accelerator of tissue healing. It is a way to manage an acute symptom while the injury is evaluated and protected.

Compression wraps may improve comfort for some ankle sprains, but they must not impair circulation. Evidence for reducing overall healing time or restoring function is limited. If the injured part is severely painful, deformed, numb, pale, or cold, simply adding a tighter compression wrap is the wrong response. Supporting a potential fracture and obtaining medical evaluation are more important than attempting to conceal swelling.

Elevation can help manage certain swelling problems but should be comfortable and should not override attention to a potentially serious injury. Nor is there a universal requirement to keep every injured joint entirely motionless for a long time. Appropriate rehabilitation and mobilization are later clinical questions, different from protecting a fresh injury during first aid.

### Communicate a useful history

A useful handoff to emergency personnel or a clinician describes the mechanism, time, changes in pain or swelling, observed deformity, open wounds, circulation or sensation changes, and what support was applied. If the person fell from height or struck their head, that information matters too. Avoid repeated testing to see whether the limb 'still works.' One careful description can be more informative than several painful attempts to reproduce symptoms.

If an injured person has significant head, neck, back, hip, or pelvic concerns, minimizing unnecessary movement and obtaining emergency care is more important than treating the situation as an isolated wrist or ankle injury. First aid always begins with the entire person: consciousness, breathing, severe bleeding, and immediate environmental hazards precede detailed assessment of a sore joint.

The guiding principle is mechanically simple and medically disciplined. An injury makes the body's usual force-carrying structures uncertain. Do not add avoidable loads. Observe circulation and function, stabilize where useful and feasible, and get help proportionate to the danger. The ability to say 'I do not know whether this is broken' is not failure. It is a reason to choose supportive actions that remain sensible across several possible diagnoses.

## Head Injuries and Concussion Recognition

A head injury can add changes in function and responsiveness to the visible mechanical injury.

A teenager falls while skating, strikes the side of the head, gets up, and says, "I'm fine." There is no obvious bleeding. A minute later the person asks the same question twice and seems slow to respond. Should the absence of blood and the ability to walk be reassuring? No. Head injuries are assessed by what may have happened to the brain, not merely by the appearance of the scalp.

A concussion is a form of traumatic brain injury that can follow a bump, blow, or jolt to the head—or a force to the body that moves the head rapidly. Symptoms can involve thinking, balance, mood, vision, sleep, headache, and nausea. Some are immediate. Others develop or become noticeable over hours or days. A person may be conscious throughout and still have a concussion. Neither a short conversation nor a single memory question reliably rules it out.

### Separate observed signs from reported symptoms

A symptom is what the injured person experiences: headache, dizziness, sensitivity to light, foggy thinking, or nausea. A sign is what someone else observes: confusion, abnormal balance, repeated questions, slowed answers, behavior change, or an unusual gaze. Children who cannot describe how they feel may show irritability, reduced feeding, or changes in normal activity. The precise pattern differs by age and individual.

First aid begins by observing the scene and checking for immediate life threats. A fall from height, bicycle crash, vehicle collision, or forceful impact may cause injuries to the neck, limbs, and elsewhere in addition to a head injury. Do not assume the most obvious bump is the only problem. If the person is unresponsive, not breathing normally, bleeding severely, or showing other immediate threats, emergency response takes priority according to current first-aid protocols.

### Recognize danger signs that cannot wait

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identifies warning signs that demand immediate emergency medical care after head injury. These include a headache that worsens and will not go away, repeated vomiting, seizures, increasing confusion or agitation, slurred speech, weakness or numbness, poor coordination, unequal pupils or double vision, and inability to wake the person or keep them awake. In a very young child, inconsolable crying or refusing to nurse or eat after an injury can also be an urgent sign.

The important pattern is not one memorized phrase but deterioration in brain function or signs of serious intracranial injury. Emergency services should be activated promptly when danger signs appear. A person with a suspected severe head or neck injury should not be moved unnecessarily unless staying in place creates immediate danger or movement is needed for lifesaving care. Avoid trying to judge internal injury severity from whether the person can stand.

Some situations warrant a lower threshold for urgent clinical assessment even without obvious deterioration. Older adults, people taking blood-thinning medication, people with a bleeding disorder, and those experiencing a significant mechanism of injury may have risks that are not visible. Local emergency triage protocols and clinician assessment guide the next steps. The absence of an external wound is not a diagnostic result.

### Do not let someone return to a risky activity the same day

If an athlete is suspected of having a concussion, the CDC advises immediate removal from sport and no same-day return. Healthcare clearance is needed before returning to activities that risk another head injury, with a graduated return-to-sport process when appropriate. Trying a few more minutes on the field to "see if they're okay" can expose an already injured brain to another impact.

That restriction does not mean a person recovering from a concussion should be isolated in a dark room for days or prevented from sleeping. Current guidance favors individualized recovery, gradual return to suitable daily activities, and follow-up with a healthcare professional. The exact timing and level of activity should be determined clinically. A first-aid lecture can explain the distinction without prescribing a personal recovery schedule.

### What useful information can a bystander preserve?

An observer can record time of impact, estimated mechanism, any loss of consciousness, what the person said or did immediately afterward, changes in behavior, and new symptoms. These observations can be more reliable than asking the injured person to reconstruct events later. If emergency services are contacted, communicate the location, how the injury occurred, the person's current responsiveness and breathing status, and any deterioration you have observed.

A person who seems confused should not be expected to drive or navigate home alone. Offer calm, clear explanations and support while appropriate help is arranged. Avoid giving food, drink, or medication when doing so could interfere with emergency assessment or the person's ability to swallow safely. If there is a suspected neck injury, minimize unnecessary movement while following dispatcher and first-aid instructions.

### The difference between recognition and diagnosis

A concussion diagnosis and assessment of serious head injury belong to healthcare professionals. Imaging may be used in selected cases to evaluate bleeding or other structural injury, but a scan is not a routine yes-or-no test for every concussion. Symptoms can overlap with other conditions, and an initial apparently normal period does not eliminate later danger.

Think back to the skating fall. Repeated questions and slowed responses are sufficient reasons to stop the activity and seek medical assessment; the next step is not to ask the teenager to demonstrate balance on the board. If the person develops repeated vomiting, progressive confusion, or other danger signs, emergency escalation is immediate. The crucial skills are noticing changes, preventing additional injury, communicating the evidence, and choosing timely care. Recognizing uncertainty is itself an essential part of good first aid.

## Internal Bleeding and a Change in Responsiveness

Visible injury is not the only source of concern; internal bleeding makes observation and handoff especially important.

A person falls hard against the edge of a staircase. There is no visible blood. They sit up, say they are fine, and ask everyone to stop fussing. Several minutes later, they become pale and unusually quiet. The absence of an open wound does not rule out a serious injury. Blood can leave the circulation and collect inside the body, and the danger may not announce itself through dramatic external bleeding.

First aid cannot locate or measure internal bleeding the way emergency medical care can. The responder's job is to recognize a concerning mechanism of injury, notice deterioration, summon appropriate help and support the person while waiting. That approach is particularly important when symptoms are delayed or initially subtle.

### Start with how the injury occurred

A substantial impact, fall, collision, crushing injury or penetrating injury can damage blood vessels or organs beneath intact skin. Some people may also be more vulnerable to bleeding because of medicines or underlying conditions. A person can be seriously injured without obvious bruising at first. The history and the changes in their behavior are therefore evidence, even when the skin looks normal.

Concerning signs after trauma include increasing abdominal tenderness or swelling, a rigid or painful region, unexplained weakness, dizziness, faintness, bruising over the abdomen or chest, and blood in vomit or urine. Coughing blood is also concerning. These signs are not specific to one diagnosis; they are reasons to seek urgent medical assessment. Severe pain, breathing difficulty, marked weakness, a new inability to stay alert or a rapidly changing condition calls for emergency response.

Some internal bleeding is unrelated to an immediately witnessed fall. Vomiting blood, passing black tarry stools or developing sudden unexplained collapse can indicate serious illness and needs urgent evaluation. First aid is not the setting for deciding which organ is responsible or whether symptoms are 'probably just stomach upset.'

### Responsiveness is a changing vital clue

When you approach, first check that the area is safe and introduce yourself if the person is awake. Ask simple questions: what happened, where does it hurt, and can they respond normally? A person who was speaking clearly but now answers slowly, seems confused or cannot stay awake has changed. That change can be more informative than a single observation of their skin color.

Check breathing and look for life-threatening external bleeding while arranging emergency assistance. The American Red Cross approach is to check, call and care. If someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally or is only gasping, follow current CPR/AED guidance immediately. If the person is breathing but not fully awake, maintain attention to their airway and follow trained first-aid positioning guidance, taking suspected spine injury into account. Do not force an injured person to stand up or walk simply to prove they can.

A responder may hear the word shock and imagine only an emotional reaction. In first aid, shock can describe inadequate delivery of oxygen-rich blood to tissues, which can develop with serious blood loss and other conditions. Signs may include pale, gray, cool or clammy skin, a rapid weak pulse if assessed appropriately, fast breathing, intense thirst, weakness and altered responsiveness. No single sign proves shock. The cluster, the circumstances and the trend matter.

### Care while help is coming

Call emergency services promptly when internal bleeding or a serious traumatic injury is suspected. Give the location, what happened, the person's approximate age if known, present responsiveness, breathing, visible injuries and how the condition is changing. If there are multiple responders, assign one to call and retrieve emergency equipment while another remains with the person.

Keep the person as comfortable and still as circumstances allow. Protect them from getting cold or overheated. In general, the Red Cross recommends that a person with signs of shock lie flat when appropriate, but breathing comfort, injury and airway risks can require a different position. If head, neck or spinal injury is suspected, avoid unnecessary movement unless needed for immediate safety, breathing or life-saving care. The purpose is not to follow a posture ritual; it is to maintain breathing and circulation while limiting further harm.

Do not give the person food or drink when serious internal injury is suspected, even if they are thirsty. Avoid giving medicines as an improvised response to pain or presumed bleeding. Continue checking breathing and responsiveness. If vomiting occurs or the airway is threatened, follow trained guidance for maintaining a clear airway while accounting for possible injury. The situation can change while help is en route.

If an object remains embedded in a wound, do not pull it out. Removing it can worsen bleeding. Give care around it according to first-aid training and emergency-service instruction. The absence of an external wound, however, remains the defining difficulty in this internal-bleeding case: there may be nothing visible to press on, and attempting to treat unseen bleeding directly is not an option.

### Tell the story in sequence

Emergency responders need more than the conclusion 'they seem worse.' They need the timeline. At about three o'clock the person fell and struck the railing. They stood and spoke normally. After five minutes they reported abdominal pain. After ten minutes they became dizzy and answered slowly. The exact timestamps may be approximate, but the sequence can be clinically useful.

Report known medication information if the person or family can provide it, particularly medicines affecting bleeding, but do not delay an emergency call to collect a perfect list. Mention known medical conditions and allergies if available. If the person loses responsiveness, communicate that change clearly rather than assuming the dispatcher knows.

The most dangerous error is to let reassurance from the first minute override evidence from the next ten. A person's willingness to dismiss an injury is not a physiological test. First aid responds to mechanisms, observations and trends, then hands the case to people with the equipment and training to diagnose what cannot be seen.

Internal bleeding shows why watching and communicating are active forms of care. The responder may not be able to stop the process directly, but recognizing deterioration, maintaining a safe environment and obtaining timely emergency care can be decisive.

## Knocked-Out Teeth and Dental Injuries: First Response and Handoff

A child falls on a playground and a front tooth comes out completely. Someone picks the tooth up from the ground. Should they put it back? Put it in water? Wait for a dentist to call? The answers depend first on a distinction that can be difficult to make at a stressful moment: whether the lost tooth was a permanent adult tooth or a primary, or baby, tooth.

A knocked-out tooth is called an avulsed tooth. When a permanent tooth leaves its socket, its root surface carries living periodontal ligament cells that help attach the tooth to surrounding tissues. These cells begin deteriorating when the root dries. Speed and handling therefore matter. A primary tooth is different: replanting it can risk injury to the developing permanent tooth and is not recommended. Both injuries deserve dental evaluation, but their immediate handling is not interchangeable.

### The first assessment is broader than the tooth

A fall capable of knocking out a tooth can also injure the head, jaw, lips and neck. Begin by checking responsiveness, breathing, bleeding and other obvious injury. If the person is unresponsive, has abnormal breathing, serious head-injury warning signs, heavy uncontrolled bleeding or another immediately life-threatening condition, activate emergency medical help. A tooth is never more urgent than airway or major trauma.

For an alert person with an isolated dental injury, the most useful early information includes the person's age, which tooth was lost, the time of injury, whether the tooth was found, and whether there is a possible jaw or facial fracture. Teeth that are displaced, fractured or loose but not fully out require different assessment. Do not forcibly manipulate a tooth when there may be a major facial injury or when the person cannot cooperate safely.

### Permanent tooth: protect the root and minimize dry time

When a permanent tooth is knocked out, find it and handle it by the crown, the white part ordinarily visible in the mouth. Avoid touching or scrubbing the root. If there is visible debris, current American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidance recommends a brief rinse lasting less than ten seconds while taking care not to damage attached tissues. Immediate replantation into the original socket, when feasible and safe for the conscious person, gives the tooth its best chance of survival, followed by urgent dental care.

This does not mean forcing a tooth into an injured mouth. An unconscious person, a person who cannot protect their airway, a tooth whose identity is uncertain, or complex facial trauma calls for emergency professional help rather than an improvised replantation attempt. When immediate replantation is not possible, focus on keeping the tooth from drying out and reaching dental care promptly.

The choice of storage medium matters. The 2024 AHA and Red Cross guidelines identify special tooth-preservation solutions, such as Hank's Balanced Salt Solution, and certain other suitable media, including oral rehydration salt solutions, preprepared rice water or wrapping in cling film to limit dehydration, as beneficial options. If these are unavailable, cow's milk or the person's saliva may be considered. Ordinary tap water should not be used to store an avulsed permanent tooth because it can damage the cells on the root. The person's mouth may create a choking hazard as a storage place, especially for a child; a separate container is preferable when using saliva.

The exact transport choice should not cause prolonged delay. A readily available appropriate medium and rapid professional evaluation are more useful than a search for a theoretically ideal medium while the root sits dry. Bring the tooth to the dentist even when it could not be replanted at the scene.

### Primary tooth: a different pathway

An avulsed baby tooth should not be put back into its socket. Primary roots occupy space near developing permanent teeth. Replantation can cause additional trauma or interfere with the permanent successor. The tooth may also be accidentally aspirated during an attempt, creating a more immediate danger. A dentist should assess the injury, soft tissues and expected eruption of the permanent tooth, but the scene response is not to press the baby tooth back in.

For a young child, caregivers may not know whether an incisor is permanent. Approximate age and eruption history provide clues, but they are not infallible. If uncertain, seek immediate dental or medical guidance and avoid forcing replantation without that clarification. Preserve the found tooth for identification and evaluation. Explain what happened clearly to the clinician, including the possibility that a missing tooth or fragment was swallowed, inhaled or embedded in a lip.

### Other dental injuries and what a handoff should include

A cracked tooth can have a visible chip, a deeper fracture or a fracture extending toward the root. A tooth that has moved inward or sideways but remains in the gum is a displacement injury, not an avulsion. Pain, bleeding, bite changes and sensitivity may be clues, but appearance alone cannot establish the depth of injury. Prompt dental evaluation is important, and the urgency increases with displacement, bleeding, severe pain, inability to close the mouth normally or associated facial trauma.

The handoff should include the injury time, whether the tooth was ever dry, how it was handled and rinsed, storage medium and duration, whether replantation was attempted, and any other injuries. The dental team may also need information about contamination and vaccination history, as well as imaging or follow-up to monitor healing and the developing teeth in a child.

Imagine two children who each lose a front tooth in a fall. The first is five, and the tooth was a primary incisor. The response is urgent evaluation without replanting the tooth. The second is twelve, and the tooth was permanent. Here preserving root-cell viability and minimizing time out of the mouth can change the long-term outcome. The scene may look similar, but one diagnostic distinction changes the first-aid decision.

Dental first aid is a good example of a broader principle: identify the structure that is injured before choosing the intervention. The aim is to avoid making a reversible injury worse, preserve the chance of recovery when possible, and get the person to appropriate care with a clear timeline and well-handled evidence.

##### Clinical distinction

- A concussion is a traumatic brain injury with possible cognitive, physical, emotional and sleep symptoms, sometimes delayed; it need not involve loss of consciousness.
- Danger signs after head injury (CDC, 2025): worsening unremitting headache, repeated vomiting, seizures, weakness/numbness, slurred speech, new worsening confusion, unequal pupils/double vision, excessive drowsiness or inability to wake. Activate emergency medical response.

##### Scenario

Two people fall. One has a bleeding scalp cut but is alert and responds normally; the other has intact skin but keeps repeating a question and seems disoriented. Explain why surface bleeding cannot rank internal head-injury risk, what to communicate, and which changes trigger emergency escalation.

### Internal Bleeding and a Change in Responsiveness

### 019000 — Internal Bleeding and a Change in Responsiveness

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Scenario worksheet.** Record mechanism and time of injury; mental-status trend; breathing pattern; pain, tenderness or swelling; visible bleeding; vomiting or blood in urine if reported; known medications; call time and handoff observations. No field observations rule out internal injury.

**Escalation.** Suspected life-threatening internal bleeding or new confusion/unresponsiveness warrants emergency medical response. Follow American Red Cross CHECK–CALL–CARE. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, follow current CPR/AED training; if breathing with reduced responsiveness, prioritize airway observation and appropriate positioning while avoiding unnecessary spinal movement.

**Boundaries.** No food or drink when serious internal injury is suspected. Do not attempt invasive diagnosis, reposition fractures or remove embedded objects. Positioning advice depends on breathing, comfort, injury pattern and current first-aid training. These are educational descriptions, not a substitute for certification or emergency dispatcher instructions.

### Knocked-Out Teeth and Dental Injuries: First Response and Handoff

### 021000 — Knocked-Out Teeth and Dental Injuries: First Response and Handoff

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**2024 AHA/Red Cross dental avulsion guidance:** Permanent tooth: handle by crown, brief gentle rinse if visibly dirty (<10 s), immediate replantation when feasible and safe; immediate dental/medical evaluation. If unable to replant, limit drying using specified media. Prefer HBSS, ORS, preprepared rice water or cling film where available; milk or patient saliva second-line. **Do not store in tap water.** Primary teeth **must not be replanted.** Comprehension of the exact guideline hierarchy is more useful than a blanket statement 'put the tooth in milk'.

**Handoff card:** Time; permanent versus primary or uncertain; tooth location; brief rinse/handling; dry time; transport medium; replantation status; head/neck/face injury; bite changes; medication/allergy history if known; dentist contact. Assess major trauma, responsiveness and breathing before dental decisions.

**Clinical sources:** Brumberg et al., *2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid*, section 9.9 Dental Avulsion; International Association of Dental Traumatology (2020), guidelines for permanent-tooth avulsion and injuries in the primary dentition. The 2024 first-aid guidance is the primary basis for lay-response storage order; IADT provides dental specialty context. Clinical editorial review remains required.
