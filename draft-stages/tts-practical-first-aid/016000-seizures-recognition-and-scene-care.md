# 016000 — Seizures: Recognition and Scene Care

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A person at the dinner table suddenly stops responding. A few seconds later they slump to the floor and begin jerking. People may panic, try to hold their arms still, or look for something to put between their teeth. Those impulses are understandable, but they can cause harm. First aid for a seizure is primarily about preventing injury, keeping track of time, recognizing when emergency help is needed, and supporting the person during recovery.

## Recognize that seizures have several forms

A seizure is a disturbance of electrical activity in the brain. It does not always look like the dramatic full-body shaking shown in films. Some episodes involve staring, brief lapses of awareness, repeated automatic movements, unusual sensations, or sudden confusion. Others involve loss of awareness, collapse, and rhythmic muscle movements. The observer's task is not to diagnose the neurological type. It is to recognize a change in awareness or movement, protect the person from the environment, and describe what actually happened.

If someone begins convulsing, note the start time as soon as you can. You do not need perfect timing to be useful, but duration is one of the most important facts for deciding whether to activate emergency services. Many seizures stop after a short interval. A prolonged seizure, or one that repeats without the person recovering between episodes, is a serious medical emergency. The established first-aid guidance uses approximately five minutes as a critical threshold for ongoing seizure activity; do not wait beyond it to seek help.

## Make the space safer

Move nearby hard, sharp, or hot objects out of the way. If the person is falling, help ease them to the ground when it is safe to do so. Place something soft and flat beneath the head if available. Remove eyeglasses and loosen restrictive clothing around the neck. Stay nearby and ask bystanders to give the person space. If practical, gently position them on their side, especially when they are not awake and aware, to help keep the airway clear. Do not force a position against violent movements or place yourself in danger.

Do not restrain their limbs or attempt to stop the seizure physically. The muscles are not moving because the person chooses to struggle, and holding them down can injure joints, muscles, or the person trying to help. Do not put a spoon, finger, wallet, or any other object into the mouth. A person cannot swallow their own tongue. Inserting an object can damage teeth or obstruct the airway. Do not offer water, food, or oral medication during a seizure or while awareness remains reduced.

Breathing may look irregular during a convulsive episode. Do not attempt mouth-to-mouth breathing during active convulsions. As soon as the seizure ends, reassess responsiveness and normal breathing. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally after seizure activity has stopped, follow emergency-dispatch instructions and begin the appropriate CPR and AED response. The distinction between activity still in progress and the condition after it stops matters.

## Know when emergency activation is indicated

Call emergency services for a first known seizure, an ongoing seizure that reaches about five minutes, repeated seizures without recovery between them, breathing trouble or choking, injury, a seizure in water, or failure to return to usual awareness. The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidance also identifies seizures in pregnancy and in infants younger than six months among the situations warranting emergency medical services. For a known seizure disorder, an established individualized seizure action plan can provide additional instructions, but the presence of the plan does not negate emergency criteria.

If the event occurs near traffic, water, stairs, or machinery, the environment may create immediate danger even for a shorter seizure. Move hazards and secure the area first. For a person with a prescribed rescue medication, follow their documented action plan only if you have the relevant training and authority. Do not improvise drug dosing based on seeing a medicine in a bag.

An important detail is that 'seizure ended' does not necessarily mean 'person has recovered.' They may be sleepy, confused, frightened, or sore. The period after the seizure can involve reduced awareness. Stay with the person, explain calmly what happened as they become responsive, and help them regain a safe position. Do not rush them to stand or leave them alone simply because the shaking stopped. If they do not recover as expected, that itself is a reason for emergency assessment.

## Make a useful observation record

When it is safe, note what happened before the event. Did the person suddenly stop speaking? Were there repeated movements on one side? Did they fall? Did the whole body shake? Approximately how long did the activity last? Did another event begin before recovery? Was there an injury or difficulty breathing? These details help clinicians distinguish types of events. A careful written description is more useful than assigning a diagnosis such as 'grand mal' after a brief glance.

People who experience seizures may feel exposed or embarrassed afterward. Keep unnecessary onlookers away, respect privacy, and speak directly and calmly when they can understand. Explain the sequence without alarm or accusation. A person who has a known condition may have a plan for contacting family or a care team; ask what they need once they are alert, while following emergency guidance when indicated.

Seizure first aid is a striking example of effective restraint by the helper: we do less physically, but we pay closer attention. Stay, make the surroundings safe, time the event, support the airway by suitable positioning, and recognize the thresholds for emergency care. Those are learnable actions. They matter more than attempting to control movements that cannot be safely controlled by force.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Response mnemonic:** STAY (remain with person and time) → SAFE (clear hazards, cushion head) → SIDE (gently position on side when unaware if safe to do so) → REASSESS (normal breathing, responsiveness, injury). Do not restrain; do not put anything in mouth; no food, drink or oral medicine until fully alert.

**Activate emergency services:** first-time seizure, about five minutes of ongoing seizure, repeated episodes without return to baseline, trouble breathing/choking, injury, water exposure, pregnancy, infant younger than six months, or failure to regain usual responsiveness. Follow dispatcher instructions. If not breathing normally after convulsive activity ends, respond according to CPR/AED training.

**Observation note:** start/end time, type of movements, awareness, injuries, breathing, repeat episodes, recovery time, prescribed action plan when known. Respect privacy.

**Review status:** 2024 AHA/American Red Cross first-aid guideline and CDC seizure first-aid guidance inform the clinical thresholds. This educational manuscript does not replace current local training, emergency-dispatch instructions, or individualized seizure action plans.
