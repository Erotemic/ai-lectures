# The First Minutes of Cardiac Arrest

Someone collapses in a public hallway. You approach and speak loudly. They do not respond. Their breathing seems absent, except for an occasional strange gasp. People gather, but nobody has yet called emergency services. In this situation, the skill that matters is recognizing that a possible cardiac arrest is happening and beginning an organized response. Waiting for certainty wastes time that circulation cannot spare.

Cardiac arrest means the heart is not producing an effective circulation of blood. It is not synonymous with a heart attack, in which blood flow to part of the heart muscle becomes blocked. A heart attack can cause cardiac arrest, but the immediate problem during arrest is failure to deliver oxygenated blood to the brain and other organs. The first-aid response must address that failure before a bystander knows its exact cause.

## Recognition and the first call

Check that the scene is safe enough to help. Try to get a response by speaking and tapping the person's shoulders. Check for normal breathing briefly. Gasping or occasional agonal breaths are not normal breathing. For an unresponsive adult who is not breathing normally, a lay rescuer should assume cardiac arrest and act.

Activate the emergency response system immediately. In the United States that generally means calling nine one one. If alone, call first and then begin compressions immediately; a phone's speaker mode lets a dispatcher guide actions while you help. If someone else is present, give specific assignments: 'You, call nine one one. You, bring the AED.' The aim is to prevent an entire group from assuming somebody else has acted.

You do not need to diagnose a heart rhythm before starting. A lay rescuer should not spend precious time hunting for a pulse. The combination of unresponsiveness and absent or abnormal breathing is the trigger for this emergency sequence.

## What compressions achieve

Chest compressions press on the chest and alter pressure in the thorax, helping drive some blood through the circulation. They do not restore normal cardiac function by themselves, and they cannot fully replace the heart's work. They buy time by maintaining some circulation while an automated external defibrillator and professional responders become available.

Place the person on their back on a firm, flat surface when that can be done safely and promptly. Kneel beside the torso. For an average adult, use two hands centered on the chest with the heel of one hand under the other, your shoulders approximately over your hands and elbows straight. Compress at a rate of one hundred to one hundred twenty per minute, to a depth of at least five centimeters, about two inches, while avoiding excessive depths greater than about six centimeters. Allow the chest to fully recoil between compressions and minimize interruptions.

The numbers serve a physical purpose. Compressions that are too shallow may move inadequate blood. A rate that is too fast can reduce filling and effective depth; a rate that is too slow provides too few compressions. Leaning on the chest between compressions reduces recoil. These details are difficult to judge without practice on a manikin that can provide feedback, which is why hands-on training improves the quality of the skill.

## Compressions and breaths

For a bystander who is untrained or unwilling or unable to provide rescue breaths, hands-only CPR for an adult is preferable to doing nothing. Continue chest compressions and use an AED as soon as possible. Trained rescuers who can provide breaths use a cycle of thirty compressions followed by two rescue breaths in standard adult CPR. Breaths should be sufficient to cause visible chest rise without excessive ventilation, and pauses should be brief.

Rescue breathing can be especially consequential when arrest has followed an oxygen problem, but different circumstances and responder training affect the detailed response. Adult CPR guidance does not automatically transfer unchanged to infants and children, drowning, or other special situations. The core adult sequence remains recognition, emergency activation, compressions, and prompt AED use.

## An AED is designed to help a bystander

An automated external defibrillator, or AED, analyzes electrical activity associated with the heart and determines whether a shock is advised. Certain dangerous rhythms, such as ventricular fibrillation, may respond to defibrillation. Other causes of cardiac arrest are not treated by a shock. This is why an AED can correctly announce 'no shock advised' even though the person still needs CPR and emergency care.

When an AED arrives, turn it on and follow its voice and visual prompts. Expose the chest as required and apply the pads in the positions shown on the device. Common adult placement puts one pad high on the right chest and the other lower on the left side; the device's diagrams govern the exact placement and any alternatives. The AED may require plugging in a pad connector. During rhythm analysis, make sure nobody is touching the person. If it advises a shock, confirm everyone is clear and deliver it as the device directs. Some devices deliver a shock automatically; others require pressing a button.

After a shock, or if the device advises no shock, resume CPR promptly according to its prompts. Do not wait to see whether the person suddenly wakes up. Minimize the time when the chest is not being compressed, apart from the necessary pauses for analysis, shocks, or other recommended actions. The AED's prompts help organize this sequence under stress.

## Practicing the response without a real emergency

A good training session is more than memorizing a list. Practice approaching a simulated collapse, checking responsiveness and breathing, activating help, speaking clear assignments, placing hands, giving compressions with feedback, and using an AED trainer. Practice what happens when the trainer says 'shock advised' and when it says 'no shock advised.' Learn how to switch compressors without long interruptions if several people can help.

In real life, emergency dispatchers can provide instructions. Continue following them and the AED until professional responders take over, the person shows signs of normal breathing or movement requiring reassessment, or you cannot safely continue. Actual courses also cover special circumstances, such as water, implanted devices, and the different needs of children.

Go back to the hallway. One person calls, another brings the AED, and one begins compressions. Nobody must solve the mystery of why the collapse happened before acting. The first minutes are governed by physiology: restore some blood flow, bring definitive emergency help, and use a defibrillator when its analysis indicates a shockable rhythm. That is the practical meaning of organized first aid.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Adult lay-rescuer sequence and evidence

Under the **2025 American Heart Association Adult Basic Life Support guidelines** and American Red Cross public guidance:

1. Confirm reasonable scene safety; check responsiveness and normal breathing briefly. Gasping is not normal breathing.
2. Activate emergency response (in the U.S., 911) **before** starting CPR when alone; obtain an AED or direct someone to retrieve it.
3. Start adult chest compressions `100–120/min`, `5–6 cm` approximate target range (at least `5 cm`, avoid `>6 cm`) with full recoil and minimal interruptions.
4. Hands-only CPR is appropriate for an untrained bystander; trained providers can use `30:2` adult compressions:breaths with brief pauses and visible chest rise.
5. Turn on the AED, follow its pad diagrams and instructions; no contact during analysis/shock; resume CPR after advised shock or no-shock decision.

The exact response may differ with patient age, pregnancy, drowning, suspected overdose, other circumstances, and responder training. AED voice prompts and emergency dispatcher directions take priority for the particular device and event. Real practice should be conducted with a CPR manikin and AED trainer, not on an uninjured person.
