# 017000 — Diabetes Emergencies: Recognition and First Response

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

During a family gathering, someone who has diabetes becomes shaky, sweaty, and confused. They have trouble finishing a sentence. Another person suggests giving them insulin. A third reaches for a glass of juice. What should a first-aid provider actually do? The immediate task is to recognize a potentially serious change in condition, check whether the person can safely swallow, and get appropriate help. The difference between low blood sugar and dangerous high-blood-sugar illness matters enormously, but a bystander must not try to solve that uncertainty by improvising medication.

## Why glucose problems affect the whole body

Glucose is a major fuel used by the body, and the brain is particularly sensitive to inadequate supply. Insulin helps the body's tissues use and store glucose. Other hormones, including glucagon, help raise circulating glucose when needed. When blood glucose falls too low, a person may become hungry, shaky, sweaty, pale, dizzy, anxious, or unusually irritable. As it worsens, they may appear confused, slur words, have a seizure, or lose consciousness.

High blood glucose may develop more gradually. Thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and dehydration can occur. In diabetic ketoacidosis, inadequate effective insulin leads the body to produce excess ketones, creating a dangerous acid-base disturbance. Warning signs can include nausea or vomiting, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, fruity-smelling breath, extreme fatigue, and confusion. This is a medical emergency requiring professional treatment. Similar symptoms do not establish the diagnosis; other acute illnesses, including stroke, may produce confusion or altered behavior.

The observer should notice the person, not just the label 'diabetes.' Ask what changed, whether the person can respond normally, whether they have a glucose monitor or established care plan, and whether anyone knows their usual behavior. A continuous glucose monitor or home meter can provide useful context if it is available and the person or their designated helper can use it appropriately. But treatment of a clearly ill person must not be delayed by a search for equipment or a perfect number.

## Low glucose: an immediate action with a firm condition

The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidance recommends oral glucose for suspected hypoglycemia when the person is **awake and able to swallow**. Glucose tablets are a preferred option when available; ordinary fast-acting dietary sugars can be used when they are not. The practical aim is to supply glucose promptly while the person can swallow safely, then observe for improvement according to the individual's plan and the guidance being followed.

The critical condition is awareness and safe swallowing. A confused person who can still clearly follow instructions and swallow may differ from someone who is barely responsive, choking, or unable to protect their airway. Never pour a drink or place food in the mouth of a person who is unresponsive, having a seizure, or cannot swallow. Oral sugar in that situation can enter the airway and cause harm.

Activate emergency medical services if a person with suspected low glucose cannot swallow, has a seizure, or does not improve within about ten minutes after oral glucose, as specified by the 2024 first-aid recommendations. Call sooner for worsening symptoms or other signs of emergency. In the United States, call 911 and follow the dispatcher. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, follow the standard emergency response for breathing assessment, CPR, and AED as directed by training and dispatch.

A person with a prescribed glucagon rescue product may have an individualized emergency plan. Family members or trained caregivers may be taught how to administer it. The form and instructions vary: injectable products and nasal products are not interchangeable in their use. Follow the actual prescribed product instructions and training; obtaining professional emergency care remains important after a severe episode. A first-aid lecture cannot substitute for a specific person's plan.

## High glucose: do not improvise insulin

A person with diabetes who is very thirsty and nauseated after several days of illness may have high glucose, dehydration, ketones, or something else. A bystander cannot safely choose an insulin dose from these symptoms. Insulin is a powerful medication with potentially serious consequences if given incorrectly. Professional care and the person's documented management plan govern medication decisions.

If there is vomiting, inability to keep down fluids, deep or difficult breathing, marked confusion, or other signs suggesting diabetic ketoacidosis, activate emergency care. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identifies diabetic ketoacidosis as a medical emergency. Someone who is extremely ill should not be left alone to 'sleep it off.' If they are alert and can swallow, their prescribed sick-day instructions may include glucose and ketone checks and fluids, but severe symptoms require urgent medical assessment rather than trial-and-error home treatment.

Not all dangerous high-glucose emergencies involve the same ketone pattern. Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state and other illnesses can also cause serious dehydration and altered mental status. It is unnecessary for a first-aid provider to distinguish these mechanisms at the scene before requesting help.

## Support the person while help is coming

Approach calmly, introduce yourself, and obtain consent when possible. Keep the person seated or otherwise safely positioned, away from stairs, vehicles, or hot equipment. Ask about medical-alert identification and their emergency plan without delaying emergency activation. Record when symptoms began, what the person ate or took if known, any available glucose reading and timestamp, what was administered, and whether the condition changed. Do not invent a medical history from an alert bracelet; use it as a clue to share with responders.

If the person is recovering after oral glucose, the episode still deserves appropriate follow-up under their care plan. They may need longer-acting food, monitoring, or medical contact depending on the cause and whether symptoms recur. A brief improvement is not proof the underlying risk has ended, particularly when medication action may continue. Keep the person supported until they are reliably alert and an appropriate next step is arranged.

The decisive lesson is about recognizing limits. A bystander can observe, offer oral glucose to someone awake and able to swallow when low glucose is suspected, recognize severe illness, call for help, and communicate precise observations. They should not force food into an impaired person's mouth or invent an insulin treatment. These boundaries make first aid effective precisely because they are specific.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Hypoglycemia response:** suspected low glucose + awake and safely able to swallow → encourage oral glucose; tablets preferred if available, simple dietary sugars an alternative. Call EMS for inability to swallow, seizure, worsening symptoms, or failure to improve within roughly 10 minutes of glucose per 2024 AHA/American Red Cross. No oral intake when unable to swallow or not awake.

**Severe hyperglycemia/DKA clues:** vomiting, inability to retain fluids, deep/rapid breathing, fruity breath, confusion, severe fatigue, dehydration; requires emergency assessment. Do not administer insulin based on bystander assumptions or use extra glucose indiscriminately when high-glucose illness is suspected.

**Glucagon:** Some people have prescribed rescue medication and an action plan; product-specific instructions and caregiver training apply. Emergency medical care is still necessary for a severe episode. Do not assume one device's directions fit another.

**Handoff:** time of first symptoms, usual baseline if known, glucose reading and device/time, food/glucose administered, rescue medication if administered, vomiting/seizure/breathing changes, medical alerts, contact/plan. Follow the dispatcher and updated local first-aid training.

**Clinical status:** Grounded in AHA/Red Cross 2024 first-aid hypoglycemia recommendations and CDC diabetes emergency guidance. Independent clinical review pending; not a substitute for individualized medical instructions.
