# 006000 — Stroke, Heart Attack, and Severe Allergic Reactions

# Three emergencies, three different mechanisms

A person suddenly cannot lift one arm. Another has crushing discomfort in the chest and breaks into a cold sweat. A third develops throat swelling and difficulty breathing after eating a food to which they are severely allergic. All three situations can require immediate emergency medical care, yet they do not have the same cause or first-aid response. A useful responder recognizes the pattern, calls for professional help promptly, and takes only actions appropriate to the suspected emergency.

In the United States, call nine-one-one for suspected stroke, heart attack, or anaphylaxis. Elsewhere, use the local emergency number. Do not postpone that call to complete a home examination or to see whether symptoms settle. Emergency dispatchers can guide immediate steps and arrange appropriate transport. A bystander does not need to establish a definitive medical diagnosis before requesting help.

## Stroke: a sudden loss of function

A stroke occurs when blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted or a blood vessel bleeds into or around the brain. The specific treatment depends on which event has occurred, so it is important not to assume that a remedy for a blood clot is appropriate for every suspected stroke. The first-aid task is rapid recognition and activation, not selecting a hospital medication.

Think of the letters B, E, F, A, S, T. Balance: sudden loss of coordination or unexplained difficulty walking. Eyes: sudden vision changes. Face: one side droops or feels weak. Arms: one arm drifts or cannot be raised normally. Speech: words are slurred, confused, or difficult to produce. Time: call emergency services immediately. These are recognition prompts, not a complete list. Sudden severe headache, especially with other neurological symptoms, can also be important.

The onset time matters. If you know when the person was last well, tell the dispatcher and medical team. If you woke to discover symptoms, distinguish the time you discovered them from the last time you were known to be normal. This information can affect treatment decisions. Do not delay the call to search for a perfect timestamp.

A transient ischemic attack may produce stroke-like symptoms that go away. Resolution does not make the event safe to ignore. The person still needs urgent medical evaluation, because transient symptoms can signal a serious vascular problem. Do not offer aspirin as a generic response to suspected stroke: a bystander cannot determine whether the event involves bleeding. Do not give food or drink to someone who may have impaired swallowing.

## Heart attack: blood supply to heart muscle

A heart attack involves insufficient blood flow to part of the heart muscle, usually because of a coronary artery blockage. The person may experience pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain in the chest. Discomfort can spread to an arm, back, neck, jaw, or stomach. Shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, unusual fatigue, or lightheadedness may appear, sometimes without the classic intense chest pain. Symptoms can start gradually, disappear and return, or be difficult to describe.

The first action is to activate emergency services and follow dispatch guidance. Have the person stop exertion and remain in a position that makes breathing easier. Note symptoms and timing, and be ready to report known medications or allergies. Do not have the person drive themselves to the hospital. Emergency medical personnel can assess and treat problems during transport.

Guidelines provide a narrow role for aspirin in some alert adults with nontraumatic chest pain while waiting for emergency help, with important exceptions for allergy or prior medical advice and a provision to defer when uncertain. This is not a universal rule for every collapse or chest complaint. The safest educational principle is to involve the dispatcher and follow current local protocols rather than experimenting with medicines. Medication decisions for suspected stroke are different from those for possible cardiac causes.

Heart attack and cardiac arrest must also be distinguished. During a heart attack, a person may remain awake and breathing. Cardiac arrest means the heart is no longer producing effective circulation; the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally. In that situation, activate emergency services, begin CPR and use an available AED according to training and dispatcher instructions. Do not wait for a chest-pain story before recognizing arrest.

## Anaphylaxis: a systemic allergic emergency

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing, potentially life-threatening allergic reaction. It can involve breathing difficulty, swelling of the lips or tongue, a sensation of throat closing, hives or flushing, severe gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness, or collapse. Not every person has a visible rash. The combination of symptoms and a possible trigger may be informative, but absence of an obvious food or insect exposure does not rule out anaphylaxis.

Epinephrine is the primary immediate treatment for anaphylaxis. A person at risk may carry a prescribed epinephrine autoinjector. The 2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross first-aid guidelines recommend that the person use the available autoinjector, with a first-aid provider assisting if needed, and that emergency services be activated. Follow the instructions for that specific device and the person's emergency plan. An antihistamine is not an adequate substitute for epinephrine when anaphylaxis is occurring.

Symptoms may improve after an injection and then require further medical assessment or treatment. Do not interpret a temporary improvement as a reason to cancel emergency assistance. If breathing becomes absent or abnormal and the person is unresponsive, recognize possible cardiac arrest and follow the CPR and AED pathway. If the person is alert, avoid unnecessary walking or standing; positioning should account for breathing difficulty, injury, vomiting, and dispatcher directions.

## A responder's shared framework

The shared steps across these emergencies are simple but consequential. Recognize that something serious may be happening. Call for help early. Describe observable findings rather than claiming certainty about a diagnosis. Record when symptoms began, what changed, any relevant exposures or medicines, and the person's response to assistance. Monitor consciousness and breathing, and be ready to change course if the person's condition deteriorates.

You can learn pattern recognition without pretending that every symptom is specific. Shortness of breath can occur in heart attack, anaphylaxis, lung disease, or anxiety. Dizziness alone has many causes. The point is not to match every symptom to one disease; it is to identify combinations and sudden changes that require immediate evaluation.

Fast action is valuable because these conditions can worsen quickly, and effective treatments are often time-dependent. The bystander's role is to shorten the interval to professional care and provide appropriate immediate assistance, not to solve a complicated diagnosis on the living-room floor.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Recognition matrix

| Suspicion | Key clues | Immediate action | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stroke | Sudden focal weakness, speech, vision, balance change | EMS, record onset/last known well | Do not assume clot versus bleeding |
| Heart attack | Chest pressure or equivalent symptoms, dyspnea, sweating | EMS; rest; follow dispatcher/clinical advice | Arrest requires CPR/AED if unresponsive with absent/abnormal breathing |
| Anaphylaxis | Rapid respiratory, swelling, skin, GI or circulation symptoms | EMS; assist prescribed epinephrine autoinjector per guidance | Antihistamine does not replace epinephrine |

### Evidence and clinical review

- The American Heart Association and American Red Cross issued the 2024 first-aid guideline used here; distinguish it from resuscitation-specific 2025 BLS updates.
- Both FAST and B.E. F.A.S.T. are recognition aids. A negative screening mnemonic is not proof of no stroke.
- Aspirin recommendations in 2024 guidance concern a subset of **alert adults with nontraumatic chest pain**; they do not apply to suspected stroke or unresponsive persons.
- This transcript is a recognition lecture, not a certification or replacement for hands-on CPR and autoinjector training.
