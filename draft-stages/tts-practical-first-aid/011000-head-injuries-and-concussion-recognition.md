# 011000 — Head Injuries and Concussion Recognition

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A teenager falls while skating, strikes the side of the head, gets up, and says, "I'm fine." There is no obvious bleeding. A minute later the person asks the same question twice and seems slow to respond. Should the absence of blood and the ability to walk be reassuring? No. Head injuries are assessed by what may have happened to the brain, not merely by the appearance of the scalp.

A concussion is a form of traumatic brain injury that can follow a bump, blow, or jolt to the head—or a force to the body that moves the head rapidly. Symptoms can involve thinking, balance, mood, vision, sleep, headache, and nausea. Some are immediate. Others develop or become noticeable over hours or days. A person may be conscious throughout and still have a concussion. Neither a short conversation nor a single memory question reliably rules it out.

## Separate observed signs from reported symptoms

A symptom is what the injured person experiences: headache, dizziness, sensitivity to light, foggy thinking, or nausea. A sign is what someone else observes: confusion, abnormal balance, repeated questions, slowed answers, behavior change, or an unusual gaze. Children who cannot describe how they feel may show irritability, reduced feeding, or changes in normal activity. The precise pattern differs by age and individual.

First aid begins by observing the scene and checking for immediate life threats. A fall from height, bicycle crash, vehicle collision, or forceful impact may cause injuries to the neck, limbs, and elsewhere in addition to a head injury. Do not assume the most obvious bump is the only problem. If the person is unresponsive, not breathing normally, bleeding severely, or showing other immediate threats, emergency response takes priority according to current first-aid protocols.

## Recognize danger signs that cannot wait

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention identifies warning signs that demand immediate emergency medical care after head injury. These include a headache that worsens and will not go away, repeated vomiting, seizures, increasing confusion or agitation, slurred speech, weakness or numbness, poor coordination, unequal pupils or double vision, and inability to wake the person or keep them awake. In a very young child, inconsolable crying or refusing to nurse or eat after an injury can also be an urgent sign.

The important pattern is not one memorized phrase but deterioration in brain function or signs of serious intracranial injury. Emergency services should be activated promptly when danger signs appear. A person with a suspected severe head or neck injury should not be moved unnecessarily unless staying in place creates immediate danger or movement is needed for lifesaving care. Avoid trying to judge internal injury severity from whether the person can stand.

Some situations warrant a lower threshold for urgent clinical assessment even without obvious deterioration. Older adults, people taking blood-thinning medication, people with a bleeding disorder, and those experiencing a significant mechanism of injury may have risks that are not visible. Local emergency triage protocols and clinician assessment guide the next steps. The absence of an external wound is not a diagnostic result.

## Do not let someone return to a risky activity the same day

If an athlete is suspected of having a concussion, the CDC advises immediate removal from sport and no same-day return. Healthcare clearance is needed before returning to activities that risk another head injury, with a graduated return-to-sport process when appropriate. Trying a few more minutes on the field to "see if they're okay" can expose an already injured brain to another impact.

That restriction does not mean a person recovering from a concussion should be isolated in a dark room for days or prevented from sleeping. Current guidance favors individualized recovery, gradual return to suitable daily activities, and follow-up with a healthcare professional. The exact timing and level of activity should be determined clinically. A first-aid lecture can explain the distinction without prescribing a personal recovery schedule.

## What useful information can a bystander preserve?

An observer can record time of impact, estimated mechanism, any loss of consciousness, what the person said or did immediately afterward, changes in behavior, and new symptoms. These observations can be more reliable than asking the injured person to reconstruct events later. If emergency services are contacted, communicate the location, how the injury occurred, the person's current responsiveness and breathing status, and any deterioration you have observed.

A person who seems confused should not be expected to drive or navigate home alone. Offer calm, clear explanations and support while appropriate help is arranged. Avoid giving food, drink, or medication when doing so could interfere with emergency assessment or the person's ability to swallow safely. If there is a suspected neck injury, minimize unnecessary movement while following dispatcher and first-aid instructions.

## The difference between recognition and diagnosis

A concussion diagnosis and assessment of serious head injury belong to healthcare professionals. Imaging may be used in selected cases to evaluate bleeding or other structural injury, but a scan is not a routine yes-or-no test for every concussion. Symptoms can overlap with other conditions, and an initial apparently normal period does not eliminate later danger.

Think back to the skating fall. Repeated questions and slowed responses are sufficient reasons to stop the activity and seek medical assessment; the next step is not to ask the teenager to demonstrate balance on the board. If the person develops repeated vomiting, progressive confusion, or other danger signs, emergency escalation is immediate. The crucial skills are noticing changes, preventing additional injury, communicating the evidence, and choosing timely care. Recognizing uncertainty is itself an essential part of good first aid.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Clinical distinction

- A concussion is a traumatic brain injury with possible cognitive, physical, emotional and sleep symptoms, sometimes delayed; it need not involve loss of consciousness.
- Danger signs after head injury (CDC, 2025): worsening unremitting headache, repeated vomiting, seizures, weakness/numbness, slurred speech, new worsening confusion, unequal pupils/double vision, excessive drowsiness or inability to wake. Activate emergency medical response.
- Suspected concussion during sports: stop participation, no same-day return; return to sport under healthcare-provider guidance, not a bystander test.
- Risk modifiers such as older age and anticoagulant use require careful medical assessment; exact decision criteria and transport recommendations belong to relevant clinical protocols.

### Scenario

Two people fall. One has a bleeding scalp cut but is alert and responds normally; the other has intact skin but keeps repeating a question and seems disoriented. Explain why surface bleeding cannot rank internal head-injury risk, what to communicate, and which changes trigger emergency escalation.
