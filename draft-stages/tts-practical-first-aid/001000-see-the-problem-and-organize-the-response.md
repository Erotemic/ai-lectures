# See the Problem, Call for Help, and Organize the First Minutes

A person suddenly collapses at a family gathering. Several people rush forward. One calls their name. Someone else runs for water. A third looks for a phone. For a moment everyone is trying to help, but nobody knows which action matters first. Emergency response is not only a collection of medical techniques. It is also a problem of attention, communication, and priorities.

The first minutes reward a small number of disciplined habits: notice what happened, recognize conditions that threaten life, summon appropriate help, and provide time-critical care using current training and available equipment. An organized response need not be theatrical. It needs to be clear enough that several people can cooperate under stress.

## Begin with the scene

Before approaching a person, notice conditions that could injure you or others. Traffic, exposed electrical equipment, smoke, structural hazards, violent activity, moving machinery and hazardous substances can turn one casualty into two. Scene safety is a real assessment, not a formula spoken after someone has already stepped into danger. If the area is not safe to enter, call emergency services and report the hazard instead of creating another emergency.

When the scene is approachable, try to establish responsiveness. Speak to the person and, when appropriate, use a gentle physical stimulus consistent with current training. Look for normal breathing. An unconscious person who is not breathing normally, or is only gasping, may be in cardiac arrest. Gasping is not normal breathing. This is a situation for immediate emergency activation and CPR/AED response, not a wait-and-see diagnosis.

The Red Cross teaches a structured check, call, care approach. Its detailed instructions depend on the person's age, condition and rescuer training. A brief audio introduction can explain the logic but cannot replace a hands-on course, where positioning, compression quality, rescue breaths and device use can be practiced and assessed.

## A call is a medical intervention in its own right

Calling emergency services brings people, equipment, and a coordinated system of care toward the scene. In the United States, nine-one-one is the usual emergency number. Provide the exact location, what happened, whether the person is responsive and breathing normally, any immediate hazards, and any other information the dispatcher requests. Stay on the line and follow dispatcher instructions when possible.

If several people are present, assign tasks explicitly. "You, in the blue shirt, call nine-one-one and report back." "You, bring the AED from the hallway." An undirected shout of "Somebody call for help" can fail because everyone assumes someone else is doing it. Specific delegation reduces that ambiguity. If you are alone, follow current dispatcher and training guidance for your situation rather than relying on an improvised rule.

Keep track of time when you can do so without delaying care. When did the person collapse? When was an AED attached? When did symptoms begin? In stroke recognition, the last time a person was known well can be especially important. Observations that seem small in the moment may help responders make decisions later.

## Recognize what cannot wait

First aid begins with recognizing immediate threats to breathing and circulation. An unresponsive person who is not breathing normally requires an urgent cardiac-arrest response. Life-threatening bleeding requires prompt bleeding control. An obstructed airway may need rapid age-appropriate first-aid actions. Serious allergic reactions, stroke symptoms, and signs of a heart attack warrant emergency activation. The exact interventions differ; these conditions should not be compressed into one generic protocol.

For an adult in suspected cardiac arrest, current Red Cross guidance includes calling emergency services, beginning chest compressions, and using an automated external defibrillator as soon as one is available. For a trained rescuer providing conventional CPR, the Red Cross describes cycles of compressions and rescue breaths, while hands-only CPR is an approach taught for some adult situations. Children and infants have important differences, so adult instructions should not be applied to them without age-appropriate guidance.

An AED is designed to analyze cardiac rhythm and advise or deliver a shock when appropriate. It is not a device that shocks every unconscious person. Its spoken prompts and visual instructions guide the user, including ensuring nobody touches the person while it analyzes or delivers a shock. In a real event, using an available AED promptly as directed is more important than understanding every detail of the rhythm algorithm.

## Bleeding has its own mechanism

The amount and speed of blood loss matter. Blood loss reduces the volume available to circulate oxygen. Direct firm pressure applied to a bleeding wound is a fundamental response for many external bleeding injuries. Severe limb bleeding may require a commercially made tourniquet applied according to current training; wounds in locations where a tourniquet cannot be placed may need other bleeding-control methods. These distinctions are why Stop the Bleed training and reputable first-aid courses teach specific techniques rather than a single slogan.

When possible, use an appropriate barrier such as gloves to reduce exposure to blood. But do not spend critical time searching for an ideal kit while a life-threatening condition goes untreated. Good preparation makes needed items accessible before the emergency, rather than forcing a person to improvise while another person is deteriorating.

## Not every collapse is cardiac arrest

A person may be unresponsive yet breathing normally. Causes can include fainting, intoxication, seizure, metabolic problems, injury and many other conditions. Do not treat all unconsciousness as one disease. Call for emergency care as indicated, monitor breathing and responsiveness, and follow current training on positioning and the possibility of injury. Do not give an unconscious person food or drink.

A seizure may involve movements, altered awareness or a recovery period. A person who fell could have a head or neck injury. Someone who appears confused may have low blood sugar, stroke, poisoning or another urgent condition. First aid cannot be a remote diagnosis. Its role is to keep the person safer, notice deterioration and pass useful observations to professional responders.

Likewise, a person who is awake but struggling to breathe is not necessarily "less serious" than someone who fainted. Their speech, work of breathing, skin color, possible exposures and speed of change matter. Give a dispatcher the observations rather than trying to impress them with a speculative diagnosis.

## Prepare the space before anyone needs help

Knowing where the first-aid kit and AED are located is useful only if they can be accessed. An organized household or workplace can check whether equipment is present, current and appropriately maintained, whether contacts and emergency location information are easy to find, and whether people have current training. A home with children has age-specific needs. A workshop has hazards that a living room does not. Preparedness is a relationship between likely events, people, communication and supplies.

Consider rehearsing a simple scenario verbally. Someone collapses. Who checks the scene? Who calls? Who brings the AED? Where is the entry door and address information for responders? Who will meet them? This exercise does not require acting out medical procedures without instruction. It teaches coordination, which is itself a practical skill.

## The discipline of the first minutes

An emergency can make every action feel equally urgent. They are not. A lost minute finding the perfect bandage for a trivial scrape is different from a lost minute before emergency activation in cardiac arrest. The right habit is to recognize which processes are time-sensitive, delegate clearly, use equipment as directed and remain willing to update the plan when new information appears.

We do not need to turn bystanders into physicians. We need people who can notice immediate danger, say what they see, activate help, and use well-practiced first-aid skills. Real resilience in an emergency is not certainty about the diagnosis. It is competent action while important facts are still unknown.

## Technical companion: source scope and training

This lecture is an orientation, not a substitute for certified First Aid/CPR/AED and bleeding-control instruction. Procedures vary by patient age and circumstance, and authoritative protocols are updated. The American Red Cross pages for **First Aid Steps**, **CPR Steps**, **Hands-Only CPR**, and **AED Steps** should be checked against current training materials before any protocol-specific revision. Local dispatchers may direct immediate care during an emergency.

**Case exercise:** In a two-person scenario with a collapsed adult who is not breathing normally, describe how to communicate location, symptoms and assignments without delaying CPR/AED intervention. In a separate scenario where a person is unconscious but breathing normally, explain why the response is not identical. Do not rehearse compressions on a person who is not in cardiac arrest; use suitable training equipment.
