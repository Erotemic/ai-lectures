# 018000 — Dehydration, Shock, and Signs of Deterioration

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A person finishing a long walk feels dizzy, sits down, and asks for water. Another person becomes pale and confused after an injury. Both may look weak, but the situations are not interchangeable. One may have a relatively straightforward fluid deficit; the other may be experiencing shock, a failure to deliver adequate blood flow to vital tissues. Shock is an emergency that can have causes far beyond dehydration. First aid begins by recognizing the difference in urgency, checking responsiveness and breathing, and calling for help before the person deteriorates further.

## Fluid loss is not the whole story

The body maintains water and dissolved electrolytes across blood, cells, and other compartments. Fluid is lost through sweat, breathing, urine, vomiting, diarrhea, and other routes. In ordinary exertional dehydration, the person may be thirsty, tired, lightheaded, or have reduced urine output. These observations are useful but not diagnostic on their own. Similar symptoms can occur with heat illness, low blood sugar, bleeding, infection, medications, or heart problems.

Shock is a different physiological concept. It means tissue perfusion is inadequate for the body's needs. Major bleeding can reduce circulating blood volume. A severe allergic reaction can alter blood vessel behavior and airway function. Heart failure or a dangerous rhythm can limit forward blood flow. A severe infection can disrupt circulation. These causes require different definitive treatments. The first aider's job is not to make a final medical diagnosis from skin color; it is to recognize the warning signs and organize an appropriate response.

Possible signs of deterioration include new confusion, fainting, profound weakness, unusual sleepiness, cool or mottled skin, difficulty breathing, chest pain, repeated vomiting, or pulses that seem weak or unusually rapid. Some people show different patterns because of age, medication, temperature, or underlying conditions. A person can have serious internal bleeding without visible blood. A person can also become unwell without describing thirst.

## Use the first-aid sequence

Begin with scene safety. Look for traffic, power, extreme temperature, and other hazards. Approach, introduce yourself, and assess responsiveness and breathing. Activate emergency medical services promptly when the person is confused, breathing abnormally, severely injured, has suspected shock, or is otherwise critically ill. Follow dispatcher instructions. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, follow current CPR and AED guidance appropriate to the responder's training.

While help is coming, address an obvious life-threatening cause such as severe external bleeding using established first-aid methods. Avoid unnecessary movement, especially when injury is suspected. Positioning should account for breathing, consciousness, trauma, vomiting, and comfort. For an alert person with suspected shock, lying flat may be reasonable if tolerated. If the person is vomiting or at risk of losing airway protection, positioning may need to favor airway safety. Do not rigidly elevate legs in every case; pain, trauma, breathing difficulty, and other circumstances can make a different position safer. The American Heart Association and American Red Cross guidance is conditional for precisely this reason.

Help the person avoid additional heat or cold stress without delaying emergency response. Monitor how responsiveness, breathing, and skin findings change. Record relevant information: the time symptoms began, observed fluid losses, medications if known, injury mechanism, and any important changes. A concise, factual handoff to responders is more useful than an unsupported diagnosis.

## Oral rehydration has conditions

For an awake, oriented person with exertional dehydration who can swallow safely and has no signs of shock, confusion, or other emergency, offering potable water or a suitable rehydration drink can be appropriate. The 2024 AHA/American Red Cross first-aid guideline supports oral rehydration under these conditions. Carbohydrate-electrolyte drinks may be useful in some exertional settings, but the availability of an ideal product should not delay access to safe drinking water when oral rehydration is appropriate.

A person who is confused, repeatedly vomiting, struggling to breathe, or unable to swallow safely should not simply be urged to drink. Fluids given by mouth can be aspirated or delay necessary treatment. Severe dehydration from prolonged diarrhea or vomiting, especially in a young child or older person, deserves medical evaluation. Water is not a substitute for addressing bleeding, heatstroke, anaphylaxis, or a cardiac problem.

The first aider must also recognize that one measurement does not establish safety. A pulse oximeter or blood pressure reading may be informative in context, but readings can be inaccurate, and apparently reassuring values do not override obvious clinical deterioration. A person who is becoming less responsive is worse even if a single displayed number looks unchanged.

## Compare two stories

In the first story, an adult finishes strenuous exercise on a hot day. They are alert, breathing normally, have no chest pain or neurologic symptoms, and feel better after resting in a cooler place and taking oral fluid. They still need observation, and worsening symptoms change the response. But the initial pattern is consistent with an ordinary exertional fluid deficit.

In the second story, someone stumbles after an injury, grows pale, cannot clearly explain what happened, and becomes drowsy. The absence of visible bleeding does not make this a minor dehydration episode. Activate emergency help, monitor breathing and responsiveness, address immediate hazards, and do not try to walk the person off the problem or force oral fluids.

The goal is to notice a trajectory rather than argue about a label. Record what you see and how quickly it changes. When the signs point to compromised circulation or consciousness, first aid is a bridge to emergency care. Recognition, safe positioning, immediate help, and clear handoff are its most important contributions.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Decision distinction.** Alert with uncomplicated exertional dehydration, normal swallowing and no shock signs: oral rehydration can be appropriate. Suspected shock, confusion, abnormal breathing, significant bleeding, altered consciousness, persistent vomiting, or severe pain: activate emergency response and prioritize airway and cause-directed first aid.

**Handoff record.** Time of onset, activity/environment, fluid losses, injury, responsiveness, breathing and changes, oral intake if any, medications or allergies if volunteered, and actions performed.

**Positioning limits.** AHA/Red Cross 2024 guidance supports supine positioning for alert suspected shock when tolerated, recovery positioning for airway risk under relevant conditions, and conditional leg elevation only without injury/contraindications. Do not delay calling EMS to find an 'ideal' position.

**Source attribution.** 2024 AHA and American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid, especially oral rehydration for exertional dehydration and first-aid positioning of the person in shock; American Red Cross dehydration guidance. Clinical editorial review pending; actual response should follow local emergency dispatch and training.
