# 005000 — Airway Problems and Age Differences

**Track:** First Aid and Emergency Response. **Background:** emergency recognition, CPR/AED concepts, and bleeding control. **Purpose:** recognize airway emergencies and understand why age changes first-aid response.

# When a person cannot move air

Breathing has two requirements: air must reach the lungs, and the body must be able to exchange gases and circulate oxygen to tissues. A person may be conscious yet unable to move air through an obstructed upper airway. Another person may have an open airway but breathe inadequately because of illness or poisoning. A third may be unresponsive and breathing only in irregular gasps, which is not normal breathing. These conditions call for prompt recognition, emergency help and different immediate actions.

The first question is whether the person can respond and breathe normally. Absent or abnormal breathing, unresponsiveness, a blocked airway and rapidly worsening respiratory distress are emergencies. Activate emergency medical services immediately. If possible, direct another person to call and obtain an automated external defibrillator while you begin the response appropriate to the person's condition and your training. The goal of this lecture is to explain what you are observing and why the response differs, not to replace supervised practice with a training manikin.

## A cough tells you something important

Imagine someone eating who suddenly coughs violently. That cough indicates that air is moving and the person is attempting to clear the airway. A forceful, effective cough is itself a mechanism for expelling an obstruction. Encouraging coughing while watching closely is different from treating the person as though the airway were fully blocked.

Now imagine the person can no longer speak or cough effectively, struggles to breathe, or makes little sound despite obvious effort. Air movement may be severely obstructed. This is the kind of choking emergency where physical first-aid intervention is needed rather than waiting for the person to clear the object alone.

Current American Heart Association guidance, revised in 2025, recommends that for a conscious choking adult or child, rescuers alternate five back blows with five abdominal thrusts until the object is expelled or the person becomes unresponsive. The back blows and thrusts are not ordinary taps or hugs; they are deliberate interventions directed at expelling an obstruction. For adults in the later stages of pregnancy, or when the rescuer cannot encircle the abdomen, current guidance instead uses chest thrusts after the back blows. Correct positioning and force matter, and practicing with a qualified instructor is the right way to learn them.

For an infant under one year, the procedure is different. Guidance recommends alternating five back blows with five chest thrusts, supporting the infant appropriately. Abdominal thrusts are not recommended for infants because of the risk of injury. The distinction is based on anatomy and the different ways forces can be applied safely, not merely the infant's smaller mass. A person who learned an older or simplified memory aid should update that training to match current instructional guidance.

## What changes if the person becomes unresponsive?

If someone who is choking becomes unresponsive, activate emergency help if that has not already happened, and begin CPR following current guidance. Chest compressions have a circulatory purpose and can also contribute to moving an airway obstruction; trained responders follow the appropriate CPR and rescue-breath sequence. Do not perform blind finger sweeps into the mouth. An object should be removed only if it is visible and can be reached appropriately according to the applicable guidance. Blind probing can push an object deeper.

An AED should be brought promptly when a person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. The device provides prompts and can assess certain heart rhythms; it is not a machine for removing a food obstruction. The presence of a suspected choking event does not eliminate the need to treat cardiac arrest if the person is unresponsive with absent or abnormal breathing.

Different age groups have different compression techniques, depths and ventilation considerations in resuscitation. Those details belong in current, hands-on CPR instruction and the age-specific algorithms that instructors teach. It is better to learn the complete technique than to improvise a smaller version of adult CPR on an infant.

## Not every airway emergency is choking

A severe allergic reaction can cause swelling of the upper airway, wheezing, skin findings, gastrointestinal symptoms or circulatory collapse. A person with asthma may have severe lower-airway narrowing. Smoke or toxic gas exposure can impair breathing even when no physical object blocks the throat. Opioid overdose can suppress breathing. The observations overlap, but the mechanisms and treatments differ.

A person who can speak only in short phrases, appears unusually drowsy or confused, develops blue or gray coloration, or has visible severe difficulty breathing needs urgent assessment. Do not assume that somebody who is quiet is getting better. A struggling person may become quiet because of exhaustion or declining air movement.

For a person with a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector and suspected anaphylaxis, first-aid guidance includes prompt use or assistance as appropriate and emergency activation. For a person with a prescribed rescue inhaler and an asthma attack, assistance may be appropriate under current guidance and within the provider's training. These are not interchangeable treatments. Medication-specific dose, age and device procedures require current authoritative instruction; using an unprescribed medication or improvising a device is not the lesson here.

## A response begins before technique

Imagine you arrive while a child is choking at a family meal. You need to recognize whether the cough is effective, alert someone to call emergency services, identify the child's approximate age, and choose the correct current response. When several people are present, assign tasks directly rather than assuming somebody else has called. If a person becomes unresponsive, the response changes immediately. This recognition-and-transition skill is as important as memorizing a sequence of hand placements.

A second scenario begins with a person breathing noisily after a suspected food allergy. They are speaking but their voice is changing and they look faint. That is not a case of applying abdominal thrusts because the word 'airway' appears in both situations. It is a rapidly evolving medical emergency that needs emergency services and the appropriate anaphylaxis response. Clear descriptions of what you see help dispatchers and arriving responders.

The central practical distinction is between an effective cough, a severely obstructed airway, inadequate breathing for another reason, and unresponsiveness with abnormal or absent breathing. First aid is the skill of noticing the transition quickly, activating help, and performing the current age-appropriate response accurately.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Age-and-condition distinction for review

| Condition | High-level current response |
|---|---|
| Conscious adult or child, effective cough | Encourage coughing, observe closely, activate help if worsening |
| Conscious adult or child, severe foreign-body airway obstruction | Per 2025 AHA: alternate five back blows and five abdominal thrusts until relief or unresponsiveness; for adults in late pregnancy or whose abdomen cannot be encircled, use five chest thrusts after back blows |
| Infant younger than one year, severe foreign-body obstruction | Per 2025 AHA: alternate five back blows and five chest thrusts; no abdominal thrusts |
| Unresponsive, absent/abnormal breathing | Emergency activation, CPR and AED as appropriate to age and current training; no blind finger sweep |
| Suspected anaphylaxis or severe asthma | Emergency activation and appropriate condition-specific first-aid guidance; not choking maneuvers |

**Scope:** This is an educational draft requiring clinical review. The 2025 resuscitation guidance is newer than the 2024 joint first-aid guideline; use age-specific 2025 algorithms for choking. Distinguish an effective cough from severe obstruction, and choking from other respiratory emergencies. Do not treat this table as a replacement for certified hands-on first-aid and CPR instruction or dispatch guidance.

### Scenario questions

1. Can the person speak or cough effectively? What changed?
2. Adult/child or infant under one year?
3. Is breathing normal, absent, or gasping? Is the person responsive?
4. Have emergency services been activated and an AED requested when indicated?
5. Does the appearance suggest foreign-body obstruction, anaphylaxis, asthma or another medical problem?
