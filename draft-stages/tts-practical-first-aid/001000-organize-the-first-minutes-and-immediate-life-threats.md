# Organize the first minutes and immediate life threats

## See the Problem, Call for Help, and Organize the First Minutes

A person suddenly collapses at a family gathering. Several people rush forward. One calls their name. Someone else runs for water. A third looks for a phone. For a moment everyone is trying to help, but nobody knows which action matters first. Emergency response is not only a collection of medical techniques. It is also a problem of attention, communication, and priorities.

The first minutes reward a small number of disciplined habits: notice what happened, recognize conditions that threaten life, summon appropriate help, and provide time-critical care using current training and available equipment. An organized response need not be theatrical. It needs to be clear enough that several people can cooperate under stress.

### Begin with the scene

Before approaching a person, notice conditions that could injure you or others. Traffic, exposed electrical equipment, smoke, structural hazards, violent activity, moving machinery and hazardous substances can turn one casualty into two. Scene safety is a real assessment, not a formula spoken after someone has already stepped into danger. If the area is not safe to enter, call emergency services and report the hazard instead of creating another emergency.

When the scene is approachable, try to establish responsiveness. Speak to the person and, when appropriate, use a gentle physical stimulus consistent with current training. Look for normal breathing. An unconscious person who is not breathing normally, or is only gasping, may be in cardiac arrest. Gasping is not normal breathing. This is a situation for immediate emergency activation and CPR/AED response, not a wait-and-see diagnosis.

The Red Cross teaches a structured check, call, care approach. Its detailed instructions depend on the person's age, condition and rescuer training. A brief audio introduction can explain the logic but cannot replace a hands-on course, where positioning, compression quality, rescue breaths and device use can be practiced and assessed.

### A call is a medical intervention in its own right

Calling emergency services brings people, equipment, and a coordinated system of care toward the scene. In the United States, nine-one-one is the usual emergency number. Provide the exact location, what happened, whether the person is responsive and breathing normally, any immediate hazards, and any other information the dispatcher requests. Stay on the line and follow dispatcher instructions when possible.

If several people are present, assign tasks explicitly. "You, in the blue shirt, call nine-one-one and report back." "You, bring the AED from the hallway." An undirected shout of "Somebody call for help" can fail because everyone assumes someone else is doing it. Specific delegation reduces that ambiguity. If you are alone, follow current dispatcher and training guidance for your situation rather than relying on an improvised rule.

Keep track of time when you can do so without delaying care. When did the person collapse? When was an AED attached? When did symptoms begin? In stroke recognition, the last time a person was known well can be especially important. Observations that seem small in the moment may help responders make decisions later.

### Recognize what cannot wait

First aid begins with recognizing immediate threats to breathing and circulation. An unresponsive person who is not breathing normally requires an urgent cardiac-arrest response. Life-threatening bleeding requires prompt bleeding control. An obstructed airway may need rapid age-appropriate first-aid actions. Serious allergic reactions, stroke symptoms, and signs of a heart attack warrant emergency activation. The exact interventions differ; these conditions should not be compressed into one generic protocol.

For an adult in suspected cardiac arrest, current Red Cross guidance includes calling emergency services, beginning chest compressions, and using an automated external defibrillator as soon as one is available. For a trained rescuer providing conventional CPR, the Red Cross describes cycles of compressions and rescue breaths, while hands-only CPR is an approach taught for some adult situations. Children and infants have important differences, so adult instructions should not be applied to them without age-appropriate guidance.

An AED is designed to analyze cardiac rhythm and advise or deliver a shock when appropriate. It is not a device that shocks every unconscious person. Its spoken prompts and visual instructions guide the user, including ensuring nobody touches the person while it analyzes or delivers a shock. In a real event, using an available AED promptly as directed is more important than understanding every detail of the rhythm algorithm.

### Bleeding has its own mechanism

The amount and speed of blood loss matter. Blood loss reduces the volume available to circulate oxygen. Direct firm pressure applied to a bleeding wound is a fundamental response for many external bleeding injuries. Severe limb bleeding may require a commercially made tourniquet applied according to current training; wounds in locations where a tourniquet cannot be placed may need other bleeding-control methods. These distinctions are why Stop the Bleed training and reputable first-aid courses teach specific techniques rather than a single slogan.

When possible, use an appropriate barrier such as gloves to reduce exposure to blood. But do not spend critical time searching for an ideal kit while a life-threatening condition goes untreated. Good preparation makes needed items accessible before the emergency, rather than forcing a person to improvise while another person is deteriorating.

### Not every collapse is cardiac arrest

A person may be unresponsive yet breathing normally. Causes can include fainting, intoxication, seizure, metabolic problems, injury and many other conditions. Do not treat all unconsciousness as one disease. Call for emergency care as indicated, monitor breathing and responsiveness, and follow current training on positioning and the possibility of injury. Do not give an unconscious person food or drink.

A seizure may involve movements, altered awareness or a recovery period. A person who fell could have a head or neck injury. Someone who appears confused may have low blood sugar, stroke, poisoning or another urgent condition. First aid cannot be a remote diagnosis. Its role is to keep the person safer, notice deterioration and pass useful observations to professional responders.

Likewise, a person who is awake but struggling to breathe is not necessarily "less serious" than someone who fainted. Their speech, work of breathing, skin color, possible exposures and speed of change matter. Give a dispatcher the observations rather than trying to impress them with a speculative diagnosis.

### Prepare the space before anyone needs help

Knowing where the first-aid kit and AED are located is useful only if they can be accessed. An organized household or workplace can check whether equipment is present, current and appropriately maintained, whether contacts and emergency location information are easy to find, and whether people have current training. A home with children has age-specific needs. A workshop has hazards that a living room does not. Preparedness is a relationship between likely events, people, communication and supplies.

Consider rehearsing a simple scenario verbally. Someone collapses. Who checks the scene? Who calls? Who brings the AED? Where is the entry door and address information for responders? Who will meet them? This exercise does not require acting out medical procedures without instruction. It teaches coordination, which is itself a practical skill.

### The discipline of the first minutes

An emergency can make every action feel equally urgent. They are not. A lost minute finding the perfect bandage for a trivial scrape is different from a lost minute before emergency activation in cardiac arrest. The right habit is to recognize which processes are time-sensitive, delegate clearly, use equipment as directed and remain willing to update the plan when new information appears.

We do not need to turn bystanders into physicians. We need people who can notice immediate danger, say what they see, activate help, and use well-practiced first-aid skills. Real resilience in an emergency is not certainty about the diagnosis. It is competent action while important facts are still unknown.

## Recognizing Urgent Conditions and Calling for Help

The scene framework gives observations an order. Those observations also make the request for help more useful than an uncertain diagnostic label.

Imagine entering a room where someone is sitting on the floor, pale and confused. There is no obvious blood, no dramatic fall, and no clear explanation. Perhaps they stood too quickly. Perhaps their blood sugar is low. Perhaps a stroke or another medical emergency is beginning. The difficulty is that the first person on the scene rarely has enough information to know the diagnosis.

Fortunately, the first decision does not require a diagnosis. It requires recognizing whether the situation may be immediately dangerous, activating an appropriate emergency response, and making observations that help others. First aid begins by sorting uncertainty, not by pretending to eliminate it.

### First, notice the scene

Before focusing on a person, look for hazards that could create additional victims: traffic, live electrical equipment, fire, smoke, falling objects, gas, unstable structures or violence. A responder who becomes injured has not improved the situation. Once the scene can be approached safely, assess the person's responsiveness, breathing and any obvious life-threatening bleeding or severe injury. The American Red Cross organizes this into a practical sequence called Check, Call, Care, with condition-dependent details.

The order is not an excuse to conduct a lengthy examination when a person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. If a person is not responsive and has absent or abnormal gasping breathing, treat that as a possible cardiac arrest: call emergency services, request an AED, and begin CPR according to current training and dispatcher guidance. Agonal gasps are not normal, effective breathing. Seconds spent debating whether they “took one breath” can delay meaningful action.

### Urgency is a pattern, not a label

Some warning patterns warrant emergency help even if the cause is uncertain. Sudden one-sided weakness, facial droop or trouble speaking can indicate stroke. Chest pressure or discomfort with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea or discomfort in the jaw or arm can indicate a heart attack. Severe difficulty breathing, sudden collapse, seizures with concerning features, uncontrolled life-threatening bleeding, or signs of a major allergic reaction also demand rapid evaluation.

Symptoms are not always textbook. A heart attack may not begin with a theatrical clutch at the chest. A person having a stroke might stay awake and insist they feel fine. Some signs are subtle or fleeting. The useful skill is to recognize a dangerous possibility early enough that definitive evaluation is not delayed.

### Stroke: remember what changed

The American Heart Association uses the mnemonic B E F A S T. B is sudden loss of balance or coordination. E is a sudden eye or vision change. F is facial drooping or asymmetry. A is arm weakness or numbness. S is speech difficulty. T is time to call emergency services. The Red Cross also describes the familiar F A S T version and other stroke symptoms, including confusion, sudden severe headache and difficulty understanding speech.

One detail is especially valuable: the time when symptoms were last known absent, often called the last-known-well time. A person who wakes with weakness may have an uncertain onset; the last time they were definitely normal can matter to hospital treatment decisions. Record what witnesses know without inventing a precise onset. Symptoms that disappear can still require urgent evaluation, because a temporary improvement does not prove that the danger has passed.

### Breathing and bleeding compete for attention

In a real emergency, the first minutes contain several possible priorities. A person not breathing normally may need immediate CPR and AED access. Life-threatening external bleeding may need immediate direct control while emergency help is activated. If several people are present, delegate distinct tasks: one calls, one retrieves an AED or first-aid kit, and another provides condition-appropriate care. When alone, use emergency dispatcher directions to coordinate what to do next.

This is the difference between a crowd and a response team. A crowd may offer simultaneous guesses. A response team assigns jobs, communicates observations and checks whether each urgent action has actually happened.

### A useful emergency call

When you call emergency services, begin with the location and the nature of the emergency. Give the address or clear landmarks, building entry details and any immediate hazards. Describe what you observed rather than presenting a diagnosis as fact: “An adult suddenly cannot raise their right arm and is having trouble speaking,” is more useful than, “I think they have a brain problem.” Report whether the person is responsive and breathing normally, and mention major bleeding or severe breathing difficulty if present.

Dispatchers may ask about the person's age, symptoms, onset, relevant conditions, medications or allergies. Answer what you know; say when you do not know. Keep the phone available, follow the dispatcher instructions, and do not hang up until directed when the situation permits. If emergency responders will need access through a locked door, arrange a clear path when possible without abandoning urgent care.

The words we choose can influence response. “He fell” does not distinguish a slip from a collapse caused by heart trouble. “She is dizzy” omits whether the symptom began abruptly, followed a blow to the head, or accompanies weakness. Precise observations make the call more informative without requiring the bystander to act as a clinician.

### What to do with uncertainty

Suppose someone is speaking but seems unusually confused. You cannot tell whether the cause is medication, dehydration, a blood sugar problem, stroke or something else. The appropriate response is not to choose your favorite explanation and wait for confirmation. Look for immediate hazards, assess responsiveness and breathing, seek emergency evaluation when serious warning signs are present, and preserve useful information for trained responders.

The same principle applies to a person with sudden severe chest discomfort. The purpose of first aid is not to conduct an at-home differential diagnosis. It is to protect life during the gap before professional assessment and to pass along accurate observations. A trained responder may provide condition-specific assistance within their training and local protocols; someone without that training can still recognize the emergency and call for help.

### Handoffs are part of care

When responders arrive, they may ask what happened, when it began, what changed, and what actions were taken. A short chronology is invaluable. “At 2:10 she was speaking normally. At approximately 2:20 she could not form words. We called at 2:22. She remained awake but had weakness on the left,” is a much stronger account than, “It was about twenty minutes ago, I think.” If details are uncertain, label them as uncertain.

First aid is sometimes taught as a set of physical procedures. Those procedures matter, and practice with instructors matters. But another essential skill is cognitive: deciding that the situation is serious, making a timely call, giving a clear description, and not losing sight of the person while searching for a perfect explanation.

The first useful decision is often to treat a dangerous possibility as urgent before you know exactly what it is. That is not panic. It is disciplined action under uncertainty.

## CPR and AED: Adult Principles and Training Practice

Someone collapses in a public hallway. You approach and speak loudly. They do not respond. Their breathing seems absent, except for an occasional strange gasp. People gather, but nobody has yet called emergency services. In this situation, the skill that matters is recognizing that a possible cardiac arrest is happening and beginning an organized response. Waiting for certainty wastes time that circulation cannot spare.

Cardiac arrest means the heart is not producing an effective circulation of blood. It is not synonymous with a heart attack, in which blood flow to part of the heart muscle becomes blocked. A heart attack can cause cardiac arrest, but the immediate problem during arrest is failure to deliver oxygenated blood to the brain and other organs. The first-aid response must address that failure before a bystander knows its exact cause.

### Recognition and the first call

Check that the scene is safe enough to help. Try to get a response by speaking and tapping the person's shoulders. Check for normal breathing briefly. Gasping or occasional agonal breaths are not normal breathing. For an unresponsive adult who is not breathing normally, a lay rescuer should assume cardiac arrest and act.

Activate the emergency response system immediately. In the United States that generally means calling nine one one. If alone, call first and then begin compressions immediately; a phone's speaker mode lets a dispatcher guide actions while you help. If someone else is present, give specific assignments: 'You, call nine one one. You, bring the AED.' The aim is to prevent an entire group from assuming somebody else has acted.

You do not need to diagnose a heart rhythm before starting. A lay rescuer should not spend precious time hunting for a pulse. The combination of unresponsiveness and absent or abnormal breathing is the trigger for this emergency sequence.

### What compressions achieve

Chest compressions press on the chest and alter pressure in the thorax, helping drive some blood through the circulation. They do not restore normal cardiac function by themselves, and they cannot fully replace the heart's work. They buy time by maintaining some circulation while an automated external defibrillator and professional responders become available.

Place the person on their back on a firm, flat surface when that can be done safely and promptly. Kneel beside the torso. For an average adult, use two hands centered on the chest with the heel of one hand under the other, your shoulders approximately over your hands and elbows straight. Compress at a rate of one hundred to one hundred twenty per minute, to a depth of at least five centimeters, about two inches, while avoiding excessive depths greater than about six centimeters. Allow the chest to fully recoil between compressions and minimize interruptions.

The numbers serve a physical purpose. Compressions that are too shallow may move inadequate blood. A rate that is too fast can reduce filling and effective depth; a rate that is too slow provides too few compressions. Leaning on the chest between compressions reduces recoil. These details are difficult to judge without practice on a manikin that can provide feedback, which is why hands-on training improves the quality of the skill.

### Compressions and breaths

For a bystander who is untrained or unwilling or unable to provide rescue breaths, hands-only CPR for an adult is preferable to doing nothing. Continue chest compressions and use an AED as soon as possible. Trained rescuers who can provide breaths use a cycle of thirty compressions followed by two rescue breaths in standard adult CPR. Breaths should be sufficient to cause visible chest rise without excessive ventilation, and pauses should be brief.

Rescue breathing can be especially consequential when arrest has followed an oxygen problem, but different circumstances and responder training affect the detailed response. Adult CPR guidance does not automatically transfer unchanged to infants and children, drowning, or other special situations. The core adult sequence remains recognition, emergency activation, compressions, and prompt AED use.

### An AED is designed to help a bystander

An automated external defibrillator, or AED, analyzes electrical activity associated with the heart and determines whether a shock is advised. Certain dangerous rhythms, such as ventricular fibrillation, may respond to defibrillation. Other causes of cardiac arrest are not treated by a shock. This is why an AED can correctly announce 'no shock advised' even though the person still needs CPR and emergency care.

When an AED arrives, turn it on and follow its voice and visual prompts. Expose the chest as required and apply the pads in the positions shown on the device. Common adult placement puts one pad high on the right chest and the other lower on the left side; the device's diagrams govern the exact placement and any alternatives. The AED may require plugging in a pad connector. During rhythm analysis, make sure nobody is touching the person. If it advises a shock, confirm everyone is clear and deliver it as the device directs. Some devices deliver a shock automatically; others require pressing a button.

After a shock, or if the device advises no shock, resume CPR promptly according to its prompts. Do not wait to see whether the person suddenly wakes up. Minimize the time when the chest is not being compressed, apart from the necessary pauses for analysis, shocks, or other recommended actions. The AED's prompts help organize this sequence under stress.

### Practicing the response without a real emergency

A good training session is more than memorizing a list. Practice approaching a simulated collapse, checking responsiveness and breathing, activating help, speaking clear assignments, placing hands, giving compressions with feedback, and using an AED trainer. Practice what happens when the trainer says 'shock advised' and when it says 'no shock advised.' Learn how to switch compressors without long interruptions if several people can help.

In real life, emergency dispatchers can provide instructions. Continue following them and the AED until professional responders take over, the person shows signs of normal breathing or movement requiring reassessment, or you cannot safely continue. Actual courses also cover special circumstances, such as water, implanted devices, and the different needs of children.

Go back to the hallway. One person calls, another brings the AED, and one begins compressions. Nobody must solve the mystery of why the collapse happened before acting. The first minutes are governed by physiology: restore some blood flow, bring definitive emergency help, and use a defibrillator when its analysis indicates a shockable rhythm. That is the practical meaning of organized first aid.

## Severe Bleeding and Immediate Control

Severe bleeding presents a different immediate threat from cardiac arrest, so its recognition and training requirements need their own explanation.

A person has a deep wound after an accident. Blood is collecting rapidly on the ground. This is a different problem from a small cut that stops bleeding with a bandage. Severe external bleeding can become life-threatening before a distant ambulance reaches the scene. The immediate goal is to reduce blood loss while emergency care is activated. To act intelligently, we need to understand what bleeding control actually changes.

The heart pumps blood through arteries, capillaries, and veins. Blood carries oxygen, nutrients, heat, and components involved in immune defense and clotting. A severe injury can damage a vessel and let blood escape faster than the body's clotting response can control it. As circulating volume falls, organs may receive inadequate blood flow. The urgency is not primarily that a wound looks frightening. It is that blood loss can compromise the person's ability to stay alive.

### Recognize the problem and call for help

Life-threatening bleeding may appear as blood flowing continuously or spurting, blood pooling rapidly, clothing becoming heavily soaked, or bleeding associated with a severely injured or amputated limb. The appearance depends on the injury and is not a precise measurement of blood loss. You do not need to estimate milliliters on the ground before acting. First make the scene as safe as you can, avoid unnecessary contact with blood when protective barriers are available, and call emergency services or direct a specific nearby person to call. Describe the location, injury, and whether bleeding is controlled.

The first physical treatment for external bleeding is firm, direct manual pressure at the bleeding site. Pressure acts locally to reduce the escape of blood and support clot formation. A clean dressing can help distribute force and absorb blood, but direct pressure matters more than a perfectly folded piece of gauze. Hold steady force rather than checking every few seconds to see whether a clot has formed. If the injury or scene is complex, follow emergency dispatcher instructions.

### Why location changes the method

A tourniquet is a device that compresses the blood vessels of a limb proximal to the injury so that blood flow to the wound is greatly reduced or stopped. For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg, current first-aid guidelines support prompt use of a commercial tourniquet when appropriate, together with direct-pressure measures. The device must be of a suitable design and applied correctly. Tightening it can be painful; its purpose is to stop the bleeding, not merely make the strap feel snug. Once applied for a real emergency, record the time and leave decisions about release to medical professionals.

A tourniquet for a limb cannot simply be placed around the torso, neck, or groin. Those body regions require different pressure-based approaches and trained techniques such as wound packing when appropriate. Wound packing means placing suitable gauze or a hemostatic dressing into a wound in a way that allows sustained direct pressure against the bleeding source. It is a skill best practiced with training equipment, because the anatomy and wound location matter. For an injury to the chest or abdomen, a deep visible wound also raises hazards that cannot be addressed by treating it like a leg injury.

If equipment is not available, direct manual pressure remains a crucial intervention. A manufactured tourniquet is generally preferred over an improvised one, which may be unreliable and can cause injury if poorly constructed. Improvisation is not something to learn from a verbal description alone. Current American Heart Association and American Red Cross guidance distinguishes the availability of equipment, location of bleeding, and the responder's training.

### What to do while bleeding is being controlled

One responder may need to sustain pressure while another communicates with emergency services. Keep the person in a position appropriate to their injury and condition, and protect them from becoming excessively cold. Observe responsiveness and breathing. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, the cardiac-arrest response becomes relevant and should follow dispatch and current training. Do not give food or drink to a seriously injured person who may need emergency treatment.

A common error is to assume that because the blood flow has slowed, the emergency has ended. A person may have lost substantial blood already, may be bleeding internally as well, or may bleed again when pressure shifts. Reassurance and continuous monitoring matter, but they do not replace emergency evaluation. Nor does a small-looking outer wound rule out a serious internal injury.

### Pressure is a mechanism, not a ritual

Consider the difference between a dressing lying on a wound and a responder maintaining sufficient direct force against it. The dressing is a material. Pressure is the action that can limit the escaping blood. A properly applied tourniquet changes the flow further upstream in a limb. Hemostatic gauze can support clotting at the site, but it still depends on an appropriate pressure technique. Each method has a distinct mechanism and a region where it is useful.

A training course with realistic simulation is valuable because people need practice recognizing severity, locating the wound, applying steady pressure, placing a commercial tourniquet, and communicating clearly. Lecture listening can explain why actions work, but it cannot establish that someone's hands have learned the skill. The practical goal is simple: recognize a threat, summon emergency help, and control bleeding with an appropriate immediate method while trained help is on its way.

## Airway Problems and Age Differences

Airway problems add another distinction: the relevant observations and trained responses vary with the problem and the person’s age.

Breathing has two requirements: air must reach the lungs, and the body must be able to exchange gases and circulate oxygen to tissues. A person may be conscious yet unable to move air through an obstructed upper airway. Another person may have an open airway but breathe inadequately because of illness or poisoning. A third may be unresponsive and breathing only in irregular gasps, which is not normal breathing. These conditions call for prompt recognition, emergency help and different immediate actions.

The first question is whether the person can respond and breathe normally. Absent or abnormal breathing, unresponsiveness, a blocked airway and rapidly worsening respiratory distress are emergencies. Activate emergency medical services immediately. If possible, direct another person to call and obtain an automated external defibrillator while you begin the response appropriate to the person's condition and your training. Here the goal is to explain what you are observing and why the response differs, not to replace supervised practice with a training manikin.

### A cough tells you something important

Imagine someone eating who suddenly coughs violently. That cough indicates that air is moving and the person is attempting to clear the airway. A forceful, effective cough is itself a mechanism for expelling an obstruction. Encouraging coughing while watching closely is different from treating the person as though the airway were fully blocked.

Now imagine the person can no longer speak or cough effectively, struggles to breathe, or makes little sound despite obvious effort. Air movement may be severely obstructed. This is the kind of choking emergency where physical first-aid intervention is needed rather than waiting for the person to clear the object alone.

Current American Heart Association guidance, revised in 2025, recommends that for a conscious choking adult or child, rescuers alternate five back blows with five abdominal thrusts until the object is expelled or the person becomes unresponsive. The back blows and thrusts are not ordinary taps or hugs; they are deliberate interventions directed at expelling an obstruction. For adults in the later stages of pregnancy, or when the rescuer cannot encircle the abdomen, current guidance instead uses chest thrusts after the back blows. Correct positioning and force matter, and practicing with a qualified instructor is the right way to learn them.

For an infant under one year, the procedure is different. Guidance recommends alternating five back blows with five chest thrusts, supporting the infant appropriately. Abdominal thrusts are not recommended for infants because of the risk of injury. The distinction is based on anatomy and the different ways forces can be applied safely, not merely the infant's smaller mass. A person who learned an older or simplified memory aid should update that training to match current instructional guidance.

### What changes if the person becomes unresponsive?

If someone who is choking becomes unresponsive, activate emergency help if that has not already happened, and begin CPR following current guidance. Chest compressions have a circulatory purpose and can also contribute to moving an airway obstruction; trained responders follow the appropriate CPR and rescue-breath sequence. Do not perform blind finger sweeps into the mouth. An object should be removed only if it is visible and can be reached appropriately according to the applicable guidance. Blind probing can push an object deeper.

An AED should be brought promptly when a person is unresponsive and not breathing normally. The device provides prompts and can assess certain heart rhythms; it is not a machine for removing a food obstruction. The presence of a suspected choking event does not eliminate the need to treat cardiac arrest if the person is unresponsive with absent or abnormal breathing.

Different age groups have different compression techniques, depths and ventilation considerations in resuscitation. Those details belong in current, hands-on CPR instruction and the age-specific algorithms that instructors teach. It is better to learn the complete technique than to improvise a smaller version of adult CPR on an infant.

### Not every airway emergency is choking

A severe allergic reaction can cause swelling of the upper airway, wheezing, skin findings, gastrointestinal symptoms or circulatory collapse. A person with asthma may have severe lower-airway narrowing. Smoke or toxic gas exposure can impair breathing even when no physical object blocks the throat. Opioid overdose can suppress breathing. The observations overlap, but the mechanisms and treatments differ.

A person who can speak only in short phrases, appears unusually drowsy or confused, develops blue or gray coloration, or has visible severe difficulty breathing needs urgent assessment. Do not assume that somebody who is quiet is getting better. A struggling person may become quiet because of exhaustion or declining air movement.

For a person with a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector and suspected anaphylaxis, first-aid guidance includes prompt use or assistance as appropriate and emergency activation. For a person with a prescribed rescue inhaler and an asthma attack, assistance may be appropriate under current guidance and within the provider's training. These are not interchangeable treatments. Medication-specific dose, age and device procedures require current authoritative instruction; using an unprescribed medication or improvising a device is not the lesson here.

### A response begins before technique

Imagine you arrive while a child is choking at a family meal. You need to recognize whether the cough is effective, alert someone to call emergency services, identify the child's approximate age, and choose the correct current response. When several people are present, assign tasks directly rather than assuming somebody else has called. If a person becomes unresponsive, the response changes immediately. This recognition-and-transition skill is as important as memorizing a sequence of hand placements.

A second scenario begins with a person breathing noisily after a suspected food allergy. They are speaking but their voice is changing and they look faint. That is not a case of applying abdominal thrusts because the word 'airway' appears in both situations. It is a rapidly evolving medical emergency that needs emergency services and the appropriate anaphylaxis response. Clear descriptions of what you see help dispatchers and arriving responders.

The central practical distinction is between an effective cough, a severely obstructed airway, inadequate breathing for another reason, and unresponsiveness with abnormal or absent breathing. First aid is the skill of noticing the transition quickly, activating help, and performing the current age-appropriate response accurately.

### CPR and AED: Adult Principles and Training Practice

**Track:** First Aid and Emergency Response. **Background:** Recognizing life-threatening emergencies and activating help. **Purpose:** teach the adult cardiac-arrest response and the physiological reason for each major step.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Adult lay-rescuer sequence and evidence

Under the **2025 American Heart Association Adult Basic Life Support guidelines** and American Red Cross public guidance:

1. Confirm reasonable scene safety; check responsiveness and normal breathing briefly. Gasping is not normal breathing.
2. Activate emergency response (in the U.S., 911) **before** starting CPR when alone; obtain an AED or direct someone to retrieve it.
3. Start adult chest compressions `100–120/min`, `5–6 cm` approximate target range (at least `5 cm`, avoid `>6 cm`) with full recoil and minimal interruptions.
4. Hands-only CPR is appropriate for an untrained bystander; trained providers can use `30:2` adult compressions:breaths with brief pauses and visible chest rise.
5. Turn on the AED, follow its pad diagrams and instructions; no contact during analysis/shock; resume CPR after advised shock or no-shock decision.

The exact response may differ with patient age, pregnancy, drowning, suspected overdose, other circumstances, and responder training. AED voice prompts and emergency dispatcher directions take priority for the particular device and event. Real practice should be conducted with a CPR manikin and AED trainer, not on an uninjured person.

### Severe Bleeding and Immediate Control

**Track:** First Aid and Emergency Response. **Background:** Emergency recognition, activation of help, and adult CPR/AED principles. **Purpose:** understand time-critical external bleeding control and the distinctions among pressure, tourniquets, and wound packing.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### First-aid decision distinctions

- **Life-threatening external bleeding:** activate emergency services; begin firm direct pressure to the site. Current AHA/Red Cross first-aid guidelines support tourniquet use for suitable severe limb wounds and wound packing in appropriate locations by trained responders.
- **Tourniquets:** use a purpose-designed commercial device where appropriate; follow hands-on training for placement, tightening, reassessment, and time recording. Do not remove or loosen an applied emergency tourniquet without medical direction.
- **Wound packing:** technique is location-dependent and requires training; it is not the same as applying a bandage over the skin.
- **Internal bleeding / major trauma:** external pressure may not address the main injury. Obtain emergency evaluation.

##### Evidence and safety scope

Primary source: **2024 American Heart Association and American Red Cross Guidelines for First Aid**, sections on external hemorrhage and tourniquets. American Red Cross public training materials provide operational illustrations. This is a training companion, not a substitute for a hands-on Stop the Bleed, Red Cross, or equivalent course. Avoid creating improvised-device instructions or using simplified steps as a clinical protocol. Verify updates to clinical guidance before publication and have a qualified reviewer assess this lecture.

### Airway Problems and Age Differences

**Track:** First Aid and Emergency Response. **Background:** emergency recognition, CPR/AED concepts, and bleeding control. **Purpose:** recognize airway emergencies and understand why age changes first-aid response.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Age-and-condition distinction for review

| Condition | High-level current response |
|---|---|
| Conscious adult or child, effective cough | Encourage coughing, observe closely, activate help if worsening |
| Conscious adult or child, severe foreign-body airway obstruction | Per 2025 AHA: alternate five back blows and five abdominal thrusts until relief or unresponsiveness; for adults in late pregnancy or whose abdomen cannot be encircled, use five chest thrusts after back blows |
| Infant younger than one year, severe foreign-body obstruction | Per 2025 AHA: alternate five back blows and five chest thrusts; no abdominal thrusts |
| Unresponsive, absent/abnormal breathing | Emergency activation, CPR and AED as appropriate to age and current training; no blind finger sweep |
| Suspected anaphylaxis or severe asthma | Emergency activation and appropriate condition-specific first-aid guidance; not choking maneuvers |

**Scope:** This is an educational draft requiring clinical review. The 2025 resuscitation guidance is newer than the 2024 joint first-aid guideline; use age-specific 2025 algorithms for choking. Distinguish an effective cough from severe obstruction, and choking from other respiratory emergencies. Do not treat this table as a replacement for certified hands-on first-aid and CPR instruction or dispatch guidance.

##### Scenario questions

1. Can the person speak or cough effectively? What changed?
2. Adult/child or infant under one year?
3. Is breathing normal, absent, or gasping? Is the person responsive?
4. Have emergency services been activated and an AED requested when indicated?
5. Does the appearance suggest foreign-body obstruction, anaphylaxis, asthma or another medical problem?
