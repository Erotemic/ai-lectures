# Romance of the Three Kingdoms: narrative and research roadmap

**Placement:** World History audio → World Literary Epics and Classics.
**Status:** seventeen complete standalone long-form lectures, 058000–074000.
**Narrative version:** the familiar 120-chapter Chinese novel, indexed using the
Chinese Text Project's received text. The episode plan does not pretend that
this chapter numbering reproduces the earliest surviving printing.

The novel's historical setting runs from the late second-century crisis of the
Eastern Han through the reunification of 280 CE. The oldest extant large-scale
printed edition, *Sanguozhi tongsu yanyi*, bears a preface dated 1522 and an
earlier preface dated 1494; the familiar 120-chapter recension owes much to the
later Mao-family edition. Luo Guanzhong is the traditional author attribution,
not a demonstrated byline for every layer of the surviving text.

## Historical evidence and version rules

**Historical baseline.** Chen Shou's third-century *Records of the Three
Kingdoms* (*Sanguo zhi*) is foundational but concise. Pei Songzhi's annotations
(completed in 429 CE) preserve quoted earlier materials, comparisons,
contradictions, and explicit critical judgments. They are separate from the
novel and should not be summarized as if each anecdote has identical evidentiary
status. Later dynastic histories and documents contribute other perspectives.

**Early modern novel.** The printed textual history passes through the 1522
edition, additional Ming printings, and the influential Mao-family recension.
The 1522 witness had 240 shorter divisions; the received 120 chapters should be
named when quoting chapter numbers. The Luo Guanzhong attribution is traditional.
Avoid claims of a single fully verified author or original autograph.

**Famous scenes needing explicit distinction:**
- The Peach Garden oath is the novel's origin scene, not a documented ceremony.
- The three brothers' fight with Lü Bu and the elaborate pass-and-gate adventure
  of Guan Yu are literary expansions of martial and loyalty traditions.
- Guan Yu's role at White Horse is historically attested; the whole road narrative
  should not be credited to Chen Shou.
- Red Cliffs and the key roles of Zhou Yu and Huang Gai are historical. The
  straw-boat arrows, ritual wind altar and private rivalries have different
  textual pedigrees. Do not use those details to diminish Zhou Yu's command.
- The southern campaign of 225 is historical. The Meng Huo seven-captures motif
  predates the complete novel; its extended marvels are later elaborations.
  The novel's ethnic categories are the viewpoint of a Chinese state-centered
  tradition, not a neutral description of southern peoples.
- The Empty Fort anecdote circulated before the novel and was criticized by
  Pei Songzhi. The version involving Zhuge Liang and Sima Yi must not be
  presented as an unchallenged historical encounter.
- The defeats at Yiling, Jieting and the fall of Shu are historically grounded;
  speeches and intimate scenes are reconstructed literary staging.

**Narration.** Each lecture begins with essential local context, including
political geography and relationships as needed. Tell scenes in the novel's
terms, then compare the historical evidence when its status makes a substantial
difference. Use original paraphrase; never recite lengthy passages from a
copyrighted modern translation. Do not mention editorial instructions or
production details within speech markers.
