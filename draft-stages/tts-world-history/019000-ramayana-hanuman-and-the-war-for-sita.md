# The Ramayana, Part II: Hanuman and the War for Sita

A woman sits in a grove under guard in the island kingdom of Lanka. Her name is Sita, and she has been abducted by the king who rules the city around her. He offers jewels, power, and a place beside him. She refuses. Far away, her husband Rama searches for the person he has lost. He has no fleet, no army, and no clear road across the sea. The most famous events of the *Ramayana* emerge from the distance between these two people, and from the friendships, bargains, acts of courage, and terrible violence required to bring them within sight of one another again.

The *Ramayana* is a great Sanskrit epic, traditionally associated with Valmiki, and one of the central narrative and devotional traditions of South Asia. There are many Ramayanas, composed and performed in different languages and communities. For this account we follow mainly the Sanskrit story: the search in the Kishkindha book, Hanuman's mission in the Sundara book, and the war in the Yuddha book. The final part of the lecture considers the later Uttara book, whose position in the literary history is debated, and the striking differences among retellings. We do not need prior acquaintance with Rama to begin. He is a prince of Ayodhya who has accepted fourteen years of forest exile to honor his father's promise; his wife Sita chose to accompany him, and his younger brother Lakshmana guards him. During the exile, the powerful Ravana seized Sita and carried her to Lanka. That is where our story begins.

## Searching without knowing where to go

Rama and Lakshmana move through the forest asking where Sita has been taken. They encounter a dying bird, Jatayu, who tried to stop the abduction. Jatayu identifies Ravana, but cannot follow him across the sky. The brothers are left with fragments: an enemy's name, a general direction, and evidence that Sita did not leave willingly. Their path leads through further encounters, including a being called Kabandha whose violent form is transformed after death. Before his release, Kabandha directs the brothers toward an ally who may help. The adventure supplies information as well as peril, a typical feature of epic searching: each figure encountered may carry a piece of the route.

They also reach the hermitage associated with Shabari, a woman of great devotion who has awaited Rama's arrival. Accounts of the encounter vary, and the familiar image of Shabari offering berries she has tasted belongs especially to later devotional retellings. The Sanskrit narrative gives her an important place as a welcoming devotee who has remained faithful to the instruction of her teachers. She belongs to a broader pattern: people outside courts can recognize or help a hero when high-status figures have failed to protect him. The search is not simply a royal command sent through obedient officers. It relies on hospitality, memory, devotion, and the knowledge of people the palace might not have noticed.

The brothers come to the region of Kishkindha, where another struggle for a throne has divided a family. Sugriva is in exile after a conflict with his brother Vali, the powerful ruler of the vanaras. These beings are often represented as monkeys, though the epic's imaginative world gives them language, politics, families, and military organization. To call them only animals is to miss the way their kingdom mirrors and complicates the human royal world. A displaced prince meets a displaced ruler. They are both seeking to recover what they have lost, but they need each other's strengths to do it.

## Hanuman and the politics of friendship

Hanuman first approaches the strangers on Sugriva's behalf. He is intelligent, eloquent, observant, and alert to danger. In many devotional traditions he becomes the exemplary servant and devotee of Rama; in the epic he is also a supremely capable diplomat who recognizes the value of words before weapons. He speaks carefully to discover who the newcomers are and why they have entered a territory already divided by conflict. Rama notices his manner and discernment. Their alliance begins with conversation, an important counterpoint to the forthcoming scenes of immense physical power.

Sugriva tells Rama about his quarrel with Vali. In a fight with a dangerous opponent, Vali had entered a cave and failed to return for a long time. Sugriva believed him dead and took responsibility for the kingdom. Vali later returned and interpreted the event as betrayal. He drove Sugriva away and took Sugriva's wife. The brothers recount the history in ways that make their conduct intelligible to themselves. Rama promises to help Sugriva recover his kingdom; Sugriva promises to organize the search for Sita. Their pact binds the two stories together. Neither ruler will accomplish his goal alone.

Rama's intervention leads to one of the epic's most argued moments. As Vali fights Sugriva, Rama shoots Vali from concealment. Vali asks what right Rama has to kill him while he is engaged in combat with another person. Rama gives a defense grounded in kingship and the judgment that Vali has violated a moral order. Yet the confrontation leaves readers with a real question: is the success of an alliance enough to justify the method by which it is secured? The text includes Vali's protest rather than erasing it. Later readers and retellers have debated the scene precisely because Rama is supposed to exemplify righteous conduct. A hero's legitimacy is tested most severely when his victory seems to threaten the values he represents.

Vali dies and Sugriva becomes ruler. But victory brings a new difficulty: the beneficiary of Rama's help delays fulfilling his part of the bargain. Monsoon weather makes travel hard, and Sugriva enjoys the restored comforts of his position. Rama's patience wears thin. Lakshmana confronts the new king, and Tara, Vali's widow, plays a crucial role in calming and interpreting the dispute. Sugriva finally assembles search parties. The alliance has survived, but the delay reminds us that political friendship requires ongoing accountability. A promise made in danger can become less urgent to the person whose own danger has passed.

The search parties travel in different directions. One group led by Angada, son of Vali, with Hanuman among its members, reaches the southern coast. Their provisions and time are exhausted. They seem to have failed. Then they learn from Sampati, an aged bird associated with Jatayu, that Sita is across the sea in Lanka. The information solves one problem and creates another. The searchers can identify a destination they cannot reach. A gulf of water separates knowledge from action, and even warriors confident on land hesitate before it.

## The leap over the sea

Jambavan, an elder among the searchers, reminds Hanuman of the extent of his strength. The encouragement is more than flattery. Hanuman's abilities are great, but the mission requires him to recognize that his strength can serve others at an extraordinary scale. He gathers himself and leaps across the ocean. The poem describes the sea, mountains, clouds, and creatures passing beneath him. His body becomes a vehicle for crossing a distance that would defeat ordinary travelers. Yet the journey tests judgment as well as force. He must avoid distraction, respond to obstacles, and remember that reaching Lanka unnoticed is more valuable than demonstrating power at every opportunity.

One figure offers hospitality on the way, another challenges his ability to pass, and a dangerous being attempts to capture him. Hanuman does not resolve each encounter in the same fashion. At times he expands himself, at times makes himself small, at times accepts an invitation and proceeds, at times overcomes an enemy. This flexibility is part of his appeal. A heroic mission does not require one fixed posture. Successful courage may involve humility, strategy, and the ability to distinguish a test from a trap.

He arrives at Lanka by night and surveys the city. The description of Ravana's capital is deliberately impressive. Its buildings, defenses, wealth, and living quarters reveal a realm with organization and beauty. It would be easy to imagine the adversary's home as a crude lair, but that would reduce the force of the problem. A magnificent city can be ruled by a man who commits an intolerable wrong. Prosperity and aesthetic achievement do not automatically establish justice. Hanuman searches the residences, trying to find Sita without exposing the purpose of his mission.

## The woman in the grove

Sita is held in the Ashoka grove. She is threatened and pressed to accept Ravana, but refuses. The narrative allows us to witness her endurance even before Hanuman can offer any help. He observes her carefully because he must be sure he has found the right person. Then he speaks in a way that will establish trust. A stranger appearing in a place where her captor commands every visible guard could easily be another deception. Sita must distinguish a messenger of hope from a new form of danger.

Hanuman recounts facts about Rama and produces a recognizable token, often Rama's signet ring. Sita responds with a token and a message for Rama. These objects carry personal recognition across a distance that neither person can cross directly. Their importance is emotional, but also practical: in a world where disguises and illusions are possible, a message must come with evidence of its sender. Hanuman has not rescued Sita, yet he has achieved something decisive. He has transformed a desperate search into a verifiable contact between people separated by force.

Why does Sita not simply leave with Hanuman? In the Sanskrit account she refuses his offer to carry her away, raising concerns about the danger, the propriety of another man's touch, and Rama's role in restoring her. Her reasoning is shaped by the social and moral world of the epic, and different retellings emphasize different elements. The decision can be read as courageous confidence, but it also deserves scrutiny because it leaves a captive woman exposed to further danger while men prepare a war. To understand the scene we should listen to her arguments rather than assign her one convenient motive.

Hanuman's mission does not end with leaving quietly. He destroys part of the grove and fights defenders, judging that he can also test Lanka's military response and send a warning. Captured and brought before Ravana, he speaks boldly. Ravana's court debates his treatment, and a decision to set his tail alight backfires spectacularly. Hanuman escapes and uses the flames to burn parts of the city. The episode is exhilarating in performance, full of motion and reversal. It also reminds us that a rescue effort is becoming a conflict whose damage will reach beyond the two royal households. Cities contain people who did not choose the conduct of their king.

Hanuman returns over the sea with the news that Sita is alive. Rama's response is a turning point. He can finally imagine a course of action that begins with reliable knowledge rather than grief and speculation. Hanuman's achievement is often remembered as an expression of devotion, but it is also an accomplishment in intelligence gathering, persuasion, and navigation. His immense strength would have been less useful without his attention to the person he was sent to find.

## A bridge and an army

Rama's allies march toward the sea. Their problem is now one of collective logistics: how can a large force reach a fortified island? In the poem the ocean itself becomes part of the negotiation. Rama demands passage; divine and cosmic powers respond; and the forces build a causeway or bridge under the direction of Nala, an engineer among the vanaras. Later tellings elaborate the miracle in different ways. The physical image has become one of the best-known scenes in South Asian religious and artistic memory: a host carrying stones and trees toward water, making a route where there was none.

The bridge shows the difference between Hanuman's leap and an army's movement. One extraordinary being can cross a gulf, but a whole coalition needs an infrastructure that lesser bodies can use. The path is made by labor, not simply by intention. In the course of the epic, political alliances, engineering, and individual heroism depend on one another. This is part of what lets the story feel larger than a private feud: rescue requires organization, resources, and the commitment of many participants.

Within Lanka, Ravana's own household is divided. His brother Vibhishana advises him to return Sita and avoid disaster. Ravana rejects the advice, and Vibhishana defects to Rama's side. What should loyalty mean when one's own ruler has committed a wrong? The epic places this question beside the earlier conflict in Ayodhya. There, family fidelity sustained an oath that injured a household. Here family fidelity would mean protecting a ruler whose decisions threaten the kingdom. Vibhishana chooses a competing duty and pays for it with estrangement from his home.

War follows. The combat is vast and often fantastical, with flying weapons, powerful spells, champions capable of changing their forms, and injuries that would defeat ordinary fighters. Ravana's son Indrajit becomes one of the most formidable defenders. He uses illusion and extraordinary weapons to trap opponents and create apparent reversals. The battles are not a simple unbroken march toward Rama's victory. The attackers suffer and repeatedly need help. Knowledge of a weapon, a timely intervention, or a healing remedy can change the result as much as courage.

One of Ravana's most memorable allies is his giant brother Kumbhakarna, whose enormous sleep and immense appetite make him larger than life. Yet he is given a tragic dimension when he recognizes the folly of Ravana's actions and nevertheless goes to fight for his brother. The character presents loyalty separated from endorsement. A person may understand that a war was wrongfully begun and still choose to protect those bound to him. The epic does not make his fate less destructive for that complexity, but the added depth gives the conflict more weight than the defeat of nameless monsters.

Lakshmana is gravely wounded in the fighting. Hanuman must go in search of a medicinal plant; in familiar versions, unable to identify the needed herb, he carries the entire mountain that bears it. The image has become emblematic of devotion in action. The task is urgent, and his response is comically extravagant as well as heroic. When he cannot select one small lifesaving object, he transports the whole place where it grows. This is an example of how an epic can make a character lovable through a feat that is powerful and slightly excessive at the same time.

## Voices from the besieged city

The story allows us to see the war from within Lanka as well as through Rama's campaign. Ravana's advisers and relatives are not all eager for battle. Vibhishana has already warned his brother that holding Sita will invite disaster. Mandodari, Ravana's wife, appears in traditions of the epic as a figure capable of recognizing the costs that arrogance brings upon a household. Her grief after the fighting places a different emotional frame around the hero's victory. A defeated ruler can have committed a terrible act and still leave people who mourn him. That does not excuse the abduction; it reminds us that the consequences of a ruler's choices extend to persons who opposed them or never made them.

Ravana himself is a complex opponent partly because his capacities are real. He is associated with ritual austerity, immense learning, and command over a splendid realm. His tragedy in the story is a failure to apply judgment to his own desire. He can understand the language of honor, kingship, and obligation, yet refuses to give back the woman whom he has taken. A villain who cannot imagine the good would be less interesting than a ruler whose accomplishments do not prevent him from causing disaster. The epic's claim about power becomes sharper when its antagonist has qualities that might have supported a better form of rule.

The war also tests how a coalition survives when its members come from different communities. The vanaras have their own leaders and quarrels. Vibhishana has abandoned one loyalty in order to act upon another. Rama's human companions have personal ties to the prince and to Sita, but not every soldier has met the woman being rescued. The army cannot be understood as one individual's enlarged body. Its participants bring decisions, obligations, skills, and exposure to harm. Hanuman's earlier reconnaissance, Nala's engineering, and the combat leadership of many figures all contribute to an outcome remembered chiefly under Rama's name.

Such distinctions are important because epics often celebrate a single hero in ways that compress the work of companions. A bridge may be named after a prince even though others build it. A message may be credited to a king even though a messenger risks his life carrying it. Listening to the supporting characters restores the collective scale of the story. It also brings the war's costs back into view. Victory is not a simple conversion of battlefield deaths into a happy ending; it is the beginning of responsibility for a damaged political world.

## Ravana's last battle

The war narrows toward the confrontation between Rama and Ravana. Ravana has lost soldiers, family members, and the possibility of a peaceful settlement. He continues to fight, and Rama ultimately kills him. The battlefield victory answers the military problem that began with the abduction. Lanka can no longer be used as a prison secured by Ravana's authority. Vibhishana is installed as ruler. The forces of Rama have achieved their objective, though the price has been paid by many more people than the original combatants.

It would be tempting for the story to end here, with the defeated captor and the rescued wife reunited. Instead comes the scene that makes the Sanskrit epic morally difficult. Rama questions how Sita can return to him publicly after living in another man's household. Sita protests. The implication that captivity makes her suspect is a grave injury added to the original violence. She enters fire to demonstrate her purity, and the divine powers vindicate her. The ordeal is known as the *agni pariksha*, the trial by fire. It has been interpreted devotionally, symbolically, critically, and in many other ways across the tradition.

There are versions that protect Sita differently, including traditions of a substitute or illusory Sita, and versions in which the emphasis of her response shifts. We should not present one interpretation as what every community believes. In the Sanskrit story the scene exposes a conflict between public reputation and intimate trust. Rama can appear to defend the standards expected of a ruler while failing to offer the protection a partner should provide to someone held captive against her will. Sita's forceful speech refuses to let the accusation pass as though she were an object whose worth somebody else can certify without hearing her.

After the vindication, the exiles return to Ayodhya and Rama is crowned. The long-promised restoration has arrived. Bharata can relinquish the regency he never wanted as a permanent possession. The homecoming is celebrated as the beginning of just royal rule, often called *Rama rajya* in later traditions. The major story has moved from one proposed coronation through fourteen years of exile, abduction, alliance, and war to a coronation finally achieved. But the restored throne does not make every question raised by the story disappear.

## The further story of Sita

The Uttara Kanda, the seventh or concluding book found in many versions of the Sanskrit tradition, continues beyond the coronation. Textual historians often regard much of this book as belonging to a different phase of the epic's growth, while many religious communities accept it as an integral part of the sacred story. Both facts belong in our account. The book contains further histories of Ravana and other figures and tells of renewed public suspicion about Sita. Rama, anxious about his reputation as ruler, sends her away while she is pregnant. She finds refuge in Valmiki's hermitage, where her sons Lava and Kusha grow up and learn to sing the story of Rama.

The framing is extraordinary. The sons perform the poem of their father's deeds in a world where their mother has borne the cost of his public authority. The story of the king returns to him as a song from the children he does not yet fully know. Sita's eventual appeal to the earth, which receives her in the conclusion of this tradition, is frequently understood as an assertion of dignity that no further public test can satisfy. It also creates a painful ending to a story often remembered for triumph. The wife whose faithful conduct supported the hero's quest does not simply resume life at court without consequences.

Many retellings change these scenes, omit them, or place different moral weight upon them. In some Rama is more transparently divine; in others Sita becomes the emotional center; in still others Ravana is transformed or the story is rearranged. Tamil, Hindi, Bengali, Thai, Javanese, Khmer, and many other literary and performance traditions participate in this history, each with its own choices. A. K. Ramanujan's well-known discussion of the many Ramayanas helps explain why it is misleading to search for one mechanical plot that all traditions must reproduce. Narrative continuity has been sustained precisely through creative variation.

## Many ways to hear the same epic

An epic this widely known has never belonged to a single medium. In one community Rama's story may be recited as scripture; in another it is acted as dance or theater; in another it is read as a literary work whose heroes can be questioned. The Tamil poet Kamban made a major retelling, and Tulsidas composed the *Ramcharitmanas* in Awadhi centuries later. The Thai *Ramakien*, Javanese performances, and many other works also reshape characters and episodes. Each has a history of its own. Calling them copies of an original misses the degree to which they create new emphases for new audiences.

This variety is especially visible around Sita. Some traditions portray her primarily through devotion and endurance. Others give her more explicit criticism of Rama or devote greater attention to the injustice of captivity and suspicion. Some introduce a protective line at the forest dwelling; others use the device of a shadow or substitute Sita to change the significance of her confinement. Such choices can alter how listeners interpret consent, responsibility, and reputation. The differences should be identified as specific storytelling decisions rather than dismissed as errors introduced by a later audience.

Ravana also changes with context. He can appear as an overwhelming antagonist, a powerful devotee with tragic arrogance, or a figure whose capacities invite a more ambivalent treatment. Hanuman may be foregrounded as a clever political envoy or as an image of the highest devotional love. The story's geographical spread into Southeast Asia created settings in which performances of the epic could belong to court ceremony, temple art, village entertainment, and local political ideas. That history is a reminder that an enduring narrative can unite people through shared characters while sustaining serious differences in what those characters mean.

A listener therefore need not choose between respecting the devotional importance of the Ramayana and asking critical literary questions. We can distinguish the Sanskrit narrative selected here, the broader traditions of performance, and the moral judgments offered by different readers. Doing so makes the epic richer, because it allows the same scenes to be heard with attention to their particular words, their cultural setting, and the questions they have continued to provoke.

## The questions that survive victory

Rama's story contains several kinds of strength. There is the strength to keep a promise that one did not make, the strength to refuse a crown acquired through injustice, the strength to search through uncertainty, and Hanuman's strength to cross an ocean while remembering the person his mission serves. None automatically guarantees good judgment in every circumstance. Rama can act with admirable restraint in the succession crisis and still face serious moral criticism for the treatment of Sita. Vali can be a formidable ruler and still wrong his brother. Vibhishana can leave his family and act from fidelity to a larger duty. The epic gives its characters commitments that cannot always be satisfied together.

The figure who may change most for a new listener is Hanuman. His powers are spectacular, but his first crucial act is speaking well to strangers. He protects a vulnerable messenger relationship by demonstrating trustworthiness, travels beyond the reach of an army, and returns with reliable information. His service links private affection to collective action. The story does not ask him to become king. That leaves him free to represent something other than the pursuit of power: an ability exercised for the sake of someone else's need.

Sita's role is no less central. She is abducted, but she does not assent to the abduction. She declines the captor's offers, determines how to answer Hanuman, and protests the accusation against her. Her power to decide is repeatedly restricted by those around her. Those restrictions form part of the epic's social world, and readers have been responding to them for centuries. A close reading should make her words audible, especially in scenes where men threaten to speak on her behalf.

The Ramayana's world is vast enough to connect royal succession, religious obligation, forest life, alliances across communities, ocean travel, siege warfare, and the intimate consequences of public reputation. But its central drama remains comprehensible. A family promise destroys an expected future. A woman is taken from her home. The people who seek her must learn to act with others. The victory that brings her back also reveals the limits of the moral and political order that made rescue necessary. That is why the story can continue to inspire devotion, art, argument, and reconsideration without being exhausted by any one telling. Its questions are still alive because its most important characters are capable of both conviction and suffering.

## Research and editorial notes (not spoken)

Main narrative boundary: *Valmiki Ramayana* Kishkindha, Sundara and Yuddha kandas; brief account of the Uttara Kanda explicitly marked as an additional textual layer. The Lanka war is a literary narrative, not independently established chronicle. Shabari's tasted berries and a Lakshmana protective line are prominent in some later tellings and are not imported into the Sanskrit storyline. Sita's fire ordeal and subsequent exile have multiple interpretations. Descriptions of animal allies use the epic's own imaginative categories; do not flatten them into modern zoological claims.
