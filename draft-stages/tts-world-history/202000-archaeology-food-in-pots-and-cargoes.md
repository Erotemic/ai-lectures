# Ancient Food in Pots and Cargoes: How Chemistry Identifies What People Traded

An archaeologist finds a pottery jar in the remains of an ancient building. There is no inscription saying what it contained. It might have held water, grain, beer, oil, or a mixture that no longer has a familiar name. After thousands of years, the vessel seems empty. Yet a few molecules may still be lodged within the ceramic pores. A second jar from a different site holds preserved peppercorns, and a third, raised from a shipwreck, has a thin resinous coating inside. How can we identify their contents? And if we can, how do we turn the identification of a substance into a claim about trade, cooking, or everyday life?

This is the work of archaeobotany and archaeological residue chemistry, joined to careful excavation and historical research. It has made ancient food and drink accessible to scientific investigation even where recipes, written accounts, and organic remains are missing. But the methods do not produce a complete ancient shopping list. A chemical signal may belong to a food, a waterproofing treatment, later reuse, or contamination introduced during burial or excavation. The challenge is to discriminate among these histories.

We will visit three case studies: fermented beverages at the Neolithic village of Jiahu in China; imported black pepper at the Roman Red Sea port of Berenike in Egypt; and oil and resin residues in ancient Mediterranean transport amphorae. We will meet Patrick McGovern, Juzhong Zhang, Jigen Tang, Zhijun Zhao, René Cappers, Roberta Tomber, Steven Sidebotham, Alessandra Pecci, Davide Tanasi, and the research groups whose work connects these objects to chemical and botanical evidence. The deeper theme is how a small, reproducible measurement becomes a historical claim without pretending to say more than it can.

## Why the jar looks empty

Pottery is fired clay. Depending on its composition and manufacturing temperature, it contains minute pores and cracks. When a vessel holds a liquid or oily material, compounds can penetrate its inner surface. Over time, many of those compounds decay or change. Water-soluble substances may be washed away. Other compounds can become trapped within the ceramic fabric and survive burial. A residue may therefore be invisible to the naked eye and still be measurable in the laboratory.

Imagine one jar repeatedly used for cooking meat and another for storing plant oil. Both might accumulate fatty substances in their walls. A third might have been lined with pine resin to make it less permeable. Chemical analysis can help characterize the residues, but it rarely discovers a perfect single molecule that uniquely names one food. The compounds that survive depend on what was present, the temperature and oxygen during use, how long it was buried, whether the vessel was reused, and how researchers sampled it.

The archaeologist first records the vessel's excavation context. Was it in a tomb, a storeroom, a kitchen, a workshop, or a ship's cargo? Was it sealed, broken, or mixed with material from later occupations? Were there visible charred deposits or coatings? A small fragment cut from the interior of a vessel can be analyzed, but that choice damages an original object. The location and size of the sample should be documented. Control samples from surrounding sediment, exterior surfaces, and laboratory blanks may help identify contamination.

One can imagine a bad analysis: someone handles a potsherd with bare, oily fingers, stores it in a plastic bag with a modern adhesive label, then scrapes off a surface deposit and reports plant oil. The laboratory may have measured real organic molecules, but the chain linking those molecules to antiquity has been compromised. A good analysis starts with the collection and treatment history of the object, not with the instrument at the end of the process.

## What the machines actually measure

One widely used technique is gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry, often abbreviated G C M S. A laboratory extracts organic compounds from a sample, chemically prepares them where needed, and introduces them into an instrument. Gas chromatography separates compounds according to how they move through a narrow column under controlled conditions. The mass spectrometer then helps identify them by their molecular masses and fragmentation patterns. Researchers compare the signals with standards, published spectra, and expected mixtures.

The result is a pattern of peaks, not a printed sentence saying 'olive oil.' Some peaks may correspond to fatty acids present in many biological materials. Others may indicate a particular family of plant resins or degradation products. A laboratory can use the combination, relative abundance, and preservation state of molecules to distinguish among interpretations. In certain circumstances, isotope ratios can add information about the biological or environmental origin of compounds, though the meaning depends on the reference data and sample history.

No analytical method works independently of archaeological reasoning. Oleic and palmitic acids can occur in many foods. A resin marker may tell us that a vessel was coated or that resin was added as an ingredient. A surviving trace may reflect the last use of the pot more strongly than its main use during earlier years. Some compounds are preserved preferentially while others disappear. The absence of a highly perishable molecule does not prove that a substance was never present.

Laboratory papers therefore compare multiple observations. Pot shape supplies another clue: a narrow-necked jar is better suited to liquids than to storing a large sack of grain, though that is not definitive. Residues on an interior lining may differ from residues in pores beneath it. Charring may suggest heating. Associated tools and plant remains can test whether the inferred use fits the archaeological setting. The strongest claim emerges when chemistry, context, and independent evidence point in the same direction.

## A drink from Jiahu

Jiahu is an early Neolithic site in Henan province, China. Its inhabitants lived thousands of years before written records about their society. Archaeologists have recovered graves, houses, pottery, musical instruments, and other evidence of a complex community. The site's organic residues attracted the attention of an interdisciplinary team investigating ancient fermentation.

In 2004, a study led by Patrick McGovern with Juzhong Zhang, Jigen Tang, Zhijun Zhao, and numerous Chinese and international collaborators reported chemical evidence for an early mixed fermented beverage. The team examined residues absorbed into pottery from Jiahu, dated to the seventh millennium before the common era. Their interpretation involved rice, honey, and fruit such as hawthorn or grape. The fruit attribution remains provisional: the fruit source was not determined with the same certainty as one would identify a modern ingredient from a sealed labeled bottle.

The researchers used analytical comparisons involving infrared spectroscopy and chromatographic methods. They compared ancient extracts with reference materials representing different possible ingredients and mixtures. The archaeological vessels' forms and contexts helped shape the interpretations, but those contextual clues were not equivalent to chemical proof. The original article itself discussed different levels of inference, from measured similarities to reconstructed uses of jars and vats.

What would it take to call the liquid fermented? Fermentation produces a variety of chemical and biological changes, and some resulting compounds may be preserved as residues. Historical precedents and comparative chemistry can make a fermented mixture a plausible interpretation. Yet we cannot recover the original microbe, recipe, flavor, alcohol concentration, or serving occasion simply by identifying a handful of residual compounds. The Jiahu result provides evidence for early beverage production; it does not give us a reproducible ancient brewing manual.

The study also examined residues from later, much younger Chinese bronze vessels, with interpretations involving rice or millet-based fermented drinks and aromatic additives. Those results belong to different periods and social settings. We should not merge them with the Neolithic Jiahu finding into one continuous, unchanged Chinese beverage tradition. Technologies and ingredients could transform over millennia. The common ground is the research method: ancient vessels can preserve traces that written texts do not mention.

## How scientists avoid inventing a recipe

Suppose a sample produces signals consistent with beeswax and a particular fruit-derived compound. Does that prove that someone mixed honey and fruit in the vessel? Not necessarily. Beeswax can enter as a container coating; plant products can arrive through reuse. The components could come from separate episodes. If multiple vessels from the same period and setting show similar patterns, and their shapes suit storing liquids, a repeated production practice becomes a stronger explanation. But even then the precise combination of ingredients may remain underdetermined.

It is useful to distinguish four steps. First, the laboratory observes molecules or spectral patterns. Second, researchers assign those patterns to chemical compounds with differing confidence. Third, they propose candidate biological sources: an oilseed, a tree resin, beeswax, or a fermented mixture. Fourth, they propose a human activity, such as brewing or storage. At each step new assumptions enter, and alternatives may survive. A scholarly article should make the chain clear enough that another laboratory can repeat the measurement and challenge the interpretation.

The Jiahu study is an effective example because its conclusion is exciting and its research design is open to close reading. It credits the scientists whose expertise lay in chemical analysis, archaeological context, plant identification, and early Chinese material culture. Without the excavation and dating of Jiahu, the measured compounds would have no secure historical setting. Without the chemical comparisons, a claim about the jar's contents would remain speculative. The question 'How do we know?' has a composite answer.

## Pepper in the desert

The next case is strikingly different. At Berenike, a port on Egypt's Red Sea coast used during the Hellenistic and Roman periods, researchers found black peppercorns preserved as botanical remains. One Indian-made storage jar contained approximately seven and a half kilograms of black pepper. Here the object is not an invisible molecular signature: it is a mass of recognizable plant material, recovered from an archaeological context.

Archaeobotanist René Cappers published a detailed study of the plant remains from Berenike, *Roman Foodprints at Berenike*, in 2006. The project involved excavations conducted by teams including Steven Sidebotham, Willeke Wendrich, and numerous specialists. Its botanical assemblages recorded a broad range of cultivated plants and possible imports. Roberta Tomber and other scholars investigated ceramics and the wider geography of Red Sea and Indian Ocean exchange. Together the findings made the port legible as part of a commercial world reaching well beyond Egypt.

Here the botanical identification is relatively direct. Black pepper belongs to *Piper nigrum*, a plant native to South Asia, especially associated historically with parts of the Indian subcontinent. A collection of characteristic peppercorns, compared with botanical reference specimens, indicates the presence of the commodity itself. An Indian storage jar gives additional clues about the container's origin and intended use. This is a stronger identification of the traded plant than a vague chemical residue compatible with several spices.

Still, what exactly do the peppercorns prove? They establish that pepper reached Berenike. They make trade involving South Asia an obvious explanation, strengthened by texts, ceramics, and other finds. They do not identify the name of the merchant, the ship, the number of ports at which the jar changed hands, or the exact plantation where the plants grew. Material origins, commercial routes, and individual journeys are different historical questions.

The anonymous author of the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* described commercial routes and goods circulating through the Red Sea and Indian Ocean in the early centuries of the common era. This text is valuable because it names ports, trading arrangements, and commodities. But it has its own perspective: a merchant-oriented account of selected routes at a particular time. Archaeology checks the account against material evidence, while also finding plants and practices that an author may not have considered worth recording.

## A port as a botanical archive

René Cappers's botanical work identified many cultivated plant species in Berenike's archaeological deposits. Some represented local or regional provisioning. Others pointed toward imported goods. The challenge is to separate plants grown near the port from those arriving from elsewhere and to distinguish food, medicinal materials, fodder, fuel, and accidental seeds.

Plant geography helps but must be used carefully. A species that originated in one region may have been cultivated far beyond its original range by the Roman period. A seed from a port does not necessarily reveal where it was harvested. For each species, researchers consider its ecological requirements, historical cultivation, associated containers, and parallels at other sites. Botanical evidence can demonstrate that a commodity was present while leaving its immediate supply source uncertain.

Now imagine excavating a rubbish layer that contains pepper, date stones, cereals, and fragments of shipping containers. Can we infer that a single household ate all those foods together? Not from that assemblage alone. A port's refuse may aggregate many activities: preparation of meals, transshipment, disposal of damaged cargo, animal feeding, and cleanup of storage areas. Several centuries of use may be compressed into what first appears as one mound. Dating and spatial analysis are needed before artifacts can be associated with particular people or practices.

In Berenike, this methodological caution is particularly useful. Imported pepper might be consumed locally, repackaged for onward transport, or discarded after spoilage. The port served as a transfer point where goods crossed between ocean shipping and inland routes toward the Nile. The existence of a spectacular pepper deposit indicates a commodity's presence in substantial quantity, but it does not tell us how ordinary an Egyptian resident's meal was or what price that person paid.

## The jars raised from the sea

Across the Mediterranean, transport amphorae carried wine, oil, fish products, and other goods. Their shapes, clay fabrics, makers' stamps, and distribution patterns have long been used by archaeologists to study trade. An amphora shape may be strongly associated with a particular product, but not every jar contains a surviving label, and containers can be reused. Chemistry can test whether traditional typological identifications hold up at particular sites.

In 2021, Davide Tanasi, Enrico Greco, Filippo Pisciotta, and Stephan Hassam published analyses of organic residues from Late Roman amphorae held at the archaeological museum in Marsala, Sicily. They used chemical methods including gas chromatography and mass spectrometry to investigate residues linked to olive oil and resins. The study shows how analytical chemistry can be applied to assemblages recovered from underwater contexts, while acknowledging that interpretations of original contents must consider conservation and reuse.

A different project by Alessandra Pecci, Miguel Ángel Cau Ontiveros, and Nicolas Garnier investigated residues from Roman and Late Antique plastered production vats. The researchers sought to distinguish installations associated with processing wine from those used for oil. This is an important complement to the amphora studies. A jar is a transport or storage object; a vat is part of an installation. The location, construction, and likely processing sequence help evaluate the chemical results.

Consider an oil-like signal in a transport jar. It might indicate olive oil cargo. But some jars were coated with resin, and some were reused for different commodities. Oil from cooking or modern conservation products might contribute molecules. If the sampling reveals plant-oil markers distributed inside porous ceramic, while controls show little contamination and the jar type is consistent with oil transport, the interpretation strengthens. If similar residues occur in a modern adhesive used during restoration, the evidence must be reassessed.

Resin creates another ambiguity. A resin-lined jar used for wine may contain conspicuous chemical markers from pine resin, while little identifiable wine residue survives. Resin was applied as a sealant and could also affect taste. If we identify pine-derived compounds, that alone does not prove that the vessel carried pine resin as a commodity. The residue could be part of the container's construction or maintenance. This is why archaeological chemistry should sample coatings and absorbed contents separately where possible.

## The problem of reuse

A pottery jar can have a career. It may be manufactured for shipping oil, then reused for water, finally broken and incorporated into a floor. Its exterior might bear soot from heating, while its interior contains older absorbed fats. Different layers of residue may preserve different stages. Even a sealed archaeological context does not make the vessel's entire use history simple.

The same is true of shipwreck cargoes. An amphora recovered from a wreck may have been loaded in one port and made in another. Its fabric could be traced to a production region through petrography or elemental analysis, while its contents originated elsewhere. A trader may have reused a container after the original product was sold. Identifying where the jar was fired is therefore different from identifying where the stored oil or wine was produced. It is possible for both answers to be correct and different.

The most informative studies bring together multiple specialists. A ceramicist examines fabric and manufacture. A botanist identifies seeds or pollen. A chemist characterizes residues. An excavator documents context. A historian reads inscriptions and commercial texts. Each contribution limits the interpretations available to the others. An old typological label might be corrected by residue chemistry; a surprising chemical signal might turn out to be a modern conservation coating known to the curator.

## A test you could design

Imagine that a museum asks whether a group of amphorae carried olive oil. You have five jars from the same shipwreck, and access to fragments suitable for sampling. What would you do? You would first document the jars' excavation and conservation histories. You would sample interiors carefully, ideally at multiple positions where ethical and conservation constraints permit. You would take blanks and compare exterior surfaces or surrounding deposits when appropriate. You would analyze the extracts against reference compounds and examine whether the observed mixture is compatible with degraded olive oil or could arise from other fats and coatings.

You would not ask the mass spectrometer to declare a verdict without context. You would compare the chemical data with amphora forms, visible lining materials, stamps, and the shipwreck assemblage. If the jars had been cleaned with modern oils, you would flag that problem. If two jars showed oil-compatible residues and three were inconclusive, you would report that pattern instead of claiming that the entire cargo was olive oil. A negative result can mean poor preservation or a sampling problem; it is not always proof of an empty vessel.

The method is systematic because another laboratory could test the same samples or a new sample of the same object. If the scientific conclusion changes, the old measurement and the new measurement should be linked to the exact artifact and sampling location. This is where source attribution becomes more than naming famous researchers. It preserves a pathway from an assertion about ancient trade back to an object, a measurement, and the people who made it.

## From an ingredient to a world history

Food is a remarkable historical subject because it joins intimate and global scales. A person grinds grain in one household. A craft worker lines a jar with resin. Farmers harvest pepper in one region, merchants carry it through ports, and a household or institution consumes it far away. These actions connect labor, environment, technology, taste, and politics. Archaeology can recover fragments of that system without preserving the participants' conversations.

Jiahu lets us investigate fermented beverages through chemical signatures that survive within pottery. Berenike lets us see a traded spice directly in a botanical assemblage. The Mediterranean amphora studies show how chemistry can test hypotheses about containers and their use histories. Each case has a different kind of evidential strength. The peppercorn is a recognizable physical object; the Jiahu interpretation involves several steps of chemical and contextual inference; the amphora results often concern mixtures of contents, coatings, and later alterations.

These cases also illustrate the history of research. Patrick McGovern's collaboration with Chinese archaeologists brought scientific chemistry to vessels whose cultural and chronological context had been established through excavation. René Cappers made an extensive botanical catalog of Berenike, which allows later scholars to reassess the origins and uses of plants. Davide Tanasi's and Alessandra Pecci's teams tested what the chemical residues of containers and production facilities could reveal. Their published methods and sampling decisions are part of the knowledge we inherit.

What remains uncertain can be stated precisely. We may know that a jar contained plant-derived compounds compatible with a fermented drink, but not its exact recipe. We may know that black pepper reached a Red Sea port, but not the path of every shipment. We may know that an amphora once held oil, but not whether the oil was its only cargo over a long working life. The uncertainties do not diminish the discoveries. They tell us what questions the evidence can answer and how additional samples might improve the answer.

Next time you look at an ancient jar in a museum, imagine the many things that have happened to it. A potter selected and fired its clay. Someone filled it, emptied it, perhaps reused it. It entered the ground or sea, where water and chemistry altered what remained. Archaeologists recorded its position; conservators stabilized it; laboratory workers extracted and measured compounds; specialists compared those compounds with modern reference materials. The vessel has become historical evidence through all those linked acts of observation.

A small residue can connect a household to a continent, but only if we preserve the difference between what was measured, what was identified, and what people may have done. That distinction is the central discipline of asking how we know the ancient world.
