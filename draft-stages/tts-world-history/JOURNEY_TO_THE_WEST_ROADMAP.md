# Journey to the West — late-imperial Chinese literature

`Journey to the West` belongs to the **late-imperial Chinese literature** browsing
section within the World History audio track. The historical Xuanzang traveled
in the seventh century; the surviving complete hundred-chapter novel is
attested by the 1592 Ming Shidetang printing. Placing the novel in an ancient
literature section would conflate those dates. Keeping the series within World
History still permits comparisons with older epic and religious traditions.

All seven entries below are **substantive, listening-ready manuscripts**, not
prompts or episode outlines. The six literary lectures tell the novel from its
opening through the return with scriptures. The seventh examines the historical
pilgrimage, earlier story cycles, textual witnesses, authorship, and reception.
Every episode is standalone in its spoken introduction and independently useful
if selected out of order. Each should run at least 30 minutes at 145 words per
minute; the manifest contains actual extracted spoken counts and estimates.

## Evidence and version convention

- **Historical journey:** Xuanzang's travels (commonly dated 629–645) and
  translation activity are known from Tang-period records and religious
  biography. *Da Tang Xiyu ji*, produced after the return with Bianji's
  participation, and the biography associated with Huili and Yancong are
  relevant historical witnesses. They have their own perspectives and cannot
  corroborate the novel's supernatural adventures.
- **Novel:** follow the familiar hundred-chapter narrative using the complete
  revised translation of Anthony C. Yu and the corresponding Chinese texts as
  reference points. Plot narration is original English prose; do not copy
  modern translated paragraphs. The Shidetang edition is the earliest
  surviving complete printed witness, dated 1592 and anonymous.
- **Variants:** the monk's Chen Guangrui family story, commonly placed in
  chapter 9 of modern editions, is not present in the same way in the 1592
  witness. Where a lecture describes it, it identifies that edition problem.
  Do not assume a modern collated chapter count reproduces the first printing
  leaf for leaf.
- **Authorship:** Wu Cheng'en is a conventional attribution. The early printed
  edition does not identify him, and the later evidence is insufficient to
  treat his authorship as certain.
- **Interpretation:** discuss allegory, Daoist cultivation, Buddhist scripture,
  family ethics, satire of institutions, and narrative form when they illuminate
  concrete episodes. Distinguish plausible readings from documented facts about
  publication, travel, and editions. Do not force a single allegorical cipher
  onto the entire work.
- **Reception:** stage/film/game adaptations are evidence about later creative
  history. Avoid letting their scenes or character designs overwrite what is
  actually in the hundred-chapter novel.

## Narration design

The main pleasure is the story: people make choices, events develop, and
consequences accumulate. Give listeners enough detail to picture each major
scene and understand why the characters' choices are difficult. Analysis should
grow out of particular scenes; it should not replace them with abstract themes.
When historical evidence is central to an interpretation, explain it within the
narration in plain language. Keep edition citations, publication specifics and
reading links outside the spoken markers. The historian's methods receive
longer treatment in 054000.

Present Xuanzang, Sun Wukong, Zhu Bajie, Sha Wujing, Guanyin, Laozi, and other
figures with their alternate English names when useful. Translations vary:
Monkey/Sun Wukong, Pigsy/Zhu Bajie, Sandy/Sha Wujing, Tripitaka/Tang Sanzang.
Explain titles once per lecture, then favor stable forms. Be careful with
words such as immortality, nirvana, emptiness, and enlightenment; these are
not interchangeable concepts.

The table is complete as a first pass across the full novel. Further essays

only when the finished manuscript has enough episode-specific analysis and
historical material to support its full length. No placeholder lectures are

## Research anchors

The individual metadata sidecars reference the exact source IDs in
`metadata/sources.json` and appear as reading links in the audio reader.
Useful starting points are Yu's revised four-volume translation, Max Deeg's
study of Chinese Buddhist travelers, the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy
entry on Xuanzang, Yu's research on the formation of the novel, the University
of Washington scholarship on Chinese fiction commentaries, and Hongmei Sun's
work on Monkey's adaptations. Chapter summaries are navigation aids and are
not substitutes for reading the novel.
