# Where Did They Grow Up? Isotopes, Teeth, and Ancient Mobility

Imagine excavating a burial beside a prehistoric monument. The bones identify a human being, but there is no inscription naming the person. The body was buried with objects associated with one archaeological tradition. Did this person grow up nearby? Did they travel hundreds of miles? Were the grave goods made locally or carried from elsewhere? A familiar historical method might begin with the style of pottery or the form of a tool and assign the burial to a cultural group. But objects move. A person can learn a craft, acquire a foreign vessel, or inherit a treasured object without ever leaving home.

Now imagine that the person's teeth contain another record. As teeth formed in childhood, their enamel incorporated tiny amounts of chemical elements from food and water. Some of those elements exist as different isotopes, atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. In certain circumstances, isotopic measurements preserve information about the environment in which the person grew up. Archaeologists can compare that signal with mapped environmental variation and ask whether the individual consumed food and water consistent with the place where they were buried.

This lecture follows two cases near Stonehenge in southern Britain. The first concerns people known as the Boscombe Bowmen, whose teeth suggested changes of location during childhood. The second concerns cremated remains from Stonehenge itself, where a research team developed a way to examine mobility even though ordinary bone chemistry had been altered by burning. Along the way we will credit researchers including Jane Evans, Carolyn Chenery, Andrew Fitzpatrick, Christophe Snoeck, Christie Willis, Julia Lee-Thorp, Rick Schulting, and their collaborators. The names are important because different teams asked different questions and developed distinct parts of the evidence.

## Atoms as environmental witnesses

Start with strontium, an element chemically similar enough to calcium that small amounts can enter bones and teeth. Several naturally occurring isotopes of strontium are stable over archaeological timescales. One, strontium eighty-seven, is also produced over immense geological timescales by radioactive decay of rubidium eighty-seven. Rocks with different ages and geological histories can therefore have different ratios of strontium eighty-seven to strontium eighty-six.

That ratio is not a personal barcode. It is a feature of the geological environment. Weathering releases strontium into soils and water. Plants take it up, animals eat the plants, and humans consume plants, animals, and water. In favorable circumstances the isotope ratio incorporated into a person's tissues reflects the biologically available strontium of their food environment. The underlying bedrock contributes, but so can transported sediments, fertilizers in modern reference data, sea spray, and food brought from elsewhere.

Why are teeth so useful? The mineralized enamel of a tooth forms during a particular period of childhood or adolescence and undergoes little normal remodeling after it finishes forming. If the enamel survives without major alteration after burial, it may preserve a chemical signal from that formative period. Different teeth form at different stages of life. A premolar and a wisdom tooth can therefore provide partially different windows into an individual's upbringing.

Bone behaves differently. In living people bone is constantly remodeled. A bone's chemical composition may represent an average over years rather than a narrow childhood interval, and after burial its porous structure can exchange material with its surroundings. For many archaeological questions, tooth enamel has therefore been preferred for reconstructing childhood origins. But enamel does not necessarily record the location of birth, nor does it identify the individual's birthplace down to a village. It records dietary exposure during the time that enamel was forming.

Oxygen isotopes add another line of evidence. Water contains oxygen atoms of different masses, and the proportions can vary with evaporation, precipitation, altitude, temperature, and other environmental processes. Patterns of drinking water often differ among regions. Oxygen isotope measurements from enamel can help distinguish environments that have overlapping strontium signatures. The two methods are not interchangeable, because oxygen also depends on physiology, food preparation, and water sources. Combining them is useful because they respond to partly different environmental processes.

## A map needs a baseline

Suppose an archaeologist measures a strontium ratio in a prehistoric tooth and obtains a number around zero point seven one three. What does that number tell us? Without environmental reference data, not much. To identify possible places of upbringing, the researcher must know what ratios were biologically available in the relevant landscapes. That means measuring or compiling data from plants, water, animals, and other contextual samples, with attention to how land use and environmental conditions have changed.

The mapping problem is harder than drawing geological boundaries on a map. Two different rock formations may release similar biologically available strontium. An ancient river may have carried sediments from elsewhere. Imported food can alter the diet signal. A coastal community may obtain substantial strontium from marine sources, reducing the apparent distinction between some inland geological areas. This is why a measured match seldom supports the claim that an individual came from one exact location. It often supports a set of compatible regions.

Here's a simple thought experiment. Imagine three regions. Region A, where the burial was found, has a tight local distribution around one value. Region B has a clearly higher range. Region C, hundreds of miles away, overlaps with B. A person whose enamel matches B but not A can reasonably be described as having consumed food from a different kind of geological environment. But choosing B over C requires additional evidence. Geography alone might make B more plausible; the archaeological network may favor another route. The isotope measurement narrows the possibilities without finishing the historical argument.

A related complication is that childhood can involve travel between environments. Suppose a family spends each summer in the hills and each winter in a valley. The enamel forming during those years may integrate food from both. Or a child in a town may eat grain grown in a distant region. The resulting chemical signal would not necessarily match the geology immediately beneath the house. A careful analysis is therefore about patterns of consumption and mobility, which archaeologists interpret in terms of possible residence. Human movement is not identical to the movement of atoms through a food supply.

## The Boscombe Bowmen

Near Stonehenge, excavations at Boscombe Down recovered a collective Beaker-period burial containing several people. Flint arrowheads contributed to the label Boscombe Bowmen. The archaeological setting provided a date range and cultural context, but the burials themselves raised questions about the individuals' origins. The Bronze Age saw the movement of people, objects, practices, and ideas across Britain and continental Europe. How could researchers separate local and nonlocal life histories?

In a 2006 paper in *Archaeometry*, Jane Evans, Carolyn Chenery, and Andrew Fitzpatrick analyzed strontium and oxygen isotopes from tooth enamel of individuals buried near Stonehenge. For three Boscombe Down adults they compared enamel from teeth that formed at different childhood stages. The results suggested a shared pattern: early childhood signals came from a more radiogenic strontium environment than signals recorded later in development. The authors considered western Britain, including Wales, among the possible origin regions. Other places with suitable geology could not be ruled out.

Notice what makes the result stronger than a single unfamiliar number. First, the authors had more than one tooth per individual. Second, different teeth represented a sequence in childhood rather than a single snapshot. Third, several adults showed a similar pattern. Fourth, oxygen isotope evidence supplied another environmental comparison. Together those observations made childhood movement a reasonable explanation, while leaving questions about exact locations and family relations unresolved.

There is a temptation to promote the evidence into a vivid biography. One could imagine three brothers arriving in Wessex to help build Stonehenge after growing up in Wales. But the chemistry does not tell us they were brothers, that they traveled together, or that they constructed the monument. The Boscombe burials date to a Beaker-period context, later than important early phases of Stonehenge. A story that merges all prehistoric activity around the monument into one scene would erase chronology as well as uncertainty.

The same study included people from other nearby burials whose dental chemistry was consistent with spending childhood in a local isotope environment. This contrast was useful. A method that declared everybody a migrant would be suspicious. Instead, the researchers found variation: some sampled individuals showed changes consistent with movement, others did not. That difference can help historians understand a landscape in which some people moved and others remained.

## The problem of burned remains

Stonehenge was also used for cremation burials. Cremation changes human remains by exposing bone to high temperatures. It can destroy organic components and alter or recrystallize mineral structures. Researchers long considered ordinary isotopic approaches to burned bone difficult because heat and burial could disturb the original chemical signal. Moreover, the individuals buried in the monument were not a random sample of everyone in the surrounding landscape. Their selection for burial may have reflected status, kinship, ritual roles, or other social factors we cannot directly observe.

Christophe Snoeck developed and tested methods to study strontium isotopes in fully cremated, or calcined, bone. Under appropriate burning conditions, the mineral structure can become resistant to later alteration and preserve useful strontium information. This work enabled a different sort of investigation from enamel analysis. Instead of sampling tooth formation in childhood, calcined bone may preserve a broader signal reflecting the final years of a person's life. That temporal difference is central, not a technical footnote.

In 2018, Snoeck, John Pouncett, Philippe Claeys, Steven Goderis, Nadine Mattielli, Mike Parker Pearson, Christie Willis, Antoine Zazzo, Julia Lee-Thorp, Rick Schulting, and colleagues published an open-access study of cremated remains from Stonehenge. The team analyzed samples representing twenty-five individuals. At least ten had strontium signatures incompatible with having obtained all their food for the relevant period from the Wessex chalk immediately around Stonehenge. The study considered west Wales a plausible source region for at least some, in the context of geological baselines and the already established Welsh sources of bluestones.

The phrase 'at least ten' needs attention. It refers to ten among the twenty-five people sampled, using the study's criteria. It does not mean that forty percent of all inhabitants of the region were migrants, or that forty percent of Stonehenge's builders came from Wales. The sampled remains were selected by the ancient burial process and shaped by subsequent excavation history. The chemistry describes those particular individuals under the method's assumptions.

## How a cremation study becomes possible

Several steps stand between an excavated bone fragment and that conclusion. Researchers must identify fragments representing different individuals to avoid inadvertently counting one person twice. They must assess burning conditions and preservation. Infrared spectroscopy can help determine whether the bone is fully calcined. Sample preparation must avoid contamination. A mass spectrometer measures strontium isotope ratios to high precision, while reference measurements map biologically available strontium in candidate regions.

At Stonehenge, the team used modern plant samples from west Wales and existing environmental data to compare the Wessex chalk and areas with older, more radiogenic geological formations. The two ranges differed enough to make some local and nonlocal classifications possible. But their maps also showed areas of intermediate values, and other parts of Britain or beyond could resemble the highest readings. A number can be outside the local range without uniquely naming west Wales.

The original excavation history matters here. Remains from Stonehenge's Aubrey Holes were investigated by William Hawley in the early twentieth century. Some were later reburied, and subsequent archaeological projects recovered and reexamined portions of this material. Mike Parker Pearson's research team contributed to that broader archaeological context. Researchers must track which fragments came from which deposits and what reburial or handling they underwent. A mass spectrometer cannot repair an undocumented context, but good records can establish which comparisons are justified.

The 2018 authors also studied carbon and oxygen measurements in the calcined bone. The carbon signal can partly reflect the wood used as fuel in a cremation pyre, rather than the person's lifetime diet. Some observations were consistent with differences in the environments from which pyre fuel came, raising the possibility that bodies were burned in one region and remains moved to Stonehenge. But possibilities of that kind demand care: a transported bag of cremated bone and the migration of a living person are distinct historical processes. Chemical results may point toward both and can be ambiguous between them.

One practical outcome of the study is that an old class of evidence became newly informative. Cremated remains, previously difficult to use for some mobility questions, could be examined using validated mineral chemistry. The research did not merely add a geographical label to an ancient person. It altered what investigators could ask of a museum collection or excavated cemetery. Equally importantly, it showed why sample history and the chemistry of burning must be understood before trusting the numbers.

## What is an isotope ratio really saying?

A laboratory result often appears on a graph as a dot. It may have error bars small enough to seem negligible. There are at least three different uncertainties behind that dot. One is the measurement uncertainty: how precisely did the instrument determine the ratio? Another is environmental variation: how representative is the mapped baseline for the food and water actually consumed? The third is historical interpretation: what pattern of residence or travel could have produced that signal?

The instrument may measure the ratio with excellent precision while the historical location remains broad. This is not a contradiction. If five regions share a similar geological signature, the chemistry cannot identify one by being measured more precisely. More precise numbers are most helpful when candidate sources differ within the instrument's uncertainty. At some point the major limitation becomes the environmental map or the range of histories compatible with the observation.

A second subtlety concerns mixing. Imagine someone who spends half a period eating food from chalk country and the other half consuming food grown on older rocks. The strontium incorporated into bone may be intermediate, but that value is not necessarily the simple arithmetic average of two regional ratios. It depends on the quantity of strontium delivered by each component of the diet and how tissues incorporate it. One region's food can contribute more strontium than the same mass of food from another. Models of mixture require concentrations as well as isotope ratios, and the time over which tissues respond.

A third subtlety concerns who can be sampled. A burial ground contains people whom a society chose to bury in that place and manner. A monumental tomb might select a political elite, a family, an age group, or people with particular ritual roles. Studies of the dead may therefore reveal mobility within that selected group, not a census of everyone who lived in the area. Care with percentages is as important as care with laboratory equipment.

## The laboratory's chain of custody

It is worth lingering on what happens after the excavator hands a tooth to an isotope laboratory. The visible instrument is usually at the end of a process. Researchers first document the specimen and decide whether it can be sampled. They may photograph it, inspect the enamel, and identify regions where a small amount of powder can be removed with minimal damage. Dentine and enamel are different materials with different post-burial vulnerabilities, so a protocol designed for one does not automatically work for the other.

The powder must be cleaned and chemically prepared. Mineral samples may need to be dissolved, and the element under investigation separated from others that could interfere with its measurement. Laboratories use blanks, reference materials, standards, and repeated analyses to detect contamination or instrument drift. A final ratio is useful only because the procedure that produced it is documented and checked. The same raw specimen processed carelessly could return a compelling-looking number that primarily reflects soil, handling, or a reagent.

An investigator also needs to think about sampling strategy before any powder is removed. If a grave contains ten individuals but the laboratory has resources for three samples, which three? Choosing the most dramatic artifacts could bias the results toward a privileged group. Choosing the best-preserved teeth may select for particular burial conditions. Sampling all available individuals might be more representative of the excavated cemetery but still not of the original population. Research design shapes what can be inferred even when every laboratory measurement is correct.

Data presentation is another source of potential distortion. A colored map may assign each person's result to the single nearest region, hiding the fact that several regions are equally compatible. A shaded probability surface is more informative, but only if the assumptions behind it are explained. An impressive visual cannot create geographic precision that the reference data lack. When researchers report a compatible origin, good work makes the competing possibilities inspectable.

Finally, replicate measurements from another laboratory can help establish that the chemical result is robust. That does not automatically validate a migration story. Two labs might agree perfectly on an isotope ratio and both be unable to decide between Wales and another old-rock landscape. Reproducibility of a measurement and discrimination among historical hypotheses are separate achievements. The chain from excavation through the laboratory and back to interpretation must be complete for the published claim to be evaluated.

## Connecting people and stones

Stonehenge presents an appealing overlap between two kinds of provenance. Geological work identifies sources of some of the monument's bluestones in western Wales, particularly the Preseli region. Isotope studies of human remains find that some individuals buried at Stonehenge have signatures compatible with western British landscapes. It is natural to place those findings side by side and imagine an interconnected community moving people and stones.

That reconstruction is plausible, but the types of evidence are not equivalent. A rock can be compared directly with geological outcrops through mineralogical and geochemical analysis. A person's bone records food environments over a period of life. The people whose remains were later buried at Stonehenge need not have transported the stones; some may have moved there for unrelated reasons. Others might have died elsewhere and had their remains carried to the monument. The observations together support connections, but they do not establish a named workforce or a specific itinerary.

It is useful to reverse the reasoning. Suppose the bluestone sources were unknown. The human isotope measurements alone would suggest that some individuals were connected to regions outside the local chalk landscape. Once independent geological work identified Welsh stone sources, the combined record made west Wales a compelling candidate. The human evidence did not originate from an assumption that everyone at Stonehenge was Welsh. The context changed how the probabilities were interpreted, and it remains possible that additional evidence will change them again.

There are ethical questions as well. Human remains are not merely chemical samples. They may have descendant communities, religious significance, or complicated collection histories. Removing enamel or bone for analysis can damage the very material that preserves evidence for future researchers. Permissions, sampling justification, consultation, and records of destructive methods belong in the research process. A valuable historical question does not eliminate the obligation to treat people and collections responsibly.

## A more precise kind of biography

We began with an anonymous burial and a question about childhood. The archaeological context tells us when and how a person was buried. Tooth enamel may tell us about geological environments during childhood. Calcined bone may record food environments during later life, under particular preservation conditions. Oxygen isotopes may add climatic or water-related information. Artifacts reveal actions and relationships that chemistry cannot read. Together, these strands can support a more detailed account of ancient mobility.

The researchers have earned distinct credits. Evans, Chenery, and Fitzpatrick demonstrated childhood changes among Boscombe Bowmen through enamel comparisons. Snoeck's methodological work made certain cremated bones scientifically accessible for provenance. The larger Stonehenge team combined that technique with burial records, geological baselines, radiocarbon dates, and the evidence for stone transport. None of those contributions can be reduced to the final colorful map of migration.

The method's best answer is often modest in geographical terms but rich historically. We may learn that a person did not spend an entire period of life within the local food environment, even if we cannot name their birthplace. We may detect a shared pattern among several individuals without knowing whether they were relatives. We may discover that cremated remains traveled while being unable to decide whether the person traveled before death. Those are substantive historical results. They narrow the space of possible histories and allow stronger questions to be asked of the next excavation.

A tooth is not a passport and an isotope ratio is not a home address. Their information depends on the landscape and the history of the sample. But the mineral record can preserve an aspect of a life that nobody thought to write down. Careful comparisons, independent archaeological context, and the work of named researchers turn that chemical trace into evidence about the movement of people thousands of years ago.

### Editorial cautions

- The Boscombe Bowmen and the earlier Stonehenge cremation burials are different samples from different periods. Do not combine them into a single migration event.
- The 2018 finding refers to at least 10 of 25 sampled cremated people; it does not estimate the origin of all Stonehenge builders.
- Strontium in food environments need not equal bedrock alone. Potential movement of food and cremated remains complicates historical interpretation.
