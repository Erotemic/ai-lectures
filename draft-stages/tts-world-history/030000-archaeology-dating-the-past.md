# Dating the Past: Clocks in Wood, Carbon, and Volcanic Ash

When an archaeologist tells you that a footprint is about three million six hundred thousand years old, what exactly has been measured? The footprint has no tiny clock embedded in it. The person who made it did not leave a date. The age was constructed by connecting a physical trace, a sequence of geological events, laboratory measurements, and the work of multiple researchers. Each link in that chain can be examined. And when the evidence changes, the date can change too.

That is the central problem of chronological archaeology. We can often establish that one event happened before another. Dating asks how to put those events on a calendar. The same methods do not work equally well everywhere. A log of wood from a desert dwelling, charcoal from a cooking fire, clay from an early Mesopotamian tablet, and footprints in hardened volcanic ash all preserve different sorts of information. To understand their dates, we need to understand the clocks and the people who learned to read them.

Let us start with a less ambitious claim. Suppose one wall cuts across a buried street. The street must have existed before the wall was constructed, assuming we have identified the relationship correctly. If a pit cuts the wall, the pit is later still. This is relative dating: we can order events without assigning a year to any of them. Relative chronology is powerful because physical relationships remain visible even when absolute dates are uncertain. Archaeologist Edward Harris's stratigraphic matrix formalized the ordering of excavated contexts in the 1970s. Its lines express a sequence of events, not a promise that we know when each event occurred.

A further complication appears when an object is older than the deposit in which it is found. An heirloom can survive for generations before burial. A fragment of old pottery may be dug out of an earlier layer and thrown into a younger pit. A charcoal sample could come from the heartwood of an ancient tree burned hundreds of years after the wood formed. The age of the material and the age of the activity we want to study are related questions, but they need not have the same answer. It takes archaeological reasoning to decide what the sample dates.

## A calendar made from trees

Some trees produce annual growth rings. In a suitable environment, a ring records a year's growth, and a sequence of wide and narrow rings reflects conditions experienced by the tree. Counting rings in one tree gives a local calendar backward from its outermost ring. Overlapping the patterns between a living tree and old beams or fallen trunks can extend that calendar farther into the past. This is dendrochronology, from the Greek roots for tree, time, and study.

One important architect of modern tree-ring dating was the American astronomer Andrew Ellicott Douglass. Interested initially in solar activity and climate, Douglass studied ring patterns in the American Southwest and, with archaeological collaborators, developed long cross-dated sequences that helped establish dates for ancient buildings. The methodological achievement was not merely counting rings in a single timber. It was showing how a distinctive sequence of narrow and wide rings could be matched between independently sampled trees, so that a timber from a building could be placed in a longer regional chronology.

Consider a roof beam from an ancient settlement. If the bark edge survives, and the terminal ring can be identified, the dendrochronologist may be able to say when the tree stopped growing. That is not automatically the date when the house was built. The wood might have been seasoned, reused, or stored. If several timbers from the same construction phase ended their growth in nearby years, and their archaeological positions make sense together, the building date becomes more secure. The result depends on agreement between ring patterns, building context, and the treatment of wood after cutting.

Tree rings have another role that is even more important for much of global archaeology. They allow us to check a different clock: radiocarbon. This produces a striking methodological loop. An astronomical investigation of trees ultimately helped researchers evaluate a nuclear-physics measurement of ancient organic matter. Such cross-checking is why the dates we hear in documentaries are considerably more than educated guesses.

## How carbon becomes a clock

Ordinary carbon includes several isotopes, atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Carbon twelve and carbon thirteen are stable. Carbon fourteen is radioactive. It is continually produced in the atmosphere, enters carbon dioxide, and passes through living things as plants grow and animals eat. After an organism dies, it no longer exchanges carbon with the environment in the same way. The carbon fourteen already present gradually decays. Its half-life is about five thousand seven hundred and thirty years. In simplified terms, after one half-life, roughly half the original radiocarbon remains.

Willard Frank Libby developed radiocarbon dating in the late 1940s at the University of Chicago, with essential contributions from colleagues including James Arnold and Ernest Anderson. The 1949 paper by Arnold and Libby, *Age Determinations by Radiocarbon Content: Checks with Samples of Known Age*, was particularly significant because it asked whether the proposed clock agreed with samples whose dates could be established independently. Libby received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960. A lecture that says merely that scientists discovered carbon dating hides this collaborative history and the experimental validation that made the method credible.

One of the samples in those early tests was wood associated with the tomb of the Egyptian king Djoser at Saqqara. That makes the experiment especially revealing. Egyptian archaeology, with its dynasties and inscriptions, offered a historical chronology against which a new physical dating method could be tested. The older historical record helped verify the new clock; the new clock would in turn help revise and compare chronologies for prehistoric societies that left fewer written dates.

In the laboratory, the measured quantity is related to the proportion of radiocarbon remaining in the sample. Modern methods often use accelerator mass spectrometry to count isotopes in extremely small samples. But laboratory precision alone cannot rescue a bad archaeological sample. Suppose charcoal is collected from a pit that was dug through an older occupation layer. If the charcoal actually comes from that older layer, its radiocarbon age may be correct while its use as a date for the pit is wrong. The physicist and the excavator are answering interconnected questions. Neither can replace the other's work.

Radiocarbon is also not the clock for everything. It is principally useful for materials that were once part of living organisms, and its practical range is on the order of fifty thousand years, depending on preservation and methods. A fired clay tablet is generally dated through context, associated organic material, and historical or typological evidence, rather than by carbon-dating the clay itself. A stone tool is not a piece of organic tissue. We can date charcoal in its secure layer, or volcanic material around it, without claiming that the stone contained its own radiocarbon clock.

## Why a radiocarbon year is not automatically a calendar year

The simplest account of carbon dating assumes that the atmosphere always contained the same proportion of carbon fourteen. Reality is less obliging. Radiocarbon production changes, and circulation through the atmosphere, oceans, and organisms is complex. The burning of fossil fuels and atmospheric nuclear testing have also altered modern levels. A laboratory's measured radiocarbon age therefore usually needs calibration to yield a range of calendar dates.

Researchers compare radiocarbon measurements with samples independently assigned calendar ages. Tree rings are especially useful: a ring in a securely cross-dated sequence gives a known year in which its carbon was incorporated. Measurements from many such rings build a curve relating radiocarbon ages to calendar years. Hans Suess and Hessel de Vries were among the researchers who documented variations in atmospheric radiocarbon and the effects of industrial carbon, helping establish why calibration was necessary. Modern internationally constructed calibration curves, including IntCal20, synthesize extensive datasets rather than relying on one laboratory or one tree.

Here is the consequence for interpretation. A carbon age of, say, four thousand radiocarbon years does not always become one exact calendar date by simple subtraction. The calibration curve can bend, flatten, or intersect a measured range in multiple places. The output may be a probability distribution over calendar years. Sometimes it has more than one peak. Researchers should present ranges with stated confidence or credibility conventions, not transform a broad distribution into a falsely precise year because one date sounds better in a story.

This is where the statistical treatment becomes part of the archaeological argument. Christopher Bronk Ramsey and other researchers developed computational approaches for combining radiocarbon measurements with archaeological ordering and other constraints. Oxford's radiocarbon work and the program OxCal are prominent examples. If a sample must belong after one excavated event and before another, that ordering can help refine a model. But the model's strength depends on the assumptions placed into it. A neat result can be obtained from bad constraints, and a careful report explains which chronological relationships were independently observed.

Why care about a dating range rather than one pleasing number? Imagine two villages that appear to have developed similar farming practices. If both dates have uncertainties spanning several centuries, we may be unable to say which practice came first. If a later discovery tightens one chronology, our hypothesis about cultural transmission may change. This is not a failure of science. It is the normal outcome of using measurements to answer a historical question with a finite amount of evidence.

## A different clock in volcanic rock

Return now to the footprints from Laetoli, in northern Tanzania. A few million years is far beyond the useful range of radiocarbon. Even if organic remains had survived, carbon fourteen decays so extensively over that timespan that ordinary archaeological radiocarbon dating would not solve the problem. The Laetoli footprint trails survived because ancient surfaces made of volcanic ash or tuff were laid down, walked upon, and buried. The scientific question is when those geological layers formed.

Potassium-argon dating works because radioactive potassium forty decays in part to argon forty. Potassium-bearing volcanic minerals can form in circumstances that set or reset the isotopic clock. After cooling, radiogenic argon accumulates. By measuring isotopes and considering the geological history, researchers can estimate the age of suitable volcanic rocks. The method is demanding: contamination, later alteration, inherited argon, or incorrectly associated layers can mislead the analysis. But its time range extends into the millions of years.

Mary Leakey's discoveries at Laetoli were carried out by a team. In 1976 Andrew Hill identified fossil animal tracks in the tuff. Peter Jones and Philip Leakey found possible early hominin footprints, and in 1978 Paul Abell recognized clear hominin impressions in a trail subsequently exposed and recorded. Mary Leakey directed the work and was central to its interpretation. In their 1982 *Scientific American* article, Mary Leakey and the geologist Richard Hay described both the discovery sequence and the geological setting. Naming these people is important because the trackway was found through observations by several researchers and many field workers, rather than through one sudden individual revelation.

Geological dating was a separate collaborative achievement. A 1976 paper in *Nature* by Mary Leakey, Richard Hay, G. H. Curtis, R. E. Drake, M. K. Jackes, and Tim White reported hominin fossils and potassium-argon dating for the Laetolil Beds. Their measurements constrained the age of fossil-bearing volcanic deposits. The famous track surfaces are commonly described as about three point six six million years old, with some summaries rounding to three point six million. A reader can trace that estimate to the stratigraphy and isotopic measurements, instead of treating the headline number as an inexplicable gift of technology.

The trackway also demonstrates the difference between dating and identifying its makers. Dating the tuff tells us when the footprints were made within the geological sequence. The shape of the impressions, their spacing, and associated fossils support arguments about locomotion and the likely kinds of hominins present. A 2021 reanalysis by Ellison McNutt and collaborators of the separate Laetoli site A prints reopened questions about locomotor diversity. That research did not make the stratigraphy irrelevant. It used newer three-dimensional methods to reconsider the biological interpretation of tracks within the established geological context.

## When clocks disagree

Suppose a site has a pottery sequence indicating one period, charcoal dates indicating a significantly older period, and an inscription naming a ruler associated with a later one. There is no rule that says the laboratory automatically wins or that the king's inscription automatically wins. The investigators must first ask whether the charcoal was residual, whether the pottery was reused, whether the inscription moved from an older structure, and whether their correlations were justified. They may discover that the apparently conflicting evidence belongs to several occupations that were initially grouped together.

Occasionally the conflict changes the wider field. A calibrated radiocarbon series can challenge an accepted periodization. The stratigraphy of a new excavation can demonstrate that a supposedly ancient style remained in use for a long time. A new edition of a king list may change an historical synchronism. These disagreements are productive when each kind of evidence is allowed to retain its particular meaning. The goal is a coherent chronology that explains the observations, rather than a victory for one technique.

The past also has many calendars. Ancient Egyptian years could be counted by reigns. Mesopotamian administrators used year names and regnal systems. Modern historians establish links between them using king lists, astronomical records, inscriptions, economic tablets, and cross-regional correspondences. An ancient source can be accurate within its own calendar and still require substantial reconstruction to assign a date in our common-era numbering. Some chronological conventions, especially deep Mesopotamian ones, have historically differed by decades or more. We should never assume every printed year came directly from a contemporary text.

There are also reasons to resist romanticizing scientific objectivity. Samples are chosen by human researchers. Funding and access determine which sites get examined. Some collections were removed under colonial conditions; some deposits have been destroyed by looting, construction, conflict, and erosion. If the most heavily sampled cities lie in one region and the poorly sampled settlements lie elsewhere, our apparent timeline of innovation can reflect an unequal research record. Dating techniques are powerful, but they operate inside archaeology's broader social and institutional history.

## The buried clock in a grain of sand

There is a class of archaeological dating questions that neither a tree ring nor radiocarbon solves well. Imagine a layer of windblown sand that accumulated around a prehistoric campsite. We want to know when the sand last lay in sunlight before being covered. The quartz grains are not organic, so radiocarbon cannot directly date them. But mineral crystals can store energy from natural environmental radiation. When sunlight reaches some of those grains, the stored signal is reset. After burial, radiation begins building it up again. In optically stimulated luminescence dating, scientists measure the accumulated signal and estimate how long the grains have been buried.

The method provides another illustration of the relationship between measurement and event. A date for the sediment can help place activity preserved within a layer, but people could have occupied a surface before or after the sand was deposited. A grain may have been only partly exposed to light before burial. A burrowing animal may introduce material from a younger level. The sample must be collected without exposing it to light that would reset the clock during the modern excavation. Specialists sometimes collect it in opaque tubes and process it under controlled illumination. The physical technique changes the field procedure: the method of dating determines how archaeologists must take care of the evidence.

Geoscientist Geoff Duller has written clear technical introductions to luminescence dating, and Historic England's scientific dating guidance explains how the method is selected and evaluated. Researchers who developed and improved quartz measurement procedures include Ann Wintle and Andrew Murray. Giving those people credit helps distinguish a usable tool from the generic phrase 'modern technology.' Luminescence offers dates for many contexts beyond the range of radiocarbon, yet it comes with its own uncertainties about the amount of radiation received and the resetting of the signal.

This is one reason an archaeological team may collect several different kinds of samples from one site. Charcoal dates a biological death or burning event. Luminescence may date the last exposure of sediment to daylight. Potassium-argon may date suitable volcanic material. A securely cross-dated timber can record the last preserved year of its growth. These are complementary observations, not competing brands of chronological truth. If their relative order is consistent with stratigraphy, our confidence in the site's history grows. If not, there is a research problem worth understanding.

## A bone, a fish, and the wrong century

Radiocarbon dates can also be affected by what an organism ate. Marine ecosystems exchange carbon with reservoirs that may have different apparent radiocarbon ages from the atmosphere. A person who consumed substantial marine foods may incorporate carbon with a different isotopic history from a neighboring person whose diet came mostly from terrestrial plants and animals. If we treat both samples as though their carbon came from an identical source, one burial could appear older than it really is. Similar issues occur in some freshwater systems. Archaeologists call these reservoir effects.

The solution is not to abandon the method. Researchers examine stable isotopes, local ecology, associated materials, and where possible terrestrial samples from the same context. Sometimes the interpretation is necessarily broad because diet cannot be reconstructed well enough. Imagine a coastal cemetery where one person's skeleton appears several centuries older than neighboring burials, but its grave goods and stratigraphic context agree with the others. Rather than inventing a family spanning impossible dates, the team should investigate dietary carbon, contamination, reburial, and sampling history. A laboratory age that looks absurd may reveal the chemistry of the sample's life rather than a chronology error in the measurement.

An additional source of error is introduced when old wood is used as fuel. Suppose a building burns and archaeologists collect charcoal from a large beam. The carbon in the inner rings was incorporated as the tree grew, potentially centuries before the building burned. The charcoal measurement can be accurate about the wood and misleading about the fire. Short-lived seeds, annual plants, or young twigs may be better samples if they are securely associated with the event. But even a seed can be residual or intrusive. The choice depends on which question is being asked, not only which material the laboratory can measure.

These examples make uncertainty easier to visualize. The laboratory supplies evidence about material. The archaeologist supplies a model of how material became part of an event. Neither is trivial. If a report supplies only a calendar date without the sample, context number, pretreatment information, and calibration method, the reader has little way to evaluate the conclusion. Good dating results invite scrutiny because their creators have kept the chain from artifact to inference visible.

## An audit trail from a date back to the ground

A good archaeological date can be reconstructed as a chain. First identify the claim: a building was constructed in a certain interval, a fire occurred during a certain century, or footprints formed on a particular volcanic surface. Next identify the actual sampled material: charcoal, a beam with an outer ring, a mineral grain in tuff, or something else. Then establish the material's archaeological relationship to the event. Was it in a sealed context? Was it reused? Does a later pit cut the layer? Only then ask how the laboratory measured the sample and what calibration, uncertainty, and cross-checks were applied.

Now attach names and publications. For tree-ring chronology, Andrew Ellicott Douglass's historical work is part of the method's development. For early radiocarbon validation, we can read Arnold and Libby's 1949 experiment and credit Ernest Anderson's research role. For stratigraphic ordering, Edward Harris's method gives us a clear vocabulary. For Laetoli, the original field and geological papers name Mary Leakey, Richard Hay, the people who recognized the trackways, and the researchers who measured the volcanic deposits. For contemporary calibration, the IntCal research team and Oxford Radiocarbon Accelerator Unit provide data and procedures that can be challenged, improved, and reused.

A date presented responsibly is therefore a compact summary of fieldwork, physical theory, measurement, statistical inference, and historical interpretation. The number on the museum label has a history of its own. Learning that history changes how we listen to an archaeological claim: we can ask what was measured, who measured it, what assumptions connect the measurement to the event, and whether another independent line of evidence agrees.

That is how a footprint becomes approximately three point six six million years old, how a beam becomes part of a dated house, and how charcoal helps place a forgotten community in a chronology shared with the rest of humanity. The clocks are real. The work of connecting them to human lives belongs to identifiable people, using methods whose evidence we can inspect.
