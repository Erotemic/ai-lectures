# Three Kingdoms, Part XIII: Seven Captures and the First Northern Expeditions

The founder of Shu-Han is dead. His sworn brothers are dead. His son Liu Shan sits on the throne, and Zhuge Liang must govern a state whose resources are smaller than those of its northern rival. He must repair the alliance with Wu, suppress unrest in the southern territories, preserve the administration, and decide whether continuing the dream of restoring the Han justifies another long war. He cannot leave a secure state behind merely by invoking the virtue of its founder. He must make one.

This movement of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* presents Zhuge Liang in two striking ways. In the south he is a conqueror who claims to win willing obedience through repeated mercy. In the north he is a commander whose complex plans are undone by the disobedience of an officer he trusted. The first is the famous story of Meng Huo's seven captures; the second includes the defeat at Jieting and the Empty Fort stratagem. Both have historical connections, but their evidence differs sharply. The challenge for a modern listener is to enjoy the novel's intelligence and pathos while recognizing that the story's depiction of the southern peoples comes through the eyes of an expansionist imperial tradition.

## Governing after a founder

Liu Shan inherits the throne as an adult still overshadowed by his father's reputation. Zhuge Liang holds a degree of authority that could be dangerous to a weaker ruler. The deathbed charge at Baidi has made his loyalty a public concern. An ordinary minister might be suspected of attempting to replace the heir. Zhuge Liang, in the novel, repeatedly declares that his powers are exercised to preserve the legitimate succession and the cause to which Liu Bei devoted his life.

The administrative tasks are less glamorous than the battles. Grain must be gathered, troops supplied, appointments made, and local relations repaired. An army campaigning in distant mountains consumes resources that the western kingdom can spare only with difficulty. The novel tends to turn these problems into opportunities for extraordinary stratagems. But the historical state's survival depended on routine civil administration and the maintenance of roads, granaries, and negotiated authority over communities whose interests were not identical to those of the court at Chengdu.

In the southern regions often called Nanzhong, resistance and political unrest create a threat to Shu's security. The novel labels many of the peoples involved with the term Nanman, conventionally translated in older texts as southern barbarians. That label is part of the political worldview of the northern and Sichuan-based courts. It should not be adopted as a neutral description of a single homogeneous population. The region contained diverse communities, leaders, languages, and patterns of exchange. The novel's exotic monsters, poison springs, and animal-led troops are devices of an imperial literary imagination, not ethnographic records.

## The regent must begin with accounts

Zhuge Liang's regency begins after a campaign that has drained the military strength of Shu. The novel remembers him as an extraordinary mind, but the first requirement of his office is ordinary and demanding: assess what remains. Soldiers need to be recruited and trained; taxes and grain must be collected without exhausting the western communities; officers must be reassigned to positions their abilities can support. The youthful ruler Liu Shan must be presented as an authority around whom rival court interests can continue to cooperate.

The minister's famous devotion can become more legible when we imagine the work rather than the legend. A day may begin with reports on granaries, troop rotations, requests for promotion, and complaints from districts whose labor obligations have become severe. A decision that benefits one army may reduce what another can consume. To keep the state functioning is to accept that some legitimate requests will be refused. The labor is a less glamorous achievement than an elaborate battlefield trick, but without it no northern expedition could be prepared.

Zhuge Liang also faces a problem in the south. The campaigns there are framed in the novel as an opportunity to pacify rebellious regions and establish stable submission. To a court in Chengdu, the concern is securing resources and communications. To communities being brought under that court's authority, the experience may mean armed intrusion, demands for tribute or labor, and the replacement of local leaders. These are different perspectives on the same expansion of power.

The novel is written from within a tradition that adopts the language and assumptions of the Chinese state. Its descriptions of southern peoples can flatten diverse communities into exotic opponents. We can tell the adventures as literature while refusing to make those categories into an accurate account of all the people living in the south. The historical southern campaign of 225 is real; its fantastical monsters and astonishing repeated encounters belong to a later narrative world.

## Meng Huo refuses to concede

The novel presents Meng Huo as an influential southern leader able to assemble resistance against Shu. Zhuge Liang marches south and defeats his forces. Meng Huo is captured, but instead of executing or permanently imprisoning him, Zhuge Liang releases him to try again. The captive insists that the result was a trick or an accident rather than proof that the invader deserves allegiance. The minister is willing to repeat the contest until Meng Huo acknowledges defeat from conviction.

The pattern recurs across encounters involving ambushes, captured soldiers, local alliances, difficult terrain, and increasingly strange opponents. In the novel Lady Zhurong, a formidable warrior associated with Meng Huo's household, fights with a skill that makes her much more than a decorative figure. Other leaders employ animals or unusual protective equipment. Some settings are designed to frighten an outsider: toxic water, dangerous forests, unfamiliar plants, and devices that seem magical. Zhuge Liang discovers how each advantage works and turns it against its users.

The repeated releases form a moral argument embedded in a military story. Zhuge Liang wants an opponent who has been defeated to recognize his authority as legitimate. A submission extracted only through fear may disappear when the invader departs. Willing acceptance, by contrast, would reduce the need for expensive permanent garrisons. The novel therefore presents leniency as practical statecraft. It can secure a durable settlement more effectively than killing every leader who resists.

But the argument must be examined from the other side. The invader sets the terms of the test. Meng Huo's refusal to submit is portrayed as stubbornness rather than as a legitimate desire for independence. Releasing a captive repeatedly may be merciful when compared with execution, while the larger campaign remains a conquest undertaken without the region's full consent. The story of benevolence can therefore function as a justification of expansion. A critical telling should retain the scene's narrative appeal without assuming that the conquered people's views have been fully represented.

## Seven captures and the demand for willing allegiance

The most memorable southern episode is the repeated capture and release of Meng Huo. A military commander might consider the first capture sufficient. A rebel leader is in custody, and the immediate armed threat appears removed. But Zhuge Liang, in the novel, seeks an outcome that a prisoner's execution cannot guarantee. He wants the opposing communities to accept the new settlement rather than merely submit while an occupying army remains nearby. Meng Huo refuses to admit defeat in a way that would make such an agreement credible.

So the cycle begins again. He is released, returns with another plan or ally, and suffers another defeat. Each episode varies the conditions. Some involve an apparently impregnable landscape; others involve new forces, special weapons, or elaborate tricks. The repetition can feel almost comic, but it has a clear political purpose. The adviser intends to make acknowledgment voluntary by exhausting the reasons his opponent can give for believing the result was accidental or unfair.

The idea is attractive because military conquest alone can produce fragile peace. Yet there is also a moral tension: a ruler who captures and releases an opponent seven times has still imposed the framework within which the opponent must eventually agree. A compelled adversary may call his final submission willing because no viable resistance remains. The novel wants to celebrate benevolent domination, and the audience can ask how much of the benevolence is visible from the defeated side.

An important source distinction sharpens the reading. The motif that Meng Huo was captured and released seven times predates the complete novel and is preserved through earlier historical or anecdotal traditions, including material discussed by Pei Songzhi. That does not establish the full range of later spectacular episodes as documented campaigns. A short remembered story grew into a sequence of encounters with distinct landscapes and enemies. The development illustrates how a moral maxim can become an extended adventure.

## What the old sources actually preserve

An early historical anecdote about repeated captures is preserved in Pei Songzhi's annotations, quoting the *Han-Jin Chunqiu* of Xi Zuochi. It states that Meng Huo was captured and released repeatedly, seven times in the familiar account, until he acknowledged Zhuge Liang's superiority. Chen Shou's primary biography of Zhuge Liang records the southern pacification more briefly and does not provide the novel's elaborate sequence of seven individual campaigns. The later novel transforms the anecdote into an extended set of adventures, adding characters, places, stratagems, and marvels.

This is a useful example of why sources should be compared rather than counted. A statement occurring in an earlier book can establish that a story circulated before the novel. It does not independently prove that every detail of the anecdote happened. The later novelist's addition of seven separate tactical episodes cannot be verified merely by pointing back to the brief earlier claim. Nor does the historical campaign itself depend on the historicity of the seven-captures motif. Shu's action in the south can be studied through other records of administration and military movements.

The novel culminates with Meng Huo accepting Zhuge Liang's command after the repeated demonstrations. The political lesson the narrator wants is clear: the best victor governs minds as well as territory. The uncomfortable lesson a listener may also draw is that the right to define another people's proper allegiance is itself a form of power. The story becomes more interesting, not less, when both insights are allowed to remain.

## A memorial before the northern march

Having stabilized the south, Zhuge Liang turns toward the north. Before campaigning he submits the memorial commonly known as the *Chu Shi Biao*, or memorial on setting out. It is a major text in the tradition of loyal service. The minister reminds Liu Shan of the founder's intentions, advises him about trustworthy officials, and explains why he feels obliged to continue the attempt to recover lost territory. The document's power lies partly in the speaker's awareness of the burden. He has inherited a promise he cannot easily fulfill, and abandoning it would seem to betray the dead ruler who entrusted him with the state.

The memorial also reveals a danger inherent in political continuity. A successor inherits goals chosen under conditions that may no longer exist. Liu Bei's plan to restore Han power was framed before several disasters, above all the loss of Jing and the defeat at Yiling. Zhuge Liang now confronts a much stronger northern regime from a smaller western base. The moral appeal of finishing the founder's work does not prove that the necessary resources are available. The novel often makes the persistence itself admirable, while later historians have argued over the campaigns' strategic wisdom.

The historical Zhuge Liang conducted several northern expeditions between 228 and his death in 234. The novel speaks in a traditional pattern of repeated campaigns, sometimes organized as six expeditions from Mount Qi, but the literary count and geography should not be imposed mechanically on the surviving campaign record. These operations involved roads across difficult mountains, the supply problems of moving troops out of Sichuan, and clashes with commanders of Cao Wei. Even brilliant tactics could achieve little if grain could not reach the army.

## The language of a memorial

Before the northern expedition, Zhuge Liang addresses a memorial to the ruler. The literary force of this document lies partly in its posture. A minister responsible for armies and finances speaks as a servant accountable to a higher office. He reminds the young emperor of the former ruler's wishes, urges fair appointments, and insists upon attention to capable officials. He also states his own intention to undertake a dangerous military task in fulfillment of an inherited duty.

The memorial is valuable because it exposes the gap between personal devotion and political procedure. Zhuge Liang may be the most powerful man in the state, but he asks that the sovereign listen carefully, reward and punish consistently, and maintain order at court. Those recommendations would be pointless if legitimacy were merely the command of the most brilliant individual. The document sets standards for an institution that must outlive the speaker.

The rhetoric of service can still be politically complicated. The young ruler is asked to rely on the regent whose advice shapes the realm. A minister who claims to act entirely for a predecessor's purpose may find it difficult to revise that purpose when circumstances change. The document's power comes from both possibilities: it can embody selfless responsibility, and it can bind the state to a military ambition whose expected cost will grow.

As with other famous texts embedded in novels, the memorial belongs to a literary and historical tradition larger than any one scene. Versions of Zhuge Liang's memorial survive in historical collections. A listener can compare its recommendations about government with the novel's image of a near-omniscient strategist. The historical minister emerges as a writer concerned with offices, appointments, and ethical consistency as well as with battlefield planning.

## A new officer with great promise

During the opening northern campaigns Zhuge Liang encounters Jiang Wei, a talented officer who has served the northern state. The novel stages his transfer to Shu through deception, testing, and recognition of intelligence. Zhuge Liang sees in the younger man a possible inheritor of the strategic skills needed to continue the cause. The relationship matters because all the heroes who founded the enterprise are dead or aging. A kingdom cannot rely on the memories of its first generation. It must recruit successors with talents capable of sustaining complex decisions.

The novel's portrait of Jiang Wei also plants the seeds of tragedy. A young strategist may inherit a mission more demanding than the resources he will later possess. To be chosen by Zhuge Liang brings honor, but it can bind a man to repeated campaigns whose possibilities are narrowing. The same quality that makes him a worthy disciple may lead him to keep fighting when a more limited policy would preserve the state longer. The book does not settle this question immediately; it makes inheritance of purpose a central element of the final arc.

## Jieting: a mountain that cannot be held by pride

A crucial northern operation depends on holding a passage at Jieting. Zhuge Liang entrusts the position to Ma Su, a cultivated and confident officer whose understanding of military theory has impressed him. Veteran commanders warn that Ma Su may know books better than the realities of command. The appointment becomes a test of Zhuge Liang's own judgment: can he distinguish the ability to discuss strategy from the ability to carry it out under danger?

Ma Su chooses a position on a mountain rather than securing the routes and water sources according to the plan. His troops are isolated, and the enemy exploits the mistake. The position falls, damaging the wider campaign. The defeat is historically associated with the Wei commander Zhang He. The novel's account may simplify tactical details, but its moral structure is direct. The officer's desire to demonstrate superior insight has made him ignore what the operation required. His cleverness is not disciplined by the physical facts of terrain and supply.

Zhuge Liang withdraws. He mourns the consequences and orders Ma Su executed, despite affection for him. The episode is commonly remembered as the minister weeping while enforcing military law. His tears do not save the officer because the defeat has cost lives and imperiled the campaign. The scene is difficult because Zhuge Liang bears responsibility too. He made the appointment, and the loss exposes a failure in his evaluation of talent. Punishing Ma Su can preserve discipline, but it does not remove the commander's duty to recognize his own error.

## The pass that rewards the wrong kind of confidence

At Jieting, Ma Su receives a responsibility on which a wider operation depends. The novel makes his confidence central to the failure. He favors a conspicuous position on high ground, expecting its natural strength to compensate for weaknesses in his deployment. More experienced officers warn that a force separated from reliable water and road access can be isolated. He treats the warning as a failure to appreciate the brilliance of his plan.

A hill may offer visibility and defensive advantages, but armies do not live on elevation. They live on supplies. If an enemy occupies the approach to water, the soldiers on the hill must choose between exhaustion and a costly attempt to fight their way down. If they cannot coordinate with neighboring units, they may possess strong local terrain while failing the purpose for which the position was assigned. The error, as the novel frames it, is not the abstract preference for higher ground. It is the failure to consider the relationship between ground, logistics, and the army's larger mission.

The loss forces Zhuge Liang into a painful choice. Ma Su is a favored officer whose learning and potential have inspired confidence. To excuse the failure because he is admired would tell every other commander that friendship can override military responsibility. To punish him makes the regent appear severe and sacrifices someone he may sincerely value. The narrative emphasizes sorrow alongside discipline. A ruler or minister can regret a penalty while considering it necessary for the coherence of command.

The historical campaign was a real setback for Zhuge Liang, and Ma Su's failure at Jieting is recorded in the historical tradition. The novel's surrounding dialogue is literary reconstruction. It asks us to see how a brilliant institution-builder can make an appointment based on misplaced confidence and must then publicly acknowledge the consequences. The perfect strategist is suddenly visible as a man who can choose badly.

## The city that appeared empty

The novel places Zhuge Liang in an apparently hopeless position with too few troops to resist an approaching northern force under Sima Yi. He orders the gates open and has attendants sweep the streets. He sits calmly above the entrance, playing music or maintaining an appearance of leisure. Sima Yi arrives and sees no visible signs of defense. Because he respects Zhuge Liang's reputation for concealed plans, he fears that the empty city is a trap and withdraws. The famous Empty Fort stratagem succeeds by using the opponent's intelligence against him.

It is a fascinating scene precisely because there is no conventional military defense. The strategist's power lies in what the enemy believes about his habits. If Zhuge Liang had a reputation for recklessness, the empty gates might invite immediate attack. Because he is known for preparation, an apparent lack of preparation becomes suspicious. Reputation has become a tactical resource. The plan works only if the enemy is more afraid of a hidden danger than of the embarrassment of refusing an open city.

The historical status of this anecdote is particularly revealing. An earlier account attributed to Guo Chong is quoted by Pei Songzhi, who argues against its plausibility with specific chronological and military objections. The novel later gives it elaborate dramatic form. Here the historical critic is already part of the story's transmission, not a modern adversary arriving centuries afterward. We possess both an early tale and an early attempt to reject it. That combination is excellent material for teaching how historians reason from conflicting evidence.

The Empty Fort tale also complicates the hero's omniscience. It relies on an adversary's mistaken inference. Sima Yi is experienced enough to know that Zhuge Liang often plans ambushes; he overapplies that expectation. Too much respect becomes a vulnerability. The same epistemic pattern occurred in the straw-boat arrows: an opponent acts on incomplete information in a manner the strategist predicts. Here the wager is even more dangerous, because a single scout entering the city could expose the bluff.


## An empty city and a full reputation

The Empty Fort scene is most satisfying when told from the approaching enemy's perspective. Sima Yi sees gates open and Zhuge Liang sitting calmly, playing a musical instrument as though he expects no danger. The city seems undefended, but everything the pursuer knows about his opponent suggests that such obvious vulnerability might be bait. He must decide whether to enter immediately or withdraw from a trap whose existence he cannot verify.

If Zhuge Liang's reputation were less imposing, the posture might fail. A weak commander sitting with open gates would probably be captured. The ruse works because the enemy credits Zhuge Liang with the capacity to stage a deception. The story therefore makes past success a form of present military power. A commander can use an adversary's expectation of his cleverness to protect himself when he has no more concrete protection.

The scene also illustrates the limits of inference from appearances. An open gate can mean lack of soldiers or confidence in concealed troops. Calm behavior can be sincere or deliberately staged. The enemy sees only the surface and must choose before certainty arrives. Readers know the trick and enjoy watching a capable opponent misread it. This is the same pleasure supplied by forged letters at Red Cliffs, compressed into the image of a scholar seated before a city.

The historical status of the story is especially instructive. An older anecdote resembling the Empty Fort stratagem was preserved, but Pei Songzhi criticized its plausibility in connection with Zhuge Liang and Sima Yi. The popular scene as told in the novel should not be presented as a straightforward event in the historical record. It is a brilliant literary illustration of reputation and strategic ambiguity. The source criticism is part of its meaning, because the legend's success in later culture demonstrates how strongly readers wished to imagine Zhuge Liang as someone who could win through knowledge alone.

## What a victory would actually require

After the first northern campaign, Shu's strategic problem remains. Wei controls extensive resources and commands forces able to reinforce threatened northern positions. Shu has a defensible basin and committed officers, but its armies must cross difficult approaches whenever they move outward. To take a fortress is not enough. The army would have to secure roads, supply new garrisons, and absorb or govern populations living beyond the old frontier.

Zhuge Liang's devotion is often described as a promise to restore Han. But in practical terms the project requires successive military operations, the rebuilding of stores after losses, the replacement of injured officers, and the continued consent or obedience of western households asked to provide the necessary resources. A court might invoke glorious ancestry; its tax collectors still need to know how much grain can safely be taken from a village.

This distinction allows the listener to respect the minister without treating his intentions as a guarantee of success. The novel admires his diligence and his refusal to abandon a difficult task. The historical record also invites the question whether those resources might have been used more effectively on defense and government. The disagreement does not have to be resolved by assigning one side virtue and the other selfishness. It is a dispute about what a small state owes to its founders' cause and to the people who live under it now.

The later northern expeditions will test this unresolved duty again and again. Every departure from the capital appears as an attempt to complete the story Liu Bei began; every return without decisive success brings the costs into sharper focus. The promise has become an institution's recurring obligation. The question is whether extraordinary devotion can make an objective attainable when geography, supplies, and the strength of the enemy continually resist it.

## The limits of the perfect strategist

Zhuge Liang's northern campaign fails to deliver the decisive restoration he wants. Ma Su's disobedience, supply constraints, and the enemy's capacity to resist undermine the operation. The novel supplies a brilliant moment at the empty city to preserve the commander's legendary standing, but the larger failure remains. This coexistence of local triumph and strategic defeat is one of the keys to understanding the late chapters. Zhuge Liang can outthink an enemy in a scene and still be unable to change the political balance across a large frontier.

He must return to Shu, maintain the loyalty of officers, prepare supplies, and defend the value of further operations to a court that cannot simply produce limitless grain. His personal sense of duty to Liu Bei sustains the effort. Yet duty becomes tragic when the means available to satisfy it diminish. The story shifts from the founding heroes' ascent toward a difficult question about persistence: how does one decide when an inherited mission remains achievable, and how much suffering may it ask of those who were not present when the promise was made?

The southern campaigns and the northern failure give two portraits of the same man. In one he is patient enough to release a defeated opponent repeatedly; in the other he demands severe punishment from a trusted subordinate. The contrast is sometimes explained as a difference between winning a population and maintaining discipline in one's own army. But it also shows that benevolence in political narratives is rarely an unconditional rule. It is applied to particular people in particular positions, according to what the commander believes will preserve the state.

The younger Jiang Wei watches this model of service. He sees an adviser who seeks to honor the founder, uses extraordinary skill, admits personal responsibility for defeat, and returns to the field despite repeated constraints. This is the inheritance he will receive. The novel's emotional center is increasingly the labor of men who serve a cause whose most famous champions are already dead. In their hands the dream of Han restoration survives, but survival is becoming different from victory.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 87–96. Nanzhong is culturally diverse; avoid endorsing the novel's pejorative ethnic classifications. The seven-captures motif appears in Pei Songzhi quoting Xi Zuochi, but the detailed episodes are later fiction. Zhuge Liang's northern memorial and Jieting defeat have historical bases. The Empty Fort anecdote is discussed and criticized in Pei's own commentary and must not be presented as securely historical. Zhang He defeated Ma Su in the historical Jieting campaign.
