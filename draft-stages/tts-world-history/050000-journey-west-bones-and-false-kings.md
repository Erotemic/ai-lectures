# Journey to the West, Part III: The White Bone Demon and the Problem of Appearances

A young woman comes down a mountain path carrying food. Four travelers have been making their way west: a Buddhist monk named Tripitaka, his brilliant and violent monkey guardian Sun Wukong, the piglike Zhu Bajie, and the quieter Sha Wujing. The monk is hungry. The woman appears generous. Monkey looks at her and sees a demon. Before his master can accept the offering, Monkey strikes the woman down with his iron staff.

The monk is horrified. A guardian has just killed someone who seemed to be offering help. Monkey insists that the apparent woman was a monster who meant to eat his master. The monk sees a corpse. Pigsy, already inclined to resent the monkey's authority, suggests that the attack arose from anger or vanity. Soon an elderly woman appears, looking for her daughter. Monkey strikes her too. Then an old man comes searching for his family. Monkey attacks him as well. From the master's point of view, the violence has become intolerable. From Monkey's point of view, each disguised visitor belongs to the same murderous intelligence.

The confrontation is among the defining scenes of *Journey to the West*, the hundred-chapter Chinese novel known from late-Ming printing. The story turns on a question more difficult than whether monsters exist in a fantasy world. Two companions disagree about the evidence before them. One sees the danger beneath appearances; the other sees an apparently innocent human killed by a ferocious protector. The audience has been allowed information that the monk lacks. The disagreement becomes a tragedy of trust within a fellowship whose survival depends on cooperation.

We will follow the stretch of the novel that surrounds this crisis, approximately chapters twenty-four through forty-two. The travelers seek Buddhist scriptures in India. Their journey is modeled very freely on the pilgrimage of the historical Xuanzang, who traveled in the seventh century. In the fiction, Guanyin has recruited supernatural companions to help the monk survive. This group can defeat spectacular enemies, yet it repeatedly struggles to decide whom to believe and when force is justified. The encounters in this part of the story show how desire, disguise, wounded pride, and sincere moral conviction can interact disastrously.

## The fruit that looks like a child

Before the White Bone Demon, the travelers come to a Daoist establishment associated with the immortal Zhenyuan. Its garden contains a miraculous tree bearing ginseng fruit. The fruits look like infants and carry the promise of extraordinary life. The host is away, but he has instructed his young attendants to welcome the pilgrim and offer rare fruit. Tripitaka is disturbed by the fruit's human form. He cannot bring himself to eat what appears to be a baby, however politely it is offered. The attendants consume the portions themselves.

Pigsy hears about the treasure and asks Monkey to obtain some. Monkey uses his powers to enter the garden and collect fruit for the companions. The theft is quickly discovered. The young attendants scold the travelers; Monkey's pride flares; he destroys the precious tree. The quarrel becomes much larger than a few stolen fruits. When Zhenyuan returns, he is furious and has the pilgrims detained. Monkey resorts to deception and violence, but he cannot solve the problem simply by escaping. The tree is ruined, and its owner has an injury that demands repair.

The narrative gives Monkey a task unlike his earlier victories over guards and monsters. He must find someone able to restore what he has damaged. He travels in search of a remedy, and Guanyin ultimately helps revive the tree. Reconciliation becomes possible. The cost of the stolen fruit cannot be erased by claiming that the fruit was tempting, that the attendants were rude, or that Monkey could overpower the household. The act needs to be repaired.

This incident gives a useful measure of the monkey's development. In Heaven he behaved as though a grand enough title justified taking whatever privilege seemed appropriate. At the Daoist garden he still responds to insult by breaking something irreplaceable. But the plot now forces him to acknowledge a responsibility beyond his immediate wishes. The host is a figure of high religious standing, not an enemy whose annihilation would be a moral solution. The episode allows Daoist and Buddhist beings to participate in a common world without declaring all their practices interchangeable. Guanyin's assistance can be read as interreligious cooperation within the novel's comic cosmology.

There is another joke with teeth: a tree whose fruit grants long life becomes the object of people who are supposed to be seeking release from attachment. Tripitaka's compassion toward what looks like an infant is a genuine trait. He refuses the fruit from concern that eating it would be a horrible act. Yet his interpretation of an appearance is mistaken. The scene prepares the reader for a far more dangerous moment when he will again judge a strange object by its apparent human form. Seeing something with compassion is admirable; knowing what it is requires additional work.

## Three disguises and one dismissal

The pilgrims enter difficult mountainous country. A demon of white bones notices that Tripitaka possesses flesh of extraordinary value. In the novel's monstrous economy, consuming the scripture monk can promise supernatural longevity. The demon first adopts the appearance of a young woman offering food, then that of her mother, and then that of the bereaved father. Each costume is chosen to provoke a particular response. The first invites gratitude, the second guilt, the third outrage at an apparently repeated crime.

Monkey sees through the disguises. His insight, however, creates an evidentiary problem. By the time the others look, the enemy can leave convincing signs of death behind. The supposed bodies make it appear that Monkey has murdered human beings. He claims the victim was a demon. The monk cannot verify the claim in the ordinary ways available to a human traveler. Pigsy exploits the uncertainty. The more vehemently Monkey protests, the more he looks like someone seeking to escape blame.

This is not simply a story in which a naive teacher refuses to believe his clever pupil. The monk has real reasons to object to his guardian's habitual resort to killing. Monkey has already struck down robbers, boasted of his prowess, and responded to offense with force. His past conduct makes his present defense difficult to assess. The demon manipulates a weakness that existed before the encounter: the companions have failed to build a trustworthy method of resolving disputes when one member sees a danger others cannot detect.

The monk finally dismisses Monkey. Depending on the telling, he gives a formal statement releasing the disciple from service. Monkey is wounded by the rejection. He knows that the master is in danger and that his own powers are the group's strongest protection, but he also refuses to remain where his good faith is denied. He returns toward his old home, the Mountain of Flowers and Fruit.

The demon has failed to eat the monk, yet the encounter has succeeded in separating him from his guardian. That is an important measure of victory for a deceiver. A direct attack on Monkey would be difficult. A manufactured conflict between Monkey and the people he is trying to protect can accomplish more. The demon's changes of appearance therefore operate at two levels: hiding the monster's own identity and changing what the companions believe about one another.

The language of the episode often invites comparison with moral and religious self-deception. Readers may identify the demon with appetites or illusions that take plausible forms. Such readings can illuminate the story, particularly the monkey's role as a faculty that recognizes what others miss. But the scene also stands as a remarkably precise drama of evidence. The monk has correct moral principles and faulty information. Monkey has better information and a history of excessive force. Pigsy has enough information to doubt and a motive to undermine a rival. The result is a failure whose causes cannot be assigned to only one character.

## What the White Bone Demon teaches the audience

The three disguises of the White Bone Demon are carefully ordered. A young woman carrying food seems likely to invite help or thanks. An older woman looking for a missing daughter invites sympathy and alarm. An elderly father seeking his family brings the social authority of age and bereavement into the scene. The sequence turns an apparent murder into a pattern of repeated cruelty. If the monk believes the visible story, his guardian has not merely lost control once; he has persisted in attacking a family.

The demon takes advantage of an ethical expectation familiar to the audience. A religious traveler who claims compassion should hesitate before harming a woman carrying food and should take the grief of bereaved parents seriously. The fraud is effective because those expectations are legitimate. That is why the episode remains painful even when the reader knows the woman is a demon. It creates a circumstance in which a duty to protect the apparently weak collides with a need to prevent harm from a hidden predator.

Monkey's ability to see through transformations is often represented by his fiery golden eyes, acquired after his imprisonment in Laozi's furnace. The power is particularly suited to the confrontation, but it has an important limitation: seeing something correctly does not mean others will accept one's testimony. The monkey must translate private perception into shared evidence. He is poor at that task. He responds to challenge with anger, makes accusations in his habitual tone of superiority, and treats his master's hesitation as an insult. He is right about the creature and unable to persuade the person who most needs to believe him.

Pigsy's role shows that a witness may shape another person's judgment without possessing better evidence. He interprets Monkey's violence through a familiar grievance, presenting the guardian as proud, reckless, and perhaps eager to display his strength. Some of those descriptions have been true in other situations. Pigsy exploits their plausibility in this one. He does not need to invent a wholly alien version of Monkey to make the master doubt him. He only needs to select aspects of Monkey's past that fit his preferred explanation.

After the final disguise is defeated, the creature's bones reveal something of its real nature. The discovery offers the audience confirmation, but it has come too late to undo the breakdown of trust. The tragedy is not chiefly that the monk fails a test with a single correct answer. It is that the fellowship has no way to repair a disputed interpretation before anger, fear, and past resentment determine what happens next. That makes the episode a remarkably strong study of evidence under conditions of urgency.

## The princess and the yellow robe

Without Monkey, the journey soon deteriorates. Tripitaka enters a region where a powerful monster lives in a cave. The monster appears in human form and has taken a princess from the kingdom of Precious Elephant. Her story introduces a domestic captivity within the larger fantasy of divine beings and enchanted lands. The princess has lived separated from her family, and the monster wants to maintain the life he has built around her. She seeks a way to communicate her plight to her parents.

Tripitaka becomes entangled in this conflict and carries news toward the royal court. The apparently courteous story of a husband and wife is more dangerous than it first seems. The monster has extraordinary powers and a concealed identity. He can present himself at court and manipulate the king's assessment of his daughter and the monk. The pilgrim, already deprived of his most perceptive defender, is transformed into a tiger through magic and displayed as a beast.

The reversal is exact. The monk dismissed Monkey because he thought Monkey had mistaken human beings for monsters. Now the monk is made to look like a monster himself. The court judges according to the evidence placed before it. The reader has another opportunity to experience how appearances can be manufactured to support a false story.

The white horse, ordinarily a patient carrier, recognizes that the party is collapsing. In some of the most memorable scenes involving his hidden identity, the horse takes a more active role and tries to intervene. Pigsy and Sandy cannot resolve the crisis alone. Pigsy eventually goes in search of Monkey, appealing to his loyalty to their master. Monkey's first response is anger at the injustice he suffered. Why should he return to the person who condemned him? But the monk remains in peril, and Monkey's connection with the mission proves deeper than the demand to be properly appreciated.

He returns. Through investigation, disguise, and combat, he exposes the monster and rescues the monk. The enemy's real status connects him with a starry or celestial order, another instance in which a terrifying power in the mortal world turns out to have relations above. The ending is not a clean endorsement of everything the heavenly authorities do. It shows how the cosmology permits monsters and gods to occupy overlapping networks of office, punishment, and privilege.

Monkey's return also repairs something between master and disciple, though not permanently. Their conflict has revealed the structural problem of the journey. The monk cannot survive every encounter without someone able to perceive and oppose disguised enemies. The guardian cannot fulfill the religious purpose of the journey if his actions are judged only by whether they succeed in killing an attacker. Both must become capable of learning from their disagreements. The story will test that capacity again.

## A pair of thieves with divine treasures

The pilgrims next confront two formidable beings often called Gold Horn and Silver Horn, who reside in a mountain cave and possess magical implements of astonishing power. They have gourds, a vessel, and other devices that can trap or destroy a victim under the right conditions. The monsters' equipment is formidable, but the larger amusement comes from elaborate trickery on both sides. Monkey changes shape, impersonates others, steals objects, loses them, and invents ways to exploit the very rules that make the magic dangerous.

A magic gourd may respond when a person's name is called and the person answers. That creates an oddly bureaucratic rule of supernatural capture. The danger lies partly in the target's own compliance with a summons. Monkey learns the operation of the devices and turns it back against their owners. The chapter sequence rewards attention to procedure: who possesses a treasure, what words activate it, who can recognize a disguise, and whether a victim must answer for the magic to take effect.

The villains are not mere anonymous beasts. Their identity leads back to an exalted religious household, where they had been associated with Laozi's world. Once again the journey passes through a system in which extraordinary harm can be caused by beings who have connections with higher celestial authorities. A common theme of the book is the difficulty of judging power by its visible insignia. An object may be sacred in one setting and predatory in another. It may be used by an immortal for cultivation and by an escaped attendant for kidnapping.

These scenes also make room for Pigsy's conduct. He can be brave, but he frequently complains about errands, hunger, or risk. Monkey manipulates him, sometimes unfairly, just as Pigsy manipulates their master. Their bickering has a practical cost. A small lie told to shift an unpleasant duty can leave somebody exposed. The quarrel is funny because it sounds so ordinary amid miraculous events. It also reinforces the need for a fellowship whose members can trust one another with responsibilities that may be tiring or humiliating.

After such ordeals the travelers sometimes reach moments of contemplative respite. The novel's verse and chapter titles invoke the mind, the moon, true nature, and the seductions of false practices. The rhythms of adventure alternate with language that interprets the road as a process of spiritual cultivation. It is possible to listen first for the comic episode and later reconsider its symbolism. The story rarely insists that one pleasure exclude the other.

## A kingdom ruled by the wrong man

A different kind of deception emerges in the kingdom of Wuji. A king appears in a dream and reveals that he has been murdered and replaced. His corpse lies hidden beneath a well. The impostor has occupied the throne and deceived the household, including the queen and prince. Here the stakes are public authority and the private intimacies of a family. A false ruler can command the court because he has successfully borrowed the visible marks of legitimacy.

Monkey must persuade the prince to investigate, draw out evidence that the king on the throne may not be genuine, and expose the deception. The real king is eventually restored through supernatural means. The story resembles the earlier conflict in Precious Elephant Kingdom, but it asks a slightly different question. If everyone has accepted a substitute as sovereign, what kind of proof could make them change their minds? Familiarity can reinforce a false identity: the longer a person has held an office, the easier it may become for others to treat that person's presence as the evidence of rightful possession.

The prince's investigation is therefore important. He cannot simply accept Monkey's assertion that the reigning king is an impostor. He has emotional reasons to believe and reasons to fear the consequences of belief. To accuse a ruler falsely would be a terrible act; to fail to recognize his murdered father would be another. The novel uses an impossible transformation to dramatize a familiar difficulty of political life: the official signs of authority may remain intact after the underlying claim has been corrupted.

Some readings see the false king as an allegory of an unpurified mind or an illegitimate principle occupying the seat of proper judgment. Such readings are supported by the religious texture of the chapters. But the episode also concerns family recognition, the politics of access to a ruler, and the vulnerability of a court that confuses continuity of appearance with legitimacy. The restoration gives the plot closure. It does not erase the years during which a king's family lived beside a usurper.

## A child who commands terrible fire

A demon who looks like a small child now becomes the center of the most dangerous confrontation in this stretch of the novel. Red Boy, connected by family to the powerful Bull Demon King, stages a scene of distress. The monk hears what seems to be a child in need. Monkey suspects a trap. The others hesitate. The question of whether compassion demands immediate trust returns in a form especially difficult for the monk to resist.

Red Boy possesses a devastating kind of fire, often called samadhi fire in English discussions of the episode. It overwhelms ordinary attempts to extinguish it and threatens Monkey himself. The attack is important because Wukong's body, hardened against execution in Heaven, is still vulnerable to specific forces. Invulnerability is never a universal property in this narrative. Each new enemy may possess a power that makes the hero's familiar solution useless.

Monkey finally seeks Guanyin's assistance. The bodhisattva subdues Red Boy and redirects him into religious service, traditionally as a child attendant. The outcome is neither an ordinary criminal punishment nor simply a victorious killing. A being who has acted with great cruelty can be restrained and placed within a discipline that may change him. The notion resembles the recruitment of the pilgrims themselves, each of whom began with significant offenses or failures.

Yet readers have reason to consider the difference in treatment between those who possess powerful patrons and those who do not. Some monsters are killed; others are claimed by deities and taken home. Red Boy's fate is shaped by Guanyin's intervention and by the religious order into which he can be incorporated. In the story world, supernatural affiliations affect consequences. This does not by itself establish a single intention to satirize religion or politics, but it supplies material for both kinds of reading.

Red Boy also gives the monk's compassion a harder trial than the White Bone Demon did. The apparent sufferer is a real childlike being in form, yet he is dangerous and deliberate in exploiting the expectation that a vulnerable figure should receive help. Monkey's suspicion is warranted. The monk's response is morally intelligible. The book places these positions in direct conflict without finding a universally safe way of deciding whom to trust.

## The king, the healer, and the child

The false king of Wuji and Red Boy provide two sharply different versions of the same problem. In Wuji, the impostor has lived in the place of the legitimate ruler long enough to gain the habit of being obeyed. The prince has grown accustomed to his father's supposed presence. To expose the deception, Monkey must reach someone inside the household who can help examine the claim. The investigation involves the prince's private knowledge, the queen's experience, and the discovery of the actual king's hidden body. It is a matter of connecting pieces of evidence that the usurper has kept separate.

The restoration of the king requires more than winning a duel. The body has to be recovered and revived, and the royal family must recognize the one who returns. In a world where transformations are possible, recognition is not a trivial ceremony. It has to undo the false relationships established by the impostor. The episode is concerned with how a community's memory can be manipulated when someone controls the public image of authority.

Red Boy works in the opposite direction. He does not acquire a king's office and wait for people to accept him. He presents himself as a child in distress and expects the monk to respond immediately. The apparent vulnerability is a weapon. His fire then provides a second weapon when the disguise has served its purpose. Monkey has to overcome both his master's urgent sympathy and a dangerous element against which his own gifts offer incomplete protection.

The intervention of Guanyin is part of a larger pattern in which a demon's future can be redirected rather than terminated. When she restrains Red Boy and gives him a new religious role, the audience is offered the possibility of transformation for an enemy. That possibility should be taken seriously. It is connected with the way Monkey himself was recruited after causing havoc in Heaven. A person may act cruelly and still become capable of serving a different purpose.

But the symmetry is not exact. Monkey had spent centuries beneath a mountain; Red Boy is drawn into a new role by a figure of extraordinary influence. Their punishments, opportunities, and privileges differ. The reader can therefore value the moral possibility of rehabilitation while asking how responsibility is distributed in a world where the patronage of the powerful changes what happens after wrongdoing. The novel makes that question available through its plot even if it does not answer it in a modern systematic form.

## Eyes, voices, and the ethics of protection

Across these chapters, there is a repeated method for creating drama. The travelers encounter someone whose visible form invites one kind of response. The truth behind the appearance would justify another. The group must act before it can establish all the facts. A shape-changing demon can impersonate a vulnerable person; a usurper can occupy a king's throne; a noble prince can be hidden under a monstrous form; a divine treasure can become an instrument of kidnapping. The reader often enjoys a broader perspective than the endangered characters, but that advantage should sharpen our sympathy for their uncertainty.

The novel repeatedly identifies Monkey with the mind, sometimes in titles that call him the Mind Monkey. A disciplined mind can recognize patterns beneath distracting appearances. Yet it is possible to be perceptive and still morally careless. Monkey's intelligence finds hidden enemies; his anger may create new ones. Tripitaka's compassion provides a principle for restraining violence; his limited information can make that restraint destructive. Pigsy's resentment distorts the available evidence. Sandy's steadiness cannot always break the deadlock. None of the virtues is enough by itself.

The historical evidence for this material is of a different kind from the evidence for Xuanzang's real pilgrimage. There is no reasonable basis for seeking an identifiable White Bone Demon in a seventh-century travel record. The episodes are parts of a later literary tradition. What can be studied historically is the existence of editions, the arrangement of episodes, the religious vocabulary, and the ways earlier narratives were adapted. The late-sixteenth-century printed novel is tangible evidence that these stories were being assembled and circulated in that form. Earlier accounts of the pilgrim, performance traditions, and other versions help scholars trace how some ingredients came together, though they do not yield a complete record of every storyteller's contribution.

It is tempting to make the whole series of trials into a list of moral examples: do not steal fruit, do not judge by appearances, do not allow a false ruler to deceive you. The novel offers something more complicated. Monkey has to steal or deceive to defeat some monsters. Religious figures can be greedy. A compassionate refusal to harm can leave a predator at liberty. A justifiable use of force can be indistinguishable, to a nearby observer, from murder. The story takes ethical rules seriously by repeatedly forcing them into situations where the characters must supply judgment rather than repeat a formula.

The most painful image remains the monk dismissing his guardian. He believes he is refusing to excuse unjustified violence. Monkey believes he is being punished for preventing a murder. Both convictions grow from events the novel has made us understand. The demon succeeds because a partnership rich in power has not yet become reliable in its exchange of information and trust.

What follows from these ordeals is therefore larger than a string of victories. Monkey returns to protect the master who rejected him. The monk continues to bear responsibility for someone whose powers he fears. Pigsy continues to irritate and assist both of them. The party persists despite what it has learned about its own frailty. The road west does not grow safer. It grows more revealing. Behind each alluring face and impressive title, the travelers must learn to ask who is acting, what they want, and what evidence would distinguish an appearance from the being beneath it.

## Source and editorial notes

Chapters 24–42 include the ginseng fruit tree, the White Bone Demon's three disguises and Monkey's dismissal, Precious Elephant Kingdom and the Yellow-Robed Demon, Gold and Silver Horn, the false king of Wuji, and Red Boy. The chapter sequence is stable as a narrative outline, while names, religious vocabulary, and the interpretation of magical actions vary across translations. White Bone is a literary figure; the historical evidence pertinent to the account is transmission of the novel and its preceding narrative materials, not corroboration of supernatural events.
