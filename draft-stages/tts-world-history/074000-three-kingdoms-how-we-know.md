# Three Kingdoms: How History Became a Novel

Three men swear to become brothers beneath peach blossoms. A strategist sits above an empty city and plays music until an army turns away. A defeated commander releases an old enemy because gratitude is stronger than his military orders. These are some of the most memorable moments of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*. They are also excellent places to ask what we know about China's era of division and how we know it. If the scenes were not recorded as history, where did they come from? If some details appear in early histories and others in late fiction, how can we avoid treating the novel as either an accurate chronicle or an arbitrary invention?

The answer begins with different kinds of texts serving different purposes. There were real wars, rulers, military appointments, successions, famines, and political murders in the period from the late second century through 280. Historians wrote accounts within living memory of much of that period. Later writers preserved alternative reports, criticized those accounts, and built new histories. Storytellers and dramatists made the figures into characters who could be recognized through gestures, speeches, and moral choices. Printers and commentators reshaped the resulting novel for readers. A responsible reading keeps these layers distinct while studying how they interacted.

This lecture will examine the record behind the story, the surviving editions of the novel, and a few cases where one famous scene looks very different depending on which witness we ask. The goal is to understand the literary achievement more fully and to make careful historical claims without asking a novel to serve as a court archive.

## The world behind the plot

The name Three Kingdoms commonly refers to the era in which Cao Wei, Shu Han, and Sun Wu existed as rival regimes. Wei's founding is usually dated to 220, Shu-Han's to 221, and Wu's formal imperial claim to 229; the period ends with Jin's conquest of Wu in 280. But the novel begins decades earlier, with late-Han court struggles and the Yellow Turban uprising of 184. It needs this earlier world because the three states emerged from a much wider field of contenders. Dong Zhuo, Yuan Shao, Lü Bu, Sun Jian, and others were not merely preliminary obstacles. Their campaigns and choices helped determine which institutions survived.

The historical record from these decades does not resemble a single continuous diary. It is a collection of biographies, imperial annals, memorials, chronologies, and later compilations that survive through complex transmission. A biography may give the decisions of one official but omit the motives of an enemy. A state history may preserve appointments and victories while explaining defeat through moral criticism. A later historian may quote an earlier work that is otherwise lost. The researcher must ask when each account was produced, what its author could have known, and what incentives shaped its presentation.

The existence of an early source can be powerful evidence that a story circulated long before the novel. It does not by itself establish that the episode happened as told. A writer close to an event may repeat rumor, use a political justification, or leave out matters that seem damaging to the state he served. Conversely, a later source may preserve information lost from earlier works. Historians therefore compare witnesses rather than assuming every older sentence is true and every later sentence is false.

## Chen Shou and the first great history

The essential starting point is Chen Shou's *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, or *Sanguozhi*. Chen Shou lived in the third century, was associated with Shu, and later served under Jin. His book contains the histories of the three rival states, generally arranged through biographies and records of ruling houses. Its composition followed the final conquest of Wu, so its author could look back on the complete period. That vantage was valuable, but it also placed him inside the political world of the eventual victors.

Chen Shou's text is concise. It preserves crucial facts about careers, campaigns, appointments, and deaths, but it leaves many motives and conversations unstated. The relative treatment of the three states raises questions of official perspective. The terminology used for rulers, the presentation of legitimate succession, and the allocation of praise could influence how a later reader judged the competing claims. The issue is not simply whether the historian liked one character. Official histories were composed in a tradition where political legitimacy shaped the formal arrangement of the past.

Take the connection among Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei. The biographies establish that Guan Yu and Zhang Fei were profoundly close to Liu Bei and served him over many difficult years. They do not document the theatrical peach-garden oath that opens the novel. The later ritual does not replace an event known from a contemporary witness. It makes a relationship whose significance is historically attested visible in a scene designed to be remembered. Recognizing that difference clarifies the precise sense in which the novel can be historically grounded without reporting every gesture as fact.

## Pei Songzhi adds voices and arguments

A major expansion came in the fifth century. Pei Songzhi was commissioned to annotate Chen Shou's work, completing his commentary in 429. He incorporated quotations from other histories, private records, biographies, and collections of anecdotes, including many texts that no longer survive independently. He sometimes supplied additional information, sometimes noted disagreements, and sometimes argued that a report was implausible. His annotations are not merely footnotes explaining difficult words. They preserve a conversation among earlier sources.

The method is invaluable because it lets us see the uncertainty within the tradition. A famous anecdote may be presented alongside another version, and Pei may explain why he accepts one or rejects both. This is a very different situation from a modern summary that offers a seamless account with no indication that the evidence conflicts. Even when Pei's own judgment is debatable, we can examine the reasons he gives. The historian has become a visible participant in the process of deciding what should count as credible.

Consider the Empty Fort stratagem. In the famous literary version Zhuge Liang opens the city gates, sits in calm view, and induces Sima Yi to retreat for fear of an ambush. A related story appears in earlier anecdotal material quoted by Pei. Pei does not simply transmit it with approval. He challenges it using chronology, the military positions of the figures involved, and the likely conduct of the forces. The later novel transforms a disputed anecdote into one of the clearest demonstrations of Zhuge Liang's genius. The distinction is not an invention of modern skepticism. An early commentator already found reasons to doubt the tale.

This case also demonstrates why reading an annotation requires attention. It is possible to find a story within Pei's commentary and proclaim that the event is historically recorded, while omitting the fact that Pei quoted the story in order to reject it. The location of a passage does not tell us whether the editor endorsed it. A proper account identifies the source being quoted and the judgment made about it.

## Histories written afterward

The *Book of the Later Han*, associated with Fan Ye in the fifth century, provides an important account of the dynasty's last decades. The *Book of Jin*, compiled much later under the Tang, treats the successor dynasty and includes material relevant to the Sima house and the final reunification. The eleventh-century *Zizhi Tongjian*, or *Comprehensive Mirror to Aid in Government*, prepared under Sima Guang, arranges a broad political history chronologically and draws on earlier materials. Each work has its own date, organization, and purposes. They are not independent eyewitnesses merely because several mention the same event.

If a later chronicle uses Chen Shou's account, agreement between them may reflect dependence rather than independent corroboration. If it quotes a lost document, that quotation can still supply information not otherwise available. Questions of textual lineage matter as much as the number of books on a reading list. A responsible researcher traces which account came from which predecessor and asks whether the differences represent new evidence, editorial selection, or political interpretation.

The period also produced writings attributed to people inside the story, such as memorials by Zhuge Liang and poetry associated with the Cao family. Their transmission must be studied individually, but these texts help complicate the fictional images. Cao Cao was not only a brutal commander in anti-Cao moral narratives; he belonged to an important literary culture. Zhuge Liang was not only a magician of battlefield traps; he was a minister concerned with appointments, taxation, diplomacy, and administration. Reading documents associated with their political and intellectual worlds lets the historical people come into view beyond their novelistic masks.

## How to test a famous scene without losing the story

Consider the oath in the Peach Garden. Its emotional importance is clear: it gives three men a chosen family and makes loyalty the standard against which their later decisions can be read. To ask whether it happened historically is to pose a different question. We would first examine early records for the men and their relationships. Chen Shou describes a close association among Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei, but the famous garden ceremony in its familiar dramatic form belongs to later storytelling. The evidence supports an unusually close relationship without supplying the novelist's complete scene.

The same procedure can be applied to Red Cliffs. We can identify a historical confrontation and the central role of southern commanders, including Zhou Yu and Huang Gai. We can then separate the accounts of military action from the novelist's expanded duels of intelligence. A straw-boat expedition to gather arrows and an altar for summoning favorable wind have specific functions in the literary plot; these details cannot be imported into a third-century campaign merely because they are the most memorable portions of a modern adaptation.

Now consider the Empty Fort stratagem. Its status is more complicated than a simple opposition between an early true record and a modern false tale. Anecdotes about a daring display of apparent weakness circulated before the surviving complete novel, and Pei Songzhi specifically questioned a version connected with Zhuge Liang. We can therefore study the history of the anecdote and the history of criticism together. The stratagem was admired enough to survive, and doubted early enough that skepticism is part of its transmission.

These examples suggest a practical sequence for every striking claim: identify the exact version being told; find its earliest recoverable witness; establish what that witness says rather than what a later summary attributes to it; compare independent evidence where available; and ask why subsequent storytellers kept or changed it. The process does not require the listener to abandon the story. It gives the story a second dimension, as an object that people across centuries continually made meaningful.

## How the stories moved toward fiction

The novel did not arise directly from one historian copying and expanding Chen Shou chapter by chapter. The story had a long life in performance and shorter prose forms. A Yuan-period illustrated or printed *pinghua* version of the Three Kingdoms narrative preserves a stage of popular storytelling. Theatrical traditions developed distinct characters and scenes, making historical figures recognizable to audiences who had not studied the histories. A performer could dramatize an argument through a sharp exchange, compress years into a meeting, or make a political lesson immediate through one act of betrayal.

The distinction between scholarly history and popular narrative should not be drawn too sharply. Educated writers knew histories, and performance traditions could carry historically informed material. Conversely, official histories sometimes preserve dramatic anecdotes that sound like stories. The novel develops in a field where written archives, moral evaluations, poetry, storytelling, and theater could influence one another. A famous gesture might be old in performance but new in a surviving printed text. A scene might borrow a historical event from another context and give it to a more famous character.

The straw-boat arrows are an example. Historical and anecdotal traditions connect a related maneuver to Sun Quan in a different setting. The novel gives the feat to Zhuge Liang in the Red Cliffs campaign, where it becomes a demonstration of his foresight and a way to outwit Zhou Yu. The story remains intelligible as a feat of exploiting fog and enemy expectations. But its literary reassignment tells us something about the novel's interests: it concentrates strategic brilliance in the adviser whose reputation it wants to magnify.

## The printing of a great novel

The earliest known surviving edition of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* was published in 1522, with a preface dated 1494. It is organized in 240 shorter textual sections rather than the familiar modern arrangement of 120 chapters. The older date of the preface is important but does not prove that a complete book identical to the surviving printing existed in 1494. It is evidence for an editorial and publication history that precedes the copy we have. The earliest surviving edition is a material witness, not a time machine to the moment of first composition.

The name Luo Guanzhong is conventionally attached to the novel. Biographical information about Luo is limited, and identifying precisely which textual stages he authored or edited is difficult. The responsible formulation is that the novel is traditionally attributed to Luo and reached its surviving printed form through a longer process. It is too simple to imagine one person inventing every episode in the book at one moment. It is equally unwarranted to deny the possibility of major literary organization by an author or editor merely because some of the stories were older.

Later editions and commentaries mattered enormously. Works attributed to a commentator named Li Zhuowu circulate in a complicated history where the name and persona of Li Zhi may not correspond straightforwardly to the actual author of every comment. By the later seventeenth century, Mao Lun and Mao Zonggang reshaped the book through editing, chapter divisions, commentary, and a stronger rhetorical orientation. The 120-chapter Mao recension became highly influential. When modern readers encounter the familiar opening reflection about a divided realm reuniting, or a strongly polarized portrait of Liu Bei and Cao Cao, the edition they use can matter.

The novel should therefore be described with a version convention. A lecture that follows the common 120-chapter arrangement ought to say so. When it cites chapters, it should not assume that an early edition has identical divisions. If a historical argument rests on a particular phrase, the phrase should be checked against the appropriate printing rather than assumed to belong to an original author whose autograph is lost.

## A date on a book does not date every sentence

The surviving 1522 printing is a landmark because it gives scholars a substantial textual witness to the novel's earlier form. Its prefaces, including one dated 1494, indicate that the book had a history before the surviving copy. But one must be careful about what follows. A preface dated 1494 is evidence associated with the edition's production and transmission; it does not put an identifiable complete novel in our hands from that date, and it does not establish that Luo Guanzhong personally composed every passage as later printed.

The printed text also preserves layers of storytelling and written history that are much older than the edition itself. The name of a warrior or the outline of a battle may be inherited from historical chronicles. A scene of witty dialogue may be shaped by earlier popular literature. A verse may derive from a later poet. Editorial revisions can reorganize episodes and alter the narrator's judgment. The book's publication history is therefore a history of assembly and transformation, even when a particular copy appears physically complete.

The Mao family recension became influential in the seventeenth century and remains central to what many modern readers encounter. It reshaped the textual presentation and commentary of the novel, and the familiar 120-chapter organization is associated with that later form. An early printing with 240 shorter divisions is therefore related to, but not simply identical with, the version in which this lecture series organizes its narrative chapters.

This distinction has practical consequences for citations and teaching. A statement such as “chapter forty-six says” identifies a place in a selected recension, not a timeless location that every historical copy necessarily shares. When a disputed scene matters, the research notes should name the edition and distinguish a translation's editorial decisions from the wording of its Chinese textual base. The narration can remain approachable while the supporting apparatus stays exact.

## Three comparisons that change the reading

The first comparison is the peach garden. History documents the closeness of Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei; fiction gives them an oath among flowering trees. The historical fact supports the emotional premise. The scene makes the bond ceremonial and gives the story a promise against which future choices may be judged. Its literary function is so strong that it can become part of public memory even among readers who have never opened Chen Shou.

The second is Zhou Yu at Red Cliffs. The historical southern commander was central to the defeat of Cao Cao. The novel preserves his importance but repeatedly makes him jealous of Zhuge Liang and susceptible to intellectual humiliation. Comparing the sources changes the political interpretation. Instead of imagining Wu saved by an outsider whose genius its own commander cannot endure, one sees a coalition in which Sun Quan, Zhou Yu, Lu Su, Huang Gai, and other southern leaders had their own strategic capacities. The novel's preference for the Shu characters has altered the distribution of credit.

The third is Zhuge Liang himself. He was a consequential administrator and commander, and he died during a northern campaign in 234. The novel surrounds him with perfectly timed stratagems, supernatural signs, and a wooden image that seems able to frighten Sima Yi after his death. The difference is not a choice between believing everything and believing nothing. A historically situated minister has become the center of a story about the extraordinary possibility and ultimate limits of intelligence.

A fourth example concerns the southern campaign and Meng Huo. An earlier anecdote reports repeated captures and releases. The novel elaborates the story with detailed contests, exoticized peoples, and supernatural hazards. The later material reveals what Chinese imperial culture found entertaining and morally reassuring about a tale of conquest. It is far less reliable as a description of the diverse communities actually living in Nanzhong. Here the historical reading must account for whose perspective organizes the narrative and whose perspectives are largely absent.

## Legitimacy and the narrator's sympathies

The novel's stance toward political legitimacy is one of its central themes. Liu Bei claims Han descent and builds a western state in the name of restoration. Cao Cao serves the Han emperor while accumulating power; Cao Pi later establishes Wei. Sun Quan and his successors hold a southern realm with its own military and administrative history. The novel often gives greater moral sympathy to Liu Bei's cause, but the historical sources employ different conventions for identifying rightful rulers. A text's use of imperial titles is not a neutral measure of which regime all contemporaries accepted.

The politics of the novel's composition also matter. Writers and editors working long after the Three Kingdoms lived in societies with their own debates about loyalty, dynastic replacement, and the dangers of powerful ministers. A story about whether one may abandon a dynasty for an effective usurper could have significance far beyond the third century. It would be a mistake to identify every fictional speech as a coded reference to one particular event in the author's age. It would also be a mistake to assume that a later historical novel has no concerns beyond its distant setting.

An effective reading can hold three questions apart. What does the story ask us to admire or condemn? What do historical witnesses support about the event or person? And why might a later audience have found this version satisfying? Those questions produce different answers and can enrich one another. A fictional oath can illuminate an ideal of loyalty without proving the oath occurred. A critical history can correct the novel's portrait of Zhou Yu without destroying the dramatic force of the rivalry. A commentary on publication history can explain why one recension became familiar without declaring earlier versions worthless.

## When a later narrator chooses a rightful kingdom

The three historical states all possessed supporters with reasons to call their rulers legitimate. Wei held the emperor's court before its dynastic transition, controlled much of the north, and developed institutions able to survive its founders. Shu claimed continuity with the Han line and preserved the image of a royal kinsman struggling to restore the old order. Wu governed a substantial southern territory through a long line of military and administrative work. A historian must describe each claim without automatically adopting the novel's preferred resolution.

The novel's sympathies are not hidden. It repeatedly invites affection for Liu Bei's cause and for Zhuge Liang's service. Cao Cao can be capable and admirable in particular moments while remaining an antagonist of that cause. The political evaluation is not simply an accidental feature added by careless storytellers. It gives the reader a center from which to interpret a crowded and sometimes bewildering history. Without a chosen moral axis, the book could risk becoming an endless succession of campaigns whose stakes are difficult to feel.

The cost of this design is that capable opponents can become foils for the favored heroes. Zhou Yu's achievements, for example, are refracted through scenes that enlarge Zhuge Liang's supremacy. The reader who compares the historical record with the novel can restore an appreciation of the southern command without losing the enjoyment of the literary rivalry. Similarly, one can read Cao Cao as a brilliant and sometimes violent state-builder without forcing every surviving action into the category of villainy.

The source comparison is therefore an exercise in double vision. One view follows the novelist's construction of a morally intelligible world. The other reconstructs what the records permit us to say about events, institutions, and participants. Where the views conflict, the conflict is informative. It tells us what later communities wanted the ancient wars to mean and how the historical participants could be reshaped to carry those expectations.

## How translation and adaptation change the experience

The novel has been translated, abridged, illustrated, adapted for stage and screen, and made into games. A short retelling may preserve only the most famous heroes and battles, losing much of the later material concerning succession and the rise of Jin. A modern adaptation may change a character's motives, simplify a political alignment, or relocate a feat to improve pacing. Such works are evidence of later reception and creative interpretation, not independent witnesses to third-century events.

Translation choices can also affect judgment. The term often rendered as *romance* in the English title does not mean a love story; it points toward an expansive historical narrative with fiction and interpretation. Personal names may have alternate romanizations. Titles such as prime minister, king, duke, and emperor need explanation because their exact historical offices may differ from a modern reader's expectations. The same Chinese expression may be translated into markedly different English moral vocabulary. When an interpretation depends on a single word or phrase, a responsible analysis checks the original rather than resting entirely on one English rendering.

Moss Roberts's complete English translation provides one major modern reference point, and the Chinese text can be consulted through digital collections such as the Chinese Text Project. Scholarly work on Ming-Qing fiction commentary and early editions offers additional context. A reader does not need to master all of this scholarship before enjoying the story. It is most useful when an episode makes a factual claim, when two translations differ significantly, or when the novel's moral framing seems to leave a person or community without a voice.


## What deserves to survive in a retelling

A lecture meant to be heard should not recite footnotes whenever a character draws a sword. The scene needs room to live. A listener should understand why Guan Yu longs to return to Liu Bei before being asked to compare versions of the five passes. The burning ships at Red Cliffs should be vivid before the chronology of printed editions is introduced. But the story can make room for the history of its telling at moments when those facts change the interpretation of the scene.

An evidence-aware retelling can therefore make a simple promise: events from the novel will be told as events within the novel; independently documented history will be identified as such; and major departures will be explained where they shape the reader's picture of a person or of the war. There is no need to interrupt every invented conversation. The critical interventions should be concentrated where an invention has become widely mistaken for a witnessed historical event, or where the novel's allocation of moral credit conceals an important accomplishment.

The *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* endures because it gives political history the form of choices made by people whose relationships we can follow. It also endures because its imagined choices raise questions that cannot be settled by determining whether a scene happened. What does a leader owe to a friend when the needs of a population diverge? When is a refusal to fight an act of prudence? Can a servant remain loyal to a dynasty by resisting its reigning court? How much trust can a ruler place in one brilliant adviser? The questions remain worth considering even when the dialogue that gives them shape was composed centuries afterward.

The historical record gives us access to a difficult era through the perspectives and limitations of writers who lived closer to it. The novel gives us access to the ways later audiences wished to remember that era. Neither access is complete. Together they explain how a succession of wars became one of the most powerful works of world literature, a story whose heroes are remembered both for what the sources allow us to know and for what generations of readers have asked them to represent.

## History, fiction, and the meaning of an ending

The novel begins with the crisis of a dynasty and ends with the reunification of the realm under Jin. The historical dates are identifiable; the plotted symmetry is literary. A reader who knows the end may think the result was predetermined. But the surviving record describes many decisions whose consequences were not known to the people making them. Sun Quan could not have predicted in 208 exactly what would happen in 280. Zhuge Liang did not know that his campaigns would be remembered through a later novel that made his perfect foresight a defining trait.

The novel's conclusion has a tragic force because the heroic cause it favors does not win. The book is not a simple record of successful moral action. It is an inquiry into the pressures that divide personal loyalty, political legitimacy, strategic competence, and historical outcome. The historian can explain that some of its most memorable scenes are fictional while also recognizing that those inventions make these conflicts visible with extraordinary clarity.

What we know comes through many kinds of evidence. Chen Shou supplies an early account organized around people and states. Pei Songzhi preserves and tests additional reports. Later histories reshape the chronology and political interpretation. Storytellers and dramatists create scenes suited to memory and performance. The surviving 1522 printing and later recensions show how a long narrative became an enduring book. Modern scholarship compares the witnesses and studies their transmission. The value of reading *Three Kingdoms* carefully is that we can hear a powerful story and still ask precise questions of every layer behind it.
