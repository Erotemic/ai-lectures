# Three Kingdoms, Part I: The Peach Garden and the Burning Capital

A man stands before a recruiting notice in a northern market town. The notice asks volunteers to help put down a rebellion. There is nothing commanding about the man's appearance, and he has no army to lead. He makes sandals and mats, has a modest livelihood, and claims descent from the old imperial house. Yet he sighs so deeply at the summons that another man stops to ask him why. The stranger is Zhang Fei: loud, wealthy enough to help equip soldiers, impatient with talk, and immediately attracted by the prospect of action. A third man, Guan Yu, will join them. Soon three men who were strangers in a marketplace will swear to treat one another as brothers, and their oath will help determine the fate of the realm.

This is the opening movement of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, the historical novel conventionally attributed to Luo Guanzhong. It tells the long disintegration of the Han empire and the struggle to establish a legitimate order in its place. Its history stretches from the Yellow Turban uprising of 184 through the eventual reunification of China under the Jin in 280. The novel reached a surviving printed form centuries after those events: the earliest known edition was published in 1522, and the familiar 120-chapter version was shaped by later editors. We can follow the drama without confusing these different dates. Historical figures and battles provide the narrative's framework, but conversations, secret motives, heroic combats, and many defining episodes are literary constructions.

The first question the novel asks is what loyalty means when the government to which one owes loyalty is already failing. The three men in the market will say they want to rescue the Han. So will other commanders. The officials claiming to act for the court will betray it. Leaders who swear to oppose tyrants will begin thinking of territory and inheritance. We will follow the events closely enough to feel the attractiveness of the characters, then ask why this same story permits admiration and distrust to grow together.

## An empire still standing on paper

For generations the Han dynasty had supplied a language of authority across much of China. Officials could refer to law, appointment, rank, ancestral obligation, and service to the emperor. Yet the late second century was marked by court factional struggles, unequal access to land and power, pressure on households, and distrust of central government. A political order can possess prestigious titles and still be unable to persuade people that its officers deserve obedience. The novel compresses this complicated background into an image of a sick imperial household. Portents appear, ministers intrigue, and people suffer while the court debates who may advise the throne.

The Yellow Turban movement is a decisive shock. Zhang Jue, also called Zhang Jiao, and his brothers organize followers using healing, religious authority, and promises of an age transformed. The novel makes their movement a threatening rebellion, introducing supernatural practices alongside the practical danger of coordinated uprisings. The historical events are not merely a colorful prologue: the rebellion of 184 was real, and the effort to suppress it drew military power toward provincial commanders. A dynasty that depends upon local armed forces to defend its sovereignty may discover that those forces acquire their own sources of authority.

In the novel, Liu Bei reads the recruiting proclamation and grieves for the country. He is described as descended from the Han imperial clan, though his life is humble. His claim gives him a reason to act and a name with possible future value. Zhang Fei, who challenges him in the market, insists that sorrow is useless unless someone gathers troops. They talk in a drinking place and encounter Guan Yu, a large, formidable traveler with a long beard and a history of fleeing punishment after acting against a local bully. The story gives each a recognizable orientation. Liu Bei listens and worries about right conduct. Zhang Fei demands immediate action. Guan Yu weighs his commitments and acts with concentrated force.

The men go to Zhang Fei's peach garden. There, amid flowering trees, they perform a ritual of brotherhood. They pledge to aid one another, to rescue the distressed, and to live and die in fidelity to their shared cause. Liu Bei becomes the elder brother, Guan Yu the second, Zhang Fei the youngest. The scene is familiar enough that audiences sometimes assume it was recorded as history. Chen Shou's third-century *Records of the Three Kingdoms* does describe the closeness of the men; it does not document the peach-garden ceremony. The novel has taken a historically attested closeness and given it a memorable beginning. A formal oath turns affection into a binding public principle, and it raises a question the book will answer at considerable cost: what happens when private brotherhood competes with responsibility to soldiers and subjects?

## The rebellion as seen from a village

It is worth imagining how different the Yellow Turban movement would look from the capital and from a farming settlement. At court, officials hear reports of rebels gathering under religious signs and threatening administrative control. To a household facing debt, crop failure, or the abuse of a local official, a movement promising healing and a transformed age might seem to answer needs that the dynasty no longer addresses. The novel's portrayal of Zhang Jue and his followers is shaped by the perspective of those who defeat the rebellion. That perspective matters, and so does the fact that the rebellion itself could involve violence against people who did not wish to join it.

The source traditions describe the movement as connected to the Way of Great Peace and to forms of religious organization capable of reaching multiple districts. A promise that the old order was ending made political sense because many people experienced the old order as failing. The novel gives the insurgents supernatural powers and lets the heroes defeat them in memorable engagements. A historical account must also consider how communities became dependent on armed groups for protection and how the suppression of one uprising created opportunities for military commanders to maintain forces beyond the period of emergency.

Imagine a local magistrate trying to decide whether to open a town's gates to passing soldiers. If the soldiers claim to be fighting rebels, the town might be safer with them. But feeding the troops could exhaust local stores, and the commander's future intentions would be uncertain. A peasant who refused the rebels' summons might still suffer when the district became a battleground. The novel's warriors arrive in a world where innocent civilians have already lost freedom of choice. This background explains why Liu Bei's claim to act on behalf of suffering people is more than a decorative virtue. It is also why every later instance of his army endangering those same people deserves scrutiny.

The Yellow Turban uprising does not by itself explain the entire fall of Han. Court factionalism, regional military arrangements, landholding, fiscal pressures, and succession conflicts continued to interact. But the revolt made a decisive demand on the state: to reassert control, it would empower the provincial officers whose forces could later resist central instructions. The process contains a trap. The army raised to defend the dynasty can become the army that prevents the dynasty from governing.

## The three companions before they become symbols

The Peach Garden meeting provides the novel with an origin that no appointment at the Han court could provide. Liu Bei belongs to a line associated with the imperial family, but his everyday circumstances are modest. Guan Yu arrives as a man carrying a history of conflict. Zhang Fei has resources and a temperament that makes him capable of immense warmth and sudden anger. They bring different things to the relationship. One can gather confidence through his name, another through restraint and formidable skill, and another through energy and material support. None yet possesses a kingdom.

The garden lets the audience hear the moment when ambition is stated as a shared commitment. The men choose one another, and the choice obliges them to face hardship as a group. The novel gives this beginning the formal beauty of ritual. It also creates expectations difficult for any human relationship to satisfy. As the three men become commanders, they will acquire duties to officers, families, and populations who have not joined this private promise. The tenderness of the first scene prepares the severity of that later conflict.

Historically, early accounts describe Liu Bei's close relationship with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. The familiar ceremony among peach blossoms is part of the literary tradition. This distinction allows us to see how story can give a documented association a dramatic foundation. We do not need a certified garden in order to appreciate the force of the vow within the novel. We need to understand how a personal bond can become a principle of government for men who have never yet governed.

The humble beginnings are also politically useful. A tale of imperial blood alone would give Liu Bei inherited standing. A tale of a common laboring life and chosen companions gives him a claim on ordinary listeners. He does not arrive as a remote ruler; he grows into the prospect of rulership through service, hardship, and dependence upon other people. The novel's opening thus makes later power answerable to an origin in which loyalty was promised without any certainty of reward.

## First battles, first recognition

Zhang Fei provides means to raise volunteers. Wealthy supporters contribute horses and equipment. Blacksmiths make weapons, including the polearms that become bound to Guan Yu and Zhang Fei in the imagination of later readers. Liu Bei's men go out to fight the Yellow Turbans, first in small encounters and then within campaigns commanded by officials with established positions. The brothers defeat enemy leaders, rescue threatened allies, and establish their courage. The battles have the satisfying clarity of martial romance. The hero sees danger, selects a difficult task, and charges at the right moment.

But the setting does not allow competence to become a simple path to promotion. Liu Bei receives only a minor appointment. When an inspector arrives and seems determined to extract a bribe, Zhang Fei loses patience. He ties the man up and beats him. Liu Bei cannot approve the violence, and the incident helps drive the brothers away from their modest foothold. The episode is comic, but its institutional implications are serious. In a system where advancement depends upon patrons and dishonest officials, the diligent and the angry may both conclude that normal procedures will not reward service. Zhang Fei's instinct is to punish the immediate offender. Liu Bei's instinct is to preserve a claim to honorable conduct. Neither can repair the system.

Some commanders gain reputations during the rebellion and its suppression. Among them is Cao Cao, a talented figure whose family links and early career give him access to official power. The novel will repeatedly characterize Cao Cao through speed of decision, a willingness to choose harsh means, and a formidable capacity to attract capable officers. It also introduces Sun Jian, a commander associated with the south, whose military success will shape the future opportunities of his sons. For the moment these men can appear to be serving a common enterprise: the preservation of the dynasty. The story's dramatic irony lies in knowing that the same service equips rival political houses.

At the capital, a different contest proceeds. Emperor Ling dies. The child ruler's relatives and the palace eunuchs struggle to control the succession and the court. General He Jin plans to eliminate the eunuchs. Others advise caution, warning that bringing troops toward the capital may turn one problem into something far worse. These are conversations about the architecture of power. Can court officials remove their opponents without giving a regional general the means to dictate the succession? He Jin's plan is undone by assassination. Violence spills into the palace, and the court's attempt to end factional rule instead creates an opening for a military strongman.

## Who governs when the emperor is a child?

The palace struggle after Emperor Ling's death turns on the vulnerability of a succession. A reigning emperor is not merely an individual with a family; he is also the office around which countless officials orient their service. When a child inherits that office, relatives and attendants gain opportunities to act in his name. The emperor's mother and her family, palace eunuchs, and civil officers may each claim to protect the throne. The court's public language remains one of obedience even as its members disagree over who may issue the instructions that count as the sovereign's will.

He Jin's decision to call outside forces toward the capital is therefore more than a tactical error. It is an attempt to solve an internal court problem by introducing a new independent power. The advisers who oppose him fear that the cure may give a frontier general leverage over the throne. When He Jin is killed and Dong Zhuo arrives, the warning becomes reality. The novel organizes the sequence through dramatic personal choices, but its political mechanism can be stated plainly: control of an army is not equivalent to an appointed place in a palace, and an outsider who possesses force need not honor the factions that invited him.

Dong Zhuo's deposition of one young emperor and installation of another reveals how a formal procedure can be used under pressure. The imperial family does not disappear. Rites continue and proclamations can still be written. What changes is who can make the decision stick. A minister who protests may be threatened or killed; an officer who refuses may be attacked. A later historian can record the reign of Emperor Xian as an authentic span of the Han dynasty while also describing his dependence on generals. The existence of a continuous dynastic calendar does not imply continuous independence of government.

The novel's sense of catastrophe becomes clearer when we remember this distinction. The fall of a state need not begin on the day someone publicly declares that the dynasty is over. It may begin when its regular offices cease to determine outcomes. People continue to obey the old titles because they still matter, but obedience is increasingly mediated through whoever can command armed men. The emperor's household becomes a place where contestants argue over the meaning of allegiance while actual policy is decided elsewhere.

## Dong Zhuo comes to the capital

Dong Zhuo, a frontier commander, approaches Luoyang with soldiers. In the chaos he encounters the young princes, and he quickly sees that controlling the emperor is more valuable than occupying any ordinary fortress. He installs himself near the center of decision-making, intimidates officials, and promotes his own men. The novel paints him as openly cruel: he feasts, threatens opponents, and treats the imperial household as something he may rearrange by command. His power rests on armies and fear, yet he still wants acts that look like lawful decisions. He does not burn the language of legitimacy immediately. He uses it.

Dong Zhuo proposes to depose the reigning young emperor and place his younger brother on the throne. In the court, Ding Yuan resists. Dong Zhuo's subordinate officers calculate the danger. Ding Yuan commands a warrior of exceptional skill, Lü Bu, whose force makes direct attack costly. A scheme therefore replaces open battle. Gifts, including a magnificent horse, are used to persuade Lü Bu to abandon his patron. In the novel he kills Ding Yuan and joins Dong Zhuo, who treats him as an adopted son. Lü Bu's immediate advantage is immense. He has gained wealth, an excellent mount, and the favor of the strongest man near the throne. But by purchasing his loyalty, Dong Zhuo demonstrates how it may be purchased again.

Here the novel begins a pattern in which an apparently private act of betrayal exposes a general political weakness. A commander who changes allegiance for rewards cannot be trusted simply because his new lord offers greater rewards. A ruler who uses such a man may enjoy a short triumph while inviting a future disaster. The book's interest in martial ability is never only about whether one champion can defeat another. It asks who can rely on the champion when the terms of obligation change.

The deposition proceeds. The older young emperor is removed; the Prince of Chenliu becomes Emperor Xian. The act makes Dong Zhuo seem to command the dynasty itself. The court continues to issue orders, but the threat behind those orders comes from an individual military household. Officials who oppose the decision cannot simply appeal to the emperor, because the emperor has become the person least able to enforce an appeal. In a striking reversal, the institution that should legitimate a commander is being manipulated by the commander to legitimate his power.

## A sword that cannot be used

Cao Cao enters the intrigue. In the novel he appears to gain Dong Zhuo's confidence while privately considering how to kill him. The plot centers on an elaborate sword, which Cao Cao carries into the tyrant's presence. Dong Zhuo is at ease, and a moment seems to present itself. Then the ruler notices something suspicious. Cao Cao converts the gesture into a presentation of the blade as a gift. The intended assassination collapses into a performance of submission, and Cao Cao departs before he can be detained.

The scene is memorable because its decisive action does not happen. Cao Cao's intelligence lies in recognizing that the opportunity is gone and changing the apparent meaning of what he is doing. With a single change of interpretation, a weapon that was meant to end a government becomes a courteous tribute to it. Such reversals will dominate the novel. The person who controls what an encounter seems to mean often survives longer than the person who simply has the stronger sword.

Cao Cao flees the capital. The novel gives him an episode at the household of Lü Boshe, a man linked with his father. Cao Cao and a companion hear noises that they interpret as preparations for murder. Acting on this fear, Cao Cao kills members of the household. The tragic discovery follows: what they heard concerned the slaughter of a pig for a meal. Cao Cao also kills the returning host, lest word spread. The anecdote is accompanied by a maxim expressing his readiness to wrong the world rather than let the world wrong him. Versions of the Lü Boshe incident occur in older materials, but their details and moral framing vary; the novel has arranged them to establish a stark portrait.

A reader might expect this to settle Cao Cao's moral status forever. Instead the book repeatedly gives him intelligence, courage, administrative aptitude, and relationships that invite respect. That tension is fundamental. A capable ruler can be violent, and a leader with honorable language can make disastrous choices. The novel's contrast between Liu Bei and Cao Cao is real, but its best scenes resist treating success as a simple certificate of virtue. We can judge the crime in this household without denying why many people later find Cao Cao worth serving.

## A coalition without a common will

Cao Cao helps organize a call to arms against Dong Zhuo. Regional lords assemble with banners, provisions, and claims of loyalty to the Han. Yuan Shao is placed at the head of the coalition. His aristocratic family name is valuable; it gives the alliance a shared language of pedigree. The assembled commanders have large armies, but their purposes are not identical. Some want the emperor rescued, some want Dong Zhuo removed, and others mainly want rivals to spend their troops first. The military problem is obvious: the coalition has forces that could fight if coordinated. The political problem is that no commander wants to surrender too much autonomy to the others.

Sun Jian advances effectively, but supplies are withheld or delayed through jealousy and maneuvering. A commander who fights well can still be made to lose by an ally who controls his grain. At a strategic pass Dong Zhuo's men resist. The novel introduces famous duels, including Guan Yu's swift killing of Hua Xiong. The deed raises the standing of Liu Bei's previously obscure band. When a high-ranking lord questions the right of an unknown officer to enter the contest, another figure wagers that talent may matter more than rank. Guan Yu returns while the wine poured before his departure is still warm. The scene condenses speed, courage, and the injustice of social prejudice into a few theatrical moments.

The coalition then confronts Lü Bu at Hulao Pass. Single champions fail to defeat him. Zhang Fei challenges the warrior, Guan Yu joins, and at last Liu Bei rides in beside his brothers. Three men who took an oath together now fight together against a fighter widely acknowledged as exceptional. The image is irresistible, and it serves a different purpose from the earlier market scene. In the market, their brotherhood was an intention. Here it has become a practical coordination of different temperaments under danger. The duel is a celebrated literary episode; the historical campaign should not be reconstructed from it as if generals settled interstate warfare by scheduled single combat.

The coalition fails to exploit its successes. Dong Zhuo withdraws westward and compels an enormous movement of the population toward Chang'an. Luoyang is ravaged and burned. Officials and ordinary households are displaced, and a capital that once embodied imperial continuity becomes a scene of devastation. The burning gives the novel's opening its cruelest political image. A man professing to govern on behalf of the emperor destroys the city's material foundations to preserve his own immediate security. Imperial legitimacy does not keep buildings standing or people safe when its guardian has become their chief danger.

## A coalition that eats its own strength

The anti-Dong campaign contains several kinds of military risk. One commander must advance first toward fortifications, drawing fire and exhausting provisions. Another may remain in reserve, hoping to enter after the enemy weakens. A third controls the supply line and can decide whether the bold commander receives sufficient grain. The coalition needs a common purpose because its members must incur unequal costs for a shared objective. Without credible commitments, the leader ordered to advance may suspect that his allies are using his losses as an opportunity for themselves.

Sun Jian's predicament makes the danger concrete. He is capable of serious fighting, yet success depends upon supplies whose control lies partly with other members of the alliance. If they withhold grain because they fear his rising influence, the entire coalition pays for their rivalry. The novel exposes this pattern through personality conflicts, but the reasoning applies beyond any one character. A force made of independent commands cannot always turn numbers into effective military pressure. The problem is one of coordinated logistics and shared trust.

Guan Yu's warm-wine feat provides a deliberately different image. A champion enters a contest; the result is immediately apparent; the wine is still warm. Nothing about provisioning, diplomacy, or divided command obscures the hero's excellence. The audience feels the satisfaction of a clear demonstration of worth against class prejudice. The juxtaposition explains why martial romance needs both kinds of scene. The duel satisfies a moral desire for talent to be recognized. The coalition's failure reminds us that a society cannot be governed by the recognition of individual excellence alone.

The story's southern commander Sun Jian and its northern commander Cao Cao are both learning what a coalition can and cannot provide. A wise contender may become less interested in leading a gathering of proud lords than in building one force whose supply, appointments, and decisions answer to him. That institutional ambition helps explain the shape of the future war. The large alliance can repel a tyrant but struggles to govern itself; smaller commands that consolidate internally become the foundations of later states.

## A capital as the home of ordinary lives

When Dong Zhuo burns Luoyang and forces the population to march west, the event is easily swallowed by the grandeur of the tale. A capital is not only a palace complex and ancestral temples. It contains shops, workshops, warehouses, schools, family houses, cemeteries, and neighborhoods whose inhabitants have spent years building relationships of work and care. The destruction of such a place affects people who never attended a coalition council and never chose to become part of Dong Zhuo's policy.

An order to relocate a court might sound almost administrative in a summary of campaigns. In lived experience it means carts on congested roads, lost goods, illness, frightened children, abandoned graves, and families unable to carry all they possess. The novel's picture of a tyrant burning palaces becomes more serious when the burning is understood as a decision imposed on a living city. The ruler preserves a military advantage by transferring the cost to subjects whose protection was supposed to justify his authority.

This is the political standard by which all the later contenders should be measured. Cao Cao will sometimes protect damaged districts and sometimes devastate them. Liu Bei will sometimes risk his army to keep fleeing civilians together and sometimes lead wars in which civilians pay for his private commitments. Sun Quan's officers will defend southern cities while also contesting control of Jing. The opening catastrophe at Luoyang gives the listener a reference point outside the reputations of great men. The consequences for people living under their power remain part of every judgment the novel invites.


## A seal in the ruins

Among the ruins Sun Jian's men discover the imperial jade seal. In the novel this object is a condensed token of rightful rule, a treasure that seems to carry the mandate of a dynasty in the hand. Sun Jian conceals the discovery instead of reporting it to the coalition. His secrecy invites suspicion and helps fracture the alliance. The incident is powerful because it brings the contradiction between shared purpose and private ambition into one portable object. The lords say they have come to save the Han, but a sign of Han authority becomes something that one house might keep for itself.

The seal will acquire a troubled career in later episodes. What matters here is the temptation it creates. If possessing a symbolic object could make a ruler legitimate, one might hope to seize the object instead of doing the difficult work of winning trust, protecting communities, and commanding an administration. Yet the symbol is not meaningless. People fight over it because public recognition matters, and recognition can shape which claims other powerful families decide to honor. The novel never wholly separates real political action from the stories told about political authority.

The allied lords disperse. Yuan Shao and other contenders turn toward their own territories. Cao Cao survives to build a base. Sun Jian has acquired an object that may increase his family's stature while magnifying its danger. The sworn brothers remain far from secure power. Dong Zhuo still has the emperor. There has been courage, a series of famous duels, and enormous destruction, but no successful rescue of the state.

The failed coalition also supplies a useful way to read the rest of the book. An alliance is a promise about future conduct. Its strength depends on whether the members can be trusted after the immediate advantage of cooperation has passed. The peach-garden oath and the anti-Dong coalition are therefore two versions of the same problem on different scales. Three men may keep their word even at personal cost. A larger gathering, equipped with armies and official ranks, may find no agreed price at which to risk its interests. Friendship and statecraft use some of the same moral vocabulary, but they operate under different pressures.


## The difficulty of serving a ruined dynasty

Consider the choices facing a provincial officer at the end of this movement. Dong Zhuo holds the emperor. Some regional commanders are gathering revenues and recruiting soldiers in their own names. A public official could remain loyal by serving the palace, but service might mean obedience to the tyrant. He could fight the tyrant, but his forces would have to take orders from coalition leaders who might be aiming at their own crowns. He could withdraw and protect a local population, but that would abandon the idea that the empire ought to have one central sovereign. These are genuine conflicts of obligation, and the novel draws much of its power from keeping them open.

Liu Bei's claim to kinship with the Han gives him a goal: he will seek to restore legitimate rule. Yet he begins as a minor leader dependent on hospitality from others. His honor is attractive, but it gives him few immediate advantages. Cao Cao, by contrast, acts decisively under pressure, reads the intentions of opponents, and has comparatively little hesitation about harsh methods. Sun Jian is a brave commander who also sees dynastic opportunity. Yuan Shao has lineage and numbers, but the coalition exposes his limitations as a leader of other ambitious men. Dong Zhuo can compel obedience, yet his very methods erode any durable reason to serve him.

The book's structure makes it easy to admire one character in a scene and reconsider him later. The first thing we learn about a protagonist need not be his permanent definition. Zhang Fei's anger can punish injustice and endanger a plan. Guan Yu's commitment can strengthen a bond and make compromise difficult. Cao Cao's improvisation can defeat tyranny and cover atrocity. Sun Jian's courage can coexist with secrecy. This is why a serious telling needs more than a list of battles. Each victory establishes a disposition that will matter when circumstances change.

The fictional oath is especially illuminating. Its historical absence from the earliest surviving account does not make it trivial. The novel supplies something the documentary record usually cannot: a clearly staged declaration of what the participants say they value before a lifetime has tested them. Later choices can be measured against that declaration. Readers who recognize the ritual as a literary scene can still ask why it became so persuasive. Perhaps the attraction lies in the simplicity of loyalty between people when loyalty to institutions has become hopelessly compromised.

For now the three brothers have survived their first fighting and encountered the obstacles of the wider world. The rebel armies have exposed the weakness of the Han. The court has fallen under a general whose claim to restore order depends on terror. A coalition of respectable commanders has failed because its members cannot agree to bear common costs. The capital has burned. And a seal, found amid the destruction, has offered its possessors the fantasy that sovereignty can be carried away in a sleeve. The novel's opening is complete in that contrast: political symbols endure, but the lives those symbols should protect are already being broken.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 1–6 of the common 120-chapter text. Principal events are told as incidents of the novel. The peach-garden ceremony, three-brothers duel at Hulao Pass, Guan Yu's warm-wine feat, and the sword presentation are literary scenes; do not present them as attested battlefield or court records. Chen Shou's biographies establish the closeness of Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei without documenting the formal oath. Note variation in historical/fictional representations of the Lü Boshe massacre. For historical context compare the *Sanguozhi*, Pei Songzhi's annotations, and *Hou Han shu*.
