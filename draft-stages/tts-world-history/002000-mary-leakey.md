# Mary Leakey: Reading the Footprints of Deep Time

Imagine finding a footprint in dried mud. It is a modest piece of evidence. You can say that something with a particular kind of foot stood there. Perhaps you can infer which way it moved, how long its stride was, or whether another set of tracks crossed its path. But the creature is gone. It may have left no skeleton anywhere nearby. Its name, its intentions, and the rest of its life are beyond your reach.

Now imagine that the footprint is three million, six hundred and sixty thousand years old.

At Laetoli, in northern Tanzania, researchers uncovered a sequence of tracks left by early members of the human lineage. The track makers had walked upright, placing one foot ahead of the other, across a surface of volcanic ash that later hardened and survived beneath more sediment. The prints are so old that the first cities, agriculture, writing, and the whole span of recorded history lie near the very end of the time separating those walkers from us.

Mary Leakey led the expedition responsible for bringing these tracks to scientific attention. She had spent decades studying stone tools, fossils, buried landscapes, and the small physical traces that people and other animals leave behind. She was one of the central figures in establishing East Africa as a source of evidence about the deep history of our lineage. But she did not arrive at Laetoli knowing the tracks were there. Nor was her career a straight line from one famous discovery to another.

Her achievement is easier to understand if we follow how she learned to read evidence, why the ground in East Africa can preserve millions of years of history, and how a handful of teeth, chipped stones, and shallow depressions in ancient ash changed the questions scientists asked about human evolution.

## Learning to see

Mary Douglas Nicol was born in London in 1913. Her father was a landscape painter, and her childhood included extended periods in France, where she visited prehistoric sites and saw cave art. The walls and floors of those caves held tangible evidence of people who had lived long before the civilizations described in most schoolbooks.

That experience offered a distinctive way into history. A historical text tells you what someone chose to write, or what a later writer decided to preserve. A cave painting, a stone blade, or an old hearth offers a different sort of testimony. You have to examine the object and reconstruct the choices that produced it. If a stone has been shaped, which marks were made deliberately? Which came from a later break? If several objects lie together, were they left during one occupation or washed together by water centuries afterward?

Mary developed a talent for drawing such objects accurately. She joined excavations in England as a teenager, working with the archaeologist Dorothy Liddell at Hembury in Devon. Drawing was more than decoration. Before the availability of digital photography and three-dimensional scanning, a careful archaeological illustration could show the angle of a flake scar, the outline of an artifact, and the differences between similar specimens more clearly than a casual photograph.

Try picturing a broken stone in your hand. One surface has a rounded bulge where it was struck. Several thin scars run away from the point of impact. The pattern tells a possible story: someone hit a larger stone with force, detached a sharp piece, and perhaps repeated the action to shape the edge. The illustrator must distinguish the marks that support that story from random damage. The scientific skill lies in learning what differences are worth recording.

Mary did not take a conventional university-degree route into archaeology. She acquired much of her training in excavations, through reading and illustration, and in the company of people already doing field research. This was a period when professional scientific institutions offered limited opportunities to women, especially those who lacked formal credentials. Her route was made possible by artistic skill and personal connections, but it still required the work of becoming technically reliable.

In the early 1930s she met Louis Leakey, a British-Kenyan archaeologist and anthropologist with an ambitious interest in African prehistory. They married in 1936. Louis had a flair for broad hypotheses, fundraising, and public explanation. Mary became known for patient excavation, close observation, and rigorous recording. The partnership was productive, but it could also make their individual contributions difficult for outsiders to distinguish. The name 'the Leakeys' came to stand for a large enterprise involving many more people than the couple.

## Why look in Africa?

Long before Mary began digging, Charles Darwin had suggested that Africa was a likely place to investigate the origins of the human lineage, partly because our closest living relatives, chimpanzees and gorillas, are African. It was a sensible inference, though a hypothesis is not a fossil. By the early twentieth century, scientists still argued over where and how our ancestors had evolved. European and Asian fossil finds received enormous attention, and many researchers were reluctant to place a small-brained African fossil near the beginning of the human story.

In 1924, a young fossil from South Africa, later called the Taung Child, was recognized by Raymond Dart as an early human relative. Its significance was controversial. The creature had a brain much smaller than that of a modern human, yet features of the skull suggested it might have held its head upright and moved on two legs. If that interpretation was correct, a large brain had not necessarily evolved before habitual walking.

That question would return decades later at Laetoli. It is worth keeping it in mind now: which traits made our ancestors recognizable as members of the human lineage, and in what order did those traits develop?

There is no reason to expect that all the changes happened together. Evolution does not follow a checklist in which an animal first stands up, then makes a tool, then becomes intelligent, then invents language. Different populations can acquire different combinations of traits, and several related species may coexist. Fossils preserve only a small, uneven sample of that history. A discovery can change which branching patterns are plausible without revealing the exact path to living humans.

The first great advantage of East African research was geographical and geological. The region includes parts of a vast rift system where the Earth's crust has stretched, fractured, and shifted. Ancient lake beds, sediments, and volcanic deposits were laid down over long periods. In some places, erosion later cut through them, exposing a layered record that had once been buried.

Think of a canyon wall as a book whose pages are made of sediment. A dark layer may record an ancient lake. Another may hold bones carried by water. A volcanic ash bed may mark an eruption that reached a wide area. The pages are not always complete, and geological forces can fold or interrupt them. Still, an archaeologist can ask whether a tool or fossil is above or below a particular ash layer, and a geologist can measure the age of minerals formed during the eruption.

This combination of stratigraphy, archaeology, and radiometric dating is one of the keys to Mary Leakey's work. A fossil rarely arrives with its own date engraved on it. Its age is established by its position in a sequence of deposits and by measurements on appropriate materials from that sequence. Getting the context right is therefore as important as recognizing the fossil.

## Olduvai, or Oldupai

The landscape most associated with Mary and Louis Leakey is a long gorge in northern Tanzania, generally called Olduvai Gorge in scientific literature. The locally grounded name Oldupai comes from a Maasai word referring to a plant found in the area. This difference in spelling is a small reminder that a place can have a history long before it enters the records of European expeditions.

Olduvai is extraordinary because erosion exposed archaeological horizons across a long span of time. Stone artifacts, animal bones, traces of ancient environments, and fossils of several kinds of early human relatives appear in different deposits. The gorge is sometimes called a cradle of humankind, but that phrase should be understood as a tribute to its evidence, not as a claim that every human ancestor lived in this one place.

Louis had visited the gorge before Mary joined him. Mary first went there in the 1930s and then returned over many seasons. For long periods the work yielded stone artifacts and animal fossils rather than the kind of spectacular hominin skull that could command newspaper headlines. Field camps were demanding. Excavating in intense heat with limited water, supplies, and equipment required practical organization as well as scientific persistence.

The excavation process itself is easy to underestimate. Suppose workers find a cluster of broken stones beside animal bones. It is tempting to imagine a prehistoric family making dinner. But many explanations can produce a cluster. The stones might have been washed downhill. Carnivores might have accumulated the bones. The objects might belong to different episodes separated by many years. Only careful mapping, geological study, analysis of fracture patterns, and comparison with other sites can begin to distinguish those possibilities.

One of Mary's enduring contributions was to take such assemblages seriously as things worthy of systematic description. She documented excavations and stone-tool industries at Olduvai in enough detail that later researchers could reconsider interpretations rather than simply accept a dramatic reconstruction of ancient life.

The simplest early stone tools at Olduvai are often described as Oldowan technology. Imagine holding a fist-sized cobble and striking it with another stone. A sharp flake can break away, leaving a scar on the core. The flake itself may be useful for cutting or scraping, while repeated blows can alter the core's shape. The technology does not require a modern-looking hand axe. Yet it involves controlled force, attention to the properties of stone, and an ability to reproduce an action.

Other deposits contain more extensively shaped large tools, including the hand axes associated with the Acheulean tradition. Those often show deliberate modification from several directions. The contrast between simple flakes and carefully worked large tools helps archaeologists discuss technological change, but the categories are not stages through which every community had to pass on a single ladder of progress.

An even more important caution concerns the toolmaker. Finding a tool beside a fossil does not by itself establish that the fossil belongs to the animal that made it. Different hominin species could live in the same landscape, sometimes at the same time. A stone can also be moved long after it was struck. Mary's fieldwork gave researchers the material needed to ask who might have made the tools, but the answers were going to change.

## An ape from a different world

One of Mary's first great fossil discoveries came in 1948 on Rusinga Island, in Lake Victoria, Kenya. She found a substantial portion of the skull of an ancient ape assigned to the genus Proconsul. The animal lived around eighteen million years ago, in the Miocene epoch.

Eighteen million years is a difficult interval to feel. The Laetoli footprints would be old even if we divided their age by five. Yet the Rusinga ape was older still. The find belonged to a much earlier chapter of primate evolution, well before the emergence of the human lineage as we ordinarily define it.

The fossil was scientifically valuable because skulls preserve relationships among structures that isolated teeth do not. The shape of the face, the arrangement of the jaws, and the proportions of the cranium can be studied together. Researchers can compare combinations of traits with those found in living and fossil primates. The interpretation of Proconsul's exact place among ape relatives has changed, but the discovery substantially enriched knowledge of early ape diversity.

This is a good place to distinguish two questions. 'Where did humans come from?' can mean where our own species, Homo sapiens, evolved. It can mean which earlier hominin species gave rise to our lineage. Or it can mean the far older evolutionary history of apes and other primates. Those questions operate on different timescales and require different evidence. A fossil ape from eighteen million years ago helps with the deepest question, without having to be the direct ancestor of human beings.

Mary's ability to recognize, excavate, and preserve such a specimen also showed why the unglamorous parts of the work mattered. The dramatic moment was finding the fossil. Much of the scientific achievement was keeping its pieces, positions, and associations intact long enough for other people to study them.

## The teeth in the hillside

On July 17, 1959, Mary was searching at Olduvai while Louis was ill in camp. She noticed teeth protruding from the ground. Excavation revealed more of a skull. The specimen became known as Olduvai Hominid Five, often shortened to O H Five, and was nicknamed Zinj.

The skull made a striking impression. It had massive cheek teeth, broad cheekbones, and bony structures associated with exceptionally strong chewing muscles. It seemed far removed from the rounded skull and modest teeth of a modern human. Louis and Mary initially gave it the name Zinjanthropus boisei. Its classification was subsequently revised; it is now generally called Paranthropus boisei.

Several details in this story are worth slowing down for. Mary located the specimen. Reconstructing it took extensive work on fragments, including the skilled labor of her collaborator Kimoya Kimeu. Louis played a major role in publicizing and interpreting the discovery. The familiar photograph or headline might feature the husband and wife, but scientific excavation is rarely the work of only the people whose names become famous.

The fossil came from deposits that could be studied together with volcanic layers. Developing radiometric dates for the sequence showed that the skull was roughly 1.8 million years old, far older than some expectations of the time. The exact numerical estimates and methods have been refined, but the impact was immediate: East Africa contained early human relatives of considerable antiquity in well-studied geological contexts.

The discovery also changed the Leakeys' fortunes. It attracted public attention and research support after years of difficult, sometimes poorly funded work. Headlines could make the fossil sound like a missing link, the long-sought creature halfway between an ape and a human. That phrase encourages the wrong picture of evolution.

Imagine a family tree. An aunt is related to you, but she is not an earlier version of you. An extinct species can tell us a great deal about the range of forms that existed in the human family without lying directly on the line that led to us. Paranthropus boisei is generally understood as part of a robust-jawed side branch that eventually disappeared. Its fossil is valuable precisely because the past contained such branches.

Its huge teeth posed another puzzle. The nickname 'Nutcracker Man' suggested an animal constantly smashing hard nuts with its jaws. The anatomy shows that it was capable of powerful chewing. Later evidence from microscopic wear and chemical signatures of diet complicated the simple image of a dedicated nut eater. Teeth can tell us about the kinds of forces an animal could handle; they do not automatically reveal every meal it preferred.

In the years after the discovery of Zinj, finds attributed to early Homo at Olduvai added to the debate about stone-tool manufacture. Homo habilis, formally named in 1964, was proposed as a more likely maker of some tools than the robust-jawed species. But a convenient name meaning 'handy man' is no proof of exclusive ownership. Much earlier stone-tool discoveries elsewhere in Africa have since widened the question of which hominins could make tools. The Olduvai evidence remains foundational even though its first interpretations were revised.

That is an essential part of Mary's story. A discovery can remain important after the explanation originally attached to it has been rejected. The specimen is still there. Its geological context is still informative. New techniques and new fossils can change what scientists believe it represents.

## A scientist in her own right

It would be possible to tell the history of the Leakeys as a succession of celebrated family finds and miss Mary's most distinctive work. Over many seasons she became responsible for large excavation programs, with rigorous systems of collecting, mapping, and publishing evidence. Her major Olduvai volumes, including a 1971 account of excavations in Beds One and Two, are records of sustained archaeological work rather than announcements of a single discovery.

There is a difference between a fossil that changes the argument and a body of evidence that makes the argument testable. A fossil may appear in a newspaper. A site plan showing exactly where tools and bones were found is what allows another archaeologist to ask whether the two belong together. A list of stone-tool types can seem tedious until someone proposes a new theory about technological behavior and needs to compare hundreds of objects, not three famous examples.

Mary was also interested in the much more recent history represented by African rock art. In the Kondoa region of Tanzania she documented paintings in rock shelters, drawing on the skills she had practiced since adolescence. These images came from people who lived vastly later than the fossil apes and early hominins of her better-known discoveries. They depicted animals and human activities, and their preservation raised its own problems: weathering, damage, and the loss of visible traces over time. She published a major illustrated account in 1983 called Africa's Vanishing Art.

The range is revealing. A scientist able to describe a stone flake from deep prehistory could also devote patient attention to paintings made by people with cultural lives much closer to our own. In both cases, she was looking for a reliable way to preserve and interpret evidence that might otherwise disappear.

Louis died in 1972. Mary's most famous work at Laetoli lay ahead. Whatever the advantages of the Leakey partnership, she did not need her husband's presence to direct a major research effort. She was an established field scientist with her own questions, methods, and teams.

## The ash that remembered a walk

Laetoli lies south of Olduvai, in northern Tanzania. Mary had visited the area decades earlier. It contains older deposits than many of the levels that made Olduvai famous, as well as animal fossils and remains of early human relatives. During the 1970s she led renewed investigations there.

In 1976, Andrew Hill noticed animal tracks in an exposed volcanic deposit. Other members of the expedition investigated the track-bearing layers. Peter Jones and Philip Leakey identified a small series of possible hominin tracks, and in 1978 Paul Abell and Ndibo Mbuika, working in Mary's team, located the better-preserved tracks at what is now called Site G. Excavation exposed an extraordinary set of trails.

The distinction among these discoveries is important. The animal prints showed that ancient footsteps could survive in the deposits. The earlier, ambiguous prints suggested that hominins might be represented. The long Site G trails supplied compelling evidence. Mary led the scientific program and helped bring the evidence into a systematic account; the individual field discoveries had identifiable contributors.

What had happened more than three and a half million years earlier? A volcano had released ash into the surrounding landscape. On a suitable surface, moisture allowed passing animals to leave impressions. Among tracks made by other creatures were those of upright-walking hominins. Later ash and sediment covered the surface, and geological processes hardened the deposits into rock. Eventually erosion and excavation brought the track-bearing layer back into view.

The preserved Site G trail extends for roughly twenty-seven meters and includes about seventy hominin footprints. That distance is short enough to cross a room or a modest garden, but long enough to show a sequence of steps rather than an isolated mark. Scientists can compare the position of one foot with the next, estimate stride length, and examine how a heel, arch, and toes pressed into the surface.

The large toe was aligned with the others rather than projecting sideways as a grasping big toe does in chimpanzees. The tracks record weight moving through feet suited to walking on two legs. Their form and spacing provide strong evidence that habitual upright walking was well established by about 3.66 million years ago. The exact details of how closely the gait resembled ours remain subjects of study, because a print is an interaction among a moving foot, a particular surface, and the weight placed upon it.

The trackway appears to preserve multiple individuals. Some steps overlap. It is tempting to picture two adults and a child on a family walk, or companions moving together across the ash. Those are possible stories, but the prints cannot establish family relationships, intention, or whether everyone crossed the surface at exactly the same moment. The strong conclusion is that several bipedal hominins left tracks in this landscape. The appealing details of their social lives are beyond what the evidence alone can tell us.

Why was this such an important discovery? Bones tell us about anatomy, but they are reconstructed from scattered parts. A pelvis or leg bone can support an inference that a creature walked upright. A footprint trail is evidence of the walking itself. It captures a movement that actually happened in a specific place, under particular surface conditions.

That is a rare kind of historical evidence. We do not know the walkers' names or thoughts. We know that, for a few moments, they placed their feet on ground that retained the marks. Something as commonplace as taking a step became a record millions of years old.

## Lucy, brains, and the order of human evolution

The Laetoli tracks became especially informative in the context of another famous discovery. In 1974, Donald Johanson and colleagues found the fossil skeleton nicknamed Lucy in Ethiopia. Lucy belonged to Australopithecus afarensis, a species that lived more than three million years ago, with a relatively small brain and anatomical adaptations for upright walking.

Lucy herself was not the person who made the Laetoli prints. Her fossil came from a different place and time. But fossils assigned to Australopithecus afarensis are known from the same general period and region as the tracks, and the species remains the leading candidate for the Laetoli track makers. We do not have a photograph of the feet that made each print, and the species attribution is an inference supported by the evidence, rather than direct identification.

Put the pieces together. Here is an early hominin with a brain far smaller than ours. Here is a footprint trail showing a form of bipedal locomotion well before the emergence of our species, and before the later expansion in brain size associated with parts of the genus Homo. Walking upright was not a reward at the end of human intellectual evolution. It was already part of the lives of some earlier relatives.

That conclusion challenges a simple story in which intelligence first appears and then transforms the body. It also challenges the reverse story, in which upright walking automatically produces large brains. The evidence establishes a sequence of traits, not a single cause-and-effect explanation for why each evolved.

Why might bipedalism have been advantageous? Researchers have proposed ways it could affect travel efficiency, carrying, feeding, heat exposure, or behavior in different habitats. Different proposals may explain different parts of the evolution of walking. Fossils and footprints help constrain them, but they do not deliver a recorded account of the selection pressures that operated over millions of years.

We should also resist imagining a solitary straight line from an ape-like ancestor to Australopithecus, then to Homo habilis, then to us. The human evolutionary tree had overlapping branches. A fossil from one branch may tell us about a shared ancestor, a specialized adaptation, or a lineage that later became extinct. The question 'Which species was first?' can conceal a more productive set of questions: which traits existed, when and where did they occur, and which populations were related?

Laetoli itself continues to raise questions. Other tracks from the site have been reexamined using newer methods, and some researchers argue that the evidence may record more than one kind of bipedal hominin living in the area. The famous Site G prints do not become less important if that is true. They become part of an even more interesting landscape of locomotor diversity.

## What a footprint can and cannot tell us

It is worth returning to the physical trace, because it offers a useful lesson in scientific reasoning.

Suppose you notice a deep heel depression followed by a mark from the ball of a foot and the toes. You might infer that a foot struck the surface near the heel and rolled forward before lifting away. But depth is affected by how wet the ash was, how much the track maker weighed, and whether later footsteps disturbed the print. A deeper indentation does not necessarily mean a heavier animal. A longer stride can depend on speed as well as leg length.

A careful scientist therefore compares many prints, studies the sediment, and checks a mechanical interpretation against living walkers and related anatomy. One track can suggest an idea. A sequence of tracks helps test it. Better measurements can support, qualify, or overturn an interpretation.

This is why the geological setting was so important. A surface of ash is not merely a neutral sheet of paper. Its grain size, moisture, burial, and later hardening determine which marks survive. A fossil trail without an account of its sedimentary context is much harder to interpret.

Mary and the geologist Richard Hay published an account of the Laetoli track-bearing deposits, explaining the volcanic layers and the history of discovery. Their collaboration illustrates how different disciplines fit together. The archaeologist describes a trace and its associations. The geologist reconstructs the formation and age of the surface. Specialists in anatomy and movement evaluate what a body must have done to make the trace. No single kind of evidence is sufficient on its own.

Preserving the footprints brought another challenge. Once exposed, a fragile fossil surface can be damaged by weather, vegetation, and visitors. After documentation and casting, the tracks were covered again for protection. In the 1990s, conservation work supported by Tanzanian authorities and the Getty Conservation Institute dealt with damage and risks from roots growing through protective covering. Discovery is only the beginning of stewardship.

The footprints belong to Tanzania's geological and cultural heritage. Their interpretation and protection involve Tanzanian institutions and researchers, along with international collaborators. Narratives that name only the most famous British or American archaeologists miss much of the labor and responsibility involved in creating and sustaining a research site.

Kimoya Kimeu's fossil preparation and reconstruction work, and the contributions of team members such as Paul Abell and Ndibo Mbuika, show why specific credit is better than a vague phrase like 'the Leakey discovery.' The question of who actually found, prepared, measured, and interpreted a specimen helps us understand the work itself. And the history of archaeology in East Africa unfolded across colonial rule and the independence of countries such as Tanzania and Kenya; the institutions and relationships through which research was conducted changed during Mary's lifetime.

## A longer human history than any written chronicle

Mary Leakey died in 1996, aged eighty-three. By then the study of human origins had become an international field drawing on fossils, archaeology, geology, comparative anatomy, and increasingly powerful laboratory techniques. Some ideas she helped establish had become familiar: East Africa possessed a rich and very old record of hominin life, early humans existed in multiple forms, and bipedal walking preceded the dramatic enlargement of the human brain.

The details continued to change, as scientific details should. Dates were refined. Species names shifted. Discoveries outside Olduvai pushed the known history of stone-tool manufacture further back. New fossils made some branches of the evolutionary tree clearer and others more tangled. Her published records could be reused precisely because they were meant to preserve observations, not merely to announce conclusions.

Now consider the difference in scale between this history and what we normally call world history. The oldest cities and written records are only several thousand years old. The first states, empires, organized religions known through texts, and named historical rulers occupy a narrow interval near the present. Laetoli belongs to a world hundreds of times older. Proconsul belongs to a world older still.

And yet the distinction is not that prehistoric individuals were without histories of their own. They moved through environments, encountered other creatures, found food, raised young, faced dangers, and lived in social relationships that mostly left no direct record. We cannot recover their biographies. We can recover some of the conditions of their lives and occasionally one moment of action.

This is where Mary's work connects with the work of any historian. A historian reading a damaged inscription and an archaeologist examining a footprint face related problems. What happened before this trace was made? Why did this trace survive when so much else did not? What does it support, and where do our own assumptions begin? The methods differ, but both disciplines depend on learning to make the most of fragmentary evidence without claiming more certainty than it provides.

Imagine once more that ash-covered ground in Tanzania. Several early hominins cross it. Their ordinary steps leave shallow marks. Rain, wind, and sediment bury the surface. Millions of years pass. Landscapes change, species appear and vanish, and eventually other members of the human lineage learn to quarry stone, grow crops, build cities, and write accounts of kings and gods.

Then scientists uncover the old surface, measure the impressions, and work out what they can about the creatures that walked there.

Mary Leakey's name belongs to that moment of recovery. She helped make the distant past legible through patient attention to what was actually in the ground. The footprints contain no speech, no dates in the language of their makers, and no declaration of their importance. They are still enough to establish something remarkable: millions of years before anyone wrote a history of humanity, our relatives were already walking across the earth on two feet.

### Chronology and evidence anchors

- **1913:** Mary Douglas Nicol is born in London (February 6). Field training begins through excavation and scientific illustration, notably with Dorothy Liddell at Hembury.
- **1936:** Mary and Louis Leakey marry. Mary's first Olduvai/Laetoli field experience dates to the 1930s, including the 1935 expedition.
- **1948:** Mary discovers a major *Proconsul* skull at Rusinga Island, Kenya. The fossil belongs to the Miocene, roughly eighteen million years ago. It is an early ape, not an identified human ancestor.
- **1959:** On July 17 at Olduvai Gorge, Mary discovers OH 5, originally named *Zinjanthropus boisei*, now conventionally *Paranthropus boisei*; the specimen is roughly 1.8 million years old. Kimoya Kimeu helped reconstruct the fragmented skull.
- **1964:** *Homo habilis* is formally named by L. S. B. Leakey, P. V. Tobias, and J. R. Napier. The name and association with stone tools do not establish exclusive toolmaker identity.
- **1971:** Mary's *Olduvai Gorge*, volume 3, publishes detailed excavation results from Beds I and II.
- **1972:** Louis Leakey dies. Mary continues directing field research.
- **1974:** Donald Johanson and colleagues discover Lucy in Ethiopia. Lucy was not found by Mary Leakey and was not an individual track maker at Laetoli.
- **1976–1978:** At Laetoli, Andrew Hill identifies animal tracks in 1976; Peter Jones and Philip Leakey identify possible hominin prints; in 1978 Paul Abell and Ndibo Mbuika locate the famous Site G trackways while working with Mary's team. Some accounts collapse these phases or assign different dates to the discovery as a whole.
- **About 3.66 million years ago:** Date commonly assigned to the Site G hominin trackways. The approximately 27-meter trackway records about seventy impressions; their species is usually inferred to be *Australopithecus afarensis*, not established by an individually identified footprint maker.
- **1982:** Mary Leakey and Richard L. Hay publish *The Fossil Footprints of Laetoli* in *Scientific American*.
- **1983:** Mary publishes *Africa's Vanishing Art: The Rock Paintings of Tanzania*.
- **1987:** The major scientific volume *Laetoli: A Pliocene Site in Northern Tanzania* appears under Mary's editorship with J. M. Harris.
- **1996:** Mary dies (December 9).

### Interpretive boundaries and narration conventions

- The opening is a generalized imagined scene. No claim is made about the exact thoughts, names, family ties, or intentions of the Laetoli track makers.
- The famous Site G tracks are broadly accepted as evidence of early bipedalism. Specific gait reconstructions and the identity or number of the walkers remain research questions; narration avoids treating a popular “family outing” reconstruction as fact.
- Mary led the Laetoli project; individual discoveries are credited to Andrew Hill, Peter Jones, Philip Leakey, Paul Abell, and Ndibo Mbuika where the published historical accounts support them. Brief biographies sometimes compress the discovery to 1976, 1977, or 1978 depending on which evidence is counted.
- *Paranthropus boisei* is not presented as a direct ancestor. Tooth size indicates chewing capacity; inferred diet also depends on microwear and other evidence.
- The association of stone tools with fossil hominins is evidence of shared context, not automatic proof of manufacture by the species represented.
- Stratigraphic and radiometric dating constrain deposit and footprint ages. The footprint impressions themselves are not assigned dates by a clock built into the tracks.
- Audio convention: “O H Five” is used when introducing the fossil catalog designation. The technical spelling of *Olduvai/Oldupai*, *Laetoli*, *Proconsul*, *Australopithecus afarensis*, and *Paranthropus boisei* can be handled by a project pronunciation glossary if needed.
