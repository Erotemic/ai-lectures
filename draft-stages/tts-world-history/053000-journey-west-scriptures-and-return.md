# Journey to the West, Part VI: The Last Trials and the Return with Scriptures

The travelers have reached the goal for which they crossed mountains and kingdoms, fought demons, endured captivity, and argued with one another. They stand in the presence of the Buddha, and the sacred writings they came to obtain are within reach. Two attendants are charged with handing over the scriptures. The attendants expect a gift. The monk Tripitaka and his companions have spent their strength on the road; they cannot furnish the kind of offering the attendants have in mind. They leave with what appears to be a collection of precious texts.

Only after they depart do they discover that the scrolls contain no written words. The travelers must turn back. The apparent completion of the pilgrimage has become one more test, and its object is the very thing they were seeking: scripture. The scene has often puzzled readers. Are the blank scrolls a higher teaching that the pilgrims were too unprepared to receive? Are the attendants corrupt because they demand payment? Is a scriptural tradition being presented as something that can be transmitted only through reciprocal exchange? The novel gives material for more than one answer and refuses to end the journey in the straightforward distribution of a prize.

This is the conclusion of *Journey to the West*, the late-sixteenth-century Chinese novel about a Buddhist pilgrimage toward India. The party consists of Tripitaka, a human monk loosely modeled on the historical traveler Xuanzang; Sun Wukong, the once-rebellious Monkey King; Zhu Bajie, a powerful piglike companion; Sha Wujing, the patient attendant Sandy; and a dragon prince transformed into a white horse. Their mission is to obtain teachings for the people of Tang China. In this telling we follow roughly chapters eighty-four through one hundred, the final stretch of a book in which the road is both an adventure through an extravagant supernatural world and a demanding path of self-cultivation.

The ending will not be a simple procession from victory to victory. Even after the scriptures are secured, one last failure of memory will overturn the party's careful preparations. The novel's final ordeal is a reminder that completing a long task does not erase the obligations accumulated along the way.

## A king who wants to kill monks

Before reaching the western paradise, the pilgrims pass through a kingdom whose ruler has sworn to destroy Buddhist monks. He is represented as aspiring to kill ten thousand, a horrifying measure of religious hostility made into an official ambition. The problem is no longer a demon stalking a cave. The authority that threatens the travelers is a human government capable of issuing orders and deploying force.

Monkey responds through deception rather than frontal assault. He changes the appearances of the party and works to undermine the king's conviction. His tricks can reach people where formal argument or a request for religious tolerance might not. The king is ultimately brought to reverse his hostile policy, and the realm receives a name reflecting respect for religious teaching.

The story has room for its usual comic inversions. A ruler determined to demonstrate power over monks is humiliated through the ingenuity of a monkey. Yet the stakes are plainly political. A king's prejudice becomes lethal when it is made law. The same world that contains monsters disguised as religious officials also contains officials prepared to persecute people in ordinary robes. The pilgrim's success involves changing what a ruler is willing to authorize.

Religious conflict appears frequently in the novel, but the way it is resolved varies. Earlier, in Chechi Kingdom, the party faced court-sponsored Daoist practitioners who subjected Buddhist monks to forced labor. Here the ruler himself is hostile. Wukong's answer depends on what kind of power is operating in the situation. The novel's religious allegiances are obvious, but its comedy also exposes the hazards of rulers turning theological claims into instruments of coercion.

## A monster and a village

In the next stretch, the travelers meet another creature seeking to devour the monk. The adventure is easy to overlook amid the more famous episodes, but its smaller scale serves an important purpose. A village has to live with the consequences of local danger whether or not an immortal hero finds the battle noteworthy. The travelers become rescuers because people along their route need help, not only because defeating the monster brings them closer to India.

The pattern has repeated often enough to invite fatigue. A demon captures Tripitaka; Monkey searches for the entrance to a cave; companions disagree; a fight follows; an elevated figure may become involved. That repetition is part of the novel's design. The journey has the rhythm of an extended religious discipline, and the same basic errors can return under changing conditions. Knowing what ought to be done in one crisis does not guarantee that one will recognize the next crisis or remain patient in carrying out the necessary work.

There is also a storyteller's advantage in the recurring structure. Each episode can be performed or retold with some independence, allowing the audience to enjoy a particular monster, trick, or landscape. The hundred-chapter novel binds these episodes into a long arc, but it retains the pleasure of shorter tales in which the outcome may be uncertain despite the audience's broad knowledge of the pilgrims.

## Drought, ritual, and the ruler's conduct

In Fengxian County, the party encounters a community suffering from a prolonged lack of rain. A heavenly explanation is given for the disaster, linking it to the misconduct of a local ruler. The rain has been withheld through a celestial arrangement of seemingly impossible conditions. Monkey learns the terms and must persuade people to change their conduct and fulfill the conditions under which relief can arrive.

This episode gives a religious interpretation of political responsibility a visible environmental consequence. The people who suffer from the drought are not necessarily those who committed the act that provoked the supernatural punishment. A ruler's behavior is imagined as capable of damaging the community's relation to Heaven and therefore the conditions of ordinary life. The story belongs to a long Chinese tradition of connecting legitimate government, moral conduct, and the order of nature, though the precise form here is fantastic.

The resulting questions are difficult. If the drought is punishment for misconduct at court, why must farmers and children suffer? When divine beings impose conditions that can scarcely be met, is the ordeal a fair lesson or an exhibition of authority? The novel is not a modern treatise on the ethics of collective punishment. It does, however, portray the effects of the ruler's conduct on people who depend on the rain. The work of relief belongs to the travelers who take the suffering seriously enough to seek its cause.

Compare this with the earlier tale in which a king wanted children's hearts to prolong his life. There the ruler's private fear of death threatened the lives of his subjects. Here an offense against the sacred order produces a disaster borne by the community. Both stories insist that the king's acts are public acts, even when they arise from personal desire. The pilgrimage has become a way to examine failures of government as well as failures of spiritual concentration.

## The princes and the stolen weapons

At Yuhua, the pilgrims encounter a royal household whose princes wish to learn martial skills from Monkey and his companions. For a time the direction of instruction changes. These travelers who have spent so many chapters seeking teachers and guides become teachers themselves. Monkey trains a prince in the use of a staff; Pigsy and Sandy also pass on martial knowledge. The scene gives the group an opportunity to transmit useful skill rather than merely use it in self-defense.

The weapons involved in the training attract thieves. Lion spirits steal them, and the theft draws the pilgrims into conflict with a family of monsters. The struggle eventually leads to a formidable figure called the Nine-Spirits Lion, whose power exceeds that of an ordinary beast. Once again an opponent's celestial connections shape the resolution.

This episode makes a compelling companion to Monkey's own education. As a young stone monkey he left his mountain to seek a master who could teach him the secrets of long life. He learned quickly and was expelled after showing off. At Yuhua he is now in the position of a teacher. The question becomes whether he can transmit his skill responsibly. A weapon such as the golden-banded staff is both an instrument of protection and a source of fascination to those who want power without having undergone discipline.

The theft brings the danger into view. The pupils can imitate technique, but their training has only begun. A master has obligations concerning what his students learn, the motives with which they acquire it, and the safeguards around powerful instruments. Monkey's older career as a rebellious apprentice gives the scene a measure of irony. He knows better than most what can happen when an accomplished pupil thinks that mastery of a technique is the same as wisdom.

## The festival and the rhinoceros spirits

In another city, the travelers arrive during a festival with lights and celebrations. The public world seems joyful, but the festivities coexist with a strange supernatural threat. Three rhinoceros spirits exploit the occasion and capture the monk. The ensuing struggle draws on animal associations, the power of stellar beings, and the coordination of Monkey with his fellow pilgrims.

The festival setting is significant. After repeated scenes of inhospitable wilderness and ruined kingdoms, a city bright with lanterns appears to offer a respite. Yet a crowded civic ritual can be used by predators who understand how to hide within its expectations. Celebration makes people gather, provides a plausible reason for unusual performances, and may draw attention away from danger. The story's anxiety about appearances remains active even as the goal comes closer.

The pilgrims survive through a combination of combat and help from powerful allies. The exact divine relationships differ from those of earlier episodes, but the broader pattern remains familiar: monsters inhabit a cosmos in which animal forms, magical capabilities, and celestial administration intersect. Their names and attributes cannot always be reduced to the zoological animals they resemble. The novel is a work of literary invention drawing on several religious traditions and a long appetite for marvels.

## The princess who is not herself

Near the end of the road, the pilgrims reach a kingdom associated with India, where a royal marriage has become entangled with another imposture. A princess appearing at court is not who she seems. A being connected with the jade rabbit has assumed her identity. The real princess has been displaced, and the marriage arrangements threaten to bind Tripitaka to a life he has vowed not to pursue.

The episode brings several long-standing concerns together. A court can recognize an impostor because the outward signs are persuasive. A ruler can make decisions on behalf of a child without knowing her actual condition. Marriage, which for most people might appear a legitimate and desirable future, is a temptation for a monk committed to celibacy and religious service. Monkey must distinguish the true princess from her magical replacement and restore the person hidden by the disguise.

A reader may recall the false king of Wuji and the false Monkey. In each case, the visible marks of identity are reproduced by someone who wants the benefits of that identity. The consequences differ: a kingdom falls under a usurper, a disciple's identity is contested, and a princess's life is taken over by a creature impersonating her. The repetition underscores how recognition is a social act. Other people have to believe an identity for the deception to change the world. The harm lies not only in a hidden supernatural trick but in the way courts and companions respond to it.

The figure of the jade rabbit also shows how a story may incorporate recognizable symbols from the broader Chinese mythic imagination. The moon rabbit appears in traditions associated with lunar imagery and the preparation of elixirs. The novel redeploys such material inside its own plot. Recognizing that background helps explain why the rabbit is not merely an arbitrary animal chosen to serve as one more monster. It does not require the viewer to treat every detail as proof that the same tale existed unchanged in earlier centuries.

## Hospitality, robbery, and an obligation to return

As they come close to their goal, the travelers receive generous hospitality from a wealthy patron, often called Kou Yuanwai. He welcomes the monks and supports religious guests. But robbery and violence intrude, and a death occurs. Accusations then fall on the pilgrims. The episode becomes a problem of justice, testimony, and restitution just when the narrative might be expected to rush toward its ending.

The hospitality has moral weight. For a very long time Tripitaka has relied on strangers who offered food and shelter. Some deceived him; others saved his life. Kou's generosity belongs to the latter class. When a benefactor is harmed and the travelers become suspected, the story cannot be resolved by declaring that their mission makes them exempt from accountability. They have to confront the consequences and clear the false accusation. Supernatural intervention eventually allows restoration and reunion.

This is an important pause before the final attainment. The journey has accumulated relationships as well as miles. Those who helped the travelers cannot be treated as disposable figures whose function ends when the pilgrims walk away. The episode places an obligation of gratitude and justice near the very end of the road. That placement prepares the reader for the final ordeal, which will also arise from an earlier promise.

## The last courts and the meaning of recognition

The late chapters continue to test what it means to know another person. In the jade-rabbit episode, the counterfeit princess can pass through formal court procedures because she occupies a recognizable place within the royal family. A stranger has to do more than claim that her appearance is false. The investigators must establish what happened to the real princess and expose the replacement in a setting where a mistaken accusation could have terrible consequences.

The motif recalls the false king and the two Monkeys, but it is altered by the circumstances of marriage. A kingdom is preparing to bind someone to a spouse through an official ceremony. If the person occupying the princess's place is an impostor, the consequences involve consent, family standing, and the monk's vow. The court's apparent certainty becomes part of the danger. Ritual procedure can give a decision the appearance of legitimacy even when its participants have misunderstood who is present.

The sequence reminds the listener that the road west has taught Monkey a difficult skill: the ability to challenge an apparently settled public story without making his own assertion the only evidence. He uses disguise, observation, and the help of higher beings when appropriate. His methods are far from those of an ordinary investigator, but the need to distinguish an outward form from the individual behind it remains coherent.

These late encounters also change how the party's progress feels. The travelers are nearer the destination, yet they are not protected from errors merely by their accumulated successes. The king who persecutes monks, the false princess, and the robbers who harm a generous host all expose different dangers: state force, counterfeit identity, and ordinary criminal violence. The book continues to treat them as separate problems requiring different responses. Proximity to the Buddha's domain does not transform the world into a safe corridor.

## The gift and the blank scrolls

The pilgrims finally arrive at the Buddha's domain and receive their sacred commission's answer. The attendants Ananda and Kasyapa, whose names evoke major figures in Buddhist tradition, oversee the transmission of scriptures. They ask for an offering. The pilgrims are surprised and dismayed. The exchange seems embarrassingly similar to the demands made by earthly officials who expect a reward for performing their duties.

The attendants initially furnish scrolls with no writing on them. When the deception or surprise comes to light, the travelers return and complain. The Buddha provides an explanation concerning the status of the unwritten teachings and the difficulty of giving away the most precious knowledge without an appropriate exchange. The pilgrims ultimately surrender a valued begging bowl associated with the Tang emperor and receive written scriptures suitable for their mission.

The incident allows several interpretations. Blank scripture can suggest a teaching beyond words, where genuine insight cannot be delivered as a set of sentences. It can also register as institutional satire: the custodians of sacred learning demand a gift and withhold the usable text when they do not receive one. The Buddha's explanation complicates both readings. The blank scrolls are not simply described as worthless, yet the pilgrims need written texts for the people who sent them.

Notice the difference between obtaining a truth and transmitting it. A teaching beyond words might signify the limits of language for a practitioner who has attained an insight. A mission to bring scriptures to China requires words, material pages, storage, transport, copying, and translation. The historical Xuanzang's achievement lay extensively in those practical labors. The novel's ending makes the material form of scripture an object of meditation just as it becomes the prize of the pilgrimage.

It is also a reminder that scripture is mediated by institutions and people. The pilgrims cannot simply seize truth as Monkey once seized the iron staff. Someone has to hand over the writings; someone has to decide which texts to give; someone has to carry them; someone must translate and teach them later. The scene's comedy about gifts is inseparable from a serious awareness that sacred knowledge moves through human and superhuman relationships in which authority, gratitude, and material support are at stake.

## A scripture must pass through hands

The final exchange of scriptures also invites attention to the difference between a sacred text as an idea and as a physical collection. The travelers have to carry bundles. A manuscript can be opened, rolled, tied, handed from one person to another, placed on an animal, or dropped in a river. It can survive an argument about the value of words and still be destroyed by water. The novel makes these ordinary material conditions visible at the very point where divine knowledge enters the story.

The historical Xuanzang's translation work gives the fictional scene added resonance. Manuscripts retrieved in a foreign language do not become intelligible to a new community merely by crossing a border. Readers must recognize scripts, decipher grammatical structures, choose equivalents for specialized terms, resolve variant readings, and preserve the results in new copies. The novel does not dramatize a long translation workshop in its final chapters, but the written scriptures it brings eastward are objects whose use would depend on precisely that kind of work.

The attendants' request for a gift can also be placed within a social world in which religious learning depends on material support. Teachers, monasteries, translators, and copyists require resources. The episode is comic because the demand feels like the behavior of an official expecting a favor before doing his duty. It remains open to a reading in which exchange is part of the transmission of something valuable. The blank scrolls sharpen the ambiguity. They may have spiritual significance, but for the Tang emperor's stated mission, the party needs a readable written collection.

This is one of the novel's most productive final tensions. Its religious imagination honors knowledge that may exceed language, while its narrative resolution depends on language surviving in a portable form. The two claims can both have force. What is difficult to express in words may still require words if it is to be studied, argued over, and taught across generations.

## The eighty-first ordeal

The party begins the return with the scriptures. In the novel's structure the pilgrimage is associated with eighty-one ordeals, a number with religious and numerological significance. A final trial is still required. The travelers come to a river where an enormous turtle once helped them cross. The turtle had asked them to inquire about his own prospects for future spiritual attainment. Amid all their extraordinary trials, the pilgrims have forgotten the request.

The turtle asks whether they brought an answer. They have none. The turtle, offended or disappointed, overturns the travelers into the river. The precious scriptures become wet, and portions may be damaged in the effort to recover them. The return that seemed secured by divine authorization has been disrupted by an obligation overlooked in the rush toward the main goal.

The episode is comic in its timing and morally precise in its cause. The pilgrims have faced monsters armed with stolen heavenly treasures, resisted royal marriages, and survived a confrontation with two identical Monkey Kings. Yet a creature who once helped them can disrupt their triumph because they failed to keep a promise. The turtle is not an opponent to be defeated through greater force. He is a former benefactor with a claim that the party has neglected.

This is an effective final test of the difference between pursuing a great mission and treating other people as instruments of that mission. The monk's dedication to scripture has demanded immense sacrifice. It has also drawn on the labor and trust of strangers. A journey that takes everything from others while returning no attention to their requests would be spiritually incomplete, whatever texts it brought home. The turtle reminds the party that gratitude is not automatically fulfilled by success.

The damaged or wet scriptures add a material note to the ending. These are physical writings, and physical writings can be lost, stained, miscopied, or destroyed. The novel's audience may relish the comic mishap. A historian of texts will also hear a familiar problem: the survival of a teaching depends on vulnerable objects passing through imperfect human hands. The journey's final risk is therefore closely related to the task that justified it.

## Homecoming and final recognition

Back in the Tang realm, the pilgrims present the scriptures and fulfill the commission. The monk's achievement is publicly recognized. The story then returns its principal figures to the Buddhist sphere, where their individual destinies are assigned in relation to their conduct and service. Tripitaka attains a high religious reward. Monkey receives the title Victorious Fighting Buddha, often translated as Buddha Victorious in Strife. Sandy receives distinguished recognition. The dragon horse is honored for carrying the monk. Pigsy's reward is shaped by the fact that he completed the journey while continuing to struggle with appetite and desire.

The different outcomes are revealing. The companions did not travel as interchangeable examples of perfect virtue. Monkey began as a rebel who wanted to defeat every limitation. Pigsy complained, ate, desired, and sometimes shirked duty. Sandy carried the load with patience. The horse endured the discipline of labor without attracting the same attention as the warriors. The end recognizes their participation without pretending they faced identical temptations or made identical progress.

For Monkey, the conclusion has an especially strong structural symmetry. He once demanded the title Great Sage Equal to Heaven because an official appointment made him feel insignificant. Now he receives a title of extraordinary religious standing through sustained service to someone weaker than himself. His old abilities have not vanished. The staff, changes of form, intelligence, and courage were essential to the expedition. What has changed is the relation between those abilities and his purpose.

The golden fillet that helped discipline him is no longer the focus of his identity. The old image of an animal restrained through pain gives way to the possibility of a being capable of governing himself. This has been an imperfect education. Monkey has used deception and violence repeatedly, sometimes to save others and sometimes in ways that invite criticism. Yet he has stayed with the mission through quarrels, humiliations, and temptations to resume a freer, selfish life.

Tripitaka's achievement is different. He has not become an accomplished fighter. His vulnerability remained part of the novel's dramatic structure until the end. His capacity to persist, maintain a vow, and seek religious teaching has been tested under circumstances that made those virtues costly. He has also made errors of judgment. A reader need not consider him infallible to understand why the story honors him.

## What arrived from the west?

The historical monk Xuanzang returned to Tang China in 645, long before the hundred-chapter novel was assembled. He carried texts, studied and interpreted Buddhist teachings, and participated in the translation and transmission of knowledge through institutions and networks of scholars. These achievements can be explored using near-contemporary historical materials, though those materials also reflect the religious commitments of their compilers. The novel's supernatural trials tell us about later storytelling, religious imagination, and changing forms of Chinese literature rather than events that historians can establish along the route.

The difference is especially clear in the scene of the blank scrolls. The fictional ending places ultimate knowledge and written scripture in a complicated relation. The historical record makes the enormous labor of textual transmission visible. A visitor who encounters an actual Buddhist manuscript has met something that survived because people wrote, copied, stored, translated, and taught it. That work cannot be replaced by an assertion that wisdom is beyond words, even though a religious tradition may also affirm that written words cannot exhaust ultimate understanding.

The novel's hundred chapters bring together a long series of shorter adventures, comic performances, religious images, and reflections on government and obligation. Some scenes invite Buddhist allegory; others draw on Daoist cultivation, bureaucracy, folklore, poetry, and martial fantasy. The unity comes from the pilgrimage and the changing relationships among its travelers. They repeatedly discover that the world is larger than the methods they used to survive the preceding crisis.

At the end, the scriptures have crossed the river and reached home, though the journey almost failed when an old promise was forgotten. The stone monkey who first rebelled against Heaven has learned to accompany a vulnerable human being. The monk who set out with a vow has endured the consequences of needing others. Their companions have struggled with hunger, vanity, fear, discipline, and fatigue. They have succeeded together, imperfectly.

That is why the final image of the turtle is worth remembering beside the splendor of the Buddhist court. The turtle does not care that the pilgrims have overcome magnificent enemies. He remembers a question they promised to ask. A story about obtaining the highest teaching ends by reminding its heroes of a small obligation to someone who helped them along the road. Their mission is complete only after they face that omission and carry on with the damaged, rescued books.

## Source and editorial notes

Narrative boundary: chapters 84–100, including the persecution of monks, Fengxian drought, Yuhua princes and lion spirits, festival rhinoceros spirits, the false princess and jade rabbit, Kou Yuanwai's household, the blank and written scriptures, the turtle's unfulfilled request, and the final honors. The blank-scroll episode supports multiple readings; do not reduce it to either a general denunciation of monks or a proof that scripture is worthless. Xuanzang's documented return in 645 is separate from the novel's fantastical ending. The eighty-one ordeals constitute the novel's religious narrative architecture, not a count of independently verifiable seventh-century incidents.
