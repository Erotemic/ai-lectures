# Three Kingdoms, Part III: The Rival Lords and the Fall of Lü Bu

Suppose you were a soldier deciding which commander deserved your future. One leader wins almost every personal combat but changes allegiance whenever a stronger patron or more tempting reward appears. Another is capable of remarkable cruelty, yet he rewards competence, studies his enemies, and builds the kind of organization that can survive a single defeat. A third appeals to kinship, generosity, and the ideals of a dynasty whose court has lost its freedom. The soldier cannot live in the abstract. He needs food, officers who issue intelligible orders, a family protected from confiscation, and some reason to believe his commander will still be present next year. This is the political world of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* during the years when Cao Cao, Liu Bei, and Lü Bu repeatedly circle the rich province of Xu.

The novel is set during the collapse of the later Han dynasty; it was written and revised much later, in the tradition conventionally attributed to Luo Guanzhong. Its characters' reputations have been shaped by centuries of storytelling. Here we will follow the intersecting campaigns in chapters fifteen through twenty-two of the common 120-chapter text. We will also travel south to meet Sun Ce, whose conquest gives his family a territorial base. This is a story about the limits of personal brilliance, the emerging structure of military states, and the danger of treating symbolic authority as a substitute for sustained government.

## The young lord across the river

Sun Jian has died in battle. His son Sun Ce is left with the name of a brave father but insufficient territory to support the future he imagines. He serves under the ambitious Yuan Shu and arranges to acquire troops in exchange for the imperial seal that his father had found. The novel understands the bargain as both practical and symbolic. Sun Ce needs soldiers now; Yuan Shu covets an emblem that may make a later claim to kingship seem more credible. Each thinks the exchange serves his own horizon. The tragedy for Yuan Shu will be that the object he receives cannot perform the political work he hopes to assign to it.

Sun Ce crosses into the lower Yangtze region and fights his way into a network of cities and alliances. He is nicknamed the Little Conqueror, a comparison with the ancient Xiang Yu. The epithet praises aggression while hinting at danger. Xiang Yu was a figure of prodigious force who lost the struggle for empire; the comparison therefore carries the possibility that splendor in battle may coexist with political defeat. Sun Ce moves swiftly, defeats rivals, and draws able commanders toward him. Taishi Ci appears as an enemy worthy of respect. Instead of treating every defeated opponent as disposable, Sun Ce makes room for capable men to join his cause.

This is how a family whose father was killed in a provincial campaign becomes a durable power in the south. Soldiers are only the first stage. Sun Ce must recruit administrators, arrange personal loyalties, and make his conquests appear useful to local elites. He benefits from the ambitions and abilities of Zhou Yu, a friend and adviser associated with the foundation of the southern regime. Sun Ce's younger brother Sun Quan is not yet the commanding figure he will become. The novel places the brothers in the same political family but prepares them for different roles: a conquering elder who advances by risk, and a younger man who will need to hold together what has been won.

Geography matters. The Yangtze basin is crossed by rivers and waterways and defended by places very different from the plains and roads of Cao Cao's northern campaigns. Armies that have been effective on land may face unfamiliar diseases, supply problems, and naval requirements. The future Three Kingdoms will emerge not simply because three heroic personalities decide to divide China, but because regional powers learn to sustain different territorial systems.

## The conquest that must become a government

Imagine Sun Ce after one of his victories, riding through a newly surrendered town. A messenger may bring news that an enemy commander has fled. His followers may congratulate him, and the town's gates may be open. Yet nothing in this scene proves that the town will remain his next spring. Someone must collect grain without ruining the harvest, assign guards who will not rob the households they protect, adjudicate disputes between local families, and convince men who served the previous authority that their livelihoods can continue. A conqueror can ride onward. A government must remain behind.

The novel gives Sun Ce a magnetic capacity to attract warriors. Taishi Ci's transition from adversary to respected companion is especially important because a ruler who destroys every defeated capable man is continually obliged to manufacture talent from nothing. To recruit yesterday's foe is to advertise that the new political center rewards ability. But reconciliation is also risky. A former enemy has followers, remembered grievances, and an independent reputation. Sun Ce must trust him enough to make the appointment credible while retaining the power to punish a future betrayal. This delicate combination of confidence and caution is a more durable foundation than victory in a duel.

Zhou Yu belongs to the same story of consolidation. He is introduced through bonds of personal friendship and shared confidence, but his eventual significance will lie in the decisions of an organized southern command. The friendship of two talented young men becomes politically effective only when they acquire the ability to mobilize ships, supplies, officers, and the cooperation of regional families. The novel's later victories at the great river are prepared here. Sun Ce is gathering the human material from which Sun Quan will build a state.

There is also a cost the narrative rarely pauses to calculate. These southern districts were not empty spaces awaiting a dynasty. They already contained inhabitants, local leaders, and competing interests. To say that Sun Ce pacified the region is to describe an outcome from the point of view of the new ruling house. For some households his arrival may have brought security; for others it meant the loss of a former patron or the necessity of changing loyalties. We can admire political achievement while remembering that the establishment of order is an experience different people assess differently.

## An arrow between two camps

In Xu Province, the smaller rivalries are just as dangerous. Liu Bei, displaced from the authority once offered him, must negotiate with Lü Bu, the warrior who seized his city. Yuan Shu tries to exploit the hostility between them by directing forces against Liu Bei. Lü Bu realizes that a decisive victory for either rival may make the other more dangerous to himself. He therefore intervenes in the conflict. The novel stages the intervention around a spectacular shot from his bow. He sets his halberd at a distance and offers to end the dispute if he can strike its small projection with an arrow. The shot succeeds, and the assembled officers agree to withdraw.

It is a rare moment when Lü Bu uses his extraordinary skill to prevent a battle rather than win one. Yet the action reveals the limits of his authority. He can impose a temporary truce by making all parties admire his marksmanship. He cannot construct a durable settlement among men whose territorial interests remain opposed. The bowshot solves a particular confrontation, not the rivalry that produced it. The episode is almost a miniature of the whole novel: a memorable feat of individual prowess briefly suspends a conflict rooted in armies, land, and competing claims.

Yuan Shu makes the mistake toward which the novel has long been moving. He claims the imperial title. He has access to the seal and possesses considerable resources, but neither gives him universal recognition. Other lords can denounce him as a usurper and unite against him even while remaining divided among themselves. The attempted enthronement exposes him to enemies and damages his credibility. The symbolism has backfired. In a political culture where legitimacy must be acknowledged by others, calling oneself emperor can make one's position weaker if other forces refuse the title.

The ancient idea of the Mandate of Heaven does not operate here as a mechanical law. The novel's characters invoke Heaven, dynastic descent, omens, titles, and ceremony because these influence political judgment. Yet armies also need regular pay and an orderly chain of command. Yuan Shu's assertion makes the distance between possessing an imperial object and possessing an effective state painfully visible.

## The archer and the impossible compromise

Lü Bu's celebrated shot at a weapon set in the camp offers a different scene of authority being compressed into a single act. Two parties are close to fighting. A mediator offers a proposition that seems absurdly simple: if he can strike a small target with an arrow, the parties must withdraw; if he fails, they may fight. Soldiers watch the distance and understand the improbability of the feat. Lü Bu draws and releases. The arrow reaches its mark. A quarrel which might have consumed hundreds of lives is suspended by a performance of personal skill.

There is a reason such a story survives. Every army depends on prestige as well as orders, and a warrior's demonstrated ability can make a promise credible in the eyes of those who have just seen him perform. The episode also supplies a short-lived picture of Lü Bu at his best. He uses the reputation acquired through violent contests to forestall another violent contest. For one suspended instant, the man who cannot consistently govern himself appears able to govern the conduct of others.

But notice what the arrow does not accomplish. It cannot settle the underlying question of who possesses the disputed ground, who will supply the soldiers, or whether either party intends to honor the settlement after the audience disperses. Its force is theatrical and immediate. If the commanders do not continue to fear Lü Bu's reputation, or if conditions change, the accord may dissolve. The very brilliance of the scene illustrates how different a performative truce is from an institutional peace.

Historical sources record Lü Bu as an extraordinarily capable horseman and archer; the novel expands that character into an emblem of terrifying single-combat power. We need not suppose that every detail of the archery contest happened as narrated to appreciate the question it asks. Can excellence at a single task generate political authority? Sometimes, briefly. But the crisis in Xu will soon demonstrate that no one can maintain a province by making every dispute into a shooting contest.

## Cao Cao loses a bodyguard

Cao Cao fights a difficult campaign against Zhang Xiu. The novel describes a surrender followed by a disastrous reversal. Cao Cao's conduct in the surrendered camp provokes an attack, and his forces are thrown into confusion. Dian Wei, the formidable bodyguard, holds a position against overwhelming enemies, buying time for his lord to escape. Cao Cao's son Cao Ang and other members of the household are killed. The narrative remembers the bodyguard through a scene of almost impossible endurance: a man fighting after weapons break and the situation has plainly become hopeless.

There is a cost to Cao Cao's personal behavior that cannot be repaired by military planning. A commander can organize soldiers brilliantly and still imperil them through a choice made for his own pleasure. The story requires us to see both the ingenuity that makes him powerful and the failures that bring disaster upon the people who serve him. Dian Wei dies performing his duty even though the circumstances that created the duty reflect poorly on his master. Loyalty can be honorable in the individual and tragic in its political consequences.

Cao Cao does not dissolve under the defeat. His advisers help him assess his position, and he returns to other fronts. This resilience becomes one of his defining characteristics. Many generals are brave in favorable circumstances; fewer can reconstruct authority after their errors become public. Cao Cao increasingly appears as a manager of people and resources whose own impulses nevertheless remain a danger.

A later anecdote involving the wounded Xiahou Dun adds another dimension to the novel's celebration of martial ferocity. An arrow strikes his eye. He removes it with such determination that the scene becomes an act of bodily defiance, although in the famous narrative he recognizes that the eye is part of the body received from his parents. The story is extravagant, and readers should understand it as martial literature rather than a medical report. It dramatizes the kind of resolve admired within the commander's circle, while leaving the larger outcome of the campaign to strategy and supply.

## Why the seal cannot command a city

Yuan Shu's possession of the imperial seal is the perfect object lesson in the limits of symbols. A seal is a small object whose force comes from a large agreement: officials must recognize the authority it represents, orders must travel through channels that still function, and armed men must believe disobedience will have consequences. Remove those supporting conditions and an impressive object can do little. In the novel, however, the seal seems for a moment to promise a shortcut through all these difficulties. Yuan Shu treats it as a sign that sovereign destiny has been transferred to his hands.

His claim to imperial rank is not an act of imagination alone. He has followers and territory; he calculates that the Han court's weakness has created an opening. But he misjudges how other commanders will respond. A man who might be tolerated as a regional governor becomes a common threat when he declares that he can supersede the emperor. Potential allies fear that accepting his claim will subordinate them, while rivals now possess a publicly acceptable reason to combine against him. The seal grants his ambition a dramatic vocabulary, but it also makes that ambition harder to disguise.

Consider the contrast between Yuan Shu and Cao Cao. Cao Cao does not initially need to announce that he is emperor. He can exercise power through the existing imperial office, receiving legitimacy from an institution whose independence he restricts. Yuan Shu wishes to shorten the path by seizing the title directly. Their methods are not morally equivalent simply because both manipulate authority, but they demonstrate why political language has strategic consequences. The name of an office can attract supporters, frighten rivals, or alienate undecided households. A title without a viable coalition is a liability.

For the listener, the seal episode is best read as part of a coherent literary design rather than as proof that one physical relic determined the course of Han succession. The novel moves the object from Sun Jian's household into Yuan Shu's ambitions to ask what people imagine possession entitles them to do. It then places that fantasy beside the stubborn material requirements of government: roads, grain, officers, trust, and an army willing to take orders.

## A declaration that makes allies disappear

Yuan Shu's decision to call himself emperor deserves to be followed beyond the symbolic seal. His claim places a demand upon every lord with whom he deals. They must either recognize his superior position or treat him as an illegitimate competitor. The earlier arrangement, under which different commanders could claim to serve the Han while exercising independent local power, no longer provides him with a comfortable ambiguity. Every diplomatic conversation has become an argument about who is entitled to command whom.

The consequences are visible in the changing responses of other powers. A rival who has not yet committed to an alliance against Yuan Shu may now find common ground with Yuan Shu's enemies. Local commanders may fear that continued association will endanger their standing. Soldiers may still fight for him out of habit or material need, but they cannot eat an imperial title. If the state supporting the claim is unable to provision its army and maintain public order, the title makes the contradiction more conspicuous.

The novel's severe judgment of Yuan Shu is built around this disproportion between aspiration and capacity. He seeks the highest formal rank before he has won the cooperation needed to sustain it. His failure makes a useful counterpoint to Cao Cao's long exploitation of the existing Han court. Cao Cao can perform many functions of sovereignty while declining, for the moment, to take the final name. In a world governed partly through beliefs about legitimate succession, the order in which acts are performed can change their political meaning.

## The city at the edge of the flood

Lü Bu's position deteriorates. He is repeatedly advised to act decisively, but he quarrels with officers and hesitates between plans. Chen Gong, an intelligent counselor who earlier opposed Cao Cao, urges him to exploit opportunities and avoid being shut inside a defensible but vulnerable city. The advice is sound in terms of military position. Yet Lü Bu cannot reliably convert advice into action. He is suspicious of colleagues, worried about private attachments, and too confident that his own ability can rescue any emergency.

Cao Cao joins with Liu Bei against him. The campaign focuses on the city of Xiapi, where water and surrounding terrain can become weapons. The besiegers manipulate the rivers and flood the position. Soldiers trapped within cannot rely on a strong wall if water destroys stores and separates defenders. The novel gives the final collapse an intimate cause as well as a strategic one. Officers whose trust has been exhausted abandon Lü Bu and seize him. The famous fighter has no room in which to demonstrate his superiority. He is made prisoner by the very people whose allegiance he failed to sustain.

Before Cao Cao, Lü Bu offers to serve, promising that his martial talents combined with Cao Cao's command would make them irresistible. For a moment the offer has an obvious attraction. Who would not want the finest warrior of his generation under a capable general? But Liu Bei reminds Cao Cao of the men who previously trusted Lü Bu: Ding Yuan and Dong Zhuo. Both were killed after granting him privileged places in their households. Cao Cao orders his execution. The lesson is ruthless and practical. A useful servant whom nobody dares trust cannot make the army more secure.

Chen Gong refuses to beg for pardon. His death gives the novel a different kind of dignity. He has made choices, some disastrous, in the service of a lord he now recognizes was not worthy of his advice. Yet he will not reverse his position simply to preserve his life. In the contrast between the two prisoners, the book measures character by responses to defeat. Lü Bu offers future utility as a bargain for survival. Chen Gong accepts responsibility for the alignment he made. Neither response can undo the violence already suffered, but the story remembers them differently.

## A counselor who cannot make his lord listen

Chen Gong's association with Lü Bu poses a different problem. He is capable of identifying opportunities and explaining the danger of allowing Cao Cao's armies to concentrate. Yet sound advice has little force if the ruler treats it as a suggestion to be weighed against immediate pleasure, suspicion, and the emotional pressure of favorites. The counselor can speak; he cannot command obedience. The stronger his warnings become, the more they may sound like criticism of the person who must receive them.

One can imagine the last meetings before the siege closes. Chen Gong knows that a defender should not surrender every road to the enemy. He asks for action while the army still possesses mobility. Lü Bu worries that such a move will separate him from people he wishes to protect, or from the center of command on which he relies. Both can name genuine risks. The tragedy is that delay resolves the argument for them, until the alternatives that once existed have disappeared.

Chen Gong's decision to meet death without pleading for advancement afterward gives the novel a severe form of self-respect. He chose the wrong man to serve, and the narrative does not absolve him of responsibility for that choice. But it allows a distinction between an adviser whose counsel was disregarded and a commander whose personal excellence could never be turned into reliable collective action. The final image is an indictment of failed listening as much as failed loyalty.

## An army after a commander's death

The fall of Lü Bu at Xiapi invites us to imagine the morning after the execution. The winner has removed the rival leader, but the dead man's soldiers still exist. So do the families who supplied them and the towns that must accommodate the victors. Officers who previously obeyed Lü Bu must decide whether to enter another service, flee, or resist. In the novel, individual surrenders and refusals become moral portraits. In a real campaign, absorbing surviving personnel is also a complex logistical act. The conquered army knows roads and fortifications that an outsider may not; it also brings grievances and competing chains of command.

Cao Cao's eventual strength rests partly on his capacity to incorporate the useful remnants of defeated rivals into a wider organization. The novel allows us to see this capacity alongside his appetite for control. To employ the defeated may be prudent and sometimes generous. It can also make a regime formidable enough to undertake new campaigns before the wounds of the last have healed. A military victory is not a pause in the story. It is an increase in resources, responsibilities, and opportunities for coercion.

Liu Bei is in a different position. He keeps moving among hosts because he lacks the stable territorial basis that would let him reward all those willing to follow him. His virtues, like his lineage, travel more easily than an army's grain. Yet his followers remain with him through repeated losses, a loyalty that suggests his reputation has practical value. The rivalry between him and Cao Cao is therefore about contrasting solutions to the same problem: how can a leader keep people attached to him when he cannot offer an uncomplicated guarantee of success?

When we hear that Lü Bu died because he betrayed his patrons, that is a fair account of how the novel frames his career. It remains a literary judgment built around selected actions. The broader political outcome is that Cao Cao has eliminated a rival who could disrupt the northern coalition of powers, while Liu Bei remains dependent on the willingness of stronger men to tolerate him. The story now has fewer spectacular warriors free to decide battles on impulse and more commanders able to determine the future through systematic war.


## The emperor's secret command

With Lü Bu gone, Cao Cao has a stronger position in the north and controls the court. Emperor Xian is a guest whose actions are carefully constrained. The novel gives him a desperate measure: he arranges a secret command urging trusted men to move against Cao Cao. Dong Cheng, a member of the imperial circle, becomes involved. The command hidden in a garment expresses how far the dynasty has fallen. Official orders should be public acts with authoritative seals and administrative channels. Here the emperor must try to communicate resistance through a concealed message.

Cao Cao's conduct has thus generated two competing understandings of the same arrangement. He can claim that he protects the emperor, maintains offices, and restores order across damaged territory. The emperor can experience that protection as captivity. A minister might sincerely admire Cao Cao's capacity while fearing the consequences of placing all public authority under one military household. These positions are not automatically reconciled by saying that Cao Cao had better armies. Military effectiveness explains control; it cannot alone settle whether the control is legitimate.

The secret command establishes an impending confrontation between Liu Bei's inherited loyalty and his dependence on Cao Cao's political world. Liu Bei has already received positions and recognition through the court. He is now linked with men who believe that the very official who maintains the court threatens it. His personal danger grows because his imperial descent gives him symbolic importance that he cannot easily disown.

## Drinking wine with a dangerous host

One of the novel's finest conversations takes place while Cao Cao and Liu Bei drink wine and examine the fortunes of their contemporaries. Cao Cao asks which men deserve to be called true heroes. Liu Bei names powerful commanders and well-known families. Cao Cao rejects them one by one. Some have pedigree but lack judgment; some are fierce but shallow; some cannot see beyond their inherited position. The talk seems recreational, but Cao Cao is testing the man beside him. Finally he identifies the two people at the table as the only figures who truly understand the demands of greatness.

Liu Bei is alarmed. If Cao Cao believes him capable of challenging for the empire, the conversation may be the prelude to arrest. At that moment a storm breaks, and Liu Bei uses the startling thunder to conceal his reaction. The episode has several layers of pleasure. It is a contest without drawn weapons, a scene in which a man skilled at hiding his ambitions must persuade a perceptive host that he has none. It also dramatizes Cao Cao's acuity. He may be morally compromised, but he can recognize political potential that is invisible to many respectable lords.

A scene like this is not evidence of a verbatim conversation in the late Han court. It is a novelist's way of making competing assessments of character audible. Cao Cao sees worth even in his opponent; Liu Bei understands that recognition can be fatal. The wine table becomes an arena in which both men must manage the appearance of ease. Their entire future rivalry is compressed into a brief social encounter.

## The value of an acknowledged name

Liu Bei's standing is transformed when he is recognized as a relative of the imperial house. The novel often calls him the imperial uncle. The title may produce respect that a small military force could never command. But it also makes him a focus of expectations. If a claimant says he is devoted to the Han, he must choose whether to defend the institution as currently governed or to resist its chief protector. A name is a resource and a burden. It allows a landless man to request hospitality from powerful lords, yet it makes rivals wonder what he will attempt if he obtains territory.

Cao Cao's recruitment policy follows a contrasting logic. He draws men of ability into an administration that increasingly serves his own objectives. A counselor does not need imperial descent to make himself valuable. A commander does not need a story about ancient virtue if he can win and hold a district. The novel is sympathetic to merit within Cao Cao's circle while also showing how merit can strengthen an order whose highest authority is not freely chosen. Liu Bei's moral claims and Cao Cao's governing capacity are both real attractions. They are also mutually threatening.

Sun Ce, meanwhile, has made the south a credible political center. His rising house cannot be judged through the northern rivalry alone. The river systems, local power groups, and commanders he has assembled create a third trajectory. The title *Three Kingdoms* promises an eventual balance that has not yet emerged. At this stage many houses could still win or lose, and a reader following the story without advance knowledge would have difficulty guessing which three would survive.


## What the death of Lü Bu establishes

The most famous death in these chapters is Lü Bu's. He has the exceptional body, the red horse, the reputation that makes challengers hesitate. Yet he is defeated by coordination, water, a siege, and the loss of trust. It is tempting to call this simply a moral lesson about betrayal. It is also a lesson about the scale of warfare. Personal fighting ability becomes less decisive as armies and administrations grow. A city must be fed; an alliance must be maintained; officers must believe promises; a ruler must accept advice he dislikes. Lü Bu is repeatedly brilliant in the smallest unit of action and unreliable at the level of the institution.

Cao Cao's victory does not confer moral innocence. His treatment of Xu Province and his behavior at Zhang Xiu's camp have shown the cost of his appetites and violence. Liu Bei's generosity does not guarantee political wisdom. His hospitality to Lü Bu helped expose Xu Province to seizure, and the secret imperial plot may put him and his followers in mortal danger. Sun Ce's success is genuine, but it rests on rapid conquest that can be undone by a single disaster. The novel is building a political universe in which virtues are partial and often compete.

By the close of this movement, the northern contest has been simplified but not settled. Lü Bu is dead. Cao Cao controls the emperor and has destroyed one formidable rival. Yuan Shao remains a much larger opponent with wide resources and a famous lineage. Liu Bei has a dangerous name and no secure independent empire. Sun Ce is creating a southern power that northern commanders have not yet fully reckoned with. The decisive struggles will increasingly be fought through armies of many thousands, supply lines, families, and calculations of allegiance. The wine table where Cao Cao identified his rival has exposed the next question: which kind of hero can survive a war for the whole realm?

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 15–22. Historical Lü Bu's death at Xiapi and Cao Cao's northern consolidation can be compared with Chen Shou and Pei Songzhi. Treat the archery truce, wine-table exchange, and heroic bodily feats as the novel's dramatic staging. Sun Ce's independent campaigns establish the southern house; the seal episode should be treated as a literary account of political legitimacy, not as an independently proven transfer.
