# How We Know the Outlaws and Their Many Endings

Imagine two readers who have both finished *Water Margin*. One closes the book after the hundred and eight heroes have gathered, received their places in a celestial order, and formed a brotherhood. The other reads onward into a negotiated surrender, campaigns fought for the emperor, a disastrous war against another rebel leader, and the deaths of some of the most admired companions. They have read overlapping stories, but they may disagree about what the work ultimately says. The first ending can make rebellion appear to reach a perfected community. The second asks whether returning to the state destroys the community built in resistance to it.

Neither reader has necessarily been deceived. Chinese novels circulated through editions that differed in length, ordering, commentary, and political emphasis. *Water Margin* is particularly revealing because its most famous alternate ending is the result of a major editorial intervention. To understand how the book was made, we have to separate at least three objects: the Northern Song events and persons that may have inspired legends, the stories developed through performance and writing over later centuries, and the printed forms that became familiar to readers. Each object supports a different kind of evidence.

The story tells us that a hundred and eight spirits were released from confinement and reborn as men who gathered at Liangshan. Historical scholarship cannot verify the opening miracle. It can investigate why a numerical pattern of thirty-six celestial figures and seventy-two earthly figures organized a long account of fighters, clerks, monks, merchants, and wanderers. The story gives us the experiences of Lin Chong and Wu Song in vivid detail. A historian may find Song-era evidence for officials, prisons, bandit suppression, taxation, and households, without finding records of those characters' specific dialogues and confrontations. The novel survives as a major witness to imagination and political criticism, while some of the background events have independent support.

## The historical setting: a government with a great deal to lose

The narrative is placed in the late Northern Song, especially the reign of Emperor Huizong, which lasted from 1100 to 1126. The Song state had developed sophisticated institutions and an economically active society. Its capital and commercial networks were centers of production, trade, art, and administration. That complexity did not prevent internal unrest or military vulnerability. Officials relied on regional structures to collect revenue, manage people, and move supplies. Discontent could arise when local power holders abused those systems.

It is tempting to begin with the dynasty's end and treat every fictional incident as a prediction of disaster. The Northern Song did suffer a catastrophic defeat; Jurchen forces took the capital in 1127 and captured members of the imperial household. But the outlaws' adventures, as organized in the novel, are not a transparent diary of the years leading up to that event. The historical crisis supplies a setting in which questions about the competence of rulers and officials are urgent. Later storytellers could use that setting to explain how people who wished to be loyal lost confidence in local authority.

Sources referring to Song Jiang and his followers indicate that the name was associated with outlaw activity in the early twelfth century. Some notices describe a band of thirty-six men operating across more than one region and provoking official efforts at suppression. The precise relationship among these notices, later anecdotes, and the characters of the novel requires care. We do not possess a record that identifies every hero with a historical person. A report that officials pursued a band under Song Jiang does not validate the scene of Wu Song killing a tiger or the entire biography of Lu Zhishen.

The number thirty-six is especially interesting because the mature novel has a hundred and eight heroes. The later arrangement places thirty-six in one celestial category and seventy-two in another. A historical or early legendary group of thirty-six could be expanded within a cosmological system without anyone believing that a contemporary report had named exactly a hundred and eight people. A changing number is evidence that the traditions developed. It is an invitation to examine the contexts in which names were added and ranked.

## A name in a record, a character in a story

A historian's first task is to avoid allowing the fame of the finished novel to determine what an earlier witness must mean. If a Song-related chronicle mentions Song Jiang and a band of followers, we can ask what that record actually claims: where the men operated, who pursued them, how the episode relates to other military concerns, and when the account was written. A few lines in a chronicle may support the existence of a named outlaw leader. They do not automatically supply the lives of all the named characters later gathered around him.

Later compilations can preserve valuable material while adding their own arrangements. A source written many decades after the alleged events may report a story current in the author's world. That is still evidence, but it establishes at least two different things: a claim about an earlier time and the presence of that claim in a later text. Comparing multiple sources can show which names and events recur independently, and which appear only after a particular narrative tradition has become influential.

The desire to find a historical original for every famous fighter is understandable. Lin Chong, Lu Zhishen, Wu Song, and Li Kui are written with such force that the reader wants them to have lived exactly as depicted. Yet the method is unsound. A character can be made from several inherited figures, or a writer can give a name a new biography. Even the historical existence of one name leaves the storyteller free to imagine every conversation and journey associated with it.

There is also a positive reason to value the literary development. The later authors and compilers worked out how to place the character of a generous clerk beside that of a violent monk or a warrior ruined by official corruption. They built connections among episodes that might once have circulated independently. Those decisions are a history of artistic invention that deserves study in its own right.

## Stories before the complete novel

Song Jiang traditions circulated in multiple forms before the surviving complete printed texts. Chronicles, colloquial tale collections, and dramatic performance helped form a shared pool of people and adventures. A performer could tell the story of one outlaw without reciting every other biography. A dramatist could make a conflict visible through dialogue and action. A prose compiler could connect these episodes through journeys, introductions, mistaken identities, and the convergence of characters at a fixed location.

This layered origin helps explain the book's structure. Its opening can feel like a succession of independent lives: Shi Jin, Lu Zhishen, Lin Chong, Yang Zhi, Chao Gai, Song Jiang, Wu Song. Each hero acquires a crisis and a memorable scene. Some portions end as though a storyteller has completed one adventure before introducing another. The eventual brotherhood gives those stories a common destination. What might appear to a reader as a loose beginning may reflect the work's inheritance from smaller cycles and performances.

It also means we should be cautious about the phrase original version. A printed book can be earlier than another surviving printed book without being the first telling of its stories. An author can reshape inherited materials so extensively that the resulting novel is an original literary creation, even if episodes and characters were widely known. We can study the development from earlier reports and tales to long prose without imagining a single moment when the entire book sprang into being unchanged.

The traditional authorial attribution is to Shi Nai'an, sometimes associated with Luo Guanzhong, who is also linked with *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*. Scholars have debated the historical identities behind the names, the division of labor if there was more than one writer, and the dates at which the long prose achieved recognizable forms. The uncertainty arises partly from the distance between the supposed authors' lifetimes and the surviving printed evidence. A title page naming an author can preserve a meaningful tradition without functioning like a modern signed publishing contract.

## The evidence of a printed book

A surviving copy gives a physical witness. It has paper, ink, typography or block carving, chapter divisions, titles, marginal or interlinear comments, and sometimes illustrations. Researchers compare these features across copies and descriptions. An edition may have a preface dated to one year and surviving leaves that were produced later. A bookseller may reprint a familiar text with small changes, or assemble material from multiple sources. Bibliography therefore requires more precision than citing one publication year for the entire novel.

The Library of Congress holds early printed material connected with *Water Margin*, including an important hundred-chapter edition with commentary attributed to Li Zhuowu, the name associated with the thinker Li Zhi. The physical book matters because it gives us a witness that can be inspected and compared. Its printed organization can be studied directly, rather than inferred from a modern synopsis. It can also reveal how commentary presented the outlaws to readers who were already debating the moral meaning of the work.

Long-recension editions of a hundred and a hundred and twenty chapters circulated in the late Ming period. The differences are substantial, especially after the heroes gather. In a widely read hundred-chapter form, the company eventually receives amnesty and fights campaigns for the Song, including against the Liao and against the rebel Fang La. The hundred-and-twenty-chapter form includes additional campaigns against Tian Hu and Wang Qing. They are more than a few added anecdotes: they change the amount of time the protagonists spend serving the state and the balance between their earlier rebellion and their later military achievements.

A reader comparing chapter numbers must know which recension is in hand. A reference to chapter ninety of one edition may not align with chapter ninety of another. Differences in translation can compound the problem. One translator may use a familiar English nickname, another a literal rendering, and a third may retain pinyin. A place name can appear in an older romanization, making two passages seem to describe different locations. Even when the action is the same, the reader's sense of tone can vary greatly with the version.

## Reading with a printed object in hand

When examining an edition, a scholar can ask questions that a modern summary cannot answer. Does the book begin with a separately numbered prologue? How are chapter titles phrased? What illustrations appear and where? Does a commentary voice intervene before or after the narrative? Is the name of a famous critic printed on the title page, and what reason do we have to associate the actual comments with that person? A printed novel is a designed reading experience, not a transparent container for a story that exists independently of form.

Two books can agree on the names of all major characters and still ask readers to judge them differently. A comment inserted after a violent scene may celebrate courage or warn against rashness. A chapter division can make a killing the end of a dramatic unit or the opening complication of the next. A preface can tell the reader to approach the work as a moral caution even when the scenes themselves encourage delight in rebellious energy.

The intervention associated with Jin Shengtan shows the stakes of these details. Removing the amnesty and later campaigns shifts the ending from the band's dissolution to its assembly. Commentarial framing can further alter the tone of that stopping point. The editor's work becomes part of what a later reader experiences as the novel itself. When a translation follows one recension, it inherits much of that editorial shaping even if its publisher uses the same English title as a translation based on another recension.

The responsible comparison is therefore concrete. Set the relevant chapter openings, endings, and commentaries side by side. Identify what one edition includes that another omits. Ask how the change affects a character's arc and the book's political conclusion. Such work often produces a more interesting answer than the assertion that the shorter text is either more authentic or more rebellious. The editions are historical artifacts with discernible choices.

## Jin Shengtan cuts off the future

In the seventeenth century the critic and editor Jin Shengtan produced an influential version that ends around the completion of the hundred and eight heroes' assembly, with a concluding framing distinct from the longer amnesty tradition. It is commonly called the seventy-chapter or seventy-one-chapter edition depending on how the prologue is counted. That variation in numbering is a small but useful warning: a label like seventy chapters should be checked against the actual arrangement of the text.

Jin's intervention was radical because it denied the reader the continuation that other versions offered. If the book ends with the community assembled and ranked, its moral geometry changes. The characters' later service to the emperor, military losses, and poisoned deaths do not form the culmination. The novel can be enjoyed as the building of an alternative order whose solidarity is the achievement. If the later chapters are retained, the same gathering becomes a prelude to an argument about whether returning to official service can reconcile loyalty and rebellion.

Scholars debate Jin's aims and the implications of his editorial method. It is tempting to say that he made the novel purely pro-rebel or purely anti-rebel by cutting the last part. Such a conclusion is too quick. His comments, framing devices, and treatment of the heroes shape how the earlier chapters are read. Editorial surgery can create a different moral ending without establishing a single political program. The difference is best studied by reading the actual words and commentaries of the two forms, rather than assigning one simple ideology to each.

The point for a listener is practical. When someone says that *Water Margin* ends in betrayal by the court, ask which text they read. When someone says that it ends in perfect brotherhood, ask the same question. Both responses can be grounded in influential versions. A lecturer who stitches the stories together without identifying this fork can make the title seem more stable than the surviving evidence warrants.

## The problem of translation

Modern English readers encounter titles such as *All Men Are Brothers*, *Outlaws of the Marsh*, *The Water Margin*, and *The Marshes of Mount Liang*. These are all ways of presenting the same broad tradition, but the editions translated differ. Pearl S. Buck's *All Men Are Brothers* helped introduce the novel to a wide English-language readership in the twentieth century, using the shorter narrative form. Sidney Shapiro's *Outlaws of the Marsh* and the work of Alex and John Dent-Young provide other entry points, with different editorial and textual bases.

A translation is a reading of a text. The decisions begin with the title. *Water Margin* evokes the edge of water, a place where ordinary roads and jurisdiction lose their straightforward relation. *Outlaws of the Marsh* names the community of people living there. *All Men Are Brothers* emphasizes their claimed bond. None is an absurd translation, and each prepares the audience to emphasize something different. The choice can affect how a listener hears the violence that occurs in the name of fraternity.

Nicknames create another challenge. Black Whirlwind is memorable as English, and the sound evokes the force of Li Kui's entrance. Blue-Faced Beast, Flowery Monk, Timely Rain, and Panther Head likewise carry compressed judgments about characters. One version may explain the Chinese phrase in a note while another simply treats the nickname as a proper name. If we want to study a character closely, it helps to distinguish the actions the text reports from the evaluations already embedded in the names by which other characters address him.

The novel's humor is particularly sensitive to translation. Long vernacular fiction moves between comic insults, sudden danger, domestic quarrels, official formulas, and battlefield rhetoric. A translation can make the language sound archaic or modern, grave or rowdy, restrained or sprawling. The comic balance is part of what needs to be preserved. A purely solemn summary of Lin Chong and Wu Song loses the places where laughter makes the violence more disturbing, because the audience has just been invited to enjoy the personality of a man about to cause serious harm.

## Comparing a story with a record

Take the tale of Lin Chong at the snowbound temple. It is detailed enough to imagine as a historical scene. We can picture the collapsed shelter, the wind, the voices outside, and the conspirators' mistake. What evidence supports those details? The long novel and the traditions that led to it provide literary evidence. They do not supply a contemporary official report of the incident. A careful historian can still ask how exile worked in the Song, what duties military instructors performed, how patrons influenced punishment, and what later readers believed about judicial corruption.

Or take Wu Song's killing of a tiger. Stories of heroic combat with animals exist in many traditions. They establish bodily prowess through confrontation with a frightening threat. The scene's prominence in drama, painting, and later adaptation tells us it has been effective as a representation of courage. It does not provide an entry in the record of wild animal attacks from which we can reconstruct an actual fight by an identifiable man.

The case of Song Jiang is different because sources independently preserve the outlaw leader's name. Still, the existence of a historical band does not imply the novel's entire ensemble, exact number, celestial identities, episodes, and moral judgments are historical. The name and the broad association with outlaw activity may be better evidenced than the surrounding narrative. Keeping that difference clear allows the historical core to be discussed without diminishing the artistry that later generations built around it.

When the novel describes a local institution, we can sometimes test its plausibility against historical scholarship. A judicial case, a military rank, a tax demand, or an official gift can be investigated by consulting Song administrative history. A good comparison can reveal where the novel has combined social realities from different centuries or where a later reader's expectations have shaped an earlier setting. That sort of study is more valuable than pretending that every named street and inn is either fully authentic or entirely invented.

## The sources of a celestial number

The one hundred and eight heroes are divided into thirty-six heavenly and seventy-two earthly figures. That division organizes the book's growing mass of names, providing both a complete total and a meaningful rank. The prologue's release of spirits gives the gathering an origin outside ordinary political life. Later, when the heroes receive their positions, the supernatural frame returns to make the scattered biographies appear to have belonged to one destiny all along.

Scholarly work on the novel's Daoist associations has shown that such numbers and celestial offices are embedded in a larger ritual and religious environment. The early bandit traditions and the mature fictional company can be studied alongside Daoist practices involving spirit generals and protective powers. This does not mean every outlaw was a historical religious official. It means the novel borrowed concepts that made its fellowship intelligible in terms familiar to audiences who knew sacred hierarchies and the language of acting on heaven's behalf.

The connection changes how we hear the book's claim to righteousness. The outlaws may defy local officials while describing themselves as executing a higher obligation. That claim can be stirring because it promises judgment beyond corrupt offices. It can also be dangerous because it makes a group less accountable to people outside its own moral circle. A leader who says that heaven has chosen his companions may find it easier to excuse actions that would be condemned in an opponent.

The celestial framework is thus part of the story's political imagination, not merely an ornamental opening. Its repetition at the grand assembly helps bring individual biographies into an apparently final order. Reading that order historically means asking what religious vocabularies the book uses and how the many editions arrange them, rather than treating the number one hundred and eight as an independently verified statistic about the Northern Song.

## The rebellion the story has not yet reached

The long versions take the brotherhood through acceptance by the dynasty, then through military expeditions. Fang La, whose rebellion belongs to the Northern Song's late history, is among the adversaries the novel transforms into a major narrative opponent. The existence of a historical uprising under that name gives the later material a different evidentiary status from a wholly invented campaign, but the elaborate sequence of personal duels and the fates assigned to individual outlaws still require literary analysis.

The historical Song state and the novel's idealized sovereign also need separation. The plot often distinguishes a fundamentally legitimate emperor from officials whose corruption prevents him from understanding the condition of his subjects. That division can allow the heroes to claim loyalty while fighting government forces. It is a political theory embodied as narrative. It cannot be assumed to describe what Emperor Huizong himself thought of every accused bandit or to furnish the historical reasons behind every rebel movement.

The later ending is painful partly because the heroes who hoped to reform their relationship with the state discover that official recognition does not secure their lives. Some die fighting; some leave service; others are destroyed by court intrigues. The details vary by recension. The broader tragic question persists: what happens when people who built a home in defiance of authority ask that authority to certify their righteousness? The shorter version refuses to answer by following them into state service. The longer forms answer with the cost of attempted reconciliation.

## A book that traveled beyond its first readers

*Water Margin* remained culturally active through images, performances, translations, and new narrative forms. In Japan, stories of the heroes inspired famous woodblock prints, including the nineteenth-century designs by Utagawa Kuniyoshi. Images gave the fighters poses, tattoos, weapons, and settings that became recognizable even to people who had not read the entire novel. Theater and later popular media likewise emphasize different figures and episodes. Wu Song can become the hero of a tiger fight; Lin Chong the victim of court malice; Li Kui a terrifying comic companion.

Reception is part of the evidence, but it is evidence for later readers. A Japanese print showing a hero's tattoos may tell us about the visual imagination of the nineteenth-century artist and audience. It does not establish that a twelfth-century outlaw possessed exactly that appearance. The distinction is especially valuable when illustrations are so familiar that they begin to feel like eyewitness portraits of literary characters.

Political readers have found the book useful for conflicting reasons. Its condemnation of abusive officials can encourage admiration of rebellion. Its terrifying depictions of bandit violence can warn against celebrating armed groups simply because they claim justice. Its amnesty ending can be interpreted as the dignity of loyal service or as a betrayal of an independent brotherhood. These interpretations are strengthened or weakened by specific scenes and editions. We learn more by examining how they use the text than by selecting the label rebel novel and treating the argument as finished.

The work's endurance also depends on its refusal to supply pure exemplars. Lu Zhishen gives help when it is needed and disrupts the religious institutions that try to contain him. Lin Chong endures official abuse before retaliating. Wu Song can rescue a friend and kill far beyond what rescue requires. Song Jiang offers timely generosity and seeks imperial acceptance, yet makes decisions that cost other people their lives. Such contradictions make the novel adaptable because each age can choose which ones to foreground.

## A test case: the campaign against Fang La

Fang La supplies a useful example of a name that belongs to recorded history and to the novel's later plot. A significant rebellion broke out in the southeast in 1120 and was suppressed in 1121. The surviving historical record can be used to examine the revolt, the state's military response, and the social conditions contemporaries described. In longer *Water Margin* versions, the imperial court sends the newly pardoned Liangshan fighters against Fang La's forces, and the campaign destroys much of the brotherhood.

The historical existence of Fang La does not verify the fictive roster of duels, deaths, or conversations that populate the novel. Nor does it prove that the hundred and eight heroes served as the campaign's decisive army in the manner described. The writer has incorporated a historical crisis into a moral narrative about men who wish to be recognized as loyal subjects. The distinction allows us to study both the event and its literary use with precision.

The comparison is especially valuable because the ending changes the ethical question. Earlier, the outlaws fought oppressive local figures and imperial troops sent against them. Now they serve imperial power against people who also call themselves rebels. The heroes must explain why their own earlier resistance was righteous while another uprising deserves suppression. The book's tragic losses make that explanation hard to accept without reservation.

A reader of the shorter Jin Shengtan version may never encounter this dilemma inside the narrative. A reader of the longer form cannot avoid it. This is why textual history changes interpretation: the version determines which political actions the characters are asked to perform and which consequences the audience is required to witness.

## What our evidence allows us to say

We can confidently identify a late-imperial Chinese prose tradition, developing from earlier stories and preserved in influential printed forms, in which one hundred and eight outlaws gather at Liangshan. We can describe different endings because surviving editions actually supply them. We can point to earlier references to Song Jiang's band and locate the fictional action in an identifiable period of Song history. We can examine Daoist cosmological language in the celestial prologue and the rhetoric of loyalty and righteousness in the heroes' self-description.

We cannot use the novel alone to establish the historical reality of every duel, arrest, marriage, and battle. We also should not assume that every editorial difference was an accident. A text that ends before amnesty makes a different claim through its shape from a text that continues until the brotherhood is dispersed. The evidence is rich precisely because these differences are visible. The book has a history of composition and a history of interpretation, each influencing what later readers mean when they say they have read *Water Margin*.

Return to those two imagined readers. The first has finished with the hundred and eight ranked in a brotherhood that could call itself an alternative moral order. The second has watched the same men use that solidarity in imperial campaigns and suffer its ruin. The disagreement between the endings is part of the book's inheritance. Knowing where the traditions diverge does more than settle a bibliography. It prepares us to hear the coming choice about amnesty as a genuine political and ethical decision, whose consequences depend upon which *Water Margin* the storyteller has decided to tell.
