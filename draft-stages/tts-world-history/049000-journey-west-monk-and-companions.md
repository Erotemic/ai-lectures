# Journey to the West, Part II: The Monk, the Vow, and the Companions

A human traveler approaches a mountain and hears a voice calling from beneath the rock. He has crossed wild country on his way to India. He has already learned that respectable robes offer little protection from tigers and monsters. The voice tells him that an imprisoned creature has been waiting for precisely such a traveler. The creature promises loyalty and service if the monk will remove a sacred seal. The monk hesitates. Nothing about the bellowing voice suggests a safe companion.

Then the traveler climbs the mountain and lifts the talisman. The stone moves. A monkey bursts free, bows before the monk, and takes his first steps along the road west. This is the beginning of one of literature's great unequal partnerships: a monk committed to compassion and religious discipline, and a guardian whose instincts run toward combat, deception, and spectacular force. Both need something the other possesses. The monk needs protection; the monkey needs a reason to direct his powers toward something beyond himself.

The story is *Journey to the West*, the late-Ming Chinese novel most familiar through the Monkey King, Sun Wukong. Its pilgrimage is loosely derived from the seventh-century Buddhist monk Xuanzang, who traveled from Tang China through Central Asia to India in search of religious learning and texts. In the novel the monk is often called Tang Sanzang or Tripitaka, a title associated with the three collections of Buddhist scripture. Here we follow the assembly of the pilgrimage: the divine decision to send it, the monk's calling, and the arrival of Monkey, the dragon horse, Pigsy, and Sandy. The narrative occupies approximately chapters eight through twenty-three of the hundred-chapter novel, although the monk's family background is arranged differently in surviving editions.

## Why the scriptures must travel

The novel approaches its human pilgrimage from the spiritual realm. The Buddha decides that the teachings should be carried to the eastern land and charges the bodhisattva Guanyin with finding a suitable pilgrim. In East Asian Buddhist traditions, Guanyin is associated especially with compassion and the response to suffering. In this story Guanyin is also an organizer, negotiator, recruiter, and occasional emergency rescuer. She travels toward China and encounters beings who will later join the expedition.

This reverse itinerary is clever construction. The future companions are introduced as people awaiting a second chance. A dragon prince has committed a grave offense and faces punishment. A former celestial official, now taking the form of a piglike creature, has fallen through misconduct. A fearsome being dwelling in the Flowing-Sand River has also been condemned. Wukong has languished beneath a mountain for centuries because he could not submit to any authority. Guanyin offers these beings the possibility that helping a scripture pilgrim may become a path of repentance and eventual release.

This changes how we might understand the party before it forms. The companions are not born as exemplary disciples. They bring histories of arrogance, appetite, violence, and error. The journey will give them occasions to act differently, but their histories will not disappear merely because a religious mission needs their help. The party will contain a dangerous range of temperaments from its beginning.

The difference between the fictional commission and the historical journey is worth establishing here. Xuanzang's own enterprise was motivated in part by disagreements he found among Buddhist teachings and translations available in China. He traveled west in the early seventh century, studied at important centers including Nalanda, and returned in 645 with a substantial collection of texts. He then worked as a scholar and translator. Accounts of his travels and biography survive from much closer to his lifetime than the Ming novel does. Their details have to be evaluated as religious and historical writings, but they give historians grounds for speaking about an actual journey, an actual person, and actual transmission of texts. They supply no evidence for a monkey who defeated Heaven or a horse who was a transformed dragon. Those figures belong to the literary tradition built around the pilgrim.

The novel places the undertaking within the court of Emperor Taizong. A dragon king's dispute over rain and an imperial official's execution of the dragon set off a chain of supernatural legal consequences. The dragon tries to manipulate a prophecy about rain, violates the terms of his cosmic office, and seeks a way around the punishment. The emperor becomes implicated. He is drawn into an underworld experience in which the dead have grievances and royal power proves insecure beyond the limits of ordinary administration. On his return to life, he supports a ceremony intended to benefit suffering spirits. A monk renowned for his religious qualifications becomes its principal figure.

Guanyin appears and announces the need for a journey to obtain scripture. The monk accepts. In the tale he receives a connection with the emperor, a commission, and ceremonial symbols of his role. Yet the promise he makes is almost frighteningly simple. He will go west and obtain the teachings. The novel has deliberately kept him physically vulnerable. Religious sincerity is not an antidote to sharp claws or an armed bandit; it is the reason he continues even when the road becomes almost impossible.

Some later editions supply a long backstory in which the monk's father is murdered, his mother is forced into a terrible predicament, and an infant placed in a river is rescued and raised in a monastery. Elements of this birth-and-family narrative have a complicated transmission; a chapter found in familiar modern forms does not appear in the same way in the earliest surviving 1592 printing. It is useful to know the story, because it helps explain the monk's moral seriousness and attachment to familial obligations in many retellings. It would be misleading, however, to speak as if every extant textual witness begins with precisely the same childhood episode. Our main road begins with the commissioning of the scripture pilgrim.

## The beginning is already dangerous

The monk heads into the wilderness. Almost immediately he discovers the distance between a righteous purpose and survival. Wild animals threaten him. An early encounter with monstrous predators costs lives and leaves him nearly defenseless. A hunter named Liu Boqin offers shelter and guides him through difficult terrain. The hunter's kindness is striking because he belongs to the everyday human world rather than to Guanyin's great sacred design. The journey could scarcely begin without help from someone who knows the land.

Approaching the mountain where Wukong is trapped, the monk learns that the voice beneath it belongs to a creature who claims a promise of deliverance. Wukong recognizes his opportunity. The monk removes the seal, and the monkey resumes his freedom with a new role. He accepts the name and duties of an attendant who walks the religious path, but the conflict between his capacities and his master's ethics appears almost at once.

A band of robbers attacks. Wukong destroys them. From his perspective the matter is straightforward: men threaten his master, and he eliminates the threat. The monk sees the taking of life as a grave violation. He rebukes his guardian. Wukong, outraged at being scolded after saving the monk, departs in anger. He has exchanged the celestial court's inability to recognize his greatness for a human master who repeatedly questions the use of that greatness.

The episode establishes the ethical difficulty the novel will keep returning to. A guardian who refuses to fight cannot protect a vulnerable traveler against every predator. A guardian who treats every opponent as someone to be killed threatens the moral purpose of the journey. The monk's compassion may be difficult to apply in conditions of immediate danger; Monkey's effectiveness cannot settle whether his actions are justified. The best moments of the partnership will involve both a capacity to perceive danger and the discipline to distinguish what response is warranted.

Guanyin introduces a device to secure cooperation. The monk receives a headband and a spell. When the golden fillet is placed on Wukong's head, the monk can recite a tightening incantation that causes intense pain. Wukong cannot simply remove the band. He returns to the road, but his obedience now has a coercive instrument behind it.

It would flatten the scene to describe the band only as wise guidance, or only as arbitrary cruelty. Within the story it restrains a being whose first reflex has been lethal force. It also makes the teacher-student bond troubling. The monk possesses a power to hurt someone who protects him. In later crises he will sometimes use that power when he is mistaken about the danger. The reader is asked to endure the fact that a genuinely compassionate master may act harshly, and a reckless disciple may recognize the threat more accurately than his teacher.

## The horse that was a dragon

At Eagle-Sorrow Stream, the monk's horse is swallowed by a dragon. Monkey confronts the creature, and Guanyin reveals the connection with the repentant dragon prince. The prince receives the form and duty of a white horse, carrying the monk toward India. The expedition therefore includes another companion whose earlier misconduct can be redirected toward service. The animal is neither a mere vehicle nor an interchangeable magical convenience. His humiliation is to accept a lowly, sustained labor, and the story values that labor alongside the spectacular deeds of Monkey.

A traveler also needs ordinary things: clothing, food, sleep, a passable road, and safe lodging. The novel devotes considerable attention to these needs. Monkey can leap enormous distances, but the monk cannot simply ride on his cloud to the destination. The pilgrimage is a bodily task and must be lived through in a sequence of places. Hunger opens the possibility of taking food from suspicious strangers. A cold night makes an apparently hospitable household attractive. A river that seems easy for an immortal to cross becomes an obstacle for a monk and a horse. The slow pace of the party supplies the conditions for the story's repeated moral tests.

At a monastery dedicated to Guanyin, the travelers meet monks who should understand hospitality. Instead the impressive robe given to Tripitaka becomes an object of envy. The monastery's elderly superior covets its splendor and conspires to obtain it. Fire threatens the compound; the coveted garment is then carried away by a black bear spirit. Monkey must recover it by guile and force, and Guanyin finally subdues the animal spirit and gives him a place in her own religious sphere.

The episode refuses a simple division between demons and pious humans. The immediate betrayal begins among religious professionals who desire a valuable object. The bear is an outsider with supernatural powers, yet the plot links him to the same desire for possession. A monk who claims to renounce worldly goods is drawn toward display, and an extraordinarily powerful guardian is sent to fight for a material sign of spiritual office. The irony is deliberate. Religious institutions are part of the world's moral landscape, which means their members can fail.

## The creature in the village

The travelers reach Gao Village, where a household complains about a monstrous son-in-law. The creature living there has a pig's appearance, a prodigious appetite, enormous strength, and a habit of trying to turn arrangements to his own advantage. He is Zhu Bajie, often called Pigsy in English. His name Bajie means Eight Precepts, though his conduct repeatedly makes those precepts difficult to recognize. Once a celestial marshal, he has fallen through lust and misconduct, and he now seeks a place in ordinary domestic life with results that the Gao family finds intolerable.

Monkey approaches the situation with delight. He uses disguise, makes the creature reveal his intentions, and finally fights him. Bajie discovers that the terrible opponent is the very Monkey King who has become the scripture monk's guardian. Since Guanyin has already offered him the possibility of redemption through the pilgrimage, Bajie agrees to join the travelers. The village gains relief; the party gains an appetite that can never be quite satisfied.

Pigsy is easy to underrate if his role is described simply as comic coward. He can fight, endure hardship, and perform useful tasks. He is also frightened by danger, distracted by food, susceptible to sexual temptation, and quick to find reasons for abandoning an inconvenient obligation. He often represents what bodily life insists on, even in a story pursuing release from attachment. The hunger and fatigue that Monkey can brush aside are never far from Bajie's mind. When the party needs someone to remind it of ordinary appetites, he obliges. When it needs someone to act generously despite those appetites, the novel tests whether he can do so.

Their relationship is a comic engine. Monkey calls out Pigsy's laziness and greed. Pigsy resents Monkey's authority and sometimes appeals to the monk against him. Both can be perceptive, and both can be unfair. The differences make them more interesting companions than a set of virtues distributed neatly among four travelers. A strong group may contain friction that makes its members accountable, but only if the friction does not destroy the purpose that brought them together.

The monk receives teaching associated with the Heart Sutra at Pagoda Mountain. In Buddhist contexts the Heart Sutra is known for its treatment of emptiness and the relation between form and emptiness. In the novel, its appearance makes the journey's religious aims audible among the battles. The monk is going to obtain sacred texts, yet he is already being given teachings that ask him to reconsider how he understands the world he encounters. The plot can pause for doctrinal language without abandoning its taste for practical difficulties.

## The wind that no one can face

Before the group reaches the Flowing-Sand River, a monster at Yellow Wind Ridge gives Monkey a vivid experience of being outmatched by an element. The creature commands a wind so fierce that ordinary fighting becomes almost impossible. Monkey's speed and staff are effective when he can reach an adversary. A storm that attacks his senses and surrounds his body changes the nature of the fight. The monk's captivity is urgent, yet the guardian cannot obtain release by repeating the same attack more forcefully.

Monkey investigates the enemy, learns something of the wind's particular character, and seeks assistance from a figure capable of countering it. Lingji's intervention makes a solution possible. The episode establishes a recurring principle of the novel's action. Having powers that seem limitless to a human spectator is different from having the right response to a particular danger. The hero's growth will depend increasingly on recognizing the structure of an obstacle and finding the knowledge or ally suited to it.

The wind episode also changes the meaning of Monkey's role within the party. He can no longer define protection as defeating anyone who threatens his master in single combat. An enemy may control a landscape, a substance, a spell, or a form of deception that leaves physical strength nearly useless. The monk survives because the guardian is willing to leave, seek understanding, and return. To an anxious captive the absence may feel like abandonment. From the guardian's perspective it is the only available form of practical loyalty.

The term pilgrimage sometimes brings to mind a traveler who advances through devotion alone. Here devotion is inseparable from the material problem of getting from one place to another. Bridges can be missing; winds can blind; a horse can be stolen; food can be poisoned or unavailable. The companions must solve logistical problems even while pursuing a religious aim. The novel's varied monsters allow it to stage those problems as spectacular adventures.

At the Flowing-Sand River, Sha Wujing's introduction continues the theme. He has been living at a point where crossing is difficult, and his violence makes the place more dangerous still. The group cannot simply announce that it has been sent by Guanyin and expect him to understand. A contest establishes his capacity; intervention establishes the terms of his redemption; acceptance of those terms establishes the possibility of continuing together. The party grows through a negotiation that resembles the transformation of an enemy into an ally more than the discovery of a ready-made servant.

## A river full of skulls

The party reaches the Flowing-Sand River. Here lives a fearsome creature, Sha Wujing, known in English as Sandy or Friar Sand. He once served in Heaven and fell from grace; now he has preyed on those attempting to cross the river. The waters resist easy passage and the creature is dangerous. Bajie fights him, Monkey intervenes, and the conflict eventually brings Guanyin's agent Hui'an, also called Moksha, to the scene. Wujing accepts the religious mission and joins the party.

His name suggests awakening to purity. His prior history is anything but pure. In images of the pilgrimage he often appears as the sturdy, patient carrier of luggage and a quieter companion than Monkey or Pigsy. That role is easy to miss when the story is condensed to Monkey's battles. A narrative in which every obstacle is solved through confrontation would have little need for Sandy. Yet a long journey requires someone to carry the load, keep order, and do work that earns little applause. The novel attaches real spiritual significance to such labor, even as it gives its most memorable speeches to louder figures.

By now the expedition has acquired its recognizable form: Tripitaka the monk, Monkey his fierce protector, Pigsy the gluttonous and resourceful fighter, Sandy the steady attendant, and the white horse carrying their master. The group is sometimes described as five pilgrims, counting the horse. The journey's allegorical readings often connect the characters with aspects of a person's nature or with religious practices. That connection is worth exploring, but their personalities also function as an ensemble in an adventure story. Monkey may save the party and still make the crisis worse. Pigsy may grumble and still accomplish necessary work. Sandy may speak little and still be the reason the expedition can continue.

## A household that is a test

Shortly after the group forms, they encounter a seemingly hospitable household. A wealthy widow and her daughters offer comfort and the possibility of marriage. The temptation is particularly strong for Bajie, who has hardly stopped regretting the loss of domestic life. Tripitaka is resolved to maintain his monastic vows; Monkey remains alert and amused; Sandy does not seize the chance to settle down. Bajie becomes absorbed in the offer, trying to arrange a marriage for himself and showing little concern for whether the mission will survive his decision.

The household is a deliberate test arranged by sacred figures who appear in disguise. When the illusion is removed, Bajie's conduct has been exposed. The scene can be played as broad comedy, with an eager suitor outmaneuvered at every turn. It also asks a serious question about the meaning of commitment. The companions have agreed to undertake a task that promises a possible future reward but imposes immediate deprivation. Bajie sees food, shelter, affection, and status available now. Why continue through hunger and danger when a comfortable life appears on the doorstep?

The question is not trivial. Monastic renunciation is a chosen discipline, and the novel often uses Bajie's reluctance to keep the demands of that discipline visible. To a person who has made a vow, temptation need not arrive as a terrifying demon. It can arrive as a perfectly furnished house with dinner waiting. The test works because the offer is understandable.

Yet the episode also reveals the authority of the test makers. They stage a deception without informing the travelers, observe their weaknesses, and punish or embarrass the one who gives in. In the universe of the novel, divine beings exercise supervision through trials that a vulnerable human might experience as arbitrary. The larger story will return repeatedly to this tension between education and manipulation. The pilgrims do not always know whether the person at the roadside is a human asking for help, a demon seeking prey, or a god testing their responses.

## A spiritual journey in a dangerous world

The first part of the pilgrimage changes the significance of Monkey's earlier adventures. Before meeting Tripitaka, he had a simple measure of achievement: escape death, win recognition, defeat anyone who opposed him. Now every encounter has a second dimension. Can he defend another person without treating that person as a possession? Can he obey a teacher who is weaker than himself? Can a teacher distinguish firm discipline from humiliation? How do companions learn to trust one another when each has reason to doubt the others' judgment?

The scripture mission gives these questions a setting. The party cannot remain at a monastery and solve every conflict through debate. Its members cross mountains and rivers, need to eat, and must enter human communities whose inhabitants have different interests. The monk is often captured precisely because he assumes that an apparently human figure deserves kindness. At other times his impulse to help is exactly what someone in danger needs. The story will not permit a permanent rule that kindness always works, or that suspicion is always justified. It makes judgment difficult by giving actions consequences.

The historical Xuanzang adds another layer of interest. The actual monk's achievement lay in sustained travel, study, collection, and translation, supported by networks of teachers, patrons, monastic scholars, guides, and translators. The novel turns those scholarly and logistical labors into fantastic obstacles and a small traveling fellowship. One can admire the imaginative transformation without using it as evidence about the traveler himself. The difference helps historians and readers ask two separate questions: what did Buddhist travel across Asia involve in the seventh century, and why did later storytellers imagine the search for scripture as a series of transformations and ordeals?

The pairing of those questions makes the novel richer. It was written in a world where Buddhist teaching had been present in China for centuries and had interacted extensively with Daoist practice, government institutions, popular religion, theater, and prose storytelling. A figure like Guanyin could be recognized in several roles. A pilgrimage to the Buddhist homeland could become a vehicle for comedy about officials, quarrels about family loyalty, and scenes of magical combat. The structure does not require the reader to choose a single genre and disregard the others.

A modern listener may also notice how much of the group's unity rests on an unequal distribution of knowledge. Guanyin knows more of the plan than the pilgrims do. Monkey sees through certain disguises the monk cannot penetrate. The monk understands the binding force of vows better than Monkey. Bajie recognizes physical needs that the others would rather ignore. Sandy often puts the mission above competitive display. None of them has all the information needed for sound judgment. The pilgrimage is therefore a repeated experiment in cooperation among people who possess different kinds of competence.

## Names, roles, and the burden of a promise

The names the companions carry preserve their complicated histories. Sun Wukong was named by a teacher when he had no human family name. Zhu Bajie's name invokes the eight precepts, a discipline he repeatedly fails to live up to. Sha Wujing's name suggests purification or awakening to purity, although his history at the river has been one of violence. The dragon horse spends most of the novel performing a task far below his former rank. Names can be aspirations rather than descriptions of an achieved state.

Tripitaka has several names as well, and the distinctions illuminate his fictional and historical identities. Xuanzang is the name of the monk known from history. Tang Sanzang identifies a scripture master of the Tang, and Tripitaka points to the canonical collections of Buddhist teaching. English adaptations sometimes use the short label Tang Monk. A listener need not memorize every form immediately, but it helps to know that they refer to one central pilgrim in the novel. The name Monkey may likewise replace Sun Wukong in a translation, and Pigsy may replace Zhu Bajie.

Names are especially important in a story full of transformation. A creature can borrow an appearance, but a name associates it with a history of actions and obligations. The monk has agreed to retrieve scriptures and must keep that promise whether he is honored by the emperor or trapped by a monster. The companions have agreed to help and must continue to make that agreement real through labor. The king of Heaven could grant Wukong an impressive title without changing his conduct. On the pilgrimage, the titles are tested by the ordinary work of staying beside another person.

Consider Sandy carrying the luggage. The monk could not sustain his journey by accepting a high-minded pledge from someone who then refused the daily work. Somebody must keep the supplies in order and lift the baggage after a fight. Sandy's labor gives his name a practical content. The white horse has a similarly demanding task. To be transformed into a useful animal may seem a punishment for a dragon prince, but serving faithfully under conditions of no public glory becomes his part in a common achievement.

The group is therefore held together by a mixture of external authority and internal obligation. Guanyin has recruited them; the monk can control Monkey's fillet; the hope of future spiritual recognition gives them reason to continue. Yet none of those pressures could substitute for the daily choice to pick up the load, return after a quarrel, and protect a companion in danger. Their difficult cooperation is one of the novel's most durable subjects.

## Five travelers, one road

Imagine them at the end of the household test. The monk has resolved to continue. Monkey has enjoyed being proved right about the deceptive invitation. Pigsy has suffered embarrassment and is already thinking about the next meal. Sandy prepares the baggage. The white horse waits with the patience required of someone whose work is to carry another. The road west is still mostly unknown.

The party has acquired an extraordinary range of powers. It has also acquired internal problems that no single victory will resolve. Monkey needs to temper force with judgment. The monk needs to learn that a frightening appearance does not always tell the truth about a person's intentions, and that a harmless appearance can be deadly. Pigsy must decide, repeatedly, whether desire or commitment will govern him. Sandy and the horse must sustain their service when those with more flamboyant gifts quarrel.

The sacred mission remains larger than any one companion. That is why the novel begins the road with people who would have failed alone. A man of religious courage needs defenders; defenders need a purpose; a glutton needs discipline; a fallen being needs an opportunity to act otherwise. The first victory of the pilgrimage is that these very different creatures manage to travel together at all. Their next challenge will be harder: to discover whether they can trust their own eyes when appearances are designed to deceive them.

## Source and editorial notes

The account follows the commissioning and assembly of the pilgrimage in chapters 8–23. The monk's parentage and the chapter often numbered 9 require editorial caution: the 1592 Shidetang textual witness and several familiar modern editions do not present identical chapter arrangements. The historical Xuanzang (travel around 629–645 CE) is established through sources nearer to his life, not through supernatural events in this late-Ming novel. The golden fillet is an instrument of coercion as well as a literary emblem of discipline; readings should address both functions.
