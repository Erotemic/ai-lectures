# World Literary Epics and Classics

A comparative curriculum in the **World History** audio collection. The works
are grouped by their literary history and source tradition, without imposing an
ancient date on a much later book. This is a dedicated **browsing section and
curriculum**, currently sharing the `world-history` playback track. Retaining
that track keeps existing lecture IDs, cached audio, and progress intact.

## Reading and listening design

The goal is a complete, compelling account of the story accompanied by careful
analysis of character, institutions, narrative art, and historical or textual
evidence. The plot remains the central experience. Comparisons with earlier
sources occur when they change a reader's understanding of a pivotal scene.

Each lecture introduces its own setting and principal characters. There are no
required prerequisite recordings, and no spoken prompts about previous
lectures. Historical records, the narrated novel, manuscript or print variants,
retellings, and later reception are distinct categories of evidence.

The collection currently includes:

- **Ancient Literature**, with the narrative traditions of Gilgamesh, Inanna,
  Sinuhe, Atrahasis, Enuma Elish, the *Iliad*, *Odyssey*, *Ramayana*,
  *Mahabharata*, *Popol Wuj*, and other ancient works. Its own roadmap specifies
  surviving witnesses and boundaries.
- **Late-Imperial Chinese Literature**, with *Journey to the West*, lectures
  048000–054000, and a dedicated historical/textual lecture. The seventh-century
  Xuanzang is the historical pilgrim, not the date of the complete novel.
- **World Literary Epics and Classics**, currently with *Romance of the Three
  Kingdoms*, lectures 055000–071000, including a research lecture on Chen Shou,
  Pei Songzhi, Luo Guanzhong, and the early printed recensions.

The section names reflect the present library navigation; they can be grouped
under a richer UI hierarchy later without renumbering or duplicating lecture
transcripts. New works should have their own distinct regions and period labels
within the broader curriculum, not be classified as ancient by default.

## Standards for a finished series

1. Map the complete primary literary work before assigning installment boundaries.
2. Tell scenes with enough character, setting, dialogue-in-paraphrase, and
   consequences that the recording is a story rather than an index of events.
3. Keep editorial and historical-source details near relevant episodes, with
   longer textual-history treatment in a research lecture when warranted.
4. Compare the edition used with earlier surviving witnesses. Avoid identifying
   a received printed text with the first telling, or a fictional episode with a
   witnessed historical event.
5. Let the narrative material determine runtime. Long installments must earn
   their length through new scenes, ideas, and evidence rather than repeated
   synopsis. No short placeholders appear in the published manifest.
6. Keep sources, editor notes, exact citation links, and production metadata
   outside the spoken region. New lectures use six-digit IDs and retain a
   three-or-more-resource metadata sidecar.
7. Avoid silently substituting later film, television, or game adaptations for
   the chosen primary version.

## Possible future series

- *Shahnameh*: Firdausi's Persian epic, its historical horizon and the
  manuscript and illustrated traditions.
- *Water Margin*: the outlaws, versions of the text, government corruption,
  moral ambiguity, and the later novel's editorial history.
- *Dream of the Red Chamber*: family life, fiction and autobiography, and the
  disputed later chapters of the received 120-chapter novel.
- *The Tale of Genji*: Heian court society, the novel's narrative strategies,
  and the source and reception history.

These are candidates, not entries in the listening-ready catalog.
