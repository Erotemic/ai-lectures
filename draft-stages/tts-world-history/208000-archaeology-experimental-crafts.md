# Rebuilding the Past: Experiments with Stone, Fire, and Ships

A craftsperson stands over a piece of flint, holding a hammerstone. A blow strikes the edge at a carefully chosen angle. A thin flake comes free, leaving a scar on the stone and a sharp edge in the hand. The flake resembles artifacts excavated from a prehistoric site. The demonstration is impressive, but what has it proved? That one person today can make a similar object using a particular technique. It has not yet proved that every ancient craftsperson used exactly that technique.

On another site, a group builds a small furnace from clay, fills it with charcoal and ore, and uses air from bellows to sustain intense heat. If a spongy bloom of iron forms inside, the team has demonstrated that the selected materials and operating procedure can produce workable metal. It has not automatically identified the actual furnaces, fuels, or labor arrangements of a particular ancient community.

Far away at sea, a reconstructed ancient merchant ship takes wind into its sail. The crew discovers that a plausible rig can propel the vessel, but loading, steering, leakage, and fatigue may expose problems nobody anticipated from the excavated timbers alone. A replica has become both a vehicle and an experiment.

These activities belong to experimental archaeology: the deliberate reconstruction of past processes under documented conditions to test archaeological ideas. The methods are powerful because objects and traces often preserve the result of an action while concealing the action itself. An experiment can narrow the range of possible techniques, reveal hidden requirements, and sometimes show that a popular reconstruction would not work.

We'll examine three cases in depth: stone-tool production, ancient smelting, and reconstructed ships. Along the way we will meet the experimentalists and excavators who developed the methods, and consider how a successful replica differs from a trustworthy claim about the past.

## Begin with a fragment of stone

Most prehistoric people had no access to industrially produced metal tools. Many made cutting edges by breaking stone in controlled ways. Archaeologists find the surviving cores, flakes, blades, and tools, sometimes in concentrations that preserve much of a working session. The objects record how stone broke, but they rarely preserve the hands and gestures that made it break.

Flint, chert, and obsidian fracture in ways that are partly predictable. A force applied near an edge can create a curved fracture through the material, separating a flake. The fracture surface may display features such as a bulb of percussion and ripples that help analysts identify how removal occurred. Those features are not exclusive to skilled human work in every context; natural impacts can also break stone. Pattern, setting, and repeated relationships among artifacts provide stronger evidence.

A particularly useful archaeological observation is the refit. When separate flakes can be fitted back onto a core, their scars and surfaces may reveal which removals occurred first. Imagine assembling a broken ceramic bowl, but with a sequence of intentional cuts: the location of each piece records a decision in a production process. Refitting can show the order of removals and sometimes where pieces were moved or discarded.

Experiments help interpret these observations. A knapper can strike a prepared core with a hammerstone, use a softer antler billet, or press with a pointed tool to detach a flake. The resulting scars, platforms, and fracture patterns can be compared with excavated artifacts. Changes in striking angle, raw material, or preparation may produce distinct tendencies.

The comparison is strongest when the knapper records what was done before examining the results. If investigators repeatedly change techniques until a tool happens to resemble a desired artifact, the exercise demonstrates possibility. If they vary one factor at a time, preserve all flakes, and compare predicted traces with archaeological finds, it becomes a more informative test.

## Who taught archaeologists to break stone?

The history of flint-knapping research extends well before modern university archaeology. Nineteenth-century investigators tried to establish whether apparently artificial chipped stones could be produced by human hands, and how to distinguish them from naturally fractured pieces. By the twentieth century, experimentation had become a systematic part of lithic study.

French prehistorian François Bordes developed influential approaches to prehistoric stone-tool industries and was himself a skilled knapper. In North America, Don Crabtree combined detailed practical knowledge with experiments on raw materials, striking techniques, and tool manufacture. His work demonstrated that skill with the material could expose manufacturing possibilities that classification from finished objects alone would miss.

The history was later examined by L. Lewis Johnson in an influential 1978 account of flint-knapping experimentation, published with comments from archaeologists and experimenters including Bordes and Crabtree. That exchange is worth remembering because it portrays the field as a conversation among people trying to discover what replication could establish, rather than as a sequence of famous lone inventors.

Crabtree paid particular attention to the properties of raw materials. Fine-grained stone allows different control than coarse, flawed stone. The same motion may remove a long flake from one piece and crush the platform of another. A replica made from superior imported flint may therefore be a poor experiment for an archaeological site where people worked with rough local stone.

Craft knowledge is evidence of a kind, but its role should be precise. The knapper's experience helps design plausible tests and interpret failures. It does not replace records of the raw material, tools, applied forces, time spent, and resulting waste. The best experiment makes it possible for another researcher to understand what was attempted and where the result came from.

## Learning from the chips left behind

Imagine an excavated workshop that contains hundreds of flakes, a few exhausted cores, and almost no complete blades. There are several possibilities. Perhaps blades were produced there and carried away. Perhaps the people made small flakes for immediate use. Perhaps later disturbances removed the finished tools. How might experiments help?

First, researchers produce a reference assemblage using a specific strategy, collecting every waste flake as well as the finished pieces. They record the quantity and shape of debris, prepared platforms, fractured cores, and broken attempts. Another experiment uses a different strategy on equivalent stone. The resulting assemblages can be compared with the excavation.

Suppose one technique produces numerous small preparation flakes before each blade removal, while another leaves a different proportion of trimming debris. If the excavated assemblage resembles the first pattern and includes core scars consistent with it, that supports the corresponding production model. It still allows alternative sequences that could generate similar waste. The argument becomes stronger through multiple discriminating observations.

The physical arrangement of objects may be equally informative. Refitting pieces found meters apart can show that a core was worked in one place and its fragments moved elsewhere. Experiments on trampling and disturbance can help estimate how much later activity might disperse flakes. But the laboratory cannot recreate every ancient action, so excavated stratigraphy and spatial recording remain indispensable.

This is one of experimental archaeology's most valuable contributions: it tells us where the diagnostic evidence should be. A tool has a shape that catches the eye. The waste from making it may contain a much more specific account of the process.

## When a furnace refuses to work

Metal technology brings a different challenge. A lump of iron ore does not become a useful knife simply because it is placed over a fire. A bloomery furnace must reach suitable temperatures and maintain a chemical environment in which oxygen is removed from iron oxides. Charcoal supplies both heat and carbon-bearing gases. Airflow, furnace geometry, ore properties, and slag behavior affect the result.

In many early iron-smelting traditions, iron could be produced below its melting temperature. The product was a porous mass of metallic iron mixed with slag, called a bloom. It required consolidation and further working to make useful stock. An experiment that obtains a bloom has completed one major step; converting it into a bar or tool is another difficult process with its own material losses.

Archaeometallurgist Peter Crew conducted long-running experimental investigations of early iron production in Britain, partly motivated by furnace and slag remains from archaeological sites in Wales. His work illustrates the value of repeated, documented trials. A single smelt may fail because the furnace wall cracks or air supply is inadequate. A second trial may succeed after a change to the ore preparation or the placement of the air inlet.

The failures are often the informative part. If a proposed furnace arrangement repeatedly makes poor iron despite plausible operating conditions, it prompts the investigators to reconsider the reconstruction. Perhaps the ancient furnace was taller, the airflow differed, the ore had been roasted, or charcoal was prepared differently. Archaeological slag and furnace-wall fragments can then be reexamined for traces that distinguish the alternatives.

This can become a cycle of prediction and test. A reconstruction predicts a certain slag composition, bloom yield, and pattern of damage to the furnace. The experiment records those outcomes. Researchers compare them with excavated slags and walls. Where they disagree, the hypothesis is revised. This is a stronger approach than building a furnace that looks ancient and declaring the result authentic.

## The craftsperson is part of the apparatus

An experiment in metalworking is difficult to standardize because skilled decisions are continuous. A smelter hears the air moving, sees the color of the furnace opening, judges the behavior of slag, and decides when to adjust fuel or airflow. Two operators using nominally identical equipment may obtain different results.

This is one reason published debates about experimental smelting emphasize recording both numbers and practical choices. Temperature readings, charcoal quantities, ore composition, air supply, fuel additions, and timing can be measured. Descriptions of the operator's observations and interventions are also important. A graph of temperature alone does not describe every event in the furnace.

The same issue arises when experiments involve traditional skills maintained by living craft communities. An archaeologist learning from experienced metalworkers may discover techniques absent from a modern engineering reconstruction. Credit should belong to the practitioners who contribute the techniques and practical judgments, rather than being attributed entirely to the academic who writes the report.

Ethnographic comparison must still be used with care. A modern smith may use a particular furnace for reasons specific to local materials, markets, and inherited knowledge. Demonstrating that the technique works under those conditions does not prove the same practice was used in the same way thousands of years ago. It provides a physically informed candidate that can be tested against archaeological evidence.

Even the amount of labor matters. A furnace may be technically feasible but require days of wood collection, ore processing, charcoal making, and heavy work. Experiments can expose this hidden cost. Such estimates help historians think about production scale and organization, though they depend on assumptions about workers, resources, and experience.

## A ship recovered from the seabed

Let us turn to a case where the original structure survived in remarkable detail. Off the northern coast of Cyprus, archaeologists excavated a Greek merchant ship that sank in the late fourth century before the common era. It became known as the Kyrenia ship. Michael Katzev directed the excavation, and J. Richard Steffy studied and reconstructed its hull construction.

The surviving timbers preserved the keel and much of the planking, along with evidence for joints, framing, repairs, and cargo. In a 1985 technical report, Steffy described how the planks had been joined edge to edge using mortise-and-tenon connections. The construction sequence was different from methods that begin with an internal skeleton of frames: much of the hull's shape was established by assembling its shell.

An archaeologist can draw the surviving timber arrangement and infer how the vessel was built. But drawings leave practical questions. How would builders bend planks into position? How would the vessel behave when loaded? Could it sail effectively with a reconstructed rig? How would the hull respond to changing seas?

A modern replica called Kyrenia II was built to investigate such questions. The project involved maritime researchers and shipbuilders, including Harry Tzalas, and drew on Katzev's and Steffy's archaeological work. It used the evidence from the excavated vessel to guide its construction while inevitably making choices where parts of the original were missing.

A reconstruction must disclose those choices. If there is no surviving evidence for part of the rigging, the modern rope arrangement remains a hypothesis. If replacement wood differs from the ancient timber, its behavior may differ. If modern safety equipment is added, it can alter weight and handling. None of these choices makes the voyage worthless; it changes what the trial can tell us.

## The replica that took on water

Sea trials produce evidence unavailable from the wreck alone. One striking account of Kyrenia II's development concerns its initial leakage. Harry Tzalas described how the replica began taking on water when launched, despite expectations that the wooden planks would swell and seal. The builders had to confront a problem through practical observation, repair, and further trials.

That episode is instructive. We might expect the replica simply to confirm the reconstruction. Instead, its behavior challenged assumptions about watertightness and the condition of the ancient hull. The original ship had survived for years and undergone repairs, including lead sheathing. Its excavated state therefore represented a vessel with a history of maintenance, not a pristine design frozen on its first day at sea.

The trials also involved loading amphorae, experimenting with anchoring and berthing, and sailing in different conditions. In the 1980s the replica undertook voyages that explored its handling over longer distances. Each observation added information about a feasible way to operate such a merchant vessel.

We can be more exact about the conclusions. The replica's successful sailing shows that the chosen reconstruction can function under the tested conditions. It can constrain estimates of performance and clarify practical challenges. It cannot establish the speed of every ancient merchant ship, the seamanship of all ancient crews, or the exact path of the Kyrenia vessel's final voyage.

A reconstructed ship is affected by its crew. Modern sailors have different training, instruments, expectations, and safety margins. Experimental results should therefore record who was aboard, what they knew, how the weather behaved, and what cargo was carried. A voyage without such information remains a demonstration rather than a repeatable experiment.

## Counterfactual tests and controlled comparisons

The hardest question for experimental archaeology is often whether a result distinguishes competing explanations. If both proposed techniques can make an object, success alone decides very little. To make progress, the experimenter needs a prediction on which the proposals differ.

Imagine two explanations for marks on an ancient stone blade. One says the edge was produced by direct percussion, with a hammer striking the core. The other says it was removed using indirect percussion, with an intermediate punch. Both can produce long flakes under some circumstances. Researchers would measure traits expected to differ, test them across several raw materials and knappers, and compare the distributions with the excavated sample. Perfect separation may remain impossible, but an experiment can establish which observations are discriminating.

Or consider two furnace designs. Both make iron. One produces abundant slag that accumulates inside the chamber; the other permits slag to flow out through an opening. Excavated slag types and furnace deposits may favor one method. A controlled experimental series can measure how varying furnace construction changes slag textures and chemical composition.

For a ship, the comparison might concern a reconstructed sail arrangement. Two plausible rigs could be tried with comparable winds, cargo, and crew. The results might show one works better in particular conditions. The finding may help explain why such a rig would have been useful without proving that an ancient sailor chose it.

This logic is closer to scientific model testing than to historical reenactment. The experiment makes the physical consequences of a hypothesis explicit and allows the excavated evidence to push back.

## Reenactment, reconstruction, and experimental test

There is room for several activities, but they answer different questions. Historical reenactment can communicate the experience of a particular period and help participants appreciate material constraints. A reconstruction may produce a plausible object using sources and skilled craft. An experiment specifies a question, method, measurements, and potential outcomes that could favor or weaken an interpretation.

Consider a museum workshop where someone makes a stone arrowhead in ten minutes. The demonstration can teach that the material fractures in controlled ways. It may also make the required skill visible. But we would need records of practice, stone quality, technique, failure rates, and debris before using the ten-minute performance to estimate prehistoric manufacturing time.

Similarly, a reconstructed house may keep occupants warm, but its performance depends on ventilation, fuel, humidity, insulation, and occupation habits. A single comfortable night cannot establish how an ancient community organized its domestic life. Seasonal measurements with controlled comparisons provide a different kind of evidence.

These distinctions guard against two errors. One error is to assume that because a modern researcher can make something, the ancient explanation is established. The opposite error is to dismiss experiments as theater because they cannot reproduce the past exactly. Their value lies between those extremes: they test the consequences of specific possibilities.

## Failure, reproducibility, and credit

An important contribution of good experiments is making failure publishable. A furnace that collapses may reveal that the proposed clay mixture is too weak. A flake that shatters may show how a particular stone responds to a blow. A replica that leaks can force a reconsideration of hull seams and repairs. Discarding these observations would bias the historical record toward successful reconstructions.

Reproducibility does not require every operator to perform identically. It requires sufficient records for others to understand the materials and procedures, repeat them in a meaningful way, and interpret the variation. In craft experiments, human skill is one of the variables. Instead of hiding it, investigators should describe it and recognize the people who supply it.

The relevant authorship can therefore be broad. Archaeologists excavate and record the original remains. Historians interpret documents. Craftspeople select materials and solve construction problems. Chemists analyze residues. Sailors conduct trials. When research reports list only the scholar who proposed the project, they may obscure the source of decisive knowledge.

This is particularly evident in experimental shipbuilding, where the skills of carpenters and sailors influence whether a theoretically plausible design works. In iron smelting, experienced furnace operators can recognize changes that an instrument alone would not explain. A well-supported interpretation credits both the craft and the analysis.

## What we can take back to the excavation

An archaeologist returns to the original flint scatter, slag heap, or shipwreck with more questions than before. That is a sign of progress. The stone experiments suggest which waste flakes to collect and measure. The smelting trials suggest which slag structures or furnace-wall changes might identify operating conditions. The sea trials suggest how to interpret repairs, rigging evidence, and cargo constraints.

Each experiment allows a claim of the form: under these recorded conditions, this technique produced these traces. If the archaeological traces match, the technique becomes a better-supported candidate. If they differ, researchers must reconsider the candidate or explain the difference. The experiments can also reveal that several techniques remain possible, a result that protects the history from false certainty.

We began with three acts of making: striking stone, smelting iron, and sailing a reconstructed ship. Each produced a working result, but the important knowledge came from observing how that result was made and what evidence it left. Experimental archaeology transforms a plausible story about the past into a set of physical questions that can be tested, revised, and connected to actual excavations.

The past cannot be repeated. The mechanisms by which people changed materials are still available to investigate. That is the opening through which experiments contribute to history.


## Making a new object versus repairing an old one

Experiments often concentrate on how an object was first made. Archaeological artifacts frequently spent years being repaired, adapted, and recycled. Those later actions may dominate the traces we excavate. The Kyrenia ship is a useful reminder: its timbers carried evidence of repairs, and the hull had been maintained before its last voyage. A replica of the ship's presumed original condition may behave differently from a model that incorporates decades of wear.

The same issue appears in stone tools. A scraper may be repeatedly resharpened, each time removing material and altering its shape. An artifact classified by its final form could have started as a larger tool with a different appearance. Experimental resharpening sequences can show which changes accumulate and what sorts of flakes the maintenance process produces. The waste from repair may then help interpret a workshop where no new tools were made.

In metalworking, recycled scrap may contain elements originating from several ore sources. A craft experiment with freshly smelted metal can reproduce one manufacturing step while missing the role of older material in the actual artifact. Investigators can therefore compare fresh production, recycling, and repair operations to see which better explains chemical composition, joins, wear, and tool marks.

## How much can a single trial measure?

A reconstructed ship might cover a certain distance in favorable weather. That distance is an observation, but converting it into the average speed of ancient trade requires repeated trials across winds, seasons, and cargo weights. The crew's decisions and maintenance needs also affect the result. Weather records and the details of the voyage are therefore part of the experimental data.

A useful comparison is to test the replica with different load distributions. As cargo moves, the vessel's trim and stability may change. Loading amphorae in different arrangements could reveal which patterns are safe or convenient. Yet excavated cargo often reflects its position at the moment of sinking, after the vessel had been damaged and shifted. The comparison is strongest when the experiment is interpreted alongside underwater excavation records.

Similarly, recording the maximum temperature reached by a reconstructed furnace reveals only one aspect of the process. To assess production, researchers also need ore mass, fuel use, slag mass, bloom quality, labor, and failed smelts. Repeating trials with altered air supply or ore preparation can reveal which variables have the largest effect. Each measurement sharpens the historical question the experiment can answer.
