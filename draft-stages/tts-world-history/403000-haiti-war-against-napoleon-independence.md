# Against Napoleon: The War That Made Haiti, 1802–1804

At the beginning of 1802, French warships appeared off Saint-Domingue carrying an expedition determined to restore imperial command. Napoleon Bonaparte had sent an army across the Atlantic. Its officers said that France would regain order in a colony whose leading general, Toussaint Louverture, had exercised extensive autonomy. For many people in Saint-Domingue, the crucial question was what restoring order would mean. They had fought for emancipation. Could a government that sent an army to disarm their leaders be trusted not to return them to slavery?

Over the next two years, the campaign became a catastrophic war for the French expedition and a foundational war for a new country. Toussaint was captured and sent away. Other commanders changed sides. Disease ravaged troops, but Haitian victory also depended on military organization, strategic choices, popular resistance, and the refusal to accept French rule. By January 1804, the generals of the victorious forces proclaimed an independent Haiti.

How did that reversal happen? How did men who had once defended French republican abolition become the leaders of an army fighting France? And what did the victory cost people who had to live through it?

## Why Napoleon crossed the ocean

The French republic's abolition of slavery in 1794 had helped Toussaint's forces defeat imperial rivals. But Napoleon sought to reassert metropolitan control over Saint-Domingue and its profitable exports. Toussaint's 1801 constitution had declared slavery abolished while also asserting local autonomy and making him governor for life. It retained formal membership in the French empire, but Napoleon would not accept its practical independence.

Charles Leclerc, Napoleon's brother-in-law, commanded the expedition. He had professional reasons to seek success and family connections that made the campaign politically important to the Bonaparte circle. His wife Pauline accompanied the expedition to the Caribbean. These details remind us that imperial decisions were made by people with careers, marriages, reputations, and expectations—not by an abstract machine called France.

French forces brought military experience, equipment, ships, and senior officers. They expected to regain control by defeating or disarming Toussaint's armies and separating the population from his authority. Public statements reassured inhabitants that abolition would be respected, but military plans and imperial policy made those assurances unstable. France's later actions elsewhere in the Caribbean would turn suspicion into an urgent warning.

The opening campaign was not a simple contest of one united Haitian army against a united French one. Officers including Henri Christophe and Jean-Jacques Dessalines made tactical decisions under pressure. Some resisted the landing; some eventually accepted French terms. Local conflicts and old rivalries persisted, and commanders could make different judgments about whether French promises were credible. The final independence coalition would emerge from these shifting positions.

## Cap-Français and the burning of a city

Leclerc's forces targeted strategic ports, including Cap-Français in the north. Christophe, associated with its defense, resisted French occupation and ordered or oversaw destruction rather than surrendering the city intact. For civilians, the destruction of buildings and the loss of shelter could be a direct catastrophe. For a commander, denying facilities to an invading army could seem militarily necessary.

This is a good example of why war cannot be understood from the campaign map alone. The decision to burn a town might delay an enemy's use of its warehouses. It also destroys homes, shops, records, and food stores. A civilian who had no part in the strategic calculation still bears the cost. The revolution's leaders were trying to defend emancipation, yet defense itself could be ruinous to the communities they sought to protect.

In the countryside, forces fought through difficult terrain that European commanders did not fully control. Haitian fighters knew roads, paths, and local supplies, although disease and shortages affected them too. The invaders held ports and major bases, but the struggle could not be decided by occupying a capital or arresting one leader. The enemy was not a fixed army whose defeat would restore the old plantation system automatically.

## The limits of European military superiority

French expeditionary commanders brought professional military experience from European warfare. They possessed artillery, trained infantry, and naval transport. Those advantages were real but did not automatically translate into control of a Caribbean countryside shaped by plantations, mountains, rivers, and local communication networks. A force could seize a port and find that supply routes into the interior were insecure.

Each ship that brought soldiers also brought logistical demands. Troops needed food, powder, ammunition, horses or substitutes, shelter, and medical care. They could consume supplies stored for civilians or disrupt harvests needed by the wider population. When units moved into unfamiliar districts, local knowledge affected how quickly they could travel and whether they could anticipate ambushes.

Revolutionary soldiers were not omniscient about the landscape, and their own armies suffered shortages and disease. But many had fought on the island for years. They knew how French forces tended to advance and how difficult it could be for a European expedition to hold dispersed positions. Some had served inside French commands and understood their methods. The revolution had created a generation of military professionals whose capabilities Napoleon's government underestimated.

This was a war in which occupying territory and controlling political allegiance were separate tasks. A town might be held by French troops while rural networks continued supplying resistance. The degree of control could change quickly, and both armies might claim authority over the same people. That uncertainty intensified hardship for civilians and made every promise of security conditional on the next campaign.

## Crête-à-Pierrot

One important confrontation occurred in March 1802 at Crête-à-Pierrot, a fortified position in the Artibonite region. Haitian defenders resisted a larger French force and eventually withdrew. Dessalines and other revolutionary commanders were associated with the defense, which became important in the memory of the war. Accounts also credit Marie-Jeanne Lamartinière with fighting there, sometimes in uniform conventionally described as male military dress. The documentary basis for particulars of her actions is less complete than that for senior officers, but her place in Haitian memory points toward women's military participation.

A heroic battle story tends to end at the most dramatic assault. A military analysis has to ask what happened after the defenders left. Did they preserve a fighting force? Did the attackers gain a position they could hold? What did each side lose in casualties, supplies, and time? The French could win a fort and still fail to secure the countryside. Haitian forces could withdraw without abandoning the larger cause.

The battle also reveals a political fact. Men and women who had experienced emancipation were prepared to fight an army claiming to act for the state that had once proclaimed their freedom. Whatever French officers thought they were restoring, they faced a population whose understanding of law and legitimate government had been transformed by a decade of revolution.

## The capture of Toussaint

During the campaign, Toussaint negotiated and withdrew from active command under arrangements that he expected to protect him. In June 1802 French officers seized him, deported him to France, and detained his family. This act profoundly damaged trust. For people already uncertain whether French promises could protect their liberty, the treatment of the best-known Black commander was a warning about the consequences of surrender.

Toussaint was eventually imprisoned at Fort de Joux and died in April 1803. He never saw the declaration of independence. Later narratives sometimes imply that he directly commanded the final campaign from captivity or planned the state proclaimed after his death. He did not. His political and military achievements prepared the ground, while the war that ended French rule unfolded after his removal.

The personal scene is stark. A man who had gained his freedom, built armies, governed a colony, and negotiated with European powers ended his life far from his wife, home, and the land where he had fought. He did not control the fate of his family. The state that had promoted him as a republican general deprived him of political and personal freedom. For supporters still in Saint-Domingue, this was a lesson about the limits of French protection.

## What happened in Guadeloupe

Events outside Saint-Domingue influenced the war's direction. In 1802, French forces restored slavery in Guadeloupe after bloody fighting. Napoleon's legislation that year maintained slavery in colonies where the earlier abolition had not taken effect, and French imperial policy was increasingly willing to revive coerced plantation production. The legal situations of the colonies differed, but inhabitants of Saint-Domingue watched a neighboring colony lose the freedom that France had once proclaimed.

An official promise that slavery would not return was now judged against actual French behavior. This did not mean every person in Saint-Domingue immediately took the same side. Some officers remained with French forces for a time; some changed allegiance more than once. But the prospect of re-enslavement strengthened the conviction that only the defeat of French military control could make emancipation secure.

There is a broad lesson about promises under coercion. The credibility of a promise depends partly on what constrains the promiser. If a ruler can withdraw liberty when a military opportunity arises, a subject has reason to demand institutions that prevent reversal. In Saint-Domingue, years of shifting colonial policy had given people very little basis for trusting a distant government that still considered them part of its imperial possessions.

## Sanité Bélair and the violence of military occupation

The history of the war includes people whose names are much less widely known than those of Toussaint or Dessalines. One was Suzanne Bélair, remembered as Sanité Bélair, a woman who served as a revolutionary officer with her husband Charles Bélair. French forces captured and executed them in 1802. Later commemorations recount her courage at execution, but the details of dialogue and gesture vary among retellings and should not be reproduced as if a recording existed.

Even without the dramatic final words often attributed to her, the documented outline is powerful. A woman had taken an armed role in a movement founded upon the refusal to be reduced to property. Her punishment by an invading army made the danger of defeat personal and visible. The prominence of her story in Haitian memory also challenges an account that reserves political action for the men who later signed declarations.

Women cooked for troops, nursed the wounded, carried information, organized households, resisted coercion, and, in some cases, fought. Surviving military records favor the actions of officers who wrote orders and reports. A careful narrative brings women into view where evidence exists, without inventing private conversations or assuming that all served in the same capacity.

## Women, families, and the work of sustaining resistance

The official military record gives us the names of generals and senior officers, but a campaign also depends upon people supplying food, making clothing, tending wounds, caring for children, moving information, and finding places to hide. These activities could determine whether a soldier returned to the field or whether a family survived the passage of rival armies. The labor was often performed by women, whose names were less likely to enter a commander's official correspondence.

Sanité Bélair is a documented exception to that archival imbalance. Her execution made her an identifiable figure in the history and later memory of revolutionary resistance. Other women appear in accounts of fighting and military support, including Marie-Jeanne Lamartinière. We should neither erase their contributions because the sources are incomplete nor turn every commemorative detail into a verified battle report. Their visible cases invite historians to ask what kinds of work the archives routinely overlook.

Family vulnerability also shaped allegiance. A government asking a soldier to surrender might promise safety to his household. If previous surrendered leaders had been imprisoned or their relatives detained, that promise would be difficult to trust. Toussaint's seizure thus mattered beyond his personal fate. It changed calculations about the risk of laying down arms, especially for men and women whose lives had already been transformed by abolition.

For many people, the war was fought in a place they wanted to remain after armies departed. Their homes and crops were not replaceable strategic counters. They needed an end to military occupation as well as an end to the legal possibility of slavery. Independence could offer that future, but the war required them to survive long enough to reach it.

## An army undone by war and disease

Yellow fever and other diseases devastated the French expedition. European soldiers arriving in the Caribbean often lacked the acquired immune experience of people who had lived in the region, though exposure and disease outcomes varied, and local inhabitants were far from immune to illness. Climate and disease contributed enormously to French losses. Leclerc himself died in November 1802.

It is tempting to say that yellow fever defeated Napoleon. That is an incomplete explanation. Disease reduced manpower, weakened units, complicated supply, and increased the cost of holding territory. Haitian resistance imposed further costs, denied secure control of rural areas, and exploited the expedition's vulnerabilities. An army decimated by illness still had to be fought, and an army could lose a campaign even after winning individual engagements.

Leclerc's successor, Donatien de Rochambeau, pursued a harsh counterinsurgency marked by extreme violence. Contemporary reports and later histories describe terror tactics and atrocities by French forces. Haitian troops also committed acts of brutal violence during the struggle. The existence of atrocities on more than one side does not erase why the war was being fought. French imperial command carried a credible threat of renewed slavery; independence forces sought to prevent it, while at times employing violence that requires judgment on its own terms.

The war could radicalize its participants. An officer who had once hoped to negotiate with France might conclude that French occupation made personal freedom impossible. A civilian whose household was destroyed might seek revenge. A commander might regard mercy as dangerous if prisoners could return in a future expedition. None of these possibilities excuses indiscriminate killing, but they help explain the increasingly uncompromising politics of the independence struggle.

## The decision for a country

As the French campaign failed, officers who had earlier served under French command joined a renewed effort to expel it. Dessalines became the central military leader of the independence coalition, joined by figures including Henri Christophe, Alexandre Pétion, and others. Their collaboration was real, though their later rivalry shows that they had not reached permanent agreement about political organization.

The coalition adopted symbols associated with a distinct political community. Haitian tradition associates the creation of a blue-and-red flag with events at Arcahaie in 1803 and credits Catherine Flon with sewing it. Historians distinguish this commemorated account from the more complicated documentary history of flags used by revolutionary forces. Even where a vivid sewing scene is difficult to verify in detail, the symbolism is understandable: a fighting force was asserting allegiance to a community beyond France.

The final campaign depended upon soldiers, provisions, intelligence, and coordination among regional forces. It also depended upon the international situation. Renewed war between Britain and France restricted French naval support. Napoleon's larger imperial priorities changed, including his decision to sell Louisiana in 1803. The failure in Saint-Domingue was one factor in that broader strategic retreat, though the Louisiana Purchase had multiple causes.

The revolutionaries could not depend on British benevolence. Britain was a slaveholding imperial power, and its commanders acted in British interests. Nevertheless, war between empires could create openings for a force determined to prevent either empire from ruling it. Saint-Domingue's leaders had learned for a decade to navigate alliances without treating them as guarantees of emancipation.

## Why the final coalition held together

The commanders who eventually expelled France had not always shared a policy or even a military allegiance. Dessalines, Christophe, Pétion, and other officers brought different experiences of slavery, freedom, regional struggle, and French service. Some had fought one another or served competing authorities in earlier years. Their coalition against Napoleon became possible when the survival of emancipation appeared to require eliminating French control altogether.

That agreement was powerful but limited. It answered the question of who should not govern the former colony. It did not settle who would distribute land, hold executive power, appoint officers, or control labor after victory. These unresolved disputes help explain why Haiti's early years contained both a successful assertion of sovereignty and violent internal political division.

The growing independence campaign also reached beyond its generals. Local communities had to decide whether to supply troops and which demands to obey. Merchants navigated the risks of French blockade, British naval power, and shifting port control. Soldiers increasingly fought under the idea of a country rather than simply a restored French republic. Military symbols and flags gave that emerging political identity visible form, even when later traditions simplified their exact origins.

The coalition's success reflected political clarity on one central point: the French expedition could no longer be trusted to preserve the freedom achieved during the earlier revolution. That conclusion enabled cooperation among leaders who would disagree profoundly once independence had been secured.

## Vertières

On November 18, 1803, Haitian forces confronted French troops at Vertières near Cap-Français. The battle has become a defining event in Haitian national memory. The army under Dessalines pressed the attack; François Capois, often remembered as Capois-la-Mort, appears prominently in later accounts of the fighting. Many famous details of his exploits are preserved through patriotic retelling rather than independently verified eyewitness transcripts.

The military outcome is clear: French control was collapsing. Rochambeau's forces were defeated and soon surrendered or evacuated. The last major French stronghold in the western colony passed out of imperial hands. The military revolution had made independence possible.

Consider what the result meant to people who had endured more than a decade of upheaval. Someone enslaved before 1791 had experienced a revolt, competing promises of emancipation, foreign invasions, and now the expulsion of the government most likely to reimpose slavery. A person born during the conflict might never have known a stable peace. Victory did not restore the dead or rebuild destroyed homes. It made possible something previously denied: a future in which the government would not legally own its population.

The language of victory can obscure the costs. Agricultural production had been disrupted, towns and farms damaged, and families scattered or bereaved. Soldiers trained in harsh warfare held extensive power. A new state would inherit both the principle of emancipation and the material consequences of war.

## Gonaïves, January 1, 1804

On January 1, 1804, at Gonaïves, Dessalines and other leaders proclaimed Haiti's independence. Louis Boisrond-Tonnerre, Dessalines' secretary, prepared a major declaration. The surviving document is an extraordinary primary source: it announces separation from France, insists upon safeguarding liberty, and demands an oath of political rupture. An early copy was later rediscovered in a British archive, reminding us that the evidence of the Haitian Revolution survives across the Atlantic as well as within Haiti.

The declaration's tone is severe. It reflects a war in which French troops had sought to restore control, threatened the achievements of emancipation, and inflicted grave violence. A proclamation is also a political act. Its authors were trying to bind military leaders to the new state and warn foreign powers against another attempt at conquest. It was not a neutral summary of every inhabitant's opinion.

The new name Haiti recalled the island's Indigenous past, although the modern state's territorial control and later border history were complex. The declaration established independence of the former French colony, not the uninterrupted possession of every part of Hispaniola under one government. It proclaimed a break with French sovereignty and set the task of securing that break through institutions, defense, and diplomacy.

Dessalines stood at the center of this moment, but the document bears the names of many officers. Those signatures remind us that even the military leadership was a coalition. Underneath it stood people who had refused slavery, carried supplies, fought, cultivated food, protected relatives, negotiated flight, or survived the destruction of their towns. The nation proclaimed in 1804 emerged from their struggles as well as from the decisions of its commanders.

## The declaration as an artifact

The declaration read at Gonaïves is often discussed as though it were a single memorable slogan. The surviving document offers more. It presents an act by military leaders, a formal renunciation of French sovereignty, and an address to the people of Haiti. It names officers who took part in the political rupture. The document was intended to make an irreversible public commitment, one that foreign governments could hear and local commanders could be held to.

An original copy of the declaration was rediscovered in a British archive in the twenty-first century by historian Julia Gaffield. That discovery illustrates the dispersal of Caribbean historical records through colonial administrative networks. A document produced in the new Haitian state could be preserved in correspondence collected by officials elsewhere. The archive's location says something about the international scrutiny facing Haiti from its birth.

The declaration is a primary source, but it is not a complete account of the country's inhabitants. Its named signatories were senior military figures, not representatives chosen through a modern national election. Its rhetoric draws sharp lines between the new nation and the French power that had invaded it. A historian can study these choices while remembering that civilians, rural cultivators, and women also helped make independence possible, often without appearing in the document.

Reading the declaration this way makes it more historically meaningful. It was the product of victory, fear, political purpose, and a revolutionary commitment against re-enslavement. It provides evidence of what the founders were willing to promise and demand. Its later history shows how those commitments were tested under the pressures of governing.

## Independence was a beginning

It is right to describe the Haitian Revolution as a victory by formerly enslaved people against the slave order. It is also necessary to ask what followed. Would the state permit people to control their land and work? Would military leaders share authority? How would the new nation respond to French threats and foreign governments reluctant to recognize its independence? What would happen to white inhabitants who had remained after the war?

These questions did not receive peaceful answers automatically. The months following independence included killings of remaining French colonists ordered by Dessalines. Later struggles divided the leadership. Decades afterward, France would demand a huge payment as a condition of recognition. None of this cancels the achievement of emancipation and independence. It shows how difficult it was to build a durable political and economic order after revolutionary war.

The war of 1802 to 1803 changed the political meaning of freedom in the colony. For Toussaint in 1801, it had still been possible to imagine autonomous emancipation under a French imperial connection. After the expedition, many revolutionaries concluded that connection itself had become a threat. Independence was the institutional answer they could impose through victory.

In January 1804, the French flag no longer governed the former colony. What remained was the human problem no military victory could solve alone: how to organize work, law, security, and political power among people who had fought to live free. The next history of Haiti begins there.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Dates and source distinctions

- **Early 1802:** Leclerc expedition lands; heavy fighting, including Cap-Français and Crête-à-Pierrot.
- **June 1802:** Toussaint seized and deported.
- **May 1802:** Napoleon's colonial slavery policy changes; French restoration of slavery in Guadeloupe intensifies fears in Saint-Domingue.
- **October 1802:** Sanité and Charles Bélair executed by French troops.
- **November 1802:** Leclerc dies; Rochambeau succeeds him.
- **1803:** Coalition for independence under Dessalines; Britain's war with France constrains naval supply.
- **November 18, 1803:** Battle of Vertières; French capitulation follows.
- **January 1, 1804:** Declaration of independence at Gonaïves.

### Historiographical cautions

- **Yellow fever:** A major contributor to French losses, not a sufficient explanation of Haitian victory.
- **1802 law:** Different colonies had different legal status; avoid saying every colony experienced identical restoration on one date.
- **Women's participation:** Sanité Bélair's execution is documented; many exact last words and detailed anecdotes are retrospective commemorations.
- **Flag tradition:** Catherine Flon's role is commemorated, but the precise scene should not be presented as a verified transcript.
- **Independence declaration:** There is a distinct formal act of independence, a proclamation or address, and later interpretive retellings. Refer to the actual primary document.
