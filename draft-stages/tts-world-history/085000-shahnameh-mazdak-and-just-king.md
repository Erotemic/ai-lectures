# 085000 — Shahnameh: Mazdak, the Just King, and the Book from India

# Shahnameh: Mazdak, the Just King, and the Book from India

A ruler sits in a court where learned men praise justice. A messenger arrives from a poor household that has paid too much, or from a distant province where officials have taken what they should have protected. The king is expected to listen, distinguish truth from flattery, and correct the wrong. This is the image that later Persian literature often attaches to Khosrow I, known as Anushirvan, the Just. Yet the story of his rise is connected to the suppression of a movement that challenged the distribution of wealth and power in his father's kingdom. That collision makes the epithet difficult and worth examining. The same ruler can be praised for administrative fairness while being remembered as a fierce opponent of social revolution.

We are in the later Sasanian part of Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*. Unlike Zahhak or Rostam, Khosrow I was a historically attested monarch, ruling from 531 to 579 of the common era. His predecessor was his father Kavad I. Under them the empire faced aristocratic rivalry, religious disputes, taxation problems, military threats, and the need to support an expensive state. The epic translates some of those struggles into memorable encounters among kings, advisers, reformers, and people harmed by injustice. We can tell the stories as literature and also use surviving scholarship to ask which political processes can be established independently.

## A reformer enters a court that fears him

The story of Mazdak is difficult because the surviving traditions do not speak with one voice. He appears as a religious or social reformer whose ideas challenge the distribution of goods and the privileges of powerful households. The later accounts that describe him often come from authors whose political and religious commitments made them hostile to him. That affects both the content and the tone of the accusations. A listener should understand the claims made by these sources and also recognize the limits of what they allow us to recover about Mazdak's own teaching.

The court of Kavad becomes a setting where reform, ideology, and political strategy intersect. A sovereign confronted by powerful landholding or priestly interests may find advantage in supporting a movement that weakens their authority. That possibility does not settle what the king or Mazdak sincerely believed. Political interest and conviction can coexist. In the epic version, however, the contest is cast in moral language about disorder, the protection of households, and the king's responsibility to choose rightly. It is told from a perspective that celebrates the restoration of conventional order under Khosrow.

We should hear the reformer's speech without treating the later narrator's condemnation as a transcript of Mazdak's program. Assertions about communal property and access to women are often repeated in polemical accounts. Because such accusations can be weapons used against a defeated religious movement, historians compare the testimonies carefully and avoid assuming they are exact descriptions of practices. The *Shahnameh* is valuable evidence for how the controversy was remembered. That is a different claim from saying it offers direct access to a sixth-century meeting of Mazdak's followers.

The tale presents a terrible ending for Mazdak and his adherents. The restoration of order is narrated through punishment, and the king who emerges as an ideal of justice participates in the destruction of a rival movement. The contradiction should remain visible. A ruler may be praised for protecting established rights while denying legal or physical protection to people labeled subversive. A reader who admires the poem can still ask whose claims were excluded from its model of justice.

## The question raised by Mazdak

A religious and social movement associated with a teacher named Mazdak gained influence in the time of Kavad. The surviving reports are difficult. Many were written by opponents or by later authors whose descriptions emphasize scandal, disorder, and threats to elite households. The movement is commonly linked with criticism of extreme inequalities and proposals concerning access to property and other resources. But the precise teachings, practices, and political organization cannot be reconstructed simply by trusting the most hostile later account. A careful retelling distinguishes what the story says from what the historian can independently know.

In the epic narrative, Mazdak's influence troubles the established order. The ruler is drawn toward him, and nobles fear that their holdings and privileges are at risk. The drama can be told as a dispute about justice. If some families have immense stores of food while others face hunger, what obligations arise? If privilege is hereditary, what claim do those excluded have to relief? And if a reformer offers a radical answer, how can a government distinguish a serious grievance from a policy that may create new coercion? The poem is generally suspicious of the movement, but the questions it raises are real enough to survive the writer's judgment of its representatives.

Kavad's position is politically precarious. An alliance with a reform movement could help him weaken nobles whose power restricts the crown. Such an alliance could also create enemies among people who command troops, land, and revenue. Political change does not occur in an empty room where a king chooses among abstract systems. It alters who controls resources, who owes service, and who can punish dissent. The established elite may describe a reform as chaos because it threatens its privileges; a ruler may favor a movement because it serves dynastic aims; the movement's own adherents may have motives distinct from both.

The story of Mazdak's downfall is violent. Khosrow and the royal order move against the movement, and the later tradition remembers mass punishment. Literary accounts use stark scenes to represent the destruction of Mazdak's followers. The point for the listener is not to enjoy a spectacle of suppression as a simple restoration of righteousness. The episode reveals the tragic difficulty of evaluating justice through sources produced in the shadow of victory. When the losing side's voices survive poorly, the official story can turn complex grievances into proof that the defeated deserved their fate.

That does not require us to reverse the polemic and declare every reported reform beneficial. The responsible historical position is narrower. The movement existed and became entangled in major political struggles; the court's response helped reshape the balance of power. Many details of doctrine and the scale and circumstances of the repression are debated. The *Shahnameh* is valuable evidence for a later narrative of the event and for the moral vocabulary through which Persian court culture judged it. It is one source in a larger historical inquiry, not a neutral transcript of both sides.

## A fair tax and the people who pay it

Khosrow I is often known in literary tradition as Anushirvan, the Just. The epithet is a claim that later rulers and authors found worth repeating. Its power comes from the expectation that a sovereign should provide more than personal magnificence. A ruler must support a treasury, defend the realm, control officials, and distribute burdens in ways that can be defended as fair. The *Shahnameh* uses scenes of judgment and advice to give this ideal a human face.

Taxation provides a useful historical test. Sasanian administration depended upon revenue from land, households, and economic activity. The reforms associated with Khosrow and his predecessor Kavad are discussed in several historical traditions. Their details and chronology require careful reconstruction, but the underlying question is clear: how can a state collect resources reliably without destroying the productive households on which it depends? A government that extracts too much may weaken the very capacity that supports its armies and cities. A government that collects unpredictably makes planning difficult for those who cultivate the land.

In a royal anecdote, a single king can notice a farmer's distress and order an official to change his behavior. In an empire, the problem is larger. The sovereign cannot listen personally to every grievance. Systems of measurement, assessment, appeal, and enforcement determine whether a declared ideal of justice becomes a predictable experience. The difference between the exemplary story and institutional reality is not a reason to discard the story. It is a reason to ask what kind of justice the story imagines and what administrative evidence would be needed to know whether subjects received it.

The king's reputation can also serve as a political standard. Later writers may tell a cruel ruler that even the mighty Khosrow was praised for restraint. Whether the anecdote about Khosrow's personal intervention occurred becomes less important to that rhetorical use than the existence of an admired precedent. A royal ideal is capable of criticizing successors precisely because it has become larger than the uncertain biography of any one ruler.

## Anushirvan and the burden of fairness

Khosrow emerges as an ideal ruler in much later advice literature. The word 'just' here carries expectations that include restraining corrupt officials, hearing complaints, protecting a realm, and distributing burdens according to some accepted standard. But the ideals are not self-enforcing. An emperor cannot personally watch every collector at every village. The state needs records, officials, schedules, oversight, and a way to correct mistakes. The romantic image of a king hearing a single oppressed subject makes a broader administrative problem visible in miniature.

Khosrow's reign is associated with substantial fiscal and military reforms. A land survey and a more systematic structure of assessment helped the government anticipate revenue. Instead of relying only on varying proportions of agricultural yield, officials could calculate obligations with reference to crops and area, while other forms of taxation applied to people and trades. The reform had roots in Kavad's reign and involved regional administrators who had to record, collect, and remit payments. These arrangements can be studied from later accounts and such surviving administrative evidence as historians can locate. They are not proved by a single flattering anecdote about the king's love of justice.

Why would regularized taxation be praised? Predictability helps the government support garrisons, roads, and officials. It can also protect a cultivator from an arbitrary demand by someone with a sword. But a fixed obligation may become oppressive if a harvest fails and no allowance is made. A system that is fair by its formula can be cruel when the conditions behind the formula change. The historical accounts describe mechanisms for remission and control, but the actual success of those safeguards depended on officials who could be corrupt or distant from the crown. The political idea of justice must therefore face material realities.

The heroic age judged kings through courage, oaths, and hospitality. The Sasanian stories add a measure that is less visible but at least as consequential: how government makes its ordinary demands on people who cannot leave their fields whenever a royal succession changes. A ruler who wins a battle may still fail his subjects in tax collection. A ruler who proclaims fairness may still depend on a collector who exaggerates dues. To bring these concerns into a long epic is to make the life of the state part of the story of kingship.

## The disguised ruler and the frightened petitioner

Stories in which a king travels among his subjects in disguise are delightful because they invert the normal conditions of access. In court, information passes through counselors and officials. A petitioner knows that the manner of speech can affect the outcome. On an ordinary road, the ruler can supposedly hear what people say when they do not know who is listening. The disguise promises a direct view of society beyond ceremony.

Yet the scene contains an unavoidable asymmetry. The disguised king may reveal his identity and act with sovereign authority; the person speaking to him cannot make a comparable transformation. An apparently casual conversation can become a judgment upon a life. The ruler's desire for truthful information is admirable, but the method itself depends on a power imbalance. A wise king must avoid using it to punish a subject who had no fair reason to know that a stranger's question was a royal inquiry.

Such anecdotes often end with swift recognition: a victim is vindicated, a corrupt official exposed, a humble person rewarded. They model responsiveness. Real systems of justice have to operate on days when the king is absent and no revelation resolves the case. The epic encourages a ruler to care about individual harms. A historian can use that encouragement as evidence of an ideal while looking elsewhere for the actual procedures of courts and provincial administration.

The ideal also depends on how we define a truthful report. A poor person might give an account shaped by fear of retaliation, and an official might tell the ruler what he expects to hear. A monarch cannot solve the problem by commanding sincerity. He needs credible pathways for complaints, ways to test allegations, and consequences that do not depend on his personal mood. The contrast between one excellent judgment and a just system gives the literary image a contemporary intellectual interest without requiring us to pretend that Khosrow personally invented modern institutional safeguards.

## A king who wishes to know the truth

Anushirvan is repeatedly represented as eager for counsel. His court gathers wise men capable of solving puzzles, interpreting foreign knowledge, and explaining the good ordering of government. The figure most widely associated with this wisdom is Bozorgmehr, an adviser whose reputation became a center for many later stories. We should be careful about attribution: a wise saying assigned to Bozorgmehr in one medieval text may have circulated separately or been attached to his name long afterward. The courtly narrative uses the adviser as an ideal of intelligence that can speak effectively to power.

One famous group of stories concerns chess. The game arrives at the Persian court from India as a challenge; the learned adviser discovers its rules or meaning, and a Persian response is devised in the form of another game, often backgammon. The story is delightful because it transforms diplomacy into a contest of interpretive skill. A foreign court does not send an invading army but a board of pieces whose operation is unknown. The receiving court must learn the system by attention and reasoning. Solving the puzzle becomes a display of culture as well as intelligence.

Historically the diffusion of chess from India into Iranian and other cultures is a serious topic in the history of games. The attribution of the invention of backgammon to a named adviser is far less secure. In the narrative, the contrast between games matters. Chess represents one kind of ordered contest, where different pieces have defined powers and placement governs possibility. Backgammon represents another, where choice and chance interact. The exchange gives the poet a compact way to describe two courts recognizing each other's sophistication without armies meeting on a battlefield.

A ruler who wants foreign knowledge must make room for translators and advisers. The court's identity is not destroyed whenever an idea crosses a border. This was already suggested by the demons teaching Tahmuras writing and by Sindokht's negotiation across the boundary of a condemned genealogy. Now the theme enters the history of books. Knowledge can come from an outsider, and a king's ability to recognize its value becomes a sign of judgment.

## A book crosses languages

The physician Borzuya becomes a guide into another kind of royal achievement: the gathering and translation of knowledge. The narrative associates his journey to India with the stories that became the Persian and later Arabic traditions of *Kalila wa Dimna*. In the tale, the physician seeks a plant or remedy whose power is described in terms of raising the dead. The solution transforms the quest. The life-giving object is understood through knowledge that revives a mind rather than a medicinal plant that defeats mortality.

This is a familiar turn from Iskandar's search for the Water of Life. The conqueror wanted a substance that would preserve his body forever and failed to obtain it. The physician seeks a remedy and discovers that wisdom can preserve something of a human life by passing from teacher to student. Books are not immortal bodies, but they enable words and judgments to survive their original speakers. The story's answer is less spectacular than a miraculous fountain and more plausible as a program of civilization.

The translation history is important and complicated. Indian narrative materials moved into Middle Persian, and an Arabic version associated with Ibn al-Muqaffa became an influential part of the later tradition. The Middle Persian text that stood between the Indian sources and the Arabic literary work is not extant in a complete independent form. We reconstruct much of its role through later witnesses. The *Shahnameh*'s episode of the physician therefore joins a real history of cross-cultural translation to an exemplary court tale about a king who values learning.

Khosrow's court becomes admirable when it treats knowledge from elsewhere as worth seeking. That ideal should not obscure the political setting. Translation may serve administration, royal prestige, and religious debate as well as intellectual curiosity. A ruler who brings a book across borders is making a decision about what will enter his court's repertoire. The transmission of stories shows how cultural influence can outlast conquest; it can also show how a text changes when new readers interpret it for their own purposes.

## The physician and the plant that cures death

Anushirvan's learned world includes a physician called Burzoe, or Borzuya. In a famous account, he hears of a plant in India whose power is said to bring the dead back to life. He obtains permission to travel and search for it. The journey is long, and the promised plant eludes him. Instead of abandoning the quest in frustration, he seeks the counsel of someone who can explain what the report means. A sage reveals that the plant is an image. Ignorance is a kind of death; learning is the medicine that restores a person to intellectual life. The treasure he should seek is a book of fables and political wisdom.

The book is associated with the Indian *Panchatantra* and its Persian and Arabic transmission as *Kalila and Dimna*. The fables use animals as characters to examine persuasion, trust, intrigue, friendship, and government. Burzoe learns its contents, and the story imagines the difficulty of acquiring a book guarded as a royal treasure. In one version he is not permitted to copy it openly, so he commits the contents to memory and sends or preserves them by indirect means. The result is a transfer of practical wisdom between courts.

The quest has an elegant narrative reversal. A physician searches for a botanical cure to literal death and returns with stories meant to cure the failures of judgment that cause political and social harm. The metaphor is not the same as saying that literacy makes a body immortal. It says that thought can recover someone who acts in darkness through ignorance. In a long epic where terrible mistakes are often made by people who refuse advice, the idea is especially powerful. Good counsel can save lives if rulers heed it before their decisions become irreversible.

The transmission of *Kalila and Dimna* has its own history beyond the *Shahnameh*. Versions traveled through Sanskrit, Middle Persian, Arabic, and many later languages, with changes and additions. Claims about who personally translated which stage must be studied rather than simply taken from a legendary court anecdote. What matters for the epic's moral world is that Anushirvan's court is imagined as eager for knowledge from India, and the lessons acquired there concern the management of human relationships. The court can become learned by listening beyond itself.

## The physician who learns what cannot be carried

The fable of Borzuya's search turns on a change in what the traveler understands by rescue. At first the promised plant seems to offer the most literal imaginable victory over death. If there is a remedy that can restore life, a physician ought to find it and bring it home. The quest therefore appears to be an expression of medical skill and royal ambition together. A great king commissions a great healer to obtain an extraordinary resource.

The answer is less flattering to the idea of possession. Knowledge can be carried across a border in memory and written form, but it cannot be treated as a bundle of plants whose power remains unchanged wherever it is planted. Stories require translation, and translation requires interpretation. The physician returns with a means of reviving understanding through teaching and fable. The court's gain depends upon people capable of listening and learning, not simply upon control of a rare object.

This is a useful counterpoint to the poem's violent struggles over royal inheritance. A kingdom may lose territory and still preserve a body of wisdom that other cultures choose to transmit. Conversely, a king may possess an enormous territory and fail to understand those who live within it. The physician's adventure offers a model of durable achievement that a royal treasury cannot fully measure.

## The danger hidden in the ideal king

Anushirvan's reputation as a model of justice should now seem both more substantial and more complicated. Administrative reform is a real historical subject; a courtly image of fair hearing is a literary ideal; suppression of Mazdak's movement is an episode remembered through highly partisan traditions. If we combine those parts into a perfect ruler who never erred, we will have replaced analysis with praise. If we reverse them into a single condemnation, we will miss the significant reforms and the reason later writers found him a useful emblem of good government.

A figure can become a symbol of justice because later people want an example against which to measure their own rulers. The symbol may simplify the historical king, but it also provides a language for complaint. A storyteller who says that Anushirvan would have listened to a poor petitioner is indirectly criticizing a contemporary official who will not listen. Advice literature can use idealized past kings to express demands for better conduct in the present. The gap between remembered model and historical person is therefore an active part of the tradition's social use.

The Mazdak episode puts pressure on the symbol because the defeated reformers also made claims about fairness. The king's justice and the movement's justice were not necessarily the same. One may emphasize stable administration, protection of property, and orderly hierarchy. Another may emphasize the suffering produced by extreme inequality and the need to share resources. Both can claim the word, and neither is established simply by holding or losing power. The epic's later readers have to judge the conduct of rulers and reformers through the sources available, mindful of whose voices have survived.

This is another version of the Shahnameh's repeated question about knowledge. Rostam failed to recognize Sohrab. Kay Kavus could not interpret Siyavash's innocence reliably. Goshtasp chose to see Esfandiyar as a threat despite his service. Anushirvan's court is celebrated for finding rules in a game, counsel in a book, and regularity in tax collection. These achievements are responses to the dangers of ignorance and arbitrary judgment. Yet the treatment of Mazdak asks whether a ruler equally praised for learning can be blind to the claims of people whose interests threaten his political order.

## The crown has more than one audience

The epithet 'the Just' binds several distinct audiences. Court officials want predictable rewards and enforceable commands. Aristocratic households want property and rank respected. Farmers and traders want assessments they can survive. Religious communities want to know whether the sovereign will protect or punish them. Soldiers want payment and competent leadership. A decision that satisfies one audience may injure another. The poem's praise of Khosrow is therefore most revealing when placed beside the conflict over Mazdak and the stories of administrative reform.

Anushirvan can represent the aspiration to a state governed by rules instead of appetite. But whose rules, and who is entitled to challenge them? The question cannot be decided by the king's title. The tradition invites admiration for judgment; it also requires us to examine the treatment of the defeated. Both can be true within the literary construction of a ruler who serves as a model of order. Leaving the tension intact makes the historical and moral lesson more substantial than a string of entertaining court anecdotes.

## A kingdom between wisdom and coercion

There is no simple stage of progress in which the early violent kings are replaced by gentle scholars. The Sasanian king is a commander, a collector of revenue, a religious patron, and the ultimate authority behind punishments. Wisdom may improve the exercise of those powers, but it does not remove their coercive character. A tax system that measures fields accurately still takes resources from fields. A court that values books still has prisons. A ruler who praises truth still has advisers who may withhold inconvenient news. Ferdowsi's ideal of kingship is attractive in part because it exists against these continuing dangers.

The fable of the life-giving plant provides a fitting conclusion. The physician cannot bring the dead back to the world of the living. He can bring a book that may prevent some preventable harms if people read and act on it. The promise is modest compared with the conquest of countries or the discovery of immortality, but it fits the recurring lesson of the epic. Human beings rarely control fate completely. They can improve the judgment with which they face the lives entrusted to them. Anushirvan is remembered as great partly because his court sought the arts of judgment. The question left open is whether a ruler's desire for wisdom extends to listening when wisdom comes from someone he is tempted to punish.
