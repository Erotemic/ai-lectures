# Berenike and the Monsoon: Reconstructing an Indian Ocean Trade Route

In a room or courtyard at a long-abandoned port on Egypt's Red Sea coast, archaeologists find a storage jar. It is made in a ceramic tradition associated with India. Inside, preserved by the dry climate, lie about seven and a half kilograms of black peppercorns. The discovery is almost comically specific. We are accustomed to historians talking about 'the spice trade' as though it were a grand abstraction. Here is an actual container full of an actual spice, deposited in a particular building and recovered by a field team who could document its surroundings.

What does this jar prove? At minimum, pepper was present at the site, and it was stored in a vessel identified as Indian. That is already valuable. But the questions come quickly. Was this pepper grown in India? Almost certainly, given the plant and historical context, yet we need botanical identification and comparative ecology to make that argument. Did the jar and contents travel together? Its condition makes that plausible, but it is an inference. Did a ship sail directly from a particular Indian port to this exact Egyptian harbor? The jar alone cannot tell us. Does it tell us who consumed the pepper, whether it was sold or offered in a shrine, or what it cost? Again, not by itself.

This lecture is about the steps between an object in the ground and a picture of ancient globalization. We will visit Berenike and its Red Sea neighbor Myos Hormos, consider what their excavators found, follow ceramics across the ocean to the Indian coast, and compare the artifacts with a remarkable Greek-language navigation and trade guide. Along the way we will credit the researchers who made each connection and distinguish physical evidence, written testimony, and modern reconstruction.

## An ocean with a seasonal rhythm

The Indian Ocean does not present the same sailing conditions throughout the year. Monsoon winds reverse seasonally over large parts of the ocean, making voyages between the Arabian Peninsula, East Africa, and India easier in some months than others. Sailors used this pattern long before the Roman Empire controlled Egypt. The period we are studying, especially the first and second centuries of the Common Era, offers an especially rich combination of archaeological and written evidence from Roman-period Egypt and the wider Indian Ocean.

Some later accounts credit a navigator called Hippalus with discovering the monsoon route. That is a story viewed from the perspective of Greco-Roman historical writing. The winds were not waiting for one Greek-speaking sailor to notice them; communities bordering the Arabian Sea already maintained maritime knowledge. We should distinguish the appearance of a technique in surviving Roman texts from its invention. Every sea lane also involved local pilots, shipbuilders, interpreters, and merchants whose skills are incompletely recorded.

Imagine a merchant planning a trip from Egypt. Goods move down the Nile to a settlement near the Eastern Desert. They cross the desert in caravans to a Red Sea harbor. A ship carries them south through the Red Sea, around Arabia, and toward the western coast of India when winds favor the passage. Another season may be needed for the return. That schematic journey helps explain why ports and desert roads became economically connected, but it is not a route copied from a single captain's log. Different merchants traveled different legs, and Indian, Arabian, African, and Mediterranean shippers operated in overlapping networks.

Berenike stood near the southern end of Egypt's Red Sea coast. It was founded in the Ptolemaic period and later became an important port under Roman rule. Its geographic position made access to Indian Ocean traffic possible but also difficult. The settlement had limited local water and food resources, and the desert roads linking it to the Nile required organized transport. Archaeologists can investigate its role in trade precisely because the town left traces of daily life, not only a famous inscription naming the harbor.

## What the excavators actually found

Steven Sidebotham directed long-running archaeological work at Berenike with collaborating teams, institutions, and specialists. His book *Berenike and the Ancient Maritime Spice Route* assembles years of field research and the contributions of investigators studying buildings, ceramics, plants, animals, texts, and imported materials. Sidebotham deserves credit for directing and interpreting the project; he did not personally identify every seed, classify every sherd, or decipher every ostracon. The distributed nature of the labor is central to understanding how the conclusions were reached.

The dry environment preserves organic material that would disappear at many wetter sites. Archaeobotanists can identify fruits, grains, spices, and other plant remains. Specialists look at seeds and their diagnostic structures, compare them with reference collections, and consider whether the sample was burned, desiccated, or disturbed. An archaeobotanist does not need a literary text to recognize a peppercorn, but a historical argument about trade benefits from knowing the precise layer and building in which it appeared.

René Cappers's work on plant remains from Berenike is especially important. The pepper cache was reported from an Indian storage jar within the area of a building interpreted in relation to the Serapis temple complex. The published field report measured approximately 7.55 kilograms of black peppercorns and described repairs to the jar. This is an excellent case because we can follow the argument in stages. First comes a documented ceramic container from a particular excavated context. Second comes botanical identification of its contents. Third comes the comparison between vessel fabric and Indian pottery. Only then do broader economic and ritual interpretations begin.

Pepper is not only a line in an ancient author's list of exotic imports. It is an archaeological quantity. Yet quantity can also mislead. Seven and a half kilograms may have been valuable, but without a complete price record from this find we cannot calculate its monetary worth. It might be commercial stock, supplies, or material put aside for a specific use. A cache in a temple-related setting encourages a ritual hypothesis but does not establish that every peppercorn was intended for an offering. Archaeologists investigate nearby deposits, storage arrangements, and comparable finds before settling on a functional interpretation.

Marijke van der Veen and colleagues have also studied food plants and consumption at Red Sea sites. Some pepper was found in different conditions and contexts, including evidence associated with burning. That gives us another question: does a burned peppercorn come from cooking, a ritual fire, discarded fuel, or accidental fire? The laboratory identification says what the seed is. Context and patterns across many samples say what people might have been doing with it. We can be confident about an import while being much less certain about its final use.

At Berenike archaeologists have also found textiles, gemstones, ceramic vessels, and other materials associated with wider networks. A cotton textile's fiber and spinning technique can suggest a production tradition. An Indian-style cooking pot may have been made by a migrant potter, shipped with goods, or copied locally. A stone from a particular geological region might have been traded through intermediaries. Each object adds a partial line of evidence. Taken together, these make long-distance exchange difficult to dismiss; taken individually, they rarely provide a full biography of their own movement.

## The other Egyptian port

Berenike was not the only harbor joining the Nile valley to Red Sea shipping. Myos Hormos, identified with the site now called Quseir al-Qadim, has supplied another large archaeological record. University of Chicago teams worked there in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Later, the University of Southampton excavated the site from 1999 to 2003, with work led and published by David Peacock, Lucy Blue, and a wider group of specialists. Their reports credit contributors on ceramics, organic remains, inscriptions, construction, textiles, and many other categories.

This case demonstrates how a place-name can become an archaeological problem. Ancient writers used the name Myos Hormos, but modern investigators had to determine which coastal ruins corresponded to it. Evidence from inscribed potsherds and documents along the desert roads helped establish the identification. Hélène Cuvigny, Adam Bülow-Jacobsen, and colleagues working on desert ostraca contributed to these connections. Their evidence was a different kind of archaeological record: short administrative notes, personal letters, and place-names that sometimes survive on broken pottery.

Think about the difference between the two lines of evidence. A sherd with a place-name can tell us a traveler or official wrote about Myos Hormos. It cannot by itself describe the shape of the harbor. A wharf built from stone, debris, and reused material can establish where ships and cargo were handled, but it does not tell us what ancient people called the port. If the inscription, road, and harbor structures point to the same location, the identification becomes stronger than any one clue.

Roberta Tomber's ceramic research on Indo-Roman trade provides another bridge. She compared pottery from Egyptian Red Sea ports with Indian ceramic traditions and examined production techniques and vessel forms. A coarse cooking pot can travel across the ocean; its everyday appearance is part of the significance. Trade archaeology once privileged luxury imports because they were easy to recognize in collections. A soot-blackened vessel suggests the presence of cooking practices, crew provisions, or resident communities, and therefore invites questions about actual people living around these ports.

A pot's shape can resemble another pot without having been imported. Archaeologists therefore consider fabric, temper, surface treatment, and distribution, sometimes with microscopic or chemical studies. A convincing identification should survive comparison with plausible local alternatives. If the same imported vessel types occur in secure levels at multiple ports, the case for a continuing exchange system grows. The pattern matters more than any isolated picturesque discovery.

## Reading a merchant's guide

The material evidence gains another dimension from a work called the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*. Its author is anonymous, writing in Greek, probably around the middle of the first century CE. The text lists ports, distances, sailing conditions, political relationships, and goods exchanged around the Red Sea, East African coast, Arabian Peninsula, and India. The ocean named by 'Erythraean' was broader than the modern Red Sea alone.

A striking feature of the work is its practical language. It reads less like a poetic celebration of adventure and more like a merchant's account of where to go, what goods are available, when winds are favorable, and who controls a market. For history, it is precious precisely because so few such practical documents survive from this network. Yet it is one observer's account, preserved through much later manuscripts. The text is not an exhaustive census of all Indian Ocean commerce.

The work has a scholarly history of its own. Wilfred Schoff published an influential English translation in 1912. Lionel Casson's 1989 edition examined its language, geography, and dating in detail. Comparing editions reminds us that translation is a scholarly activity. A port name may be uncertain; a commodity term may refer to different products; a geographical distance may use an unfamiliar measure. A line from a translated source is not a GPS coordinate. Editors reconstruct what the ancient author wrote, and historians still have to compare it with actual ports and artifacts.

The *Periplus* discusses the major Indian port Barygaza, commonly identified with Bharuch in western India, and ports on the Malabar coast including Muziris. It describes trade between regions bordering the Arabian Sea and emphasizes seasonal voyages. It also notices trade along the coast of East Africa, which should keep us from depicting the Indian Ocean as a simple two-point route between Egypt and India. Arabian, African, and South Asian merchants had their own interests and political settings.

Here is a useful exercise in evidence. Suppose a text lists pepper as a commodity traded from southwestern India. Then an archaeologist finds black pepper in Roman-period Berenike. The two observations support an inference that this commodity moved in the network the text describes. But they still do not prove that the author witnessed this particular shipment, that the pepper came from one named port, or that all cargoes were organized alike. We are matching broad patterns, not identifying a warehouse receipt.

## Following pottery to India

The coast of India provides further evidence. At Arikamedu, near Puducherry, excavators recovered Mediterranean amphora fragments and other materials that prompted comparisons with Roman-era trade. Mortimer Wheeler directed investigations there in 1945 with an Archaeological Survey of India team including A. Ghosh and Krishna Deva. Jean-Marie Casal followed with French excavations. Vimala Begley and collaborators later reexamined the chronology and interpretation of the site, demonstrating that its history extended beyond a simplified Roman trading-station model.

This sequence is a useful warning about the history of archaeological interpretation. It is easy to discover imported Mediterranean pottery and write a story in which Romans introduced trade to a passive Indian coast. That overlooks existing Indian coastal economies, craft traditions, and connections across the Bay of Bengal. Later work gave Arikamedu a longer local history and asked how imported goods fit into ongoing production. What had once looked like a colonial outpost becomes a more complicated settlement with overlapping technologies and contacts.

The ceramics can tell us that some vessels had Mediterranean production affinities. They do not establish that Roman settlers owned the entire site, or that every amphora arrived with its original contents intact. The same amphora might be reused. A local workshop might imitate foreign forms. Pottery shapes can become fashionable independently of direct settlement. To investigate, specialists compare fabric, stratigraphy, kiln evidence, and distribution beyond one site.

On India's southwestern coast, the location of ancient Muziris has attracted extensive attention. Excavations at Pattanam in Kerala have revealed evidence for an important settlement with maritime connections, and researchers have proposed associations with Muziris. The exact identification and local geography remain subjects of debate. A famous name in a text should not be made to fit a site merely because the site yielded imported material. Archaeologists ask how descriptions of rivers, harbors, contemporary settlements, and material culture line up—and how the shoreline itself has changed over time.

There is another remarkable document: the so-called Muziris Papyrus, an Egyptian Greek text involving a cargo from India, loan and customs arrangements, and quantities of valuable goods. It adds economic detail beyond the *Periplus*. But the papyrus describes a particular transaction rather than all trade. It can be combined with the pepper jar and amphora distributions to show a system involving finance, taxation, navigation, and transport. The three kinds of evidence are different and mutually informative.

A ship's cargo did not move straight from spice garden to banquet table. Goods could be assembled near production centers, transported on rivers, passed through regional markets, sailed across the ocean, carried by camel through Egypt's Eastern Desert, placed on Nile boats, and eventually reach Mediterranean consumers. People at each stage could profit, impose taxes, lose cargo, bargain, or repackage goods. Trade routes are human institutions as well as lines across water.

## Reconstructing a port as a working place

It is helpful to leave the map for a moment and imagine a harbor as a working environment. Sailors need water and food. Ship carpenters repair hulls and rigging. Porters unload amphorae. Merchants compare quantities and prices. Officials ask who owns a consignment and whether taxes are due. Residents cook food, throw away broken pottery, repair clothes, and bury their dead. A long-distance trading port would produce evidence of all these activities, but the resulting objects would be mixed with local life.

This is why the distribution of finds across a site matters. Pepper recovered near a storehouse may have been cargo awaiting transfer. Pepper in a kitchen deposit might indicate consumption. Pepper in a shrine or temple precinct can support an interpretation involving ritual activity. Yet buildings change use. A room called a warehouse by one excavator might later prove to have been domestic, or a storage jar might be reused long after its first voyage. Specialists reconstruct the order of floors, pits, fills, and repairs so that an object is placed in a sequence of activities rather than merely in a place-name on a plan.

Suppose we find an imported jar carefully placed into a pit, with its rim near the floor surface. That differs from finding one broken sherd in a garbage layer. A deliberately installed container may have been used for storage over time. If its surface shows repairs, people valued it enough to maintain it. The jar at Berenike carried physical traces of repair. This detail invites a story about the reuse of a valuable container, though it does not tell us who repaired it. Archaeology makes human behavior visible in these modest acts of maintenance.

Now suppose we compare several archaeological seasons. The first season yields three peppercorns. The second finds many more in carefully screened samples. A later season uncovers a storage jar full of them. It would be a mistake to assume that pepper imports increased in that order: the sequence describes modern discovery, not ancient consumption. Excavation area, sampling strategy, sieve mesh, soil conditions, and laboratory attention all affect how many specimens are recovered. The archaeobotanical record becomes interpretable when researchers publish where samples came from and how they were processed.

## A document about money and cargo

Written evidence can sometimes reveal the financial machinery behind the goods. The Muziris Papyrus, a Greek document from Roman Egypt, contains an arrangement associated with trade from India and calculations connected to transport and taxation. Scholars disagree about details of the document's reconstruction and economic implications. Its existence nonetheless lets historians ask questions that a pepper jar cannot answer: how credit might be advanced, how goods were valued for customs, and how commercial obligations followed cargo from a distant coast into the Egyptian administration.

A papyrus might record a contractual quantity or obligation without preserving the goods themselves. Archaeologists, by contrast, may find goods without a contract. When the two are combined, the historian should resist converting one particular loan into a universal banking system or one large pepper cache into an average shipment. A transaction can be exceptional. Archaeological deposits can be biased toward accidents and disposal. An evidentiary argument improves when we ask whether the text and finds are samples of the same scale of activity.

Our surviving paperwork also has an institutional bias. Contracts and customs calculations record what was important to people able or required to write them. Sailors' skills, pack animals' care, and workers' injuries may be absent from the texts. Archaeological structures, worn tools, and food remains can partially correct the imbalance. They cannot restore every missing voice, but they make it harder to mistake a merchant's ledger for the whole history of a port.

The combined evidence offers a plausible division of labor rather than a list of known individuals. Goods were grown and processed in South Asia, accumulated by merchants, moved by crews through seasonal waters, handled at Red Sea harbors, taken across Egyptian desert roads, and financed or taxed by people who might never have sailed. Every stage required practical knowledge. The Greek text survives because someone copied it. The pepper survives because dry deposits protected it. Their survival is a historical contingency, and the argument about trade depends on recognizing the limits of both archives.

## People behind the labels

The phrase 'Indo-Roman trade' is convenient, but it can conceal too much. Rome ruled Egypt. It did not govern the Arabian Sea, all Indian ports, or the communities of East Africa. Egyptian, Nubian, Arabian, Indian, and other sailors and merchants had roles that written sources preserve unevenly. Archaeology broadens the record through objects made and used by people whose names do not appear in the surviving Greek texts.

Excavation has its own collaborative structure. Botanists such as Cappers and van der Veen identify plant material; ceramic specialists such as Tomber compare vessel traditions; historians such as Casson interpret literary geography; Sidebotham and the Berenike field teams record the site; Peacock and Blue's teams investigate Quseir al-Qadim; Wheeler, Casal, Begley, and Indian archaeological collaborators establish sequences across the sea. Many local excavators and conservators remain less visible in publications than principal investigators. A responsible account names documented contributors while recognizing that archival credit is incomplete.

The distinction between a find and its interpretation also protects us from false precision. It is reasonable to say Berenike's pepper demonstrates long-distance movement of a spice associated with South Asia. It would be premature to name the farmer, the ship, the exact departure harbor, and the purchaser. The archaeological record has gaps, but it is rich enough to reject the idea that substantial contacts across the Indian Ocean began only in the modern era.

## A complete evidence chain

Let us return to the pepper jar and deliberately reconstruct the reasoning. Its excavators document the deposit in a specific building. Ceramic study identifies manufacturing characteristics consistent with an Indian tradition. Botanical analysis identifies the seeds as black pepper. A published field report records the quantity and condition. Other sites yield Indian-style pottery, cotton textiles, or related materials. The *Periplus*, a separate written source, describes shipping networks and pepper trade in the relevant era. Excavations on the Indian coast reveal goods and technological connections moving in the other direction. This is a converging argument involving context, laboratory identification, comparative typology, texts, and regional archaeology.

Now remove one piece. Suppose we had only a translated Greek text that listed pepper. We would know someone described the trade but might not be able to document how goods were stored at Berenike. Suppose we had only a jar, without stratigraphy or botanical identification. We might call it an exotic container without understanding the cargo. Suppose we had just a few amphora sherds from Arikamedu. We might overstate the scale and nature of Roman participation. The conclusions improve because distinct kinds of evidence limit each other's ambiguities.

We should also ask what evidence is missing: whole ships and crews on most of these ancient routes, records of wages, voices of women and enslaved workers, prices at many intermediate markets, and the details of local ecological effects. The archaeology of food imports can teach us about consumption, but not every person who encountered a foodstuff. A textual guide can give sailing advice, but not describe every storm or every community along a route.

There is a final corrective to the familiar idea of the 'Silk Road.' The term often conjures a caravan crossing deserts, with exotic cloth passing between China and Rome. Ancient interregional exchange was also maritime, and it involved bulky ordinary goods as well as luxuries. Its history was made by sailors adjusting to winds, artisans producing containers, traders managing credit, workers hauling cargo, and people living in port towns. The evidence from Berenike and its connected sites is a record of that system at several scales.

A jar of pepper does not explain the whole Indian Ocean. But it can anchor an explanation that is tested from both shores. Once we identify the people who recovered and studied it, the layers where it rested, the plant inside, the pot that held it, the texts describing similar voyages, and the wider distribution of imported goods, the claim becomes far stronger than a romantic story of mysterious ships. We know an ancient ocean was connected because independent material records meet one another across it. The map is an argument assembled from the evidence, and each connection has a history of researchers who made it visible.

## Research and attribution notes (not spoken)

### Evidence distinctions

- The 7.55 kg pepper jar is a specific excavation find; separate identification of *Piper nigrum*, manufacturing attribution of the container, and interpretation of its setting.
- Classical texts such as the *Periplus* are source testimonies, not exhaustive lists of all real voyages.
- Seasonal monsoon navigation long predates surviving Roman-era descriptions; do not credit its invention to the named Hippalus.
- Arikamedu has an extended local history and should not be reduced to a Roman colony.
- Pattanam–Muziris identification remains a scholarly question; do not present it as settled.
- Contributions of local fieldworkers are less documented than named principal investigators; avoid invented names.

### Editorial cautions

- Do not interpret a jar's provenance as proof that its contents traveled on a single direct voyage.
