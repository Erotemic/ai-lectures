# The Popol Wuj: How a Maya Book Survived

Open a book in a library in Chicago, and two columns of writing face each other. On one side is K'iche', a Maya language spoken in the highlands of Guatemala. On the other is Spanish. The book contains a story in which makers attempt to create people, twins descend into an underworld, and ancestors establish the communities whose descendants still live in Guatemala. It is usually called the Popol Vuh, or, with a spelling closer to contemporary K'iche' usage, the Popol Wuj.

The book in Chicago is not a surviving pre-conquest Maya codex. It is a manuscript copied by a Dominican friar around the beginning of the eighteenth century, long after Spanish armies entered the Guatemalan highlands. The original writers of the K'iche' account are not securely identified. The earliest manuscript they produced is lost. Yet the stories are plainly not the invention of that friar. Their language, forms of expression, historical allusions, and relationships with other Maya traditions give us ways to investigate how they reached us.

How can we learn from a story when the earliest surviving copy was made by someone whose religious mission was to change the beliefs of the people who told it? How much of the work belongs to people whose names have been lost? What does a translator add, and what does a translator accidentally remove? The answers depend on a combination of manuscript study, language, archaeology, historical documentation, and the expertise of living K'iche' speakers.

This lecture is about the journey of a text, and about the researchers who made that journey legible. Its central evidence is the manuscript itself, cataloged at the Newberry Library as part of Ayer manuscript fifteen fifteen. But the story begins in a world in which books, memory, and performance had already been connected for centuries.

## A living language behind a famous book

K'iche' is one member of the Maya language family, not another name for all Maya languages. Its speakers live in Guatemala today. The highland communities from which the Popol Wuj emerged have their own political and cultural histories, and the stories carry references to particular lineages and places. They do not offer a single universal mythology on behalf of every Maya people.

This is an important corrective to a familiar way of introducing the book. We might hear that the Popol Wuj is the Maya creation story, imagine an extinct civilization, and place the book on a shelf beside Greek mythology. The text does contain creation narratives, but it also tells of the Hero Twins, a succession of beings who fail or succeed in becoming human, and the histories and genealogies of K'iche' communities. Its narrators are interested in who their people are, where they came from, and what gives their political and ritual order meaning.

The surviving K'iche' writing is arranged in a form rich in parallel expressions. An idea is often spoken twice with related, nonidentical phrases. Names and titles appear in pairs. That pattern is meaningful: a translator who compresses two lines into one may preserve the basic event and still erase something essential about how the original tells it. Linguists and translators have spent decades studying these structures. Monro Edmonson, Dennis Tedlock, Allen Christenson, and the K'iche' scholar and poet Luis Enrique Sam Colop have offered distinct ways of representing the poetic organization.

When the work describes ancestors or sacred action, it is speaking in forms recognizable within a particular language community. A close reading needs competence in that language as well as information about ancient monuments. It also needs care in moving between periods. The K'iche' account written down after the conquest was connected to older Maya cultural traditions; it was produced in a transformed colonial world. We cannot simply assign every sentence to a thousand years earlier, or assume that colonial pressure invented the narrative from nothing.

## What existed before the surviving manuscript?

Long before European arrival, different Maya societies used writing on stone monuments, ceramics, and folded books. Only a small number of pre-conquest Maya screenfold manuscripts survive, and none is the original manuscript of the Popol Wuj. Colonial destruction of many Indigenous books is part of this history. The surviving record is therefore deeply selective. What we can read now reflects not only what was written, but also what escaped destruction and what people later chose to preserve.

The existence of Maya hieroglyphic writing does not mean that the K'iche' authors wrote the Popol Wuj in the same script and then mechanically converted it. The earliest reconstructed written version of the work is generally placed in the sixteenth century and is thought to have used the Latin alphabet to represent K'iche'. That alphabet had become available to Indigenous writers in colonial Guatemala. A pre-conquest prototype in exactly the later literary form has not been found.

The Newberry Library's research guide allows for earlier codices or mnemonic screenfolds in the history of the tradition. Such objects could support recitation and memory. But a possible antecedent is not an identified surviving manuscript. We should resist the temptation to draw an unbroken line from one hypothetical book to the eighteenth-century copy, as though every page were documented along the way.

The lost sixteenth-century text is also not the oldest layer of the stories. Myths and ancestral traditions had histories before anyone set down the version we know. Oral narration can preserve material through generations, while changing wording, emphasis, and arrangement. That is neither a defect nor evidence that every later variation is equally ancient. It tells us that the object we study is a version of a tradition in motion.

The anonymous K'iche' authors deserve the first credit. They organized and preserved the account under the pressure of colonization. The people who sustained its stories in speech also contributed, though surviving documents seldom record their individual names. The loss of those names is a fact about the archive, and it should influence how confidently we distribute credit.

## A friar's two columns

Francisco Ximénez was a Dominican priest working in Guatemala around 1700. He studied local languages and produced linguistic and religious material for the colonial church. At Chichicastenango, he copied a K'iche' version of the Popol Wuj and supplied a Spanish translation alongside it. The surviving manuscript is generally dated to the opening years of the eighteenth century, with institutional guides allowing a somewhat wider range.

This was an immense act of textual preservation. Without the K'iche' column, later scholars would have been dependent on Ximénez's Spanish interpretation. Yet it is misleading to describe him simply as the person who saved an ancient book. His work took place within the religious structures of colonial rule. His purpose included understanding and converting Indigenous communities. We can recognize the consequence of the copy while asking what access he had, what he changed, and whether the people whose knowledge he recorded consented to its later circulation. The historical record does not answer every such question.

The manuscript is more complicated than two isolated columns of a heroic story. Catalog records place the Popol Wuj within an assemblage of language-learning and missionary texts, including Kaqchikel, K'iche', and Tz'utujil grammar and devotional materials. That physical association helps establish the copy's circumstances. It also cautions against imagining a modern bookstore edition, detached from its colonial setting.

The Newberry's description notes corrections within the K'iche' column. There are changes and repetitions that may tell us something about copying or oral performance. The library suggests that some features might reflect recitation, possibly by more than one voice. That is a hypothesis prompted by the manuscript, not a tape recording of an eighteenth-century performance. To test it, scholars can examine handwriting, spacing, corrections, syntactic rhythm, and comparable K'iche' texts.

Picture a copyist encountering a repeated phrase. Did the repetition come from a storyteller? Was it a deliberate parallel structure? Was a word copied twice by mistake? These questions require different evidence. If the supposed repetition is also a familiar poetic device in K'iche', that supports one reading. If an adjacent line has been crossed out and overwritten, that supports another. Manuscript analysis begins at a scale smaller than the grand story: the material details of a hand trying to fix language on a page.

## Follow the book through the collectors

For a time, Ximénez's manuscript remained in Guatemala. In the nineteenth century it entered a very different world, that of European antiquarians and collectors. The French cleric Charles-Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg acquired it and published a K'iche' text with French translation in 1861. That publication helped introduce the work to a wider scholarly audience. Brasseur's interpretation and editorial choices also influenced later readings.

The exact circumstances by which he acquired the manuscript have been debated. Brasseur claimed to have purchased it in the 1850s from an Indigenous leader named Ignacio Coloche in Rabinal. The Newberry Library reports that account but marks it as disputed. This is an example of a provenance problem. A surviving book can be physically real and intellectually invaluable while its route into a collection remains imperfectly documented. Ownership claims by collectors are themselves historical sources to assess, rather than certificates of truth.

After Brasseur's death, the manuscript passed among collectors and dealers, including Alphonse Pinart and Charles Leclerc, before Edward E. Ayer purchased it. Ayer's collection became part of the Newberry Library in Chicago, where the manuscript has been held since the early twentieth century. The library provides a digital version, so a scholar no longer needs to depend only on a nineteenth-century printed edition to see how Ximénez arranged the text.

These changes in custody affected scholarship. Brasseur's printed divisions and conventions could make an ancient narrative easier to navigate, but they could also create the impression that our familiar chapter structure existed in the lost K'iche' source. Different editors have organized the work in different ways. A reader who compares translations may discover variation caused by the manuscripts, variation caused by editorial decisions, and variation caused by the translator's understanding of K'iche'. Those are separate questions.

A physical manuscript also ages. Ink, binding, and paper demand conservation. The Newberry has documented microscopic examination of ink, stabilization, repair of tears, and imaging designed to reduce future handling. Conservation makes the record available while also changing the physical conditions under which scholars encounter it. Digital images can magnify marks and bring them to readers around the world. They cannot supply the smell of the paper or automatically resolve the provenance of the copy. They are an added witness to the surviving object, not a time machine to the sixteenth century.

## Translation is another layer of evidence

Suppose you know the familiar episode in which the makers finally form people from maize. In one English version, the account may be phrased as flowing prose. In another, paired phrases may be laid out as lines of poetry. Both can be based on the same K'iche' words. The difference may alter your sense of how the story moves: as a sequence of events, as an invocation, or as an argument built through repetition.

The history of translation lets us study those choices. Adrián Recinos published a widely circulated Spanish translation in 1947. Delia Goetz and Sylvanus Morley helped make that line of interpretation familiar to English-language readers. Monro Edmonson's 1971 edition drew attention to the text's parallel poetic structures. Dennis Tedlock's translations brought linguistic and performance-oriented questions to a wider audience. Allen Christenson later provided a detailed scholarly translation and a more literal, line-by-line companion, grounded in the Newberry manuscript.

K'iche' scholars have their own essential place in this history. Adrián Inés Chávez produced a multi-column edition in the late 1970s, comparing the copied text, a revised orthography, and Spanish renderings. Luis Enrique Sam Colop, a K'iche' linguist and poet, published influential work that attends to the language's poetic form and to the name Popol Wuj. These are substantive interpretive contributions, not merely local endorsements of interpretations produced elsewhere.

Consider a translator choosing between two possible meanings for an unfamiliar word. One strategy is to consult a colonial dictionary. Another is to ask whether the word survives in contemporary speech. A third is to compare its use in parallel expressions and related Maya languages. Each is valuable and each has limits. Colonial dictionaries may reflect missionary categories. Contemporary usage may have changed. A parallel expression may be metaphorical. Strong interpretation comes from comparing these lines of evidence, with fluent speakers and historical linguists participating in the conversation.

There is also a reason translations can disagree without anyone being careless. A poet may deliberately choose a word that evokes more than one association. In K'iche' parallelism, two nouns may overlap without being exact synonyms. An English translation forced to choose one simple word can narrow the meaning. A translator who preserves every repetition may create prose that sounds stiff to readers unfamiliar with the original form. Those tradeoffs should be explained, not hidden under claims that one English edition has eliminated all ambiguity.

The title itself illustrates the issue. Popol Vuh remains widely recognized. Popol Wuj better reflects modern K'iche' spelling conventions used by many scholars and speakers. The variation is evidence of a history of transcribing sounds through different writing systems, and of who has been given authority to name the work for a global audience.

## Can archaeological evidence test the story?

The manuscript alone cannot answer every question about the age of its motifs. Here archaeology offers a second kind of evidence. Maya imagery on pottery and monuments depicts figures, places, and episodes that scholars have compared with later stories of the Hero Twins. The hieroglyphic script of Classic-period lowland Maya cities preserves names, royal events, dates, and religious language. Researchers such as Michael Coe drew attention to connections between Classic Maya imagery and stories preserved in later highland texts. Subsequent decipherment and iconographic work have expanded the comparisons.

But we need to state precisely what an image can establish. If a vessel shows twins or an underworld scene resembling a later story, that supports a history of related imagery and narrative ideas. It does not prove that a specific sentence in the Newberry manuscript was spoken identically a thousand years earlier. Similar figures may play different roles in different communities. The geographical and chronological distances between Classic lowland inscriptions and colonial highland K'iche' prose have to remain visible.

Maya hieroglyph decipherment is a remarkable scientific and scholarly achievement in its own right. Yuri Knorosov's phonetic approach, Tatiana Proskouriakoff's historical interpretations of inscriptions, and generations of Maya epigraphers helped recover a writing tradition that colonial and later scholars had often misunderstood. Yet translating Classic-period inscriptions is not the same task as reading the alphabetic K'iche' Popol Wuj. Different scripts, periods, languages, and genres are involved. They can illuminate each other without collapsing into one continuous archive.

There is a further distinction between archaeology and analogy. A ceramic scene might resemble a literary episode because both participate in widespread Maya religious patterns. We can investigate that resemblance through symbols, dates, findspots, and related texts. We cannot use a resemblance alone to reconstruct a lost book, or assume every painted scene illustrates a page that has survived. A scholarly interpretation is strongest when it specifies exactly which features are shared and which remain different.

That precision can make the story more interesting. We may discover that some themes are older than the surviving text, while other passages reflect sixteenth-century political conditions. The book can preserve pre-conquest religious thinking and also be a sophisticated response to a new colonial world. Its authors were living in history, not copying a timeless encyclopedia.

## Who wrote it, and for whom?

The named author question is tempting. If we identify the person who wrote the lost sixteenth-century manuscript, perhaps the entire problem becomes simple. But the available evidence does not support a confident single name. Scholar Ruud van Akkeren examined proposals about possible authors and the political interests represented in the work. One candidate previously discussed is Diego Reinoso, a K'iche' scribe associated with another document, the Título de Totonicapán. Van Akkeren argued that the political alignment of that text is not straightforwardly compatible with assigning him the Popol Wuj.

This is how a historical argument can work even when it fails to deliver a satisfying name. Rather than selecting the only famous scribe available, researchers compare documents, lineages, and political claims. They examine whether the supposed author would have had reason to praise the groups celebrated by the text. A proposed attribution becomes less persuasive when the documents' loyalties conflict. The result may be an anonymous author, or multiple contributors, whose work we can study without inventing a biography.

The text's interest in K'iche' ancestors and ruling lineages also cautions against reading it as a neutral census of every Maya tradition. It preserves a particular way of making political and sacred history. Ancient and early modern literary works often do this. A genealogy can connect people to places and authority; a sacred origin narrative can explain why a community regards certain obligations as binding. The narratives have literary power and historical purposes at the same time.

We should also ask who had an opportunity to preserve the stories and who did not. Colonial literacy, church education, language policy, and violence affected which voices reached an archive. A text that survives because someone with access to paper and Latin letters wrote it down is evidence of that opportunity, not evidence that only literate men possessed knowledge. There may have been specialists in storytelling, performance, and ceremony whose names are irrecoverable from the surviving manuscript. Naming the gap is more accurate than inventing a roster.

## Reading the physical page today

Let us return to the Newberry manuscript. A responsible edition starts with the object, not with a polished quotation in translation. A researcher notes how many folios survive, what other materials are bound with them, where corrections occur, and whether the manuscript is copied from another source. A transcription must decide how to represent older spellings without quietly modernizing every peculiarity. A translation must communicate both meaning and form. A historical interpretation must then distinguish what the manuscript actually says from the social setting inferred around it.

Take a small example. If the manuscript writes two parallel names together, a printed edition might place a comma between them. Another might join them with the word 'and.' Another might interpret them as one paired title. The punctuation and conjunction may be the editor's invention. That does not make punctuation illegitimate; modern readers need it. But the historian should know where it came from. The difference can influence whether a character is imagined as one being, two beings, or a formal pairing of qualities.

Now consider dates. The manuscript's paper and handwriting place the surviving copy in a colonial period. Textual references and the history of Latin-letter literacy support the inference of an earlier K'iche' composition. Parallels in older Maya art support the age of some themes. These are not three independent measurements of the manuscript's original creation date. They measure or constrain different things. A listener should not walk away thinking that a Classic-period painting has dated every word of a sixteenth-century text.

Finally, there is a question of access. The library's digitization lets researchers examine the copy without making a transatlantic trip, while conservators reduce damage to a fragile manuscript. That access is valuable. It coexists with an ethical history in which Indigenous intellectual property and sacred literature passed through missionaries, collectors, and institutions abroad. Scholars and librarians can document provenance, provide transparent editions, and collaborate with K'iche' communities. They cannot undo that history simply by scanning the pages.

## Other manuscripts change the question

There is another way to resist the idea that one book speaks for an entire civilization. We can place the Popol Wuj beside other Indigenous documents from the colonial Guatemalan highlands. Among them are the *Título de Totonicapán*, a K'iche' text concerned with ancestry and political authority, and the *Annals of the Kaqchikels*, often known through later titles and translations. They are not alternative copies of the Popol Wuj. They come from different textual circumstances and present their own perspectives. Precisely because they are different, they can help historians evaluate shared names, political relationships, and remembered events.

Imagine two neighboring communities recording a past war. One says its ancestors defended their lands against an aggressor. The other describes the same conflict as a rightful campaign. Agreement on the existence of a conflict can be informative; disagreement about its moral meaning is informative too. A historian looks for the dates and lineages each text emphasizes and asks whether a shared event is being used to support different claims to legitimacy. That method avoids choosing one Indigenous document as the definitive voice of all the others.

The material histories of such texts are equally important. A text may survive only as a copy, a translation, or a publication made long after the alleged events. Comparing names does not make the copies contemporaneous. A name can also move through translation in different spellings. Identifying two spellings as referring to the same person may require phonetic analysis, genealogy, and historical context, rather than visual resemblance alone.

Ruud van Akkeren's study of Popol Wuj authorship demonstrates the value of this comparison. Instead of claiming that a surviving signature has been found at the end of the narrative, he compares political affiliation and textual content. A proposed author must fit the social commitments of the work attributed to him. We may still lack a secure identification when the comparison is finished. Yet we have learned more about the communities arguing through documents, and we have made the attribution problem more precise.

The same exercise reveals something about genre. The Popol Wuj can tell a sacred story, describe ancestral migrations, and legitimate a political lineage in the course of one composition. A modern librarian may classify it as mythology or literature. An early colonial writer could have understood it as a form of history with political consequences. Those labels organize research, but they do not explain the book by themselves. Its original audiences did not necessarily divide sacred knowledge, artistic narrative, and political memory into the categories used by modern university departments.

## How an edition can reshape the public memory

Once a translation becomes famous, it begins to circulate independently of its source. A school anthology may select the creation of the maize people, omit the lineages, and call the excerpt the Maya creation myth. A comparative mythology book may focus on the Hero Twins and the underworld. A historian may be most interested in the named K'iche' ancestors. All three readers encounter a real part of the work. None encounters the whole by reading that excerpt alone.

If one influential translation regularizes parallel phrases, later summaries may reproduce its simpler sentence. After enough retellings, readers can mistake the translator's stylistic choice for the structure of the K'iche' original. That is why a scholarly edition with facing text is more than a courtesy to specialists. It allows a reader with the relevant language knowledge to challenge a rendering, and it reveals which choices belong to editors. Modern photography of the manuscript adds yet another level of accountability: a doubtful transcription can sometimes be checked against the mark on the page.

Even a facsimile cannot resolve everything. A faded letter might be ambiguous. A line break may reflect the width of a column rather than a poetic pause. A spelling might indicate the sound system of the copyist's K'iche' or the habits of a Spanish-speaking transcriber. None of those problems is solved by consulting a modern dictionary alone. They require comparison, argument, and, often, the knowledge of speakers who understand the language as more than a sequence of dictionary entries.

The ethical question follows from the methodological one. Who gets to decide which edition represents the work in classrooms, museums, and recordings? A responsible answer should give K'iche' scholars and communities meaningful interpretive authority, while still allowing disagreement and critical discussion. Respect for a living literary tradition does not require pretending that it has only one approved reading. It requires refusing to erase its speakers from the story of how their text became known.

## What survives in the answer

How do we know the Popol Wuj? We know it through the words of K'iche' authors and storytellers whose full identities have not survived; through the manuscript copied and translated by Francisco Ximénez; through the collection and publication history of Brasseur de Bourbourg and others; through the material work of librarians and conservators; and through generations of translators, including K'iche' scholars who have recovered the texture of their own language in the text.

We also know it through comparison with archaeology, art, and other documents. Those comparisons strengthen some inferences and reveal the limits of others. The Newberry manuscript is an extraordinary witness, but it is not the original voice of every ancient Maya community, and it is not an untouched object from before the conquest.

If we read the story of the Hero Twins now, we can enjoy its wit, ingenuity, and unsettling view of the underworld. We can also hear another story behind it: people preserving a language and a cultural memory across a period of profound disruption, followed by centuries of copying, translation, disagreement, and recovery. The second story gives us a way to appreciate the first more carefully. And it leaves us with a principle that applies well beyond Maya literature: every text has a history as an object, and every translation adds a history of its own.

### Evidence chain

1. **Surviving witness:** Newberry Library, Ayer MS 1515, K'iche' transcription and parallel Spanish translation by Francisco Ximénez, circa 1700–1715. Cataloged manuscript is an early eighteenth-century witness, not the lost sixteenth-century source.
2. **Earlier composition:** Scholars infer a sixteenth-century K'iche' Latin-letter version, probably based partly on earlier oral and written traditions; its authors remain unidentified.
3. **Circulation:** Charles-Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg published a K'iche' text and French translation in 1861. His claim about acquisition from Ignacio Coloche is disputed. Alphonse Pinart, Charles Leclerc, Edward E. Ayer, and the Newberry occur in the documented collection history.
4. **Modern scholarship:** Monro Edmonson (1971), Adrián Inés Chávez (1979), Dennis Tedlock (1985/1996), Allen J. Christenson (2003/2007 and companion), Luis Enrique Sam Colop (K'iche' and Spanish editions), Ruud van Akkeren (2004), Christenson and Frauke Sachse (editorial history). Different editions make different interpretive choices.
5. **Comparative archaeological evidence:** Classic-period iconography and epigraphy may establish older shared motifs but do not date every passage of a much later written version.

### Editorial cautions

- Do not identify the lost K'iche' authors by name or portray Ximénez as the original author.
- Do not assert a specific surviving pre-conquest codex was the direct source of Ayer MS 1515.
- Distinguish Classic lowland Maya iconographic parallels from the colonial highland K'iche' manuscript.
- Use **Popol Wuj** in narration; treat **Popol Vuh** as the widely recognized older spelling, not an independent work.
