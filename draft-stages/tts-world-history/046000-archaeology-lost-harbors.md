# The Lost Harbor: Reading an Ancient Coastline

Stand on dry ground near Rome's modern airport and picture ships arriving from across the Mediterranean. The fields around you do not look like a bustling ancient harbor. Yet around two thousand years ago this landscape was part of the port system that supplied the imperial capital. The Roman port called Portus, built near the mouth of the Tiber, had immense basins, canals, warehouses, and quays. It was engineered for ships, cargoes, workers, and a city whose food requirements reached far beyond Italy.

How can an archaeologist reconstruct a port when its shoreline has moved, its basins have filled with sediment, and portions of its buildings have disappeared beneath new land? A plan of the visible ruins would leave much of the original system missing. A literary description might name a harbor without telling us how deep its water was, how it was maintained, or where merchants transferred cargo. To answer those questions we have to read the coastline itself as a historical document.

This lecture follows two major examples. The first is Portus and neighboring Ostia in Italy, where ancient buildings, geophysical surveys, and sediment cores have helped recover a landscape transformed by the Tiber delta. The second is Caesarea Maritima on the eastern Mediterranean coast, where underwater excavation and recent digital surveys reveal an artificial harbor built with Roman hydraulic concrete. In both places the problem is not simply locating stonework. It is establishing when the harbor operated, what ships could do there, and what processes eventually changed it. We will credit the researchers who recovered the evidence, including Simon Keay and his colleagues at Portus, geoarchaeological teams studying the Tiber sediments, and Avner Raban and his collaborators at Caesarea.

## A port is an engineered landscape

Imagine a harbor as a series of practical questions facing a sailor. Is there enough water over the seabed for the vessel's draft? Is the entrance sheltered from waves? Can the crew find a safe anchorage in changing weather? Is there a quay where cargo can be transferred? If the ship cannot enter the basin, is there a place for smaller boats to take its goods to shore? Can heavy cargo be moved onward by canal, river, road, or animals?

A stone wall in the water may address one of those problems, but walls are not the whole system. Coastal sediment can build a sandbar across an entrance. A river can deposit mud in a basin. Winter storms can damage a breakwater. A harbor may function well when it opens, require repeated dredging later, and eventually become too shallow for the ships it once served. Each phase leaves material evidence, although not always in the same place.

This makes harbors different from many inland monuments. The shoreline is not a fixed frame around an archaeological site. It is part of the changing site. Water depth, river sediment, tidal circulation, land subsidence, sea-level variation, and human construction all alter the geometry that made a harbor possible. A modern aerial photograph may therefore suggest a landscape quite different from the one its builders knew.

An archaeologist can begin with written descriptions, paintings, maps, and surviving structures, but those sources offer partial views. A Roman coin may show the emperor's harbor as an emblem of power. It might accurately portray some broad features while simplifying scale or leaving out inconvenient marshes and maintenance works. Roman writers might celebrate the construction as a political achievement while neglecting the labor required to keep it functional. A port must be reconstructed by connecting such representations with the earth and water that remain.

## Portus and the problem of a city that ate

In the first century of the common era, the emperor Claudius initiated a large harbor north of the mouth of the Tiber. The port was later enlarged by Trajan, with a well-known hexagonal basin, and connected to routes carrying goods toward Rome. Ostia, the earlier river port near the river mouth, formed part of the wider transport landscape. These installations were linked but not identical, and they changed over generations.

Why build a complicated artificial port at all? Rome depended on large quantities of grain and other materials carried across the Mediterranean. Deep-water ships needed facilities where they could unload. Barges could then move portions of their cargo along the Tiber. An imperial port was also a political statement, visible evidence that the state could coordinate engineering, supplies, and maritime power. Its monumental form and its everyday freight work were intertwined.

The Portus Project was established by Simon Keay, working with the British School at Rome, the University of Southampton, Italian archaeological authorities, and many collaborators. Keay, Martin Millett, Lidia Paroli, and Kris Strutt published an influential archaeological survey in 2005. Its methods included systematic ground survey, geophysics, and analysis of standing and buried remains. The work was designed to recover the plan of a large, complex site without first digging trenches across every part of it.

Geophysics gives us a good example of what archaeological images do and do not mean. A magnetometer records variations in magnetic properties. A buried wall, ditch, or furnace may produce a different signature from surrounding soil. Ground-penetrating radar can register reflections from boundaries underground. Electrical resistance methods detect differences related to moisture and the ability of ground materials to conduct current. An apparent line in one data set is therefore a physical anomaly before it is an ancient wall. Its interpretation improves when it aligns with other evidence or can be checked by excavation.

Surveyors may map a long rectangular building beside a basin and propose a warehouse. Its shape, access, floor surfaces, and associated goods must then support that reading. A row of large rooms could have stored cargo, hosted offices, or served some other purpose at different times. Portus specialists have investigated warehouse structures, a small amphitheater, cisterns, a building traditionally called the Palazzo Imperiale, and possible ship-related facilities. None of these findings emerged from one revealing photograph. They required multiple seasons and different forms of recording.

The survey also helped connect Portus to the surrounding river landscape. Work on the Isola Sacra and in the direction of Ostia traced canals and previously poorly understood structures. The landscape developed as a network: basins for arrivals, transfer areas, channels, roads, warehouses, and settlements. A harbor is less like a single pier than a logistical machine whose parts must work together. Understanding how Rome was supplied requires reconstructing those connections rather than selecting one grand building as the answer.

## A core of mud can record a port's history

Now set aside the buildings and imagine a slender cylinder of sediment brought up from a former harbor basin. The cylinder contains layers laid down while the basin was in use and after it ceased operating. Each layer is a sample of past conditions, but the history is more complicated than a neatly stacked calendar.

Coastal geoarchaeologists examine grain size, shells, pollen, chemical indicators, and microorganisms whose ecological preferences are known. Ostracods, tiny crustaceans with preservable shells, can be especially useful. Different species thrive under different salinities and environmental conditions. Their fossil assemblages may help distinguish marine influence from river or lagoon conditions. Pollen may reflect surrounding vegetation, while sand and silt layers can reveal energetic water movement or a quieter basin.

A core is not a magic depth gauge. Suppose two levels in a borehole contain a change from open-marine organisms to more brackish-water forms. That might reflect silting, reduced seawater exchange, a new barrier, or another environmental shift. Scientists compare the fossil assemblages with sediment textures, dates, and other nearby cores. A single sample tells us less than a mapped sequence. The depth of a sediment layer does not translate automatically into the depth of water above it, because the seabed itself has been changing.

Researchers working on Portus, including geoarchaeological teams publishing sediment and ostracod studies, have investigated the Claudian basin, the Trajanic harbor, and connecting channels. A 2019 study of a core from the Canale Traverso analyzed an especially informative sediment sequence in a narrow canal. The study interprets changes in deposits as evidence relevant to dredging and the operation of canal gates. The point is that maintenance can leave a recognizable signature in mud even when the ancient workers left no written maintenance manual.

Consider the difference between a gradual accumulation of fine sediment and an abrupt surface where earlier layers appear to have been removed. The second pattern might be explained by dredging. But erosion caused by strong currents could create something similar. Archaeologists therefore look for correlations across cores, historical constraints, and the sediment's composition. A trench cut for a new canal might also truncate an older sequence. To interpret a missing layer, one must understand how the layer was lost.

How do we know the age of a sediment transition? Organic remains can sometimes be radiocarbon dated, but not every object in a sediment layer was produced at the time it was deposited. Shells can have reservoir effects. Older materials may be washed in by the river. An identifiable historical building horizon can add a separate chronological constraint. Combining dates with an ordered stratigraphic sequence is often more reliable than treating any one sample as the definitive answer.

Portus has changed enough that some of its ancient maritime installations now stand inland. The Tiber moved sediment and built its delta outward. Modern land uses obscured and rearranged remains. The distance from a ruin to the present coast cannot be used to reconstruct an ancient harbor without accounting for this long environmental history. A shoreline on a historical map is evidence from a particular date, not a boundary valid for all time.

## A worked reconstruction from three cores

To see how this reasoning works, imagine researchers laying out three boreholes across a possible ancient harbor. This is a teaching example, rather than a description of one particular Portus trench. The first is drilled close to the seaward entrance. The second is in what a geophysical plan suggests was the inner basin. The third is on land near a supposed canal. Each produces a long cylinder of sediment, photographed and sampled in a laboratory. The question is whether the three locations once belonged to the same functioning water system.

Near the entrance, the lowest sample contains sand and marine shells consistent with relatively active seawater. Above it is fine silt with fewer marine organisms. At the inner basin, the lowest levels show a quiet-water environment, but higher levels are rich in organic material and increasingly brackish microfauna. At the possible canal, a sharp cut truncates older deposits and is filled with sediments that appear similar to those in the basin. This set of observations could support a history of an open harbor that gradually became restricted, with a canal excavated or reworked to preserve passage.

But every part of that story has an alternative. The sand near the entrance might come from a storm event rather than ordinary wave activity. The brackish signal could reflect a short-lived change in river flow. The cut might be erosion by a channel rather than an engineered dredging event. The similarity between sediments does not guarantee that they accumulated simultaneously. The archaeologists would need chronology and spatial geometry before drawing a harbor plan.

First they would establish the absolute elevations of each core. The depths measured below today's surface are not enough: one drill begins on a low field, another on an artificial embankment. Then they would map dates from suitable organic materials or independently datable artifacts, looking for their order within each core. A piece of driftwood could be older than the sediment around it. A shell could require correction for a marine reservoir effect. The dating model must allow those possibilities rather than turning measurements into overprecise calendar years.

Next the team would test whether corresponding deposits occur at the same time and at physically plausible heights. If an apparent harbor floor lies above the level that a sea-going vessel could have reached in the proposed period, something is wrong: perhaps the sediments have been misidentified, the local relative sea-level curve is incomplete, or the building was never a harbor basin. Conversely, a deep ancient channel connected to a river and bordered by quays would strengthen the interpretation. Historical navigation was constrained by the shape of the seabed, not by what modern maps call a port.

Finally researchers would compare the sediment story with the architecture. A breakwater can reduce wave energy, creating finer deposits behind it. A narrow gate or channel can alter circulation, encouraging some sediments to settle. An engineered dredging operation might remove layers and leave a sharp boundary that cuts through the sequence. Each structural hypothesis predicts particular kinds of environmental evidence. A good reconstruction tries to make the architecture and the sediment agree, while allowing one to challenge the other.

The value of coring is that it recovers traces of activities that need not have left a durable monument. A wooden dredging tool may be gone, but the episode that removed an accumulated layer of mud can remain detectable. The investigators do not see the ancient workers performing the maintenance; they infer a process from a physical interruption. This is also the limit of their knowledge. Even strong evidence for dredging will not identify the crew, wages, or command chain unless another source preserves those details.

Scientists often display the resulting interpretation as a colorful sequence of maps. Such maps are useful when their dates and uncertainties are explained. Yet a coastline drawn with a single crisp line may conceal the fact that much of the boundary was interpolated between a handful of cores. A responsible archaeological reconstruction should show the evidence locations and distinguish areas that were observed from areas inferred. Otherwise a beautiful map can turn a plausible environmental model into an apparent eyewitness account.

## The harbor that sank below the waves

Caesarea Maritima presents another version of the problem. Herod the Great developed a major harbor, often called Sebastos, on a coast without a naturally protected bay sufficient for the project. Its builders used hydraulic concrete in large breakwaters. These structures created sheltered water where the exposed shoreline alone had offered limited protection. Caesarea became a major city and port, but the harbor's structures also deteriorated and parts are now submerged.

Archaeologists John Peter Oleson, Robert Hohlfelder, Avner Raban, and Robert Vann published an early report on the Caesarea Ancient Harbor Excavation Project's work in the 1980s. Underwater survey and excavation documented construction features and materials in the harbor system. Later work, including Raban's published research and studies undertaken by colleagues after his death, has explored the design and development of the harbor in greater detail. Their contributions illustrate why underwater archaeology requires a specialized form of field recording.

Working underwater changes nearly everything. Divers have limited bottom time, visibility can vary, moving water can be dangerous, and sediment may cloud the view when disturbed. A block recorded as a collapsed piece of a breakwater has to be located relative to other blocks, foundations, and the modern seabed. Archaeologists may use measured control points, photographs, drawings, and survey instruments. They need to establish which elements were built in place and which were moved later by waves or structural collapse.

Concrete supplies another kind of evidence. Roman marine concrete was manufactured using combinations of lime, aggregate, and suitable volcanic materials. It could set in wet conditions and was employed in ambitious coastal construction. At Caesarea, the arrangement of construction blocks and breakwaters can reveal how builders created foundations and enclosed water. But finding Roman concrete does not by itself tell us which exact workshop supplied every ingredient or how long a given structure remained serviceable. Mineralogical testing, construction sequence, and comparative engineering research must be read together.

Harbor archaeologists have proposed mechanisms for decline involving structural failures, waves, and sedimentation. The relative role of each can differ across parts of a harbor and across time. A dramatic collapse layer may record one destructive event, but a harbor can already have been losing capacity through gradual shoaling before a storm brought down a wall. Conversely, people may repair one entrance even as another section becomes unusable. The aim is to reconstruct a process rather than find a single date when a busy port supposedly became an empty ruin.

Recent researchers have returned to Caesarea with methods that earlier divers did not have. A 2025 study by Gil Gambash, Ehud Arkin-Shalev, John Wood, Emmanuel Nantet, and Timmy Gambin used underwater photogrammetry and related remote-sensing methods to investigate the harbor entrance. By combining many overlapping photographs taken from known or reconstructable viewpoints, a computer can estimate the shape and position of structures in three dimensions. The result can be inspected, measured, and compared with earlier plans.

A digital model is not independent of the diver's work. Water distortions, poor visibility, insufficient overlap, or missing scale control can introduce errors. A beautiful visualization may give an impression of certainty that the observations do not support. The researchers use the model to ask archaeological questions: are different parts of the entrance built in different styles? Could later work have modified the original harbor? How much passage room remained after damage? Those questions make photogrammetry a scientific instrument rather than merely an illustration.

## Could a ship have entered?

A harbor reconstruction can also be tested against the physical requirements of the ships that supposedly used it. Imagine an ancient merchant vessel with a draft of several meters: its keel must remain above the seabed, and sailors need additional clearance to allow for waves, sediment shoals, and variations in water level. A theoretical route through a reconstructed harbor can be drawn from the basin to the open sea. Researchers can then ask whether the reconstructed channel was deep and wide enough at the relevant date.

The difficulties appear immediately. Ancient ship dimensions come from a limited and uneven collection of wrecks, pictures, and written accounts. A harbor may have accommodated several classes of craft, with the largest ships stopping offshore while smaller boats carried goods inside. A shallow basin is therefore not necessarily evidence that an important port never operated. It might be evidence of a particular system of transfer and labor. We should resist using one hypothetical ship as the sole standard for declaring the ancient harbor navigable.

A related question concerns when ships could travel. Seasonal winds, winter storms, tides, and river conditions affected access. A protected harbor might be especially valuable during one season while another approach was difficult during strong winds. Infrastructure that appears generous in a static cross-section may have been hard to enter under particular weather conditions. Experimental sailing, comparative ship construction, and written navigational accounts can add constraints, but they must be applied to the appropriate region and period.

At Caesarea, recent underwater measurements of the entrance have been interpreted as suggesting that passage could remain possible even while other harbor components deteriorated. That matters because a ruined breakwater does not necessarily mean that all shipping stopped on the day it failed. Ports can shrink, adapt, or change function. At Portus, continuing dredging and canal modifications similarly make sense as evidence of a working system responding to environmental change. The archaeological task is to describe changing capacities, not just label the port 'open' or 'closed.'

The strongest reconstruction combines three layers of reasoning. First comes geometry: the probable shape and depth of basins, entrances, and channels. Second comes chronology: which parts of that geometry existed at the same time. Third comes operation: what vessels and cargo-handling practices the resulting system could support. A harbor plan that gets the geometry right but combines structures from centuries apart may depict a facility that never existed. Adding a timeline changes the drawing from an attractive map into a historical explanation.

## The problem of finding what is no longer there

What should we do when the expected harbor seems to be missing? One possibility is that its structures were never substantial. Another is that they were made from perishable materials, dismantled for reuse, buried by sediment, or submerged. An ancient anchorage may have relied on a sheltered bay and modest facilities, leaving little permanent masonry. The absence of a monumental pier does not mean the coastline had no maritime activity.

The converse is also important: a substantial waterfront building is not automatically a harbor. To show that ships used a location, archaeologists may examine ancient water depth, sea access, landing surfaces, anchors, harbor ceramics, cargo residues, or the way nearby routes connect with the coast. When several sources agree, the interpretation strengthens. When they disagree, the disagreement can expose a mistaken reading of a wall or an incorrect model of the ancient shoreline.

Shoreline reconstruction also raises a scale problem. Sea level can change globally, regionally, and locally through different mechanisms. Land may rise or sink. A delta may grow because a river delivers enormous volumes of sediment even while relative sea level changes in another direction. The nearest modern tide gauge cannot by itself settle what happened at a port two thousand years earlier. Geoarchaeologists distinguish processes and calibrate models against independent markers such as dated beach deposits, archaeological quays, and sedimentary transitions.

We should also ask whose behavior a harbor plan captures. An emperor may finance a basin, but thousands of people use it: pilots, dock laborers, shipbuilders, merchants, porters, customs officials, cooks, cleaners, and families. Written records and monuments disproportionately preserve names of rulers and powerful patrons. Archaeology may expose storage and residential zones that reveal something of the workforce, but identifying specific jobs from material traces is difficult. A warehouse tells us a great deal about logistics while rarely naming the person who carried the sacks.

## Ancient infrastructure, present evidence

Both Portus and Caesarea demonstrate that infrastructure has a biography. It is imagined, funded, designed, constructed, used, repaired, adapted, and abandoned or transformed. A surviving pier may belong to one of several stages rather than the original plan. Mud may be evidence of an ancient problem solved repeatedly by dredgers. A submerged breakwater may show how engineers attempted to change the sea, and how the sea later changed their work.

The historical knowledge comes from specific researchers and their records. Simon Keay's project and Italian collaborators helped map Portus on land. Sediment specialists supplied a changing environmental history. Avner Raban, John Peter Oleson, Robert Hohlfelder, and their teams established major parts of the underwater record at Caesarea. New digital surveys have asked new questions of structures whose existence has been known for decades. None of these approaches is sufficient alone.

The great lesson is that a port is not a static mark on a map. Ancient maritime travel depended on shallow channels, shifting currents, engineered barriers, repetitive maintenance, and people who knew when and how a ship could enter. By recovering the physical conditions under which those people worked, archaeologists turn a vanished shoreline into a more precise account of ancient movement and economic life. They can identify the harbor's shape and changing possibilities even when its sailors left almost no words behind.

### Editorial cautions

- A high-resolution photogrammetric model is an interpretation constrained by image coverage, scaling, and underwater visibility; do not equate visual fidelity with complete excavation.
- Portus chronology spans distinct Claudian, Trajanic, and later modifications; do not describe the whole basin system as built in one reign.
- Different archaeological interpretations of the causes and dates of harbor decline should not be compressed into a single catastrophe.
