# How We Know: evidence, excavation, and attribution

This is an archaeology strand **within World History**. Its lectures are
standalone audio essays. They investigate not only ancient history but the
history of the evidence and the research that produced our present account.

## Attribution and evidentiary standard

- **Say whose result it is in the spoken lecture**, at the point where it is needed for understanding. Credit ancient authors, known scribes, excavators, field team members, translators, museum curators, method developers, and modern researchers according to their distinct contributions. Avoid presenting collaborative findings as single-person discoveries.
- **Distinguish original evidence from interpretation**: a find in a documented context, an instrument reading, a paleographic interpretation, a printed reconstruction, a popular paraphrase, and a literary tradition are different things.
- **Explain provenance**: findspot and context, collection histories, dates of manuscripts versus composition, and relevant excavation and translation histories.
- **Use published research**: original field reports and publications first when accessible, followed by critical editions, scholarly reinterpretations, and institutional overviews. Credit authors, publication dates, and contributions in metadata and non-spoken notes.
- **Acknowledge missing credit**: older excavation rosters can omit local workers and assistants. Name documented contributors, explain the gap when needed, and do not invent identities.
- **Make revisions teachable**: show what evidence led a later researcher to revise an earlier claim. Avoid blanket pronouncements when the stratigraphy, sample, or text witness supports a more precise account.
- **Keep audio listenable**: narration should credit people naturally, without reading bibliographic URLs or citation keys. Place full links and detailed evidence mappings after the spoken markers and in `metadata/`.

## Future candidates

- **The Swahili coast as an archaeological archive** — Kilwa, Shanga, East African ceramics, coral architecture, coins, and competing narratives about local and overseas participation.
- **Ancient harbor reconstruction** — shoreline change, sediment cores, coastal geoarchaeology, and why ports disappear or move.
- **Shipwreck science and conservation** — wood, salt, corrosion, cargo residues, underwater photogrammetry, and the limits of chemical provenance.

The sequence should remain selected for depth and interesting lines of
inquiry, without turning each named site or technique into a mandatory
lecture. Continue the mathematics curricula independently.
