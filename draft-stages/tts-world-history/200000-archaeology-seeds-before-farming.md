# Seeds Before Farming: How Archaeologists Reconstruct Ancient Diets

Imagine lowering a bucket into a trench, filling it with gray earth, and then pouring the earth into water. Small fragments rise to the surface: seeds as dark as charcoal, splinters of nutshell, bits of stem, a few insect remains. The pieces are not attractive museum objects. Some are smaller than the dots in printed text. But if their location was recorded carefully, they may reveal what a family ate, what plants grew near a settlement, and whether people were experimenting with cultivation centuries before familiar domesticated crops appeared.

How can a seed tell us about an economy? How does an archaeologist distinguish a plant gathered in the wild from a plant grown deliberately? And what changes when the excavator begins to recover all the tiny things that earlier excavations simply let fall through their sieves?

We will follow these questions through the history of archaeobotany, the study of plant remains from archaeological contexts. Our principal case will be Ohalo II, a camp on the shore of the Sea of Galilee occupied about twenty-three thousand years ago. Archaeologist Dani Nadel and colleagues excavated it; researchers including Ehud Weiss, Dolores Piperno, Irene Holst, and Ainit Snir studied its exceptional plant remains. We will also visit the Neolithic settlement of Çatalhöyük in central Anatolia and consider the work of Amy Bogaard and other specialists in prehistoric farming. Along the way we will see how a change in recovery technique can alter an entire account of human subsistence.

## What an excavator used to see

A century ago an archaeologist might describe a prehistoric settlement through its walls, graves, stone tools, and animal bones. These were the things most likely to be noticed and saved. Large bones could be lifted from a trench. Stone tools survived millennia. Charcoal might be collected for dating. But a charred grass seed, no bigger than a pinhead, could be lost in the soil. Seeds rot in many environments, and even those that survive may be too small for ordinary excavation screens. A report written only from conspicuous finds risks describing a diet that happens to resemble the excavator's collection method.

This is not just a technical inconvenience. It can become a historical argument. If a site yields spear points and the bones of deer, one might imagine a community supported almost entirely by hunting. If small seeds, legumes, and tubers are missing because nobody collected them, the apparent dominance of meat is partly an artifact of excavation. We cannot reconstruct the relative importance of different foods merely by adding up what survived under unequal conditions. Recovery methods determine which hypotheses the evidence can even test.

In 1968, American archaeologist Stuart Struever published a paper called *Flotation Techniques for the Recovery of Small-Scale Archaeological Remains*. He explained a way to process bulk soil in water and recover tiny material overlooked by conventional screening. Flotation was not a magical new principle invented from nothing. Investigators had experimented with washing and separating archaeological deposits before, and other field teams developed their own apparatus. Struever's contribution was to set out a practical archaeological procedure and a clear argument about sampling bias. A relatively inexpensive method could broaden the kinds of evidence routinely recovered.

The basic idea is easy to picture. Put a measured sample of sediment into water and gently agitate it. Some charred plant remains are light enough to float. They can be collected on fine mesh as a light fraction. Heavier objects settle or are caught separately. The fractions are dried, sorted, counted, and identified. The method works best under suitable conditions and must be adapted to the soil, material, and preservation state. Not every archaeological seed floats; not every floating fragment is ancient. A careful project records how much soil was processed, which contexts were sampled, what equipment was used, and what was lost in treatment.

Imagine two rooms excavated at the same settlement. Ten liters of soil from one hearth yield eighty charred cereal grains. Ten liters from a second yield five. The tempting interpretation is that the first room processed far more grain. But first ask whether the deposits are comparable. Did one hearth burn for twenty seasons and the other for one? Was one floor swept and the other abandoned suddenly? Could ash from a roof or a rubbish heap have been deposited in one room? Do we have repeat samples? A concentration of charred seed is evidence of how material accumulated, not a straightforward inventory of meals.

## Why the plants survived

Archaeologists rarely find a complete prehistoric pantry. Each preservation environment filters the evidence differently. Charred seeds can survive because heating changes their chemistry and makes them resistant to ordinary biological decomposition, although a seed burned too fiercely may become ash. Waterlogging can preserve delicate organic material when oxygen is limited and microbial activity is suppressed. Dry caves and deserts sometimes preserve plant fibers and fruits. Mineralization, impressions in pottery, and microscopic starch residues offer other routes to identification. Every pathway has its own distortions.

Consider a household where people ate fresh fruit every day and cereal bread once a week. The fruit may leave few recognizable remains if pits and peels were discarded outside the settlement and rotted. A single accident that burns a basket of grain can create a large, conspicuous archaeological deposit. An archaeobotanist would be mistaken to conclude that the community mostly ate grain simply because grain dominated the surviving sample. The abundance of remains must be interpreted in relation to cooking, storage, burning, disposal, and decay.

Identification is another problem. Under a microscope, the arrangement of cells and the shape of a seed coat may distinguish a genus from related plants. Some species have characteristic grain shapes, glume bases, or other durable features. Reference collections of modern plants are essential: dried seeds, charred comparison specimens, and photographs taken under controlled conditions. But heating distorts shapes. A fragment might only be identifiable as wheat or barley, or even as an indeterminate grass. Honest identifications stop at the level the surviving features justify.

The botanist's Latin species name is therefore a result of comparison and judgment. It is not a label printed on the artifact. Two experts may disagree about a damaged grain, especially when wild and domestic forms overlap. Their disagreement can be resolved in part by better microscopy, larger reference collections, or a different kind of evidence. A specialist might count several hundred grains and report both secure identifications and ambiguous cases. That is more informative than presenting a false level of precision.

## A camp emerges from the lake

The archaeological site of Ohalo II came into view when the water level of the Sea of Galilee dropped in 1989. For thousands of years, lake sediments had protected a campsite on the southwestern shore. The excavations directed by Dani Nadel recovered evidence for brush huts, hearths, a human burial, stone tools, animal remains, and a striking number of plant fragments. It is one of those cases in which preservation allows a detailed investigation of ordinary domestic activity far earlier than most written records.

Ohalo II dates to approximately twenty-three thousand years ago, during the last glacial period. Agriculture based on domesticated cereals was still thousands of years in the future. The people who lived there were hunter-gatherer-fishers, acquiring wild resources in a landscape that differed from today's. They possessed skills, technology, and knowledge of plants. They did not need to be farmers to process grains into food.

In a 2004 study, Ehud Weiss and colleagues examined more than ninety thousand plant remains recovered from Ohalo II. The authors included Dani Nadel and specialists in plant identification and prehistoric subsistence. They reported a remarkable range of edible plants, including wild cereals and many small-grained grasses. That evidence helped shift the discussion of prehistoric diets. It showed that substantial use of wild grasses occurred many millennia before the appearance of recognizable domesticated wheat and barley.

The large number is impressive, but the reasoning does not rest on one large number. The team asked which plants were present, in what proportions, and where they were found. They compared them with modern reference specimens and with assemblages from later sites. They considered whether the grass seeds might represent animal bedding or fuel rather than food. The presence of particular seeds beside a grinding stone made a food-processing interpretation more plausible. Each line of evidence narrowed the alternatives without eliminating every uncertainty.

The 2004 work also revisited a larger idea in prehistoric archaeology: the broadening of human diets before the adoption of farming. Archaeologist Kent Flannery had argued decades earlier that changes in population and resource use could lead people to exploit many smaller, less immediately rewarding foods. That argument was often discussed in terms of animal remains. Ohalo II gave researchers a rare opportunity to consider plants as part of the same history. The point was not to replace a story about hunting with a story about grain. It was to recover evidence for the breadth of the food economy.

## A trace of flour on stone

Another study in 2004, by Dolores Piperno, Ehud Weiss, Irene Holst, and Dani Nadel, investigated microscopic starch grains on a stone tool. The researchers used comparisons with modern plant starches to identify evidence compatible with processing wild cereals, including barley and probably forms of wheat. The technique adds a second level of observation to the site. Large charred seeds indicate which plants were present. Starch grains on a working surface may show what kinds of plants were processed at a particular tool.

Starch is stored energy inside plant cells. Different botanical groups may produce granules with characteristic sizes, outlines, and features seen under polarized light. Processing can damage or alter them. A laboratory can examine granules recovered from ancient stone crevices and compare them with reference materials. The method is powerful, but contamination control is essential. Modern starch occurs in the air, on clothing, in adhesives, and on handling surfaces. Archaeologists must document how tools were recovered, stored, cleaned, and sampled. Blank controls and independently sampled surfaces help distinguish original residues from later contamination.

Suppose a grinding stone has starch in its pores. We can propose that plant material was processed there. We cannot automatically deduce the exact recipe, the frequency of use, the identity of its owner, or whether every starch grain came from the same meal. If the same tool was used over years, traces from multiple episodes may survive together. Researchers test the interpretation against tool wear, associated seeds, the surrounding hearth, and the tool's position in the excavated floor. The strength comes from agreement among partly independent observations.

At Ohalo II, there is also evidence of fire and cooking. It is tempting to imagine a loaf of bread being baked. A hearth and cereal starch make preparation of cooked cereal food plausible, but no one can read the exact preparation from these traces. Flour might have been mixed into a paste, cooked on stones, boiled, or heated in another way. A good reconstruction can offer possibilities while explaining which traces each possibility would leave.

## Did they cultivate the grain?

Now comes a more difficult question. Did Ohalo II's inhabitants merely gather wild grasses, or did they deliberately encourage their growth? Cultivation and domestication are different things. People can sow, water, weed, or harvest wild plants without changing those plants genetically over generations. Conversely, a domesticated crop has inherited traits shaped by a history of human selection, whether or not a particular archaeological household planted it.

One of the familiar traits archaeobotanists examine is the way cereal heads break apart. In many wild cereals, ripe seeds scatter when the ear shatters; that is useful for natural reproduction. Domesticated forms often retain grains on the stalk longer, making harvesting more efficient. The relevant structures may be preserved as charred spikelet bases or other botanical remains. But the transformation was gradual, samples can be mixed, and domestication traits are not a perfect switch. Date, environment, and population variation all affect interpretation.

In 2015, Ainit Snir, Dani Nadel, Ehud Weiss, and collaborators proposed that plant remains at Ohalo II included signs of disturbed habitats and possibly early experiments in cultivating wild cereals. They studied plants that today are associated with fields and other human-disturbed ground. This was an important and provocative interpretation, but the inference is harder than demonstrating that wild grain was gathered and processed. A disturbed patch near huts, a natural habitat edge, and a deliberately tended plot can all favor some of the same species. The evidence can motivate a hypothesis about trial cultivation without showing that an agricultural economy already existed there.

This distinction is exactly what makes Ohalo II educational. An archaeologist may be confident about the site's age, quite confident about a large collection of wild grass seeds, and much less confident about whether those plants were sown. The confidence level changes with the claim. If we ask, 'Did people grind wild cereals twenty-three thousand years ago?' several observations support yes. If we ask, 'Did these particular people invent agriculture?' the evidence does not justify such a conclusion. A question may be historically exciting and still demand restraint.

## Weeds as witnesses

There is a second way to study cultivation, which appears in later prehistoric farming settlements. Many plants that grow among crops are not planted deliberately. They arrive with seed stocks, spread into tilled ground, or thrive where grazing, irrigation, or soil disturbance alters the habitat. Their seeds can become charred with harvested grain. Because different weed species prefer different growing conditions, an assemblage of weeds may preserve clues about the fields where cereals grew.

Amy Bogaard and other archaeobotanists have investigated prehistoric farming methods using ecological characteristics of weeds, cereal remains, and the contexts in which they were recovered. At Neolithic Çatalhöyük, the archaeobotanical record includes cultivated crops, wild plants, chaff, and the remains of activities that brought them into buildings. Researchers ask about soil fertility, cultivation intensity, harvesting methods, and whether households managed nearby or more distant plots. They compare assemblages across contexts and use ecological reference data rather than assuming every seed has the same meaning.

Here is a simplified example. Suppose the cereal harvest from one ancient community contains seeds of plants that prefer well-watered, fertile soil, while another contains plants characteristic of drier, more disturbed ground. It may be tempting to infer different farming strategies. But first consider how the grain was cleaned. An early stage of winnowing leaves a different collection of weed seeds from a final cleaning stage. A household may burn chaff from one task and store clean grain from another. A pit full of chaff cannot be compared directly with a cooking pot full of food. The archaeological context and the processing sequence must be understood before the ecological signal becomes useful.

At Çatalhöyük, even a single building may contain deposits from construction, occupation, burning, rebuilding, and deliberate closure. Residents frequently rebuilt structures, and plant material might enter a building as fuel, dung, flooring, food, or fodder. A weed seed found in a hearth could have arrived inside an animal's dung rather than in a human meal. Microscopic remains of dung, ash chemistry, and comparisons across features may help separate these pathways. The plant record can reveal a household economy, but only if we work out how plants moved through the household.

## The missing plants

A useful archaeobotanical analysis also asks what is absent from the sample, and whether absence is meaningful. If no olive stones are recovered from an inland settlement, does that mean nobody there ate olives? Perhaps. Or perhaps olives were consumed without their pits reaching the sampled hearths. Perhaps the deposits were too acidic, or the pits were burned until unrecognizable. Perhaps the excavated neighborhood did not represent the community's food stores. Absence becomes informative only when we understand preservation and sampling well enough to say the plant would probably have been found had it been present.

This issue becomes especially important when comparing sites. A site in a dry cave may preserve baskets, plant fibers, and desiccated seeds. A village buried in wet clay may preserve different organics. A burnt building may preserve stored grain in a spectacular cache. A settlement excavated decades earlier may have collected no bulk soil samples at all. Comparing raw counts across these conditions can create a false history of changing diets. Archaeobotanists instead use standardized sampling when possible and document recovery conditions as part of the result.

Now return to our bucket of sediment. If it came from a sealed hearth with a known volume, recorded stratigraphic context, and carefully controlled processing, the floating fragments can be attached to a particular episode of activity. If it came from a mixed spoil heap, the seeds are much less informative, even if someone identifies every one perfectly. Laboratory skill cannot replace a lost excavation context. Conversely, meticulous excavation without microscopic identification leaves many questions unanswered. The two kinds of expertise must work together.

## From households to environmental history

Plant remains can also reveal environments beyond the household. Pollen preserved in lakes and wetlands may document long-term vegetation changes. Charcoal particles can help investigate histories of burning. Phytoliths, tiny silica structures produced by many plants, can persist after organic tissues disappear. Sediments, stable isotopes, and ancient DNA may contribute different pieces of the reconstruction. These methods cover different spatial and temporal scales. A pollen core from a lake may tell us about regional vegetation, while a concentration of grain in a kitchen tells us about one household's handling of food.

A common error is to treat these scales as interchangeable. If a regional pollen record shows less forest after a certain date, it suggests landscape change. It does not prove that the inhabitants of one excavated house personally cut down the trees. A charred seed in that house may identify a plant brought from outside the immediate settlement. An ancient botanical landscape is reconstructed by combining different samples whose histories and scales we know. The outcome is often a map of possibilities, not a photograph of a vanished countryside.

There is also human knowledge behind every plant specimen. Ancient people recognized seasons, soils, seed maturity, and the effects of harvesting. They chose tools for grinding, built places to store food, and decided when to invest labor in cultivation. Archaeology can rarely recover their individual names, but it can reveal the technical competence embedded in ordinary practices. Modern archaeobotanists build a separate chain of knowledge: excavation, sampling, identification, ecological comparison, and interpretation. Credit belongs to field workers and laboratory specialists as well as to the authors whose names appear on the journal article.

This is why the achievements at Ohalo II are collective. Dani Nadel directed excavation and helped establish the site context. Ehud Weiss and collaborators examined large botanical assemblages. Dolores Piperno and Irene Holst contributed to microscopic starch analysis. Ainit Snir and coauthors tested further hypotheses about plant use and cultivation. Their studies did not simply add more facts to a known story. They changed which questions could be asked about people living twenty-three thousand years ago.

## What a seed can prove

Let us assemble a careful chain of inference. A sealed archaeological floor is dated through independently evaluated methods. Flotation and hand collection recover plant remains whose exact locations are recorded. A specialist compares the material to modern collections and identifies wild cereals and grasses. A grinding stone bears microscopic residues compatible with processing these plants. Similar plant taxa appear in repeated samples, rather than only in one odd context. Each step contributes evidence for a history of plant collecting and food preparation.

Now look at what the chain leaves open. It does not tell us every meal eaten at the site. It does not turn a wild cereal into a domesticated crop. It does not show how labor was divided among the site's residents. It cannot automatically distinguish a small tended plot from gathering in a naturally rich patch. These questions invite experiments, new discoveries, and more comparisons; they cannot be answered by making the original claim sound more confident.

The larger historical lesson is that farming was preceded by a vast history of plant knowledge. Human beings collected, processed, stored, and cooked foods long before the familiar grain-based agricultural economies emerged. The transition to farming did not begin when people first noticed edible seeds. It involved changes in plants, landscapes, labor, storage, settlement, and social life that unfolded at different times in different places.

A seed is a small witness. It tells us much more when someone records exactly where it was found, learns how to recognize it, and asks what other activities could have brought it there. The development of archaeobotany made some of the least imposing objects in a trench into evidence for the most consequential changes in human history. The next time we imagine the origins of farming, we should imagine not only fields and sickles, but also a lake shore twenty-three thousand years ago, a grinding stone, and the patient work of recovering thousands of plants that would otherwise have gone unseen.

### Interpretation checks

- The presence of wild grain and starch processing is much better established at Ohalo II than a claim of systematic cultivation.
- Flotation and sampled soil volume affect recovered plant distributions; raw assemblage size is not a measure of dietary calories.
- This lecture credits multiple investigators rather than presenting plant research as a single discovery.
