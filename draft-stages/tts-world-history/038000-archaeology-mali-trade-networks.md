# Finding Mali's Trade Networks in the Ground

Imagine following a merchant across the Sahara in the fourteenth century. His caravan moves between oases. He carries salt, cloth, or other goods south, and he hopes to return with gold, ivory, or products whose value depends on the market where they will be sold. At the edge of the desert, he exchanges information with people who know the river valleys beyond. His journey enters a network far older and more intricate than any one ruler's ambition.

How can a historian recover this world? Traders did not leave a continuous diary of every journey. Goods were consumed, reused, melted down, or carried far from where they were made. Accounts written in Arabic survive, but some authors lived at a distance from the West African places they described. The empire of Mali itself changed across time, and the relationship between its rulers and individual commercial towns was not uniform. To reconstruct the history, we need more than the famous image of Mansa Musa carrying gold.

We need to investigate settlements under the earth, fragments of metalworking equipment, imported materials, botanical remains, manuscript collections, and the geographical logic of the Niger River and the Sahara. We also need to credit the scholars and excavation teams who made those objects intelligible. Archaeology does not simply illustrate the words of medieval travelers. Sometimes it reveals commercial and urban histories that written sources scarcely noticed, and sometimes it requires us to reduce the certainty of an attractive story.

This lecture follows three principal areas in present-day Mali: Jenné-Jeno in the Inland Niger Delta, Essouk-Tadmekka near the Sahara's southern edge, and Timbuktu at the meeting point of riverine and desert exchange. They belong to different centuries and research histories. Together they show how to distinguish trade as an economic process from the later prestige of famous kings and cities.

## Begin with the geography

The Sahara is often imagined as a blank barrier separating two worlds. It is a vast desert that imposes severe logistical constraints, but it has also contained routes connecting populations. Travel required knowledge of wells, water storage, seasonal conditions, political alliances, and the care of transport animals. Routes changed as conditions and states changed. A line on a modern map showing a caravan route is a reconstruction, not a road preserved continuously under the sand.

South of the desert, the Niger River bends across the Sahel. In its inland delta, seasonal flooding supports agriculture, fishing, and transport. A community here could supply grain, fish, and other staples to neighboring populations while acquiring stone, metals, salt, or finished goods from farther away. Such exchanges create material traces, though the traces may be dispersed. A copper object found in a settlement without a local source of copper is evidence of movement of material. It is not automatically evidence that a particular merchant visited from a named city.

The chronological problem is equally important. Medieval accounts of the Mali Empire often describe the thirteenth through fifteenth centuries. Archaeological deposits in the same broad region can be a thousand years older. If urban settlements existed before Mali emerged, we cannot claim that the empire invented West African cities. If a deposit at a site dates to the eighteenth century, it cannot directly verify a transaction in Mansa Musa's reign. A careful account of commerce has to keep both geography and chronology attached to every claim.

Older histories often gave the main explanatory role to caravans arriving from North Africa. The question was how trade from abroad generated towns south of the Sahara. Modern excavations have shown that indigenous settlement, food production, and regional exchange were already substantial before the best-documented Islamic-era trans-Saharan networks. That finding changes the historical explanation. Long-distance commerce mattered greatly; it entered an inhabited and economically active landscape.

## The city older than the empire

Jenné-Jeno, also written Jenne-jeno or Djenné-Djeno, is an archaeological settlement mound near modern Djenné. Roderick McIntosh and Susan Keech McIntosh began sustained investigations there in 1977. Their excavations and survey of surrounding settlements transformed the study of early West African urbanism. The work involved excavation crews, local knowledge, specialists studying ceramics and other materials, and the practical cooperation of communities in the region. The published research identifies the McIntoshes' leading analytical contributions; it should not make everyone else disappear.

The lowest occupational levels show iron-using populations by about the third century before the common era. The settlement grew substantially through the first millennium after that. The McIntoshes argued that by roughly the eighth to eleventh centuries Jenné-Jeno was part of a dense urban and rural settlement system. This preceded Mansa Musa by centuries. Pottery, craft activity, and the relations among nearby settlements permit inferences about how that system worked.

How does one recognize a city when it lacks the palace or monumental temple expected from a familiar ancient Mediterranean model? One answer comes from density and specialization. A large mound occupied over time, close to associated communities performing different economic tasks, may represent an integrated urban system even if its political institutions do not resemble a walled royal capital. Survey matters as much as a spectacular trench. By walking, mapping, sampling, and testing surrounding settlements, archaeologists can ask whether people lived in a regional network rather than a single isolated center.

The McIntoshes' research also found imported materials and evidence of regional exchange. Stone and some metal resources were not naturally available in the floodplain. Copper in later first-millennium levels points toward wider contacts, possibly involving Saharan sources. But archaeologists have to distinguish the existence of copper from a proven map of the precise route it followed. An object can pass through several hands before reaching the household where it was discarded. Its final location records the end of one journey, not the entire itinerary.

The environment supplied substantial resources of its own. Floodplain rice, fish, and other products helped support dense communities. In reassessing early commercial history, Roderick and Susan McIntosh emphasized the productive capacity of the Inland Niger Delta. Rather than treating West African settlements as passive depots for imported luxuries, their interpretation puts agricultural, fishing, and craft economies near the beginning of the explanation. Long-distance trade operated because people were already producing things others needed.

This is a case in which archaeology revises the timeline supplied by certain written histories. Medieval authors may have recorded cities known to their contemporaries. Their descriptions cannot establish when all earlier settlements began. The ground contains a longer history, with its own uncertainties about population, authority, and local social organization. Jenné-Jeno was largely abandoned by around 1400, while later Djenné remained important. We should not treat the two as a city whose location and institutions stayed identical across fifteen centuries.

## An experiment in dating an exchange

Suppose excavators uncover a shard of glass and a piece of copper in the same layer as charcoal. Radiocarbon dating of the charcoal can estimate when the organic material stopped exchanging carbon with the atmosphere. Stratigraphy can indicate whether the layer predates or follows another deposit. Ceramic typology and imported-object comparisons can add information. Together these may place the glass and copper in a broad historical period.

But the date does not necessarily tell us when the glass was manufactured. The object could have circulated for decades before it broke. The charcoal might come from wood cut long before it burned. Disturbed deposits may mix materials of different dates. Careful reports describe the context and the basis for the chronology. The date becomes persuasive through agreement among methods, rather than because one scientific instrument gives a number.

A second question is the material's origin. Researchers can compare glass composition, ceramic fabric, metal impurities, or manufacturing techniques with known production areas. A chemical match may support an origin or technology, though it can be complicated by shared raw materials and recycling. A find of imported glass is evidence of contact. It does not reveal the nationality of the person who carried it or the language spoken in the market where it was purchased.

Trade also leaves absences. Salt dissolves and is consumed. Textiles rot unless preservation conditions are favorable. Gold is highly recyclable and can disappear from an archaeological site even when the town handled enormous quantities of it. A historian who expects to find whole warehouses of gold might wrongly conclude that the trade did not exist. The more useful question is what processes should leave traces after the valuable products have been removed.

## Tadmekka and the gold that left

At Essouk-Tadmekka, near the southern Sahara, archaeologist Sam Nixon conducted systematic investigations in 2005. He published an account of the excavations in 2009. The stratified sequence, extending back into the first millennium of the common era, revealed a commercial settlement associated with early Islamic trans-Saharan exchange. This evidence is particularly valuable because written geographers had discussed the town, but until modern excavation much of its ordinary material economy remained difficult to see.

A remarkable discovery concerned fragments of ceramic objects with small depressions, associated with gold-working evidence. Nixon, materials scientist Thilo Rehren, and archaeometallurgist Maria Filomena Guerra published an analysis in 2011. They argued that gold had been melted into small, relatively standardized lumps, potentially facilitating exchange. Chemical and microscopic examination provided a way to investigate what craft workers did, even though the gold itself was mostly gone.

We should picture the evidence accurately. The archaeologists did not excavate a royal mint with a room full of stamped coins. They found pieces of equipment and residues that required interpretation. The early paper used the language of coin moulds. Subsequent numismatic analysis cautioned that the products may have been unstamped blanks rather than coins in the narrower sense of marked objects issued under an authority. That distinction affects the conclusions we draw about political control. A standardized lump of gold can be useful for merchants without carrying a king's name or guarantee.

Here the argument becomes satisfyingly testable. A porous ceramic mould has depressions whose shape and size can be measured. A crucible fragment carries particles that can be examined chemically. If material analysis identifies very pure gold, that tells us something about processing and potential use. Rehren and Nixon later published evidence from crucibles suggesting that workers used glass to help process gold-bearing material. These are technical traces of skilled labor, not simply illustrations of how wealthy an emperor was supposed to be.

The same evidence has limits. We cannot calculate the annual volume of the entire Saharan gold trade from a few mould fragments. We cannot prove that Mansa Musa controlled Tadmekka's gold production centuries earlier or later merely because both belong to a broad region called Mali today. The excavated material belongs to dated contexts and specific craft practices. It supports claims about early trade mechanisms, while the organization and scale of commerce require other evidence.

Consider how differently the story reads once we credit workers. Gold production depended on miners and washers, people selecting and transporting fuel, craft workers preparing vessels, merchants arranging transactions, and communities provisioned along routes. A clay fragment from Tadmekka preserves the skill of people whose names are absent from most written histories. Their work created the value that rulers later displayed.

## The accounts written far away

Now place the excavation reports beside medieval Arabic texts. Al-Bakri, writing in the eleventh century in al-Andalus, assembled geographical knowledge about West Africa. His descriptions are important, but he did not personally travel through every place he mentioned. We have to ask where his information originated and how it was transmitted. His account also predates Mansa Musa, so it cannot directly describe that ruler's reign.

Al-Umari, a fourteenth-century author writing in the Mamluk world, recorded accounts associated with Mansa Musa's pilgrimage and his visit to Cairo. His description supplies information about how Musa's wealth and conduct were remembered outside Mali. It was mediated through informants, not a neutral measurement of Mali's annual gold production. A spectacular report of spending or an estimate of attendants tells us what was reported and believed in a particular setting. It should not be converted into an audited economic statistic.

Ibn Battuta traveled in West Africa in the fourteenth century and described places, customs, and political relationships. His observations have a different evidentiary status from secondhand geographical writing, although travel narratives are also shaped by expectations, genre, memory, and later textual compilation. We can compare what he reports with other written and archaeological records rather than choosing one author to speak for an entire society.

Later sources introduce another chronological layer. The chronicler Abd al-Rahman al-Sa'di wrote the *Tarikh al-Sudan* in the seventeenth century, describing earlier West African histories as well as a more recent period. Leo Africanus, writing in the sixteenth century, offered accounts of regional commerce that became influential in Europe. Their works can preserve valuable information and later recollection, but a seventeenth-century chronicle is not a contemporary witness to a thirteenth-century event merely because it narrates one.

Texts and objects can sometimes correct each other. If an author describes standardized unstamped gold in a trading town, the mould evidence at Tadmekka becomes suggestive. If the material can be independently dated to a relevant period, the comparison grows stronger. If the artifact has a different function or date, the resemblance is weaker. The strongest interpretation is not the one with the most exciting match; it is the one that states exactly which claims have independent support.

There is also a language and survival problem. Arabic accounts preserve some voices while leaving out many oral histories, local records, and the views of nonelite traders. Indigenous accounts and traditions deserve study in their own right, but oral narratives must be assessed with attention to performance, changing circumstances, and the purposes of their tellers. No single kind of record provides a complete picture.

## Timbuktu: a famous city and a difficult excavation

Timbuktu occupies a place in popular imagination as a city of wealth, learning, and distant caravans. Its historical importance is real, but different aspects of that reputation belong to different centuries. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage took place in the 1320s. Timbuktu's greatest documented prominence as a scholarly and commercial center developed particularly in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. A lecture that turns every celebrated manuscript into an object from Musa's personal court would distort the chronology.

The city itself presents archaeological challenges. Much of the historic center is occupied and has been repeatedly rebuilt. Excavation must consider living residents, buildings, buried utilities, preservation, and access. Windblown sand complicates the surface picture. Archaeologists cannot dig an entire city in search of a neat foundational layer. They select limited areas, record what they encounter, and identify which historical periods the deposits actually represent.

Timothy Insoll published archaeological research on Timbuktu, including a preliminary survey in the 1990s and trial excavations undertaken in 1998. Those excavations recovered pottery, beads, glass, metal objects, shells, spindle whorls, and other traces of everyday life. Much of that assemblage informs the city's postmedieval history, particularly the eighteenth through twentieth centuries. The work is relevant to trade and urban life, but it cannot be used without qualification as direct proof of what the city looked like in 1324.

This is precisely why an unglamorous list of pottery and beads can be more informative than a claim about legendary wealth. Pottery can reveal food preparation and consumption. Spindle whorls point to textile production. Glass objects and beads can be studied for manufacturing and exchange. The assemblage can show residents participating in a mixed economy, not just a line of camel caravans carrying gold. Every object still needs a date and context before it enters the historical reconstruction.

Timbuktu also has monumental architecture and an immense manuscript heritage. Its mosques have been rebuilt and repaired across generations, which helps preserve them but complicates attempts to identify the exact fabric of an earlier century. A manuscript can carry a date, an owner's note, a place of copying, or a record of scholarly transmission. That information opens questions inaccessible to excavation alone. Yet not every manuscript found or preserved in Timbuktu was written there, and many surviving volumes are later than Mali's fourteenth-century imperial height.

## The people who saved the manuscripts

The manuscript libraries of Timbuktu were not waiting undiscovered for a foreign scholar to arrive. Families, scholars, copyists, and custodians preserved collections over generations. These were working intellectual resources: texts on law, religion, mathematics, astronomy, language, literature, and other subjects moved through teaching and copying networks. Their owners and readers participated in historical knowledge production long before today's digitization projects.

The threats to those manuscripts are material as well as political. Paper decays; bindings fail; insects and humidity cause damage; floods and fire can destroy a collection. During the 2012–2013 crisis in northern Mali, manuscript custodians and local networks helped protect and move large numbers of manuscripts. Abdel Kader Haidara and associated librarians have received attention for preservation efforts, but the work involved many people whose collective contribution should remain visible. UNESCO and other institutions have supported conservation and access projects.

A manuscript's journey during a modern emergency is part of its provenance. If a collection is moved for safety, responsible researchers need documentation of its location and handling. Digital images help preserve information, but a photograph does not automatically capture paper composition, binding history, or every erased marginal note. Cataloging also demands choices about titles and authors when the volume itself may have several hands or texts.

The manuscripts support claims about intellectual networks as well as exchange. If a volume's colophon identifies a copyist and date, that is evidence of particular activity. An annotation connecting a teacher to a pupil can help reconstruct a scholarly relationship. A legal document may illuminate commercial practice. A general statement that Timbuktu had manuscripts does not tell us the number of students at any one school or prove that a specific merchant purchased a particular shipment of salt.

There is a productive connection here between textual and material archaeology. A manuscript can mention a market or commodity while a dated excavated assemblage demonstrates the consumption or production of related goods. The two kinds of evidence can converge, but they also have their own patterns of preservation. A precious book is saved because people consider it important. A broken pot survives because it was thrown away. The resulting archives are differently biased.

## Can we identify the gold of Mansa Musa?

The most familiar image of Mali's wealth appears in the Catalan Atlas of 1375, where a crowned ruler commonly identified as Mansa Musa holds a golden object. The image shows how Mediterranean mapmakers represented Mali after Musa's pilgrimage. It does not tell us how much gold he actually possessed, and it was not drawn by a participant in the pilgrimage. We know the map's date and cultural setting and can study its visual message, but it is evidence of an outside representation rather than an audited royal inventory.

Could modern science trace gold in a North African coin back to a specific West African mine? Chemical and isotopic techniques can sometimes characterize metal sources and processing histories, but gold circulates, is remelted, and can be mixed. Mining areas may share geological characteristics. A strong claim requires reference samples, careful method, and an understanding of recycling. Without that, identifying an individual coin as Musa's gold would be a much stronger conclusion than the evidence supports.

The material at Tadmekka gives a more grounded kind of connection. It demonstrates technical practices relevant to the movement of gold from West Africa into wider exchange systems before the best-known accounts of Mansa Musa. Jenné-Jeno demonstrates that urban and regional commercial networks grew long before Musa. Timbuktu's manuscripts and archaeology testify to different periods of learned and mercantile life. Together these findings make the famous ruler part of a long history rather than the inventor of a trading world.

Trade networks themselves are difficult to assign to a single state. Merchants move through overlapping jurisdictions and family relationships. A ruler may tax a route without producing the goods it carries. A market town may thrive when a neighboring kingdom declines. When historians describe the Mali Empire's wealth, they should distinguish the sources of gold, the places where it was processed, the routes through which it moved, and the political mechanisms that extracted revenue. The objects do not answer every institutional question, but they prevent us from pretending that the whole system was simply the king's treasure chest.

## Reconstruct a trade route without inventing it

Let's finish with an exercise in historical inference. Imagine a ninth-century deposit at Tadmekka containing gold-working equipment, and a roughly contemporary northern coin with high-purity gold. A merchant's geographical text describes a southern gold trade. Does this prove that the particular coin was made from gold processed at Tadmekka? No. It establishes a plausible commercial setting and a technological capacity, with important gaps between origin and destination.

Now add compositional analyses, find locations of other objects, dates established through stratigraphy, and independent descriptions of transport or markets. Each additional line of evidence constrains the possibilities. We may become confident that gold crossed the Sahara through a network of towns including Tadmekka, while remaining unable to assign a known coin to one workshop. That is a meaningful historical achievement.

Imagine a second inference. At Jenné-Jeno, excavators find a long history of ironworking, imported materials, and dense settlement. Medieval travelers later praise commercial towns in the Niger basin. The evidence supports a developing regional economy extending across many centuries, but it does not license the claim that one continuous government administered every settlement in exactly the same way. Similarity in economic function is not identity in political organization.

A third inference concerns Timbuktu. Surviving manuscripts show a scholarly network; excavated beads and ceramics help document consumption and craft; architectural history points to sustained religious and civic investment. Their dates overlap in some cases and diverge in others. A careful historian can describe the city as a center of exchange and learning while admitting that the physical evidence for its earliest phases is limited. That is more informative than pretending the city has been completely excavated.

## The economy beneath the legend

How do we know that medieval West Africa was interconnected? We have dated settlements and archaeological distributions, specialized production equipment, imported and exchanged materials, written accounts by authors with identifiable circumstances, and manuscript traditions preserved by African communities. None supplies a complete ledger. Their conjunction supports a history of regional production and long-distance exchange whose depth extends beyond any single ruler.

The people who made that history include farmers cultivating floodplains, fishers and metalworkers, caravan organizers, miners and gold processors, copyists and teachers, and the many residents of towns whose names no manuscript records. Modern research has its own workers and contributors: the excavation teams of Roderick and Susan McIntosh, Sam Nixon and colleagues, Thilo Rehren and Maria Filomena Guerra, Timothy Insoll and his collaborators, and Malian custodians and scholars who preserve written heritage. Credit for reconstructing the past belongs to those different activities, not to one famous explorer or a single heroic discovery.

There is a larger lesson in the comparison. A story about Mansa Musa can teach us how a ruler displayed wealth to the world. Archaeology asks how people produced, moved, measured, and transformed goods in the centuries around him. When the narrative and the ground disagree, we should find out why. When they converge, we should say what the agreement demonstrates. That method gives us a picture of Mali and the wider Sahel that is richer than the gold held in the hand of a king on a medieval map.

### Evidentiary boundaries

- **Jenné-Jeno:** Susan Keech McIntosh and Roderick J. McIntosh published on urban settlement from the third century BCE through the late medieval period. This predates the medieval Mali Empire; do not use it as a straightforward excavation of Mansa Musa's court.
- **Essouk-Tadmekka:** Sam Nixon's 2005 excavations, 2009 report; Nixon, Thilo Rehren, Maria Filomena Guerra (2011) on gold working; Rehren and Nixon (2014) on crucibles and glass-based refining. Subsequent numismatic analysis distinguishes unmarked gold blanks from inscribed coinage.
- **Timbuktu:** Timothy Insoll's surveys and trial excavation largely illuminate later archaeological phases. Manuscript dating and inscription evidence must be assigned to their actual production and circulation periods.
- **Written sources:** al-Bakri is an eleventh-century compiler; al-Umari used informants about the fourteenth-century pilgrimage; Ibn Battuta's travel narrative has its own mediation; al-Sa'di and Leo Africanus are later voices. None is a statistical government report of the gold trade.
- **Modern custody:** Timbuktu collections are kept by libraries and families; Malian custodians and multi-person preservation networks are original contributors to what survives.

### Editorial cautions

- Do not describe gold blanks as certain state-issued coins or a royal mint.
- Do not treat a later-period Timbuktu artifact as proof of fourteenth-century activity.
- Do not attach Jenné-Jeno's early city to one named Mali emperor.
- Do not infer precise quantities of trans-Saharan gold from a handful of crucibles or medieval prestige reports.
