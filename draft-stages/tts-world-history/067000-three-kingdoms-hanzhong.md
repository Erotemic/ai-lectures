# Three Kingdoms, Part X: Hanzhong and the Rivalry Over Jing

Liu Bei has conquered Yi Province and at last commands a region that could support an enduring state. Cao Cao has concentrated power in the north and operates through the Han emperor. Sun Quan controls the lower Yangtze and has no intention of becoming a subordinate to either rival. The three-cornered political pattern envisioned by Zhuge Liang is taking shape. But the lines between these emerging powers are not settled, and the strongest arguments for holding territory can conflict with the commitments that made a coalition possible. The mountain routes of Hanzhong and the river corridor of Jing now become matters on which entire regimes may depend.

The historical-romance novel *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* tells these campaigns through duels, clever letters, diplomatic banquets, and the careers of celebrated officers. Its account combines real events with literary embellishment. We will follow the dispute over Jing, the fierce fighting near Hefei, and the contest for Hanzhong. The connecting question is what happens when a successful alliance must define exactly what each partner is owed.

## A banquet across a disputed river

Sun Quan's envoys demand that Liu Bei's side return territory in Jing. The disagreement has been postponed through a sequence of arrangements and excuses, but Liu Bei's conquest of Yi makes delay harder to justify. Guan Yu holds an important position in Jing, guarding territory whose loss could sever the strategic connection between the western state and the approaches to the north. Sun Quan's side believes that a promise of eventual return should now be fulfilled. Liu Bei's advisers believe the territory remains essential to the larger restoration project.

The novel dramatizes the dispute through a banquet attended by Guan Yu. Lu Su, negotiating on Sun Quan's behalf, hopes to use personal conversation and the setting of a meeting to force a settlement. Guan Yu arrives with a small entourage and carries himself with the confidence of a man who expects his reputation to protect him. Danger is implied in every courtesy. A feast can be an occasion for good faith or the cover for arrest, and the participants cannot entirely know which until it is too late.

Guan Yu uses presence and controlled menace to prevent his hosts from overwhelming him. He departs without accepting the terms Sun Quan seeks. In the novel the moment becomes another demonstration of his heroic composure. Historically, Lu Su's role in negotiating Jing's distribution deserves more independent attention, and some anecdotes give a different balance of agency. The novel's preference for Guan Yu directs the listener's admiration toward the warrior, but the political question remains unresolved. A successful escape from a difficult meeting is not the same as a successful treaty.

## The north changes the scale of its claims

Cao Cao meanwhile continues expanding and consolidating the power of his house. His official honors grow, as does the distance between his formal status as servant of the Han and his practical authority. The novel describes the struggle of officials who see his rise as usurpation, sometimes culminating in executions. The emperor's household remains in danger precisely because its name is useful to the commander who increasingly behaves like a sovereign.

In the western north Cao Cao campaigns around Hanzhong, a region of strategic routes between the north and Sichuan basin. Zhang Lu has governed through a religious-political community with its own institutions. His submission or defeat gives Cao Cao access to territory that threatens Liu Bei's western base. A commander holding the mountain approaches could make a supposedly secure Yi Province vulnerable from above. The contest is therefore not simply about prestige. It concerns the entrances through which an army can reach the grain stores and cities on which Liu Bei depends.

The novel also gives episodes to extraordinary men such as the magician or recluse Zuo Ci, who humiliates Cao Cao through feats that seem to escape normal physical law. These scenes operate differently from the campaign narratives. Cao Cao can command officers, kill dissenters, and build an administration, yet an eccentric adept may laugh at the limitations of his power. The episodes are comic and unsettling. They remind readers that even a lord aspiring to control a realm cannot control every meaning people assign to his authority.

## The border that protects the heartland

Hanzhong lies in a region of mountains and river approaches north of the Sichuan basin. To hold it is to influence access to the west, and its mountain corridors can become points where a relatively small force delays a larger one. An army wishing to approach the basin from the north cannot simply march in a straight line toward Chengdu. It must move through terrain where supply lines narrow, where weather may change the usability of roads, and where a defender familiar with crossings and heights can make progress expensive.

Cao Cao's acquisition of Hanzhong creates a serious problem for Liu Bei. The western province he has just taken can no longer be treated as a sanctuary beyond the ordinary reach of the northern war. If Cao Cao uses Hanzhong as a base, Shu's future may be determined by the defense of its northern entrances. A campaign to capture the district is therefore not merely a display of ambition. It can be understood as an attempt to move the frontier away from the basin's most vulnerable approaches.

The commanders still must decide how much risk such security is worth. Mountains favor defense but also threaten invaders with exhaustion. Every advance requires food and coordination; every retreat exposes men on confined paths. A ruler who has recently expended substantial resources conquering Yi must weigh the benefits of a stronger frontier against the costs of another war before his new administration has settled.

The novel's military episodes can sound like a succession of individual stratagems, yet the strategic objective remains steady: turn geography into durable protection. Hanzhong cannot be valuable merely because a hero's banner has been raised over it. It is valuable if roads can be held, passes provisioned, officers stationed, and the people beyond it spared repeated invasion. The story of Liu Bei's rise has moved from finding any place to rest to organizing a layered defense for a state.

## Zhang Liao at Hefei

Sun Quan's forces confront Cao Cao's defenders at Hefei. In a famous episode, the Wei commander Zhang Liao leads a bold attack that breaks the confidence of a larger army. The success helps give his name enduring force in the southern imagination. The novel's details dramatize the effect of a commander who identifies an enemy's moment of vulnerability and acts before the larger force can respond. In a battle conducted near fortified positions and constrained approaches, local timing can undo numerical advantage.

The historical battle and Zhang Liao's reputation have a strong basis in the sources, though individual scenes and speeches reflect literary arrangement. His actions show that the commanders of Cao Cao's house cannot be reduced to anonymous agents of their lord's will. They have independent skill, followers, and reputations. One consequence of a durable military state is that it can produce officers capable of making decisions when the principal ruler is absent. Sun Quan must learn to respect the danger of the northern command even when Cao Cao himself is not before him.

The fighting also clarifies the position of Wu, the southern regime. Sun Quan cannot concentrate solely on disputes with Liu Bei. His northern frontier remains exposed. A campaign against Jing may create an opening for Cao Cao. A concession to Liu Bei may make the western claimant stronger at Wu's expense. Rulers operating between multiple threats cannot solve every problem by defeating one opponent. They must choose which risks to bear simultaneously and which bargains might last long enough to reduce the others.

## Hefei and the danger of assuming the south is safe

While Liu Bei's cause rises in the west, Sun Quan's government faces its own security problem along the lower Yangtze. The city of Hefei controls routes that connect the northern interior with the approaches to the southern regime. The struggle there demonstrates that a state capable of surviving Cao Cao at Red Cliffs can still be threatened by a smaller and well-led defending force. Rivers and naval skill provide advantages, but no frontier can be treated as permanently secure.

The novel gives Zhang Liao a remarkable reputation through the fighting near Hefei. His attacks disrupt expectations that Sun Quan's superior numbers will determine the result. The scene is especially revealing because the commander whose heroism is celebrated serves Cao Cao's northern regime. Even a novel that favors Liu Bei's legitimacy cannot ignore the discipline and courage of capable men under its antagonist. To deny such ability would make the subsequent wars less convincing.

Imagine an attacking camp whose leaders expected a relatively quick success. A sudden enemy assault reaches parts of the position before commanders can organize an orderly response. Rumors magnify the event; officers must establish who has survived and where reserve troops can still be concentrated. The attackers may continue to outnumber the defenders, yet the political goal of the expedition becomes less attainable because confidence has changed. This is the same relationship between perception and organization visible at many earlier battles, now seen from the southern side.

For Sun Quan, the lesson is not to abandon the struggle, but to recognize the difference between controlling a river and controlling every road to the river. The southern state needs defensive depth, experienced officers, and an ability to recover from defeat without fracturing. The novel's growing division into great regimes is not a division into safe and unsafe territories. Each political center remains vulnerable at the margins where an opponent has strong local command.

## Veteran skill against a difficult mountain

Liu Bei's forces prepare to contest Hanzhong. The novel gives important roles to Huang Zhong and Zhang Fei. Huang Zhong is an older commander whose martial reputation has survived the assumption that age must diminish effectiveness. His success depends partly on patience, judgment, and willingness to exploit terrain. Zhang Fei, notorious for temper and drinking, is increasingly capable of calculated tactics as well as ferocity. The cast has matured from the days when a victory might be represented by one champion riding into an open field.

The campaign is slow and exhausting. The mountains channel movement and make supply routes vulnerable. Cao Cao cannot simply bring the mass of his northern army through every pass at once. Liu Bei's troops must defend access points while preparing to strike at the right moment. The novel stages bold assaults and reversals, making individual commanders embody military functions: one holds a pass, another raids supply, another draws a rival into unfavorable ground. The names matter because each commander represents a specialized way of meeting the terrain's demands.

At Mount Dingjun, Huang Zhong defeats and kills Xiahou Yuan, one of Cao Cao's important relatives and commanders. The historical death was a major setback for Cao Cao's side. In the novel the victory is built into an account of disciplined timing by an older warrior, overturning the assumption that martial glory belongs only to youth. The episode also contrasts with the earlier deaths of Dian Wei or Pang Tong. A man can have many years of service and still be lost at the decisive point of a campaign. Commanders may be personally close to the ruler and still unable to escape the consequences of terrain and enemy action.

Cao Cao comes to support his army. The campaign becomes a question of whether a northern ruler should keep paying the price of an increasingly difficult position. The novel gives him a famous ambiguous remark about chicken ribs: something with a little value that is difficult to abandon but hardly worth possessing. A subordinate named Yang Xiu interprets the remark as a sign of impending retreat and acts on that interpretation. Cao Cao becomes angry at the presumption. The anecdote exposes the dangers of understanding a ruler too publicly. A clever courtier may infer what the lord intends but cannot safely announce it before the lord chooses to own the decision.

## Why a veteran's name can be an advantage

Huang Zhong gives the novel a way to challenge the assumption that greatness must belong to the young. His achievements near Hanzhong depend on courage, but the narrative makes his age visible so that each victory becomes a judgment against those who dismissed him. A veteran has seen situations in which a hasty attack failed and others in which delay sacrificed an opening. Experience does not guarantee correct choices, but it can sharpen a sense of timing that no title or boast can supply.

His rivalry with younger officers also reveals the social politics of command. Military organizations must reward physical endurance and immediate readiness, yet they cannot afford to disregard judgment accumulated over decades. The moment an older commander is assigned a difficult position, the appointing ruler expresses confidence that may strengthen an entire unit's morale. The honor of an individual becomes part of the force's collective expectations.

The celebrated battle at Mount Dingjun is therefore more than a contest between old and young warriors. It rewards a commander whose abilities were measured too narrowly by appearance. It also reminds the listener that Cao Cao's officers are not interchangeable obstacles. Xiahou Yuan's loss is a blow to a functioning northern command with its own relationships and experience. The triumph of one great veteran is the catastrophe of another army's defense.

## The mountain that measures patience

The Hanzhong campaigns bring Liu Bei's forces against experienced northern officers, among them Xiahou Yuan. The novel dramatizes the risks of commanders who respond too quickly to provocations or underestimate an adversary's preparations. In mountain warfare, the choice of position may be more important than the apparent valor of the soldiers assigned to it. A force on a height can observe movement and threaten an approach. A force trapped below may be exposed to attacks from more than one direction. Yet occupying high ground is not automatically sufficient; its relation to food, water, and roads determines whether it can be defended.

Huang Zhong's role brings the theme of age and reputation into the open. A veteran can be dismissed because his hair has whitened, but experience may permit an assessment of timing that a younger officer misses. The novel delights in scenes where an older warrior's determination defeats prejudice. His skill is not merely the memory of earlier strength. It includes judgment about when to expose a force and when to hold it in reserve.

The defeat and death of Xiahou Yuan at Mount Dingjun are major steps toward Liu Bei's control of Hanzhong. The historical battle and the commander's death are well established; particular speeches and feats in the novel have a different status. The distinction should not obscure a practical point: one commander lost at a critical sector can disorganize an army's local defenses and create opportunities for an enemy who is prepared to exploit them.

For Cao Cao, the campaign demands a decision about whether to continue contesting a region whose supply costs have become severe. A ruler may be tempted to preserve prestige by refusing withdrawal. He must decide whether the resources needed to defend Hanzhong would be better employed protecting the much larger northern base. The apparent surrender of a coveted frontier may be strategically rational if continued fighting threatens more valuable positions. Both sides, at different moments, must remember that possession and effective power are not synonyms.

## Intelligence and the politics of command

Yang Xiu's fate belongs to a wider strand in the novel's portrait of Cao Cao. The northern ruler values intelligence but is also sensitive to challenges to his authority. The ability to solve riddles, anticipate intentions, or notice contradictions in official language can make a man useful. It can also make him a rival in an arena where the ruler wishes to retain control over timing and public meaning. A court rewards sharpness until sharpness begins to expose what the ruler wanted concealed.

This tension is present in every successful political house. Advisers are employed because a ruler cannot personally know everything. Their influence depends on the ruler's willingness to hear uncomfortable knowledge. But if the adviser becomes publicly indispensable or seems to direct the ruler's decisions, others may accuse him of overstepping. The novel will later develop the relationship between advisers and rulers through Zhuge Liang's exceptional authority after Liu Bei's death. Here it shows Cao Cao trying to maintain the distinction between receiving advice and surrendering control.

Hanzhong finally becomes a victory for Liu Bei. Cao Cao withdraws, leaving Liu Bei with control of the strategically valuable region. The result strengthens the western state's independence and threatens Cao Cao's southern approaches. It also gives Liu Bei the confidence and political standing to adopt a new title, King of Hanzhong. His claim to continue the Han cause now has territory, armies, and ceremonial expression.


## Names, suspicions, and a bowl of chicken ribs

The novel's chicken-rib incident condenses uncertainty into an enigmatic phrase. Cao Cao issues or repeats a peculiar expression, and Yang Xiu interprets it as a sign that his master wishes to withdraw from Hanzhong: the ribs have flavor but offer little meat. The inference makes a memorable story about excessive cleverness. Yang Xiu has understood the private meaning of an ambiguous signal, but he acts on that understanding in a political environment where a subordinate is not authorized to expose his ruler's intentions.

The episode is about more than a puzzle. Military commands must be clear enough for officers to carry them out, yet the decision to retreat can cause panic if it becomes public too early. An interpreter who announces what he believes the ruler truly wants may undermine the appearance of resolution that keeps the army functioning. On the other hand, a court in which accurate interpretation is punished can drive perceptive advisers into silence. A regime that depends on an individual's unquestioned prestige may struggle to distinguish disloyalty from information that makes the individual seem less decisive.

Yang Xiu's fate in the literary narrative is linked to his reputation for understanding too much and to the danger of being visibly clever around a suspicious superior. The historical reasons for his execution involve court and succession politics that cannot be reduced to a single pun. The novel chooses an incident that makes the problem immediately recognizable. A subordinate solves the riddle, and the solution itself becomes an offense because it reveals something the superior did not wish revealed.

The comparison with Zhuge Liang is suggestive. Both men are celebrated for intelligence. Zhuge Liang's renown grows because Liu Bei publicly commits to seeking and accepting his advice. Yang Xiu's insight threatens Cao Cao's control over what is to be known. The difference is not that one leader invariably welcomed reason while the other invariably hated it. Their households and narrative roles are different. But the paired stories let us examine how the political handling of expertise may determine whether cleverness becomes an asset or a danger.

## Why a mountain frontier does not end danger

Holding Hanzhong gives Liu Bei a stronger northern position, but mountainous ground is no permanent guarantee. A pass can be supplied in one season and isolated in another. An officer assigned to a remote garrison must judge whether to resist a siege, retreat toward supporting units, or send for help that may arrive too late. The terrain concentrates opportunity for defenders and the consequences of a mistake.

The government in Chengdu must maintain the region from behind. Grain must move up from the basin; roads need repair; troops require rotation; and commanders need clear instructions about what to defend if several approaches are threatened. The reader who hears only that Liu Bei has become King of Hanzhong may miss the new obligations created by the title. The king has acquired an expensive frontier and must demonstrate that its possession improves the security of the people who support him.

For Cao Cao, the northern withdrawal does not destroy the wider state. A ruler with control over the heart of the north can choose where to concentrate afterward. This explains why the western victory, though important, is not a completed defeat of Wei. The geography that protects Shu also limits how readily a western army can project force deep into the northern plains. The next generation will face the same problem during the northern expeditions.

## A frontier victory that cannot repair an alliance

Liu Bei's capture of Hanzhong is a watershed in his ascent. A man who once relied on the hospitality of others now holds western territory with a defensible northern frontier and can raise himself to a major political rank. Yet his new strength intensifies a problem that the campaign has not solved. The Sun house still disputes control of Jing, and Guan Yu occupies a sensitive position between the two regimes.

The western victory may encourage Liu Bei's supporters to regard the future as open. They can imagine advancing from Hanzhong into the north, striking at Cao Cao, and restoring their chosen form of Han legitimacy. The plan depends, however, on a secure eastern relationship. If the Sun kingdom feels excluded from the benefits of coalition victory, it has incentives to reconsider its position. An alliance that once looked necessary to both sides may become less stable as one partner succeeds.

Military geography therefore joins political psychology. Liu Bei's western defenses are stronger, but his territories remain separated from the contested middle river by difficult routes. Guan Yu must protect the eastern position while the regime's attention is drawn toward Hanzhong. Sun Quan and his advisers cannot be expected to ignore the opportunities created by that division. The next catastrophe is prepared not by one man's temper alone, but by a settlement in which both allies believe the same ground is indispensable to their security.


## The northern and southern fronts belong to one strategy

Guan Yu's position in Jing makes sense only when considered alongside Liu Bei's Hanzhong campaign. If the eastern commander threatens Cao Cao while western forces secure the mountain frontier, the northern regime must divide its attention. This resembles the basic design of Zhuge Liang's Longzhong plan: use separated approaches and an alliance with the south to constrain a much larger enemy.

But the strategy has an essential dependence on Sun Quan's goodwill or at least restraint. Without that condition, Guan Yu's eastern operations invite danger from the very region once regarded as friendly. The western and eastern fronts may succeed individually while the realm fails collectively because its political support system breaks. The difficulty is especially sharp when commanders far from one another act on imperfect news.

The novel admires the rising power of Shu as Liu Bei achieves a kingly title. Its larger plot shows why success can create a new hazard. The stronger Liu Bei appears, the more his former ally may fear that the unfinished division of Jing will become permanent. The listener now understands the pressure under which the next major commander operates: the heroic offensive to the north proceeds alongside a diplomatic relationship that has become a threat to the rear.

## The courtiers, the claimant, and the title of king

After years of wandering, Liu Bei begins to look like the ruler of an enduring territory. His advisers urge him toward the title King of Hanzhong. The designation invites immediate comparison with the founding of the Han dynasty, because Liu Bang was once King of Han before he became emperor. A title can therefore contain an argument about history. Liu Bei's supporters may see his new rank as a step toward restoring the legitimacy of his ancestry. Opponents may regard the same act as the promotion of a regional military strongman who does not possess an uncontested appointment from the reigning emperor.

The distinction matters because the Han court still exists under Cao Cao's control. Liu Bei cannot simply claim to be defending the dynasty without explaining how his own kingly authority relates to the emperor in the north. The novel is sympathetic to his position and depicts the title as recognition of a cause that has survived repeated loss. A historical account recognizes a broader political competition in which each regime had reasons to characterize its own powers as legitimate and its rivals' claims as disorderly.

Rank also changes relations inside Liu Bei's government. Senior companions have accompanied him through hardship; western officers have joined more recently; administrators possess different kinds of expertise from battlefield commanders. Appointments create hierarchies that may reward old service but also alienate capable newcomers. The scene in which great men receive titles is therefore more than ceremonial splendor. It is an effort to distribute authority in a coalition that has grown too large to be held together solely by memories of a common journey.

Guan Yu's relationship to these promotions exposes the difficulty of turning personal prestige into an administrative order. A celebrated commander may judge a rank not by its formal duties but by whether it places him beside a person he considers his inferior. The ruler needs him to accept that a state contains multiple kinds of merit. A man famous for one form of excellence may feel diminished by the recognition of another. This household of sworn brothers is being asked to live within the rules of a territorial government, and the transition will be painful.

## A king without an emperor's decree

Liu Bei's elevation raises an obvious question. If he claims loyalty to the Han, by what authority may he assume kingship while Emperor Xian remains on the throne under Cao Cao's watch? The novel frames the act through petitions and counsel from his followers, who see it as necessary to match the titles and resources of competing powers. The formal language tries to keep the assumption of power within a framework of Han restoration. Yet the political logic is unmistakable: Liu Bei needs to be recognized as a ruler of the territory he commands.

The difference between claim and capacity has narrowed. Years earlier Liu Bei could say he would save the dynasty but lacked a province. Now he governs Yi and Hanzhong, appoints officers, and supports a large army. His political project has become concrete enough to compete. The cost is that he has moved closer to the practices of other powerful lords. He adopts honors, negotiates over disputed land, and prepares military advances that may leave ordinary people exposed to war.

Sun Quan's situation remains tense. Jing is still disputed; Guan Yu remains its formidable defender. Cao Cao has lost Hanzhong but retains vast resources. The arrangement anticipated by Zhuge Liang is more fully realized than at any earlier point, yet it rests on fragile cooperation between Liu Bei and Sun Quan. The novel is approaching the most damaging break in that relationship. A commander whose personal honor made him famous will find himself holding a region so strategically important that no amount of individual courage can guarantee its security.

The political lesson of these campaigns is that success deepens obligations. Every conquered province requires administration, every new title invites comparison with established claims, and every treaty creates expectations of future conduct. The three houses now have enough power to threaten one another seriously. Their leaders can no longer behave like wandering heroes who leave a ruined place and begin again elsewhere. They have populations that depend on their decisions. The destiny of Jing will reveal how hard it is to keep personal reputation, strategic necessity, and the terms of an alliance aligned.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 66–72. Treat the banquet between Lu Su and Guan Yu as an episode whose viewpoint differs between literary and historical texts. Zhang Liao's defense of Hefei and Huang Zhong's victory over Xiahou Yuan have historical foundations. Zuo Ci and the chicken-rib anecdote should be treated with their textual status made clear; the former is a supernatural literary strand.
