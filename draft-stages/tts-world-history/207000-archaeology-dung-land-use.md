# Dung, Dust, and Pastoral Landscapes: How Archaeologists Find People Who Moved

An archaeologist walking across a dry East African landscape may see a scattering of potsherds and stone, a low rise, and very little that resembles a house. Imagine that this place was once a herding settlement. Families brought cattle, sheep, and goats into enclosures at night. People cooked, repaired tools, traded stories, and then moved on. The shelters vanished. Organic refuse decayed. Wind and rain altered the surface. After centuries, what should an excavator look for?

One answer lies in material most excavators once hoped to clean away: dung. Animal waste can decay until no recognizable pellet survives, while leaving microscopic minerals, plant fragments, and chemical signatures in soil. With careful sampling, such traces can reveal where animals gathered, what they were eating, whether dung was burned as fuel, and how living spaces were arranged. They can sometimes supply evidence for pastoral life where visible buildings are absent.

To appreciate why this works, we need to think like an archaeologist and a soil scientist at the same time. Soil is not simply dirt covering artifacts. It is an archive made by plants, animals, people, water, fire, and later organisms. The problem is to identify which processes generated a particular deposit, and whether several independent measurements tell the same story.

We will begin with the chemistry of a cow's digestive tract, move to field investigations of Maasai livestock enclosures, and work through how microscopic deposits can be distinguished from ordinary soil and ash. Then we will consider what those measurements say about grazing, crops, fuel, and ancient landscape change. The researchers will be part of the explanation because the method was built by comparing known modern deposits with archaeological deposits whose histories were hidden.

## An invisible signature inside a visible pile

Dung begins as food processed by an animal. A grazing cow, goat, or sheep swallows plants that contain hard silica particles, soluble minerals, and various organic compounds. Digestion changes the mixture. Most recognizable plant tissue eventually decays after the waste is deposited. But some inorganic particles are resistant enough to survive for long periods, even after the original pellet disintegrates.

One important class is phytoliths: microscopic silica structures formed within plant tissues. Different plant groups tend to produce characteristic shapes, although most individual forms are not exclusive to one species. Grasses often leave distinctive phytolith assemblages. They can enter a deposit through animal dung, thatch, straw bedding, burned fuel, or naturally accumulated vegetation. A phytolith-rich soil layer is therefore suggestive but ambiguous without context.

Another class of particles is especially informative. Under a microscope, some herbivore dung contains tiny round grains of calcium carbonate called fecal spherulites. They may measure only a few millionths of a meter across. M. G. Canti's 1997 and 1998 studies investigated their composition and optical properties, helping researchers distinguish them from superficially similar particles produced by plants or other natural processes.

The spherulites have an ordered internal structure that responds predictably to polarized light. In a thin section of sediment, a skilled analyst can see the pattern and compare it to reference material. The particles are common in the dung of some ruminants, including sheep and cattle, but their frequency varies with species, forage, and conditions. Their absence cannot establish that no livestock were present, especially where dissolution or burning has changed the deposits.

You can hear why this matters if you imagine looking at a gray patch of soil. Its color may give almost no hint of its origin. Under magnification, however, it could contain spherulites, abundant grass phytoliths, compacted laminations, and the products of decomposed organic matter. Those features together may support the interpretation that animals were repeatedly penned there.

## What archaeology borrows from living knowledge

How do researchers know what an ancient animal enclosure should look like after its dung has decayed? They can study modern or recently abandoned enclosures whose histories are known, record how those deposits change, and use the observations to build testable expectations.

Ruth Shahack-Gross, Fiona Marshall, and Steve Weiner published a 2003 study of abandoned Maasai settlements in Kenya. They compared sediments inside livestock enclosures with sediments from adjacent areas, using mineralogical and microscopic methods. This was geo-ethnoarchaeology: combining archaeological questions with geological analysis and careful study of places whose recent human use can be reconstructed.

The modern settlements in that study were not anonymous laboratory models. Researchers relied on local Maasai knowledge, including information about the ages and uses of abandoned enclosures. That local account made it possible to connect sediment characteristics with observed activities and with time since abandonment. In subsequent publications, Shahack-Gross, Marshall, Kathleen Ryan, and Weiner examined spatial organization and the implications for older pastoral sites in East Africa.

A Maasai cattle enclosure is a useful reference because cattle deposit a great deal of waste in a concentrated space. Over time, thick organic layers may develop. When abandonment ends the daily input, microbes, water, and weather begin to transform the deposit. Some original organic matter disappears, and mineral signatures become relatively more important. The study of sites abandoned for different lengths of time helps researchers understand what survives.

But a modern reference is not an instruction to label every ancient deposit 'Maasai.' Different communities organize livestock differently. Ancient diets and rainfall patterns may have differed from those in recent settlements. The value of the experiment is a physical relationship: repeated animal penning can produce certain sediment properties, and we can test whether those properties survive weathering. Archaeologists then evaluate the ancient site on its own terms.

## Reading a floor as a sequence of activities

Imagine a small block of intact sediment cut from an archaeological floor. Instead of scattering it into a bag, the excavator consolidates the block, brings it to a laboratory, impregnates it with resin, and cuts an extremely thin slice that light can pass through. This is soil micromorphology. It preserves relationships among particles and microscopic layers that would be lost if the soil were simply washed through a sieve.

Under the microscope, the researcher can ask whether a layer was trampled flat, accumulated slowly, burned, or redeposited by flowing water. A thin horizon of dung mixed with charcoal may indicate cleaning and burning. A compacted surface beneath it may have been walked on repeatedly. Fine deposits that fill cracks could have arrived after abandonment. One physical section contains a small history of formation events.

A key concept is formation process. A deposit can be created by the activity researchers want to understand, and then modified by many later activities. Rain can wash dung particles downhill. Burrowing animals disturb stratigraphy. Soil chemistry can dissolve minerals. Fire destroys some substances and creates others. If we mistake the later alteration for the original event, the historical reconstruction becomes unreliable.

For example, suppose a floor contains charcoal and spherulites. One explanation is that dry livestock dung was collected and burned for fuel. Another is that a fire burned across an enclosure floor. A third is that ash from a dung fire was discarded into the floor deposit from elsewhere. The mineral particles establish something about the materials present, while the distribution and layering help distinguish how they arrived.

The same reasoning applies to buildings. A room with concentrated dung signatures could have housed animals, or it could have received dung used as a floor additive or fuel waste. The architecture, access routes, repeated resurfacing, associated artifacts, and spatial pattern across the site can favor one explanation. A single chemical reading rarely decides the matter.

## Testing a proposed cattle enclosure

Let's build a worked example. Archaeologists excavate a circular feature on a dry ridge. They find compacted soil, a few postholes, and little else. Someone proposes that cattle were kept inside. How could this claim be tested?

First, map the feature and take samples from several parts of it: the center, the edge, any entrance, and the surroundings. A comparison sample from apparently undisturbed soil is indispensable. If the same mineral signal occurs everywhere, it may reflect regional geology rather than animal use. If it is concentrated inside and fades outside, that pattern calls for an explanation.

Second, inspect thin sections. Are there repeated thin layers with compacted material and organic-rich residues? Are there spherulites whose optical properties match those of known herbivore dung? Are phytoliths present in associations consistent with grasses? Do the layers suggest steady accumulation or a single dumping event?

Third, consider chemistry and mineralogy. High concentrations of phosphate may arise in places where waste and organic matter accumulated, but phosphate is also present in other kinds of refuse. Chemical measurements should be interpreted with microscopic context. Some research has used carbon and nitrogen isotope patterns in sediments to investigate livestock waste, again requiring comparisons with known environmental backgrounds.

Fourth, date the relevant layers. If a piece of charcoal lies in the floor, ask whether it comes from the animal-use phase or from an older fire. If dung-derived organic matter is dated, ask whether the sample could include later roots or mixed materials. A date applies to the sample analyzed; assigning it to the construction or abandonment of the feature requires a stratigraphic argument.

Finally, compare this candidate enclosure with the wider settlement. Where did people cook? Where were animals watered? Is there a path to grazing ground? Do pottery and lithic distributions support habitation nearby? An enclosure is part of a landscape of movement, not an isolated ring on a map.

This example shows how a dung hypothesis gains support: not by producing a single impressive micrograph, but by generating several mutually consistent expectations that competitors must also explain.

## Animals, crops, and the same misleading particle

A phytolith may have passed through a goat's gut or fallen from a roof made of grass. It might enter the soil as manure spread on a field. It might be released when straw is burned as fuel. The identification of a plant particle therefore does not, by itself, establish how people used the plant.

Researchers investigate these possibilities by looking at combinations of plant remains and where they occur. Charred grain in a storage pit suggests a different activity from scattered stalk phytoliths in a stable floor. A concentration of crop-processing debris near a threshing area may reveal the separation of edible grain from chaff. At an ancient field, manure particles may reveal fertilization practices, particularly when paired with archaeological ridges, irrigation traces, and crop remains.

The circularity trap is worth hearing. Suppose an archaeologist knows a site had sheep because there are sheep bones. A phytolith-rich layer is then called sheep dung. In a later publication, that supposed dung is cited as independent proof of sheep herding. The evidence has merely been counted twice. A robust study tests whether the sediment actually has diagnostic features of dung and explains how the bones, deposits, and dates relate.

Animal species can also complicate interpretation. Canti's comparison showed that spherulite abundance varies across dung types. The particles may be destroyed or dissolved under some soil conditions, and different feeding strategies change plant microfossil assemblages. Even an apparently reliable ruminant signal usually identifies a class of material better than a precise herd composition.

To reconstruct species, researchers also examine bones, teeth, footprints, dung macroremains if preserved, and sometimes DNA. Each technique helps answer part of the question. Herd composition, number of animals, season of occupation, and social management require more information than evidence of dung alone.

## Fuel: an overlooked use of waste

Dung was and is used for more than disposal. In landscapes where wood is limited or valuable, dried dung can serve as fuel for cooking and crafts. It has also been incorporated into building materials and used to enrich fields. These practices influence where dung particles end up.

A hearth rich in phytoliths might have burned grass, straw, or dung. Burning can change mineral particles, alter carbonate structures, and leave ash pseudomorphs—mineral traces of material consumed by the fire. Researchers combine fuel-residue studies with experimentally burned reference samples and ethnographic observations to ask what the original fuel was.

Dung fuel also changes a settlement's demand for wood. If people relied partly on dried animal waste, the pattern of charcoal in their hearths could underrepresent their total fuel consumption. Conversely, an abundance of charcoal might record a special craft event rather than routine cooking. Environmental history becomes more accurate when archaeologists investigate the full range of combustible materials.

The same material moves through several human systems. Plants feed animals. Animals produce milk, meat, hides, labor, and waste. Waste becomes fuel or fertilizer. Fuel fires create ash that is discarded or worked into floors. Centuries later, each stage may leave related microscopic traces, but distinguishing the stages depends on their context.

A productive archaeological question is therefore not simply 'is there dung here?' It is 'what was dung doing here, and what changes did it undergo before we sampled it?'

## A dry valley, a wet valley, and what survives

Preservation varies across environments. In a dry settlement, microscopic particles can remain stable for long periods. In wet or acidic soils, carbonate spherulites may dissolve. Waterlogged sites might preserve recognizable plant material while transforming other minerals. Repeated burning changes the residues further. Two communities practicing similar herding might therefore leave different archaeological records.

This makes negative evidence especially difficult. If an East African site lacks the signature measured in an arid reference enclosure, we need to ask whether local weathering could have removed it. If a deposit is rich in dung particles, we need to ask whether water concentrated them from a wider catchment.

Site sampling matters as well. Archaeologists typically excavate a small part of a settlement, and microscopic analysis is expensive. A pit sampled from one end of a site may miss the area where livestock were kept. A published absence may mean only that no dung traces were found in the sampled contexts, using the methods applied at that time.

Studies of recent sites help identify those limits. Shahack-Gross and colleagues were able to follow how materials in known livestock areas changed after abandonment. The method benefits further from reference collections of modern plant tissues, soils, animal waste, and experimentally burned fuels. The quality of an ancient inference depends heavily on the range and relevance of these comparisons.

## Soil chemistry without magic fingerprints

Microscopic particles are not the only evidence available. Researchers also study organic molecules that survive after feces decay. Certain sterols and their breakdown products, together with bile acids, can help distinguish human and herbivore waste. The relative proportions are important because no single compound necessarily belongs to one species or one activity.

This is the same kind of inference used in ancient food-residue studies, adapted to sediments. A molecule is extracted, separated and measured. Researchers compare it with reference mixtures and controls. Then they evaluate whether the depositional environment could have altered it. A chemical resemblance becomes a historical argument only when the sampling and comparison are explained.

An advantage of multiple methods is that their biases may differ. A carbonate spherulite might dissolve under certain conditions, while a diagnostic organic residue survives. Conversely, degradation could remove organic compounds but leave silica phytoliths. If several proxies converge on a dung-rich interpretation, confidence can improve. If they disagree, that is information about preservation, mixing, or the original activities.

The strength of a measurement should also match the question. If scientists find chemical traces consistent with herbivore waste, we can investigate whether animals were present. We cannot infer from chemistry alone who owned those animals, how authority was organized, or whether a community was permanently settled or seasonally mobile.

## Pastoral history is more than a search for buildings

Why devote an entire lecture to ancient dung? Because conventional excavation can favor communities that built durable structures, wrote records, or buried objects that museums prize. Mobile pastoral communities often organized their lives differently. Their historical significance is not proportional to the number of stone walls they left.

Evidence for cattle enclosures, temporary camps, and the changing use of grassland helps recover histories of movement and land management. It allows researchers to investigate pastoral economies, relations among herders and farmers, and the ecological knowledge necessary to sustain livestock in variable environments.

In East Africa, the earliest food-producing communities included mobile herders. Fiona Marshall and other researchers have emphasized the importance of studying pastoralism on its own terms rather than assuming a farming village is the standard form of productive life. The geo-ethnoarchaeological work with Maasai settlements supplied techniques for finding activities that earlier excavation strategies could miss.

Of course, modern Maasai settlements cannot be projected directly onto communities that lived thousands of years earlier. Technologies, herd management, political institutions, and climate changed. The legitimate comparison is more restricted and more powerful: certain repeated actions have observable physical consequences, and studying those consequences in a context of known history helps us recognize them where texts are absent.

## A landscape can preserve several histories at once

Consider an archaeological area with a series of ancient enclosures, small agricultural terraces, a streambed, and a scatter of pottery. One researcher studies sediment chemistry. Another maps field surfaces and erosion. A third identifies animal bones. A fourth dates charcoal and plant remains. Community members contribute knowledge of water, herding routes, and the history of land use. The combined picture may reveal alternating periods of livestock keeping and cultivation, or several activities practiced together.

The soil does not automatically assign each layer to a named group. A landscape can be shared, reused, and transformed by successive communities. People may move through it in seasons rather than generations. A field can be fertilized with waste from animals kept elsewhere. A pasture might later be plowed, mixing earlier layers.

This is why mapping, sediment coring, microscopic sampling, and excavation records must connect. A single soil sample gives a local observation. A spatially distributed set of dated samples can support a reconstruction of changes through time. A regional synthesis adds the histories of other sites and environmental evidence such as pollen or lake sediments.

Even then, climatic variation alone cannot explain a social transformation. Drought may change the availability of pasture or water, while political conflict, markets, disease, and collective choices shape how communities respond. Archaeological residues can illuminate parts of that interaction; they should not be asked to serve as an entire history of a people.

## The record beneath our feet

We began on a dry hillside where few traces of buildings survived. Research on known livestock enclosures showed that repeated penning can leave a recognizable assemblage of microscopic minerals, plant particles, and compacted layers. Canti's studies helped identify fecal spherulites. Shahack-Gross, Marshall, Weiner, Ryan, and their collaborators connected these measurements to the formation and later alteration of pastoral deposits.

We learned to test the interpretation with spatial controls, microscopy, dating, and alternative explanations. We also learned how fuel use, crop processing, manure, weathering, and later human activity can create similar patterns or alter the expected signature. Each process leaves a partial record. Together, those records can recover histories that a survey focused on monuments would miss.

An enclosure may have vanished, but the work of keeping animals there changed the ground. Archaeologists who know how to read those changes can make a strong case for ancient pastoral activity while remaining careful about the social lives and identities that the sediments cannot directly reveal.


## Can we recover a season of occupation?

Seasonality is one of the attractive possibilities of pastoral archaeology. A camp occupied only during wet months may have played a different economic role from a base used throughout the year. Dung deposits can contribute to this question because the kinds of plants eaten by animals vary with local vegetation and season. But the path from a phytolith to a calendar month is long.

Researchers first need a reference study of the local vegetation: which grasses mature when, how their microscopic structures vary, and how long animals may retain evidence of a plant in their digestive systems. Next they need to show that the archaeological deposit belongs to a restricted occupation episode rather than decades of repeated use. If the dung was swept into a heap over many visits, seasonal signals may be mixed. Other evidence—young animal teeth, plant seeds, water availability, or repeated hearth surfaces—can sometimes narrow the interpretation.

Imagine finding fresh-looking dung pellets in a cave. Their preservation may be striking, but the pellets' apparent freshness is not an age measurement. A dry environment can protect material for centuries. A reliable seasonality claim rests on contextual dating and the seasonal significance of the inclusions, not on how familiar the pellets look.

The same care applies to reconstructing grazing pressure. A thick concentration of dung may indicate that animals gathered repeatedly at one point. It does not by itself tell us how many animals ranged over the surrounding hills. To understand landscape impacts, investigators need regional vegetation records, erosion evidence, settlement distribution, and, where available, records of fire or changing rainfall.

## When the animals leave, the people remain in the chemistry

Livestock are one way humans change soils, but many activities contribute to a settlement's chemical pattern. Ash from cooking raises some mineral concentrations. Food preparation adds plant remains and fats. Human waste contributes a different mixture of organic compounds. Cleaning redistributes all of these materials. A former household may therefore show overlapping chemical zones even if its walls have vanished.

An analytical strategy can start with grids of systematically sampled soil, measuring phosphate and other elements, and then select representative points for microscopic study. If a phosphate-rich area also contains layers of animal dung and compacted flooring, it may have been an enclosure. If it contains food refuse, hearth traces, and human fecal biomarkers, a different explanation becomes plausible. Measurements acquire meaning through spatial pattern and controlled comparison.

Modern analytical techniques can detect very small residues, but increased sensitivity brings contamination concerns. Fertilizer applied to the land centuries after abandonment, modern grazing animals, or recent animal burrows may introduce the same compounds being sought. Investigators need to date layers, inspect disturbance, and sample modern background soils. A convincing land-use reconstruction is the one that survives those checks.
