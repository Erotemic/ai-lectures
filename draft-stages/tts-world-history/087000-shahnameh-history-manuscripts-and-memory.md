# Shahnameh: How the Book of Kings Survived

A poet in northeastern Iran sets out to turn the remembered history of kings into verse. He works for years. Around him, dynasties rise and compete, patrons die, language and courtly culture change, and stories older than his own lifetime circulate in written and spoken forms. He will never meet most of the people who eventually hear his work. Yet more than a thousand years later, readers recognize the names of Rostam, Sohrab, Siyavash, Zahhak, and Kaveh. The poem has survived far longer than the political order whose fall it recounts. How do we know what the poet wrote, where his stories came from, and which parts of the vast narrative have connections to a verifiable past?

The poet is Abolqasem Ferdowsi, and the work is the *Shahnameh*, the Book of Kings, completed around 1010. It is often estimated at roughly fifty thousand rhymed couplets, though the total varies with manuscripts and editions. The poem begins with the first legendary rulers and ends with the fall of the Sasanian dynasty in the seventh century. It is written in New Persian, in a literary world shaped by centuries of Islamic rule and by the renewed prominence of Persian as a language of poetry and administration. Its scope is immense, but it is not an encyclopedia containing every fact about Iran. It is a constructed literary history. What it includes, how much space it gives an episode, and what voices it uses are choices inherited from sources and shaped by the poet.

The right way to investigate the epic is to separate several questions that are sometimes collapsed. We can ask about the poet's life; about earlier royal narratives; about the text that circulated after his death; about the depiction of historical rulers; and about the way performers, patrons, artists, and translators have changed the experience of the work. These questions need different evidence. A dated manuscript is excellent evidence that a version of the poem existed at the time it was copied. It is not proof that a dragon described within it existed. A Sasanian inscription can help establish the actions of a historical king. It cannot tell us what an eleventh-century poet intended by a scene of family reconciliation.

## A poet and a political world

Ferdowsi lived in the eastern Iranian cultural world around the late tenth and early eleventh centuries, at a moment when Persian literary culture was flourishing under dynasties whose authority and language had complex relationships to earlier Iranian history and to Islamic political institutions. The poem is conventionally associated with Tus in Khorasan. We know far more about the broad cultural environment than about many details of the poet's private life. Later stories about his relations with royal patrons may be vivid and memorable without furnishing contemporary eyewitness testimony for each encounter.

Why would someone devote decades to the history of kings whose kingdoms were gone? One answer lies in language. Writing a vast narrative in Persian made a powerful claim about the capacity of the language to carry moral argument, heroic memory, and complex historical inheritance. Another answer lies in political imagination. The poem provided a repertoire of examples that later readers could use to praise, question, or warn rulers. A king who hears what happened to Jamshid or Goshtasp does not receive a simple instruction, but he encounters an image of royal failure that can remain in memory longer than a formal lecture on conduct.

The poet's use of inherited traditions was active work. A long epic is not produced merely by copying a list of earlier names. Voices must be shaped, related episodes arranged, transitions built, and the moral consequences of one decision carried forward into later scenes. The loss of a father must still be felt when the son becomes king. A bird who once protected an abandoned infant must return when that infant's grown child faces disaster. These are literary achievements whether or not every episode has an earlier analogue.

The familiar stories of Ferdowsi's poverty, disappointment, or quarrel with a patron deserve sympathetic treatment and documentary caution. They tell us how later generations imagined the dignity of a great poet in relation to political power. To reconstruct exactly what was promised, paid, or refused, we need witnesses closer to the events. The poem itself is the most substantial surviving evidence for the scale and character of Ferdowsi's accomplishment, and not every later anecdote about its creation has equal authority.

## What we know about Ferdowsi

Ferdowsi is associated with Tus in the region of Khorasan, in northeastern Iran. Scholars place his life roughly in the late tenth and early eleventh centuries. He belonged to a world in which local landed families, often described as *dehqans*, maintained traditions of Iranian history and aristocratic culture. Later biographies tell colorful stories about his relations with Sultan Mahmud of Ghazna, about courtly rewards withheld or insultingly delivered, and about the poet's final years. Some elements are famous; not all can be established from evidence close to his lifetime.

The most valuable starting point for the poet's own circumstances is what can be responsibly inferred from passages in the poem, studied with attention to manuscript transmission and possible later additions. He refers to his labor, to the passage of time, to patrons or supporters, and to losses within his life. Those passages give an intimate voice to a work otherwise concerned with kings who lived long before him. But the presence of an autobiographical-sounding passage in a later manuscript is not an automatic certificate that every detail came unchanged from Ferdowsi's hand. A textual critic has to examine its witnesses and placement.

The story of a poet cheated of his reward is emotionally satisfying, especially in a poem that often condemns ungrateful kings. It may also be a later narrative shaped to fit the poet's reputation. We can discuss the legend without presenting it as a documented contract dispute. The broader political setting is secure: Ferdowsi's work took shape as power shifted in the eastern Iranian world, and its completion is associated with the Ghaznavid period. Its language and cultural purpose cannot be understood apart from that environment.

## The archive that no longer exists as one book

The name *Khwaday-namag*, or Book of Lords or Kings, refers to a body of Middle Persian royal historical tradition that preceded Ferdowsi. It is convenient to picture a single manuscript on a desk, passed without interruption into a Persian verse epic. That picture is misleading. The underlying courtly narratives circulated and were adapted; the Middle Persian historical compilations are not preserved as a single complete work that we can set beside Ferdowsi's poem line by line. Their existence and influence are reconstructed through related texts, later quotations, and the organization of subsequent Persian and Arabic accounts.

The tenth-century Persian prose *Shahnameh* associated with Abu Mansur is another major part of that reconstruction. Its introductory material survives, but the entire prose compilation does not. It helps us understand the collective and court-supported effort to gather Iranian royal history before Ferdowsi's poetic version. The poet inherited a project already underway. He made a new work of art from traditions that other people had been collecting, arranging, and transmitting.

Daqiqi's contribution is a particularly tangible case of inheritance. Ferdowsi incorporates a stretch of verse associated with the earlier poet in the Goshtasp and Zoroaster material. We can therefore see, within the great poem itself, a moment where one poet acknowledges the work of another and takes responsibility for continuing it. This is a better guide to the epic's composition than a romantic image of a solitary author inventing every episode from nothing. The result remains Ferdowsi's vast artistic architecture while openly including an earlier voice.

The layers do not authorize indiscriminate claims of antiquity. A character may have an ancient Iranian name; that does not establish that every episode involving the character is equally old. The tale may have been expanded, reorganized, or given different religious emphases over many centuries. For each question, we ask what the earliest relevant witnesses actually preserve. That method can confirm an older motif in one case and leave an exact line of transmission unresolved in another.

## The books behind the Book of Kings

Ferdowsi did not invent all the kings and heroes whose stories he tells. A substantial body of Iranian royal and heroic traditions existed before him. Middle Persian works, Arabic historical writing, early New Persian prose, and separate epic tales transmitted overlapping material. One significant precursor was a prose *Shahnameh* compiled under the patronage of Abu Mansur Muhammad ibn Abd al-Razzaq in the tenth century, associated with the year 957. The work itself is largely lost, although its preface survives. Scholars generally regard the tradition represented by this prose compilation as central to Ferdowsi's undertaking.

Behind these sources stands the wider genre often called the *Khwaday-namag*, the Book of Lords or Kings, associated with Sasanian royal history. It is tempting to imagine one pristine book written at the Sasanian court, handed through successive translators, and then turned into Ferdowsi's poem without change. The historical evidence is less tidy. Scholars have argued that related compilations and versions circulated, with different emphases and routes of transmission. Later historians could use materials drawing on royal traditions without copying a single identical lost archetype. When several medieval authors tell similar stories, the task is to reconstruct dependence and shared inheritance, rather than to declare that one lost book has been recovered by inference.

This matters in the great legendary episodes. The names of some early kings and heroes occur in much older religious traditions. The figure associated with Jamshid has connections with Yima; Kay Khosrow has an earlier Iranian counterpart; dragons, royal glory, ordeals, and divine birds occur in traditions that predate Ferdowsi's final poem. But continuity of a name does not mean every later episode was present from the beginning. A poet may inherit a figure and a few crucial actions, then give them new psychological depth and narrative surroundings. The history of a story is often a history of transformation.

The poet Daqiqi provides a visible case. He had begun composing an epic account in Persian verse before his death. Ferdowsi incorporated approximately a thousand couplets of his treatment of the reign of Goshtasp and the establishment of Zoroastrian belief. The inclusion is identified in the poem. It lets us see a predecessor's work embedded within the much larger text that became famous. When asking who authored a passage, then, the answer cannot simply be that every surviving verse was first composed by Ferdowsi. The poem itself acknowledges an earlier voice.

## Copyists are part of the story

The earliest surviving dated complete or near-complete manuscript witnesses come well after Ferdowsi's own lifetime. A manuscript dated 1217, commonly associated with Florence, is particularly important for the textual history. It is not the author's autograph. By the time it was copied, generations of performance and manuscript transmission could already have affected individual verses and the organization of sections. Later splendid manuscripts preserve the poem in forms shaped by their own patrons and scribes.

Imagine a copyist deciding whether to include a passage that appears in one exemplar and is absent in another. The choice might reflect confidence in a local tradition, the condition of a damaged page, an effort to make the story more coherent, or expectations from a patron. The result can become part of the version that another reader encounters a century later. This is why a modern critical edition compares witnesses rather than assuming that the most lavish manuscript is necessarily the most accurate text.

A famous illustrated copy offers an especially vivid object lesson. The *Shahnameh* made for Shah Tahmasp in the sixteenth century includes paintings produced by artists of extraordinary skill. Those images can tell us much about the artistic and courtly world of Safavid Iran: clothing imagined for epic figures, architecture, landscape, emotional emphasis, and the scenes patrons wished to celebrate. They are not photographs of Rostam, nor visual evidence that an episode happened in the Bronze Age. The historical object is the illustrated manuscript itself, with its own date, makers, and afterlife.

Different editions may vary in spellings, lines, arrangement, and inclusion. A lecturer cannot responsibly settle every textual controversy in the middle of a battle scene, and need not try. But when a difference changes a character's agency or the meaning of an ending, it belongs in the analysis. Some retellings also blend Ferdowsi with episodes from independent romances and later Persian poems. Good source notes allow a listener to tell which version is being narrated and where comparisons lead.

## The surviving manuscripts arrive late

No autograph manuscript written by Ferdowsi is known to survive. The poem was completed around 1010, but the earliest securely dated surviving manuscript is a partial copy from 1217 preserved in Florence. That is a gap of more than two hundred years. Another important complete manuscript dates to 1276, with a small number of early witnesses known from roughly that period. Many of the rich illustrated copies that now shape the popular image of the epic are later still. The chronological gap is not a reason to dismiss the entire poem; it is a reason that editing it demands care.

Imagine several scribes copying an enormous manuscript in different cities. One misreads a word, another adjusts a line to match familiar usage, another restores a passage from memory, and another adds a well-loved episode that circulates in a related storytelling tradition. Not every change is deliberate, and not every deliberate change is dishonest. A scribe may think that he is repairing a damaged text. Over many generations, those individual decisions can produce meaningful variation in the poem's length, wording, and even the sequence of episodes.

A modern critical editor compares manuscripts, considers their ages and relationships, and decides which reading is most plausibly close to an earlier text. The influential critical edition associated with Djalal Khaleghi-Motlagh is one example of this scholarly work. It does not remove the need for judgment, since even the earliest known manuscript is separated from the poet by centuries. But careful comparison is far better than assuming that whichever edition happens to be easiest to buy reproduces an untouched original.

The oldest manuscripts are also important because they correct a common visual expectation. Many people first encounter the *Shahnameh* through sumptuous miniature paintings. Those paintings are extraordinary evidence for later patronage and artistic interpretation. They are not illustrations drawn while Ferdowsi sat at his desk composing. The early surviving copies from the thirteenth century are not illustrated in the grand courtly manner of the later famous manuscripts. The visual tradition developed through specific workshops, patrons, and artistic communities after the poem had already circulated for generations.

## A book that rulers wanted to own

The epic became a prestigious object for courts far beyond Ferdowsi's own political environment. Rulers commissioned manuscripts whose calligraphy, bindings, pigments, and illustrations demonstrated wealth and cultural authority. A famous example is the *Shahnameh* made for Shah Tahmasp in the sixteenth century, associated with the Safavid court at Tabriz. It comprises hundreds of folios and more than two hundred remarkable painted scenes. Such a commission gathered skilled workers and enormous resources around a story of Iranian kings.

Why would a later ruler invest so much in a poem that repeatedly shows kings failing? Part of the answer is legitimacy. A dynasty could connect itself with a history of sovereignty, courtly splendor, wisdom, and martial courage. But the manuscript also offered a repertoire of cautions. It portrayed rulers who become arrogant, advisers who speak truth, and subjects who resist oppression. A court commissioning the book did not control every interpretation that later viewers might draw from it. The same image of Kaveh lifting a blacksmith's apron can be admired as the founding of legitimate rule and remembered as a challenge to the arrogance of any sitting ruler.

The illustrated tradition also shapes what episodes people remember. A painter must choose a moment. Rostam and Sohrab grappling in battle is visually compelling; Siyavash riding through fire can fill a page with color and motion; the Simorgh carrying Zal is instantly recognizable. But a long negotiation about whether Esfandiyar should bind Rostam is harder to render in one image. Repeated selection of dramatic scenes can make certain events seem more central to the poem than their line counts alone would suggest. Artwork adds a history of emphasis, one that deserves study rather than being treated as decoration.

The poem also lives beyond precious manuscripts. Storytellers performed episodes for audiences, sometimes adapting language, pacing, and gesture to the occasion. The long tradition of recitation and dramatic narration helped keep characters in circulation among people who would never own an illuminated book. Modern editions, translations, theater, music, and digital media continue that process. There is no single unchanging experience of the *Shahnameh*. There is an identifiable literary work whose reception has been sustained through many media and communities.

## The king in the textbook and the king in the story

The density of external evidence changes as the poem proceeds. There is no archaeological record that establishes Jamshid's legendary flying throne or the exact acts of Zahhak's serpents. The epic's first kings can still preserve ideas about social divisions, fire, craftsmanship, and moral authority. Their historical value lies chiefly in tradition and symbolic organization. Presenting them as a dated prehistoric chronicle would add a certainty the evidence cannot bear.

Alexander occupies a different category. A Macedonian conqueror and the collapse of Achaemenid power belong to a well-attested history, but the epic's claim that he was Dara's brother is part of a romance tradition. The historical event supplies a frame around a legendary family drama. When we reach the Sasanians, the surviving coins, inscriptions, monumental art, and several chronicle traditions permit increasingly specific comparisons between a named ruler and the poem. Those sources can confirm a reign while disagreeing with the episode the poem chooses to make famous.

A responsible lecture therefore changes its evidentiary language with its subject. We can say that the story makes Kay Khosrow vanish into the mountains. We can say that the narrative associates Khosrow I with a model of justice. We can say that Yazdegerd III's reign and death belong to the final phase of the Sasanian monarchy. These sentences do different kinds of work. They need no distracting ritual disclaimer; they need accurate verbs and enough context for the listener to know whether we are in myth, inherited legend, or historically reconstructible events.

## Historical evidence changes across the epic

The beginning of the poem is openly legendary. Kayumars as the first king, Hushang's discovery of fire, and Zahhak's serpents do not yield verifiable dates for political or technological history. These scenes carry mythic explanation and moral meaning. The early Iranian religious traditions that preserve related names can establish the antiquity of certain motifs, while archaeology studies human technological development through material remains. Neither kind of evidence authorizes us to report that one of these kings personally invented a tool in a particular year.

The heroic age is similarly difficult to turn into a chronicle. Rostam and Sohrab are compelling literary characters; no contemporary record gives us the exact date of their duel. Siyavash's fire ordeal, Kay Khosrow's disappearance, and Esfandiyar's invulnerability belong to the world of epic narration. Scholars can map relationships between these stories and older Iranian traditions and study their internal ethics. That task is valuable precisely because it does not require inventing a historical battlefield for every mythical encounter.

The evidentiary landscape changes when names become more closely aligned with historical rulers. Alexander conquered the Achaemenid Empire, and the political results can be studied through varied sources. The *Shahnameh* nevertheless gives him a legendary Iranian father and a fantastical quest for the Water of Life. The Sasanian kings from Ardashir onward are historically attested through coins, inscriptions, architecture, and external narratives, but their epic portrayals are often embellished with romances and exemplary anecdotes. Even in the final centuries, when battles and reigns can be dated, the narrator's invented speeches and dramatic explanations should not be quoted as eyewitness testimony.

One especially instructive case is the poem's treatment of the Arsacids. The Parthian state lasted for centuries, yet the epic treats its rulers relatively briefly. That distribution of literary space does not track historical importance. It reflects the available traditions, the shape of the narrative inherited by the poet, and the choices involved in making a continuous Book of Kings. A historian must use other sources to reconstruct Parthian government and relations with Rome. An epic's omissions are evidence about its composition, not proof that omitted history did not happen.

## Translation changes the experience

A reader approaching the work in English needs to know whether a translation is complete, abridged, in verse, or in prose. Arthur and Edmond Warner produced a public-domain English verse translation intended to cover the large poem; it reflects the scholarship and English poetic conventions of its time. Helen Zimmern's *Epic of Kings* is a famous older prose retelling, enjoyable but selective. Dick Davis has made major contributions to modern English access, including a celebrated translation that offers a curated and shaped experience of the poem. A listener can gain a great deal from each, but they are not interchangeable witnesses to every line of the Persian text.

Translation choices can alter a character's tone. A phrase rendered as a declaration of destiny in one version may seem like an expression of confidence in another. Terms concerning sovereignty, honor, faith, and royal glory carry connotations not exhausted by one English equivalent. The names themselves have multiple transliterations: Rostam and Rustam, Siyavash and Siavash, Iskandar and Eskandar. Different spellings should not make us imagine different characters. When a particular claim depends on the wording of a passage, the reader should compare the Persian original and reliable scholarly commentary rather than resting entirely on one convenient English sentence.

An audio lecture faces a further problem. A listener cannot see a footnote while following a battle, and too many scholarly interruptions make the story impossible to inhabit. A good narrative course therefore makes its evidentiary distinctions where they alter the meaning of a pivotal event. We can tell the magical birth of Rostam as the poem tells it and explain that the associated surgical account is legendary. We can tell the death of the historical Yazdegerd and note that sources differ about the exact circumstances. We can explain that Alexander's Persian parentage is a romance device while continuing to explore what the device accomplishes. The source list can carry the precise reading references outside the spoken narrative.

## The reader inherits a decision

English translation requires many choices. A translator must decide how to render the poem's meter, repeated formulas, royal titles, and shifts between martial grandeur and intimate grief. A prose version can follow the action with flexibility but may lose the sound and pressure of the couplets. A verse version can preserve formal momentum but must choose what to sacrifice to the demands of English. A selection can give readers powerful episodes while leaving the transitions and later royal cycle less visible.

Names are another example. A single character may appear as Rostam or Rustam, as Esfandiyar or Isfandiyar, depending on the transliteration system and the translator. The pronunciation used in a spoken course should be consistent enough for recognition without implying that one English spelling is uniquely correct. The same principle applies to Iranian and Turanian place-names. Listeners need to follow the story; they should also be able to find the people and places in scholarly sources that use another accepted form.

Translation choices shape moral interpretation. If a line describing grief is compressed into a triumphant battle summary, the audience may lose the author's complication of victory. If an ancient or medieval derogatory label is modernized without comment, the listener may miss the political perspective of the source. If it is repeated without context, the narration can sound as though it endorses the label. The translator and lecturer both exercise judgment. The goal is to retain enough of the poem's difficulty that its generosity and its harshness remain visible.

Ferdowsi's work endured because it supplied stories that people could inhabit, argue about, paint, recite, and adapt. The preservation of those stories does not mean that all audiences agreed with each king or every judgment. The variety of copies, translations, and retellings shows a work repeatedly reentered by new communities. We inherit a poem whose voices reach us through many hands, and we become part of that history whenever we choose which version to tell.

## Whose memory is preserved?

The *Shahnameh* is commonly called the national epic of Iran, and the phrase captures an important aspect of its reception. Yet the poem predates modern nationalism and circulates across a much wider Persianate world. It has been treasured in regions with different modern borders, languages, religious communities, and political histories. Its heroes are not the exclusive property of one later government. Describing its cultural significance should include both the deep attachment many Iranians feel toward it and the broader histories of its circulation in Central Asia, South Asia, Anatolia, the Caucasus, and beyond.

The poem's narrative ethics also resist a perfectly uniform political reading. It honors rulers but lets a blacksmith challenge a tyrant. It praises Iranian champions but gives a Turanian adviser such as Piran moral distinction. It gives women significant roles as protectors, negotiators, rulers, and lovers, even though the royal genealogy often places male descent at the center. It mourns the pre-Islamic dynasties while being produced in a Muslim literary environment. Different audiences have drawn different emphases from these features, and the poem is large enough that a reading focused only on royal triumph misses much of its achievement.

We can therefore ask of each episode two sets of questions. Within the story, who wishes for what, what knowledge do they possess, and what choice produces the result? Outside the story, how old is our witness, which other accounts are related to it, and what kind of claim could that evidence support? These questions enrich one another. Knowing the story of the Simorgh makes the fatal arrow against Esfandiyar more painful. Knowing that the tale was transmitted through a complex manuscript tradition makes it possible to study how the scene was told and retold without mistaking that history of telling for a datable event.

The *Shahnameh* ends with the collapse of a dynasty and with the endurance of a poem. Ferdowsi's work preserves royal names, but its deepest achievement may be the ability to make successive generations imagine the costs of power in human terms. We remember a father who killed the son he failed to recognize, a prince who kept a promise and lost his life, a blacksmith who refused to validate the court's lie, and a king who gave up a throne because he feared what prolonged splendor might make him. Their stories reach us through sources that must be compared with care. The care is part of respect for the work. A poem can tell the truth about the hazards of human judgment even when the events it narrates are legendary, and historical research can show us precisely what sort of truth each witness is capable of carrying.
