# Three Kingdoms, Part IX: The Road into the Western Kingdom

Liu Bei has a plan for restoring the Han, but the plan requires him to take possession of regions that already have rulers. The most promising lies in the west, beyond difficult approaches and defended by mountains and river valleys. Yi Province has farmland, cities, and routes that might sustain a kingdom. It is ruled by Liu Zhang, another man connected to the Han family. If Liu Bei enters as a guest and later takes the territory, will he be carrying out a larger duty or breaking the rules that give his own cause moral force? *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* does not permit a heroic restoration of legitimate government to proceed without this question.

This movement follows Liu Bei's campaign into Yi while Cao Cao confronts the formidable western warrior Ma Chao. Two sets of operations unfold on different fronts, but they illuminate each other. Cao Cao learns to turn rivalries among his enemies into military victory. Liu Bei discovers that occupying a defensible region may require actions that his public image was designed to resist. The novel is a later historical romance, and we will distinguish its dramatic duels, predictions, and private negotiations from the broad history of the campaigns.

## The warrior from the west

Cao Cao has become the dominant power in the north. His arrangements with Emperor Xian give him official authority, yet commanders on the western frontier do not all accept his control. Ma Chao emerges as a fierce opponent. He belongs to a military family with its own reputation and following, and the novel frames his actions partly as vengeance for Cao Cao's treatment of his kin. Han Sui is another major western commander. Their cooperation could endanger Cao Cao's position if their armies remain coordinated.

The campaigns bring spectacular scenes of flight and disguise. The novel tells how Cao Cao is forced to cut his beard and discard identifying features of his clothing to escape pursuit. A general known to command armies must temporarily become a fugitive trying not to be recognized. The humiliation makes Ma Chao's martial excellence tangible without requiring the reader to follow every troop formation. The northern ruler survives through improvisation, and the episode converts appearance into a battlefield risk: a visible mark of command can become a target.

Ma Chao fights Xu Chu, one of Cao Cao's strongest officers, in a duel narrated with extraordinary physical intensity. The contest honors both men, but a duel cannot decide the campaign if their commanders continue feeding and regrouping troops. Cao Cao eventually uses distrust between Ma Chao and Han Sui. Messages and staged interactions give each reason to suspect the other of private arrangements. Their alliance weakens, and Cao Cao defeats them. The method resembles the false intelligence used at Red Cliffs, but the beneficiary has changed. Cao Cao, once deceived by an apparently secret letter, now exploits a similar vulnerability in opponents who cannot verify one another's intentions.

The historical struggle in the northwest involved shifting alliances and complex frontier politics. It should not be reduced to a duel and one clever letter. The novel selects those events because they render the strategic problem intelligible. Two commanders can field formidable troops and still lose if their cooperation becomes uncertain. Their armies must trust orders transmitted across divided forces. A suspicion at the top can affect thousands of soldiers who never see the disputed message.

## The famous warrior in unfamiliar country

Ma Chao's appearance reminds us that western military power is not simply the weaker mirror of Cao Cao's northern armies. The frontier regions contain networks of local authority, cavalry traditions, and leaders whose reputations were built in conflicts partly separate from Liu Bei's long wanderings. Ma Chao brings those experiences with him. The novel's admiration for him is not just admiration for speed or strength; it is recognition that a large political realm contains capable men whose authority originates beyond the central court.

His eventual participation in Liu Bei's cause matters because it widens the coalition. Western soldiers may view the newcomers from Jing differently when a celebrated commander familiar with their own military world joins them. An army can be persuaded by public demonstrations of courage, but it can also be persuaded by signs that respected leaders have accepted the new regime. A shift in allegiance near the top can influence the confidence of people who have not yet fought a decisive engagement.

This is one reason the novel gives so much attention to encounters between individual commanders. A duel can substitute dramatically for the harder-to-narrate processes of recruitment, recognition, and the establishment of authority. In the real world a single combat cannot transfer an entire region's loyalty. In a romance it can make that transfer feel emotionally intelligible. We see the warrior acknowledge a worthy opponent, and the wider political world seems to take shape around the recognition.

## Invitation into a defensible province

Far to the west, Liu Zhang governs Yi Province and is worried about threats around his borders. Advisers disagree about whether to seek Liu Bei's help. One faction believes an able member of the Han clan might protect Yi against other powers. Another warns that admitting a renowned commander with his own troops will create an internal rival impossible to dislodge. The warnings are not mere selfishness. A government may invite military aid and then lose the authority to decide when the visitors should depart.

Liu Bei is tempted. Yi would give his cause the material basis it lacks. Zhuge Liang's strategic design has long identified the region as a potential foundation for an independent kingdom. Yet Liu Bei owes moral consideration to Liu Zhang, who shares his claimed dynastic affiliation and extends hospitality. The novel portrays a tension between the adviser's belief that a major political good requires seizing an opportunity and the ruler's desire not to become indistinguishable from the opportunists he condemns.

Pang Tong becomes central to the debate. A gifted adviser, he reasons that Liu Bei cannot continue indefinitely as a landless protector of other people's territory. The people of Yi may eventually be better served by a leader with the capacity to defend them. But a prediction of better government does not erase the coercion required to replace their ruler. The moral issue lies in whether an anticipated benefit may justify deceiving an ally or host. The novel's sympathy for Liu Bei allows such arguments to be heard, but the unfolding violence makes them uncomfortable.

Liu Bei enters Yi under a friendly arrangement. There are meetings, public courtesies, provisions, and military plans directed against supposed outside threats. Advisers suggest that he seize Liu Zhang at an opportune moment; he refuses or delays, unwilling to establish his rule through a conspicuous breach of trust. Eventually relations worsen, resources are withheld, and the expedition shifts from hospitality toward open conquest. The transition is one of the crucial moral ambiguities of Liu Bei's career. He has pursued a course that made conflict increasingly likely while hoping to preserve the image of a reluctant participant.

## The invitation that contains a conquest

Liu Zhang governs Yi Province, a territory whose western basin and surrounding mountains give it both rich resources and defensible approaches. Cao Cao's activities to the north create fear, and Liu Zhang's own administration contains rival advisers and unresolved local resentments. When Liu Bei is invited to assist with an external threat, the arrival of a celebrated kinsman of the Han may seem like a prudent answer to immediate danger. But an invited army possesses its own ambitions. Once it enters a difficult-to-access province, removing it may be far harder than admitting it.

The novel allows Liu Bei to express reluctance at the prospect of harming a host. The reluctance is central to his moral image. He wishes to restore the Han and protect people, but that objective now seems to require taking territory from a man who has not committed an obvious crime equal to the loss he faces. Advisers insist that political opportunity cannot always wait for the convenient moral situation. If Liu Bei refuses, Cao Cao may acquire the province, and the cause Liu Bei has nurtured for years may remain too weak to survive.

This is a genuine conflict between the language of right action and the logic of territorial survival. To say that Liu Zhang is incompetent does not automatically create a right to depose him. To say that Liu Bei possesses worthy motives does not erase the costs of a campaign. The novel often resolves the tension by making Liu Zhang's failures and Liu Bei's apparent generosity increasingly visible. A critical listener can still ask whether the events provide a moral authorization or merely a plausible political argument after the fact.

The invitation is also an intelligence problem. Liu Zhang's advisers disagree about Liu Bei because they are making forecasts under uncertainty. Some regard a related Han lord as a useful partner. Others understand that Liu Bei's veteran followers and accomplished strategists could easily become a second center of power inside the province. For the host, an allied force may be most dangerous just when it is most capable of defending him. The novel's slow movement from welcome to suspicion turns that contradiction into a political tragedy.

## A map can tempt a man before he has an army

The western campaigns are shaped by geographical knowledge held unequally among the contenders. A man who knows the roads through Yi, the locations of passes, and the relationships among its officials can transform a vague ambition into a practical possibility. The novel turns such knowledge into the image of a map offered to an outsider. Once Liu Bei sees the province as a coherent arrangement of routes and strongholds, he can imagine taking it. The act of seeing does not make the acquisition righteous or easy; it changes what he believes is possible.

An adviser who supplies that map makes a political choice. He has decided that the present ruler is not the best person to command the land he knows. The consequences fall upon communities whose futures can be altered by details carried in the adviser's memory. The novel asks the audience to admire foresight and to accept the eventual establishment of a more capable government. But it also lets us consider how a person inside a regime can become the most effective guide for an invading army.

The role of roads is particularly important because Yi's natural defenses are double-edged. They can protect the province from northern forces, but they can also trap an army that has advanced too far without securing its supply. A western expedition can be delayed or destroyed at a small number of difficult crossings. A defender who understands those crossings need not be stronger in every open battle. The invitation to enter Yi therefore provides Liu Bei with an opportunity that a direct hostile invasion might never have afforded.

## The uneasy guests at a banquet

The novel repeatedly uses feasts and meetings to place commanders in situations where hospitality and violence coexist. A guest can sit close enough to his host to share a cup, while officers outside the hall wait for instructions that might transform the gathering into an arrest or assassination. Ceremonial courtesy becomes tense because each participant knows that a political agreement is only as secure as the armed people willing to enforce it.

For Liu Bei, the question of whether to seize an opportunity against Liu Zhang at such a meeting becomes a test of his professed character. If he refuses to exploit a host's vulnerability, he preserves a standard of conduct that the novel associates with his rightful claim. If he declines too long, his advisers may accuse him of wasting the only chance to create a secure state. The audience hears both the appeal of honor and the impatience of soldiers whose futures depend on his choice.

There is no simple way to make the disagreement vanish. An adviser may judge a coup at a feast less destructive than a long campaign. A ruler may answer that the means by which he acquires a province will shape how its inhabitants regard him afterward. The calculations address different consequences and different timescales. The banquet scene turns the abstract tension between expediency and legitimacy into a confrontation in which one decision might prevent hundreds of later deaths or create a precedent of betrayal.

The novel does not always maintain the same balance. It often arranges events so that Liu Bei's reluctance appears noble and the ultimate conquest appears necessary. That is part of its sympathy for him. We can grant the force of the dilemma without allowing a favorable narrator to settle every moral question on the ruler's behalf.

## Fa Zheng and the art of choosing a different patron

A conquest becomes possible not only because an outsider brings troops, but because insiders decide to cooperate with him. Fa Zheng is among the advisers associated with the turn toward Liu Bei. In the novel he can look like a man whose skill finally finds a deserving employer. From the standpoint of Liu Zhang's government, his choice is a breach of confidence. From Fa Zheng's standpoint, the change may appear justified by weak leadership and the possibility of constructing a more effective order. The same action receives different descriptions depending on who survives to tell the story.

This is a recurring problem in histories of dynastic transition. The future victor can honor defectors as men of foresight; the defeated house can remember them as traitors. Yet neither praise nor blame is adequate without the circumstances. A subordinate may possess legitimate reasons to criticize his ruler, including the suffering caused by poor appointments or indecision. That still leaves open the question of what obligations he undertook when accepting office and who will pay for the change of allegiance.

For Liu Bei, the arrival of capable local allies reduces the difficulty of governing a newly conquered territory. An army from outside cannot readily understand every fortress, family network, and local dispute. Insiders offer routes, knowledge of resources, and the legitimacy that comes from familiar faces. The victory is therefore partly a negotiation over administration. The novel gives fierce battles their place, but the transfer of an entire province requires people who can translate military success into obedience after the fighting ends.

Fa Zheng's later influence in the campaign for Hanzhong helps explain why the conquest of Yi is not simply an episode that adds land to Liu Bei's possessions. He is assembling a government in which trusted men from different regions must serve together. The relationships may remain difficult. Leaders who come west with Liu Bei have long histories of shared danger; local advisers possess knowledge and interests the newcomers lack. A wise ruler cannot govern the basin by treating every western official as an alien while retaining only old companions.

## An adviser at the wrong crossing

Pang Tong directs operations during the fighting in Yi. The novel assigns him strategies, displays his confidence, and then gives him a famous death at a place associated with fallen phoenixes. He is killed by an ambush while advancing through difficult terrain. The name of the place becomes a cruel narrative omen for the adviser called the Young Phoenix. His death arrives just when the campaign needs his guidance most. Like Sun Jian's early death and Zhou Yu's death after Red Cliffs, it cuts across the expectation that talent should guarantee survival.

The novel's surrounding details amplify fate and warning. A horse, an exchange, and the naming of a road acquire ominous significance in the retelling. The historical Pang Tong was killed in the campaign into Yi, but the precise literary choreography is not a reliable reconstruction of the incident. What matters to the narrative is that planning does not remove risk from the person doing the planning. An adviser can calculate enemy motives and still die because an arrow reaches him on a dangerous road.

Liu Bei mourns him deeply. Zhuge Liang and other officers come to support the campaign. The organization must continue despite the loss of a major mind. A cause that has become dependent on extraordinary individuals must learn to survive the deaths of those individuals. Pang Tong's loss is also a reminder of the limits of the famous paired epithets, Sleeping Dragon and Young Phoenix. A prophecy that possessing both would make success likely cannot make a man invulnerable on a battlefield.

## The river and the fallen counselor

Pang Tong's role brings one of the most painful transformations in this movement. He is introduced as a strategist whose capacities are not immediately evident to every observer. Where Zhuge Liang is given the splendor of the Sleeping Dragon, Pang Tong is often associated with the Phoenix Fledgling. Such titles promise exceptional talent, but the work of campaigning can place even the most gifted counselor in the path of danger that foresight does not prevent.

The novel's death scene at the Valley of the Fallen Phoenix turns a place-name into a warning and a loss. Pang Tong advances into hostile ground and is struck down; the landscape seems to have anticipated the fate of the man whose nickname it echoes. In the historical account, Pang Tong was killed during the campaign around Luocheng. The elaborate omen and precise staging belong to the fiction's design. The shared fact is that Liu Bei loses an important adviser during the difficult western expedition.

The loss interrupts the comforting notion that a brilliant strategist can protect an army from tragedy. Even a carefully planned operation requires people to move along vulnerable roads and under enemy weapons. Intelligence may reduce danger; it cannot abolish it. The reader who expected Pang Tong to deliver elegant solutions must confront the possibility that the finest minds can die before their plans have matured.

The episode also changes the balance among Liu Bei's companions. The remaining advisers must assume greater responsibility, and Zhuge Liang's presence in the west becomes more urgent. Liu Bei has sought a stable province to protect his cause, yet the road to that stability requires sacrifices from the people most responsible for making it possible. It is easy to call the eventual acquisition of Yi a strategic success. The death of Pang Tong makes the cost audible before the triumph arrives.

## Zhang Fei and an enemy worth sparing

The campaign into Yi allows Zhang Fei to demonstrate something beyond ferocity. He captures the veteran defender Yan Yan, who refuses to bow before a conquering general. Yan Yan says, in effect, that there are men whose heads may be cut off but whose principles cannot be made to bend. Zhang Fei at first threatens execution; then he recognizes the captive's courage and honors him. The defeated defender becomes someone whose loyalty has earned respect from an enemy.

The scene is an answer to one of Zhang Fei's earliest incidents. When a corrupt inspector obstructed Liu Bei, Zhang Fei used anger to humiliate him. Here he has the power to kill a captured opponent but chooses restraint. The difference suggests growth, though the novel will never make Zhang Fei a uniformly disciplined officer. His admiration for brave resistance is a martial virtue that can expand his ability to govern soldiers rather than merely frighten them.

It also exposes the tragedy of civil war among people who recognize similar values. Yan Yan is not dishonorable because he fought for Liu Zhang; his loyalty to an existing ruler may be admirable. Zhang Fei is not dishonorable because he serves Liu Bei. Both may be acting according to principles whose application puts them on opposite sides of a siege. Respect across the line does not automatically settle which lord should command the province.

## A general at the captured gate

Zhang Fei's encounter with Yan Yan dramatizes a different possibility: that the defeated can retain dignity and still become part of a new political order. In the novel, Zhang Fei's reputation for violent temper precedes him. He can intimidate enemies and subordinates alike. When he captures Yan Yan, a defender who refuses to abase himself, the expected confrontation seems likely to end in execution. Instead, courage in defeat alters the captor's judgment.

Yan Yan resists the logic that a captured man must flatter a victorious general. His defiance makes visible a kind of loyalty that Zhang Fei, despite his own faults, can recognize. By honoring the captive, Zhang Fei momentarily becomes greater than his anger. The narrative uses this scene to enlarge a character often presented through noise and appetite. He is not merely a fierce fighter. He is capable of responding to moral strength when he sees it in another person.

The scene is valuable because it provides a model of incorporation less cynical than simple expediency. A conquered officer can be valued for refusing to betray his original duty before circumstances forced his surrender. Such a person may make a trustworthy servant precisely because he did not change sides at the first threat. The paradox resembles Cao Cao's admiration for Guan Yu. The moral qualities that trouble the victor during an encounter may become the qualities that make a former opponent useful in peace.

Still, the episode cannot stand for every experience of the western conquest. Battles kill people who never receive a dramatic chance to display their virtues before a generous commander. The narrative selects an exchange that allows one warrior to become better. A historical account of conquest must also consider the fate of ordinary defenders, towns that resisted, and civilians who had no influence over the officers at their gates.

## A warrior changes the balance

Ma Chao, after setbacks in the northwest, becomes involved in the western conflicts and ultimately enters Liu Bei's service. The novel gives him a confrontation with Zhang Fei, a thrilling meeting of two men famous for strength and aggression. Yet the final shift is produced through negotiation and political calculations. Ma Chao's followers need a place within a durable command, and Liu Bei needs talent and the appearance of broad support. The duel supplies drama; the transfer of allegiance alters the campaign.

The addition of Ma Chao symbolizes a widening of Liu Bei's movement. It is no longer merely the small band of brothers who fought the Yellow Turbans. It can attract men from different regions and military traditions, some of whom previously opposed the forces with which Liu Bei is associated. This development is necessary for state formation and morally complicated. An army based only on old friendship cannot govern a large population. An army that admits former enemies must create rules of trust stronger than personal nostalgia.

Liu Zhang's position becomes untenable. Liu Bei's armies press toward Chengdu, and advisers on both sides consider the cost of prolonged resistance. The novel presents a surrender that spares the city from an even more destructive ending. Liu Bei takes control of Yi and begins arranging a government. After years of displacement he finally possesses a region with the resources and defensive geography to support his wider ambitions.


## The warrior whose arrival changes the campaign

Ma Chao enters the story as a formidable western warrior, associated with fierce battles against Cao Cao and with a reputation that makes even veteran commanders wary. His movement into Liu Bei's orbit is one of the ways the western campaign becomes politically decisive. The arrival of a celebrated fighter can exert pressure beyond the specific engagement in which he participates. Defenders may conclude that the invading force is too strong to resist; supporters of Liu Bei may believe victory is at hand.

The famous contests between Ma Chao and Zhang Fei are written for the pleasure of martial comparison. Each represents a different lineage of reputation, and neither can be casually reduced to the other's inferior. The exchange of blows becomes an argument about what courage looks like when two capable men refuse to yield. Yet Ma Chao's historical path through rival western powers, defeat, and eventual service under Liu Bei was a matter of shifting alliances and military necessity. The novel's duel cannot substitute for that political history.

Liu Zhang's surrender follows an accumulation of defeats and defections. The reader may feel relief that the costly expedition has ended; a new ruler has gained the basin and the capacity to govern. But the novel has spent enough time showing the host's predicament that triumph cannot be morally simple. Liu Bei may now be capable of mounting a serious resistance to Cao Cao. The territory that makes resistance possible was taken from another ruler who believed he was defending his own position.

That unresolved tension remains one of the novel's great virtues. It admires Liu Bei's humanity and still places him where humanity cannot be separated from force. The dream of a restored political order acquires the granaries, roads, and mountain defenses it needs. It also acquires the obligations of a conqueror: to protect those now subject to his rule, to tolerate people who once opposed him, and to refrain from treating the success of his campaign as evidence that every decision made along the way was just.


## A ruler's first test after winning

The entry into Chengdu does not end Liu Bei's responsibilities. He must now govern people who have not necessarily welcomed his army. Former supporters of Liu Zhang may fear punishment, while advisers who helped the conquest expect rewards. The original followers who endured years of uncertainty may regard new western officials as undeserving of equal standing. This is a fragile mixture from which to build a state.

A ruler who distributes every office to old companions may lose local knowledge and create resistance among experienced administrators. A ruler who rewards every recent defector may seem to betray those who suffered for him. The problem can be managed only through credible appointments, predictable laws, and a political language that gives the newly governed a place within the emerging regime.

The novel praises Liu Bei when he treats former opponents with consideration. Such scenes show how conquest can be followed by incorporation rather than extermination. They also raise the standard against which his future campaigns will be judged. Once he possesses the western basin, he no longer has the excuse of being a desperate guest without resources. The welfare of a substantial population now depends on his judgment. The achievement of a permanent home turns the wandering hero into a ruler who can be held responsible for what his state does.

## Whose legitimacy has been restored?

This conquest is a major achievement for the Longzhong Plan. With Jing as an eastern base and Yi in the west, Liu Bei can imagine a kingdom capable of opposing Cao Cao. Yet the achievement cannot be described as an uncomplicated restoration of legitimate rule. He has displaced Liu Zhang, who was also connected to the Han house. He has taken territory after initially entering on terms of assistance. His advisers argued that long-term order justified the move. The novel largely supports Liu Bei's eventual rule, but the means remain available for criticism.

The problem is familiar in political history. A claimant may justify intervention by saying that an existing ruler is weak, badly advised, or unable to protect the population. The claim can be sincere, self-serving, or both. An intervention can improve administration for some while imposing war on others. Literary sympathy for Liu Bei directs attention toward his intentions and the virtues of his advisers. A careful listener should also ask what choices were available to the households caught between armies, whose preferences can be difficult to recover from an elite-centered narrative.

The novel's presentation of Pang Tong reveals another cost. He saw the path into the west clearly and helped persuade Liu Bei to take it. He died before he could participate in the government his advice helped create. Strategy converts dangers into a course of action; it does not promise that the strategist will live to benefit. The eventual kingdom will owe much to men absent from its ceremonial triumphs.

Meanwhile Cao Cao remains a formidable rival, and Sun Quan still has reason to demand that Liu Bei clarify the status of Jing Province. A territorial success in the west may strengthen the argument that land previously treated as temporary should now be returned. Liu Bei's advisers will have reasons to resist, because Jing is part of the plan for future campaigns. The new balance therefore contains the seeds of conflict between the very southern allies who saved each other at Red Cliffs.

The story's deepest question in these chapters is whether a good end can redeem difficult means. Cao Cao wins by dividing rivals; Liu Bei wins by replacing a host; Zhang Fei shows mercy to a defeated officer; Pang Tong dies while making a moral gamble practicable. None of these outcomes alone supplies a universal rule. The novel asks readers to consider the quality of the people making the decisions and the pressures under which they acted, while the historical consequences continue to accumulate beyond individual intention.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 58–65. Compare the historical northwest campaigns and Yi Province conquest with Chen Shou and Pei Songzhi. Ma Chao's family and regional allegiances require careful chronological treatment. Pang Tong's death is historical; the falling-phoenix dramatic omens belong to the novel. Liu Zhang's loss of Yi should not be disguised as voluntary handing over in a fully consensual transaction.
