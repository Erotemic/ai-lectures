# Olduvai and Laetoli: How Fossils, Stones, and Footprints Become Evidence

Imagine looking down at an imprint in a surface of ancient volcanic ash. You can see a heel and the front of a foot. The print is surrounded by other impressions: animals moved across this place when the ash was damp. It hardened, and later deposits protected the surface for millions of years. Now a fieldworker has exposed it again. No one wrote a report at the time the foot touched the ground. Yet researchers can ask whether its maker walked upright, how the foot moved, and approximately when the event occurred.

Nearby, in the broad geological sense of northern Tanzania, another archive lies in a gorge. Its sediments contain stone tools, animal bones, and fossils of human relatives. Researchers have excavated individual objects from layers laid down near an ancient lake, then tried to understand what the objects say about behavior. Were the stones deliberately struck? Were animals butchered? Were the bones collected by people, carnivores, water, or some mixture of them? Who made the tools, and how old are the layers?

Olduvai Gorge and Laetoli offer complementary forms of evidence. The gorge preserves long archaeological and fossil sequences. Laetoli preserves an ancient surface crossed by living bodies. Together they have changed accounts of early human evolution. The stories are often compressed into a single family name: Leakey. But the research involved generations of Tanzanian workers and researchers, field archaeologists, geologists, laboratory specialists, and later teams that disagreed over interpretation.

This lecture reconstructs how the discoveries became scientific evidence. We will follow the famous 1959 skull from the point of discovery to its evolving classification, then investigate the tools and animal bones around it. We will move to the Laetoli footprint surface, trace the people who recognized it, and learn how its age and biological significance were argued. We will finish by seeing how old field records can be used to ask genuinely new questions today.

## Before the famous skull

Olduvai is a place, not the invention of an archaeological expedition. Local people knew the gorge and its landscape. Its scientific history under colonial rule is usually told through a series of visiting European researchers. Early twentieth-century work included Wilhelm Kattwinkel and Hans Reck, who helped publicize the fossil-bearing deposits. Reck developed a geological framework that later investigators revised. Louis Leakey began expeditions to Olduvai in the early 1930s. Mary Leakey worked with him there in 1935, and the two returned repeatedly.

The gorge is valuable partly because erosion exposes a thick sequence of sediments. These layers accumulated in different environments as lakes, streams, volcanic eruptions, and terrestrial habitats changed through time. One deposit can contain material washed from another place; another can preserve an ancient occupation surface close to where activity occurred. A vertical stack of layers provides a relative sequence, but the age of each layer is a separate problem. A geologist must identify units, correlations, disturbances, and the volcanic deposits available for dating.

Mary Leakey became especially important for systematic excavation and classification of stone artifacts. Her work was painstaking. A tool's position, relation to animal remains, and technological form could be studied together because the excavators kept records and collections. In 1971 she published a major account of excavations in Beds I and II. That sort of field monograph rarely becomes the centerpiece of a popular documentary, but it is how a spectacular discovery becomes a reproducible body of evidence.

Louis Leakey was an energetic organizer, interpreter, and public advocate. Mary had her own expertise and research record, as did colleagues such as Philip Tobias, John Napier, Richard Hay, and numerous excavators and preparators whose names receive less attention. The fame of the family sometimes collapses these distinct contributions. We should resist attributing a geological date to the person who found a fossil or a fossil to the person who gave a press interview about it.

The site's history also has a political setting. The crucial mid-century research began in colonial Tanganyika, and Tanzania achieved independence in the early 1960s. Permissions, museum collections, field employment, and international funding shaped which studies could happen. Modern Tanzanian institutions and scientists are central to the continuing archaeology. A scientific history that describes the land only as a stage for visiting researchers loses part of the story of how the evidence was collected and preserved.

## A skull in Bed I

In 1959 Mary Leakey found a striking fossil cranium in Olduvai's Bed I, at a locality commonly called FLK Zinj. In museum catalogs it is Olduvai Hominid Five, or OH 5. Louis Leakey initially named it Zinjanthropus boisei. Its later classification changed: today it is commonly assigned to Paranthropus boisei. It had enormous cheek teeth, powerful chewing adaptations, and a skull distinct from that of modern humans.

Why did the discovery have such impact? It was a relatively complete early hominin skull from a geological context containing stone artifacts and animal remains, in a region already known for its fossil record. It gave researchers new anatomical information and a reason to intensify work. It also challenged some popular assumptions about the order in which changes in the human lineage had occurred. The fossil was older than many people at the time had imagined for associated stone-age material.

But there is a question hidden in the word 'associated.' Does finding a fossil near stone tools establish that the animal whose skull we found made the tools? No. A fossil and a stone tool can come from related deposits without belonging to the same individual or even the same biological species. A surface may have been visited repeatedly. Bones can be moved. Different hominins can occupy a landscape within overlapping periods. Later evidence of early Homo at Olduvai added to the problem of assigning tools to their makers.

Philip Tobias undertook detailed anatomical study of the OH 5 cranium. John Napier and Tobias collaborated with Louis Leakey in the 1964 proposal of Homo habilis, based on additional finds from Olduvai. These classifications were not measurements directly printed on the fossils; they were interpretations of anatomical variation, relationships, and available comparisons. Researchers continue to debate the boundaries among early hominin species. The important lesson is that identifying a fossil, dating its context, and assigning behavior to its species are three separate arguments.

A museum label that says Paranthropus boisei represents a defensible current taxonomy. It does not imply that Mary Leakey was mistaken to recover the fossil under a different name. The anatomical specimen remains available to be measured, scanned, compared, and reclassified. Science keeps the object while changing the best explanation of its evolutionary place.

## Giving a skull an age

Once OH 5 was found, a crucial question was how old the fossil-bearing deposits actually were. The layer could be ordered relative to other strata, but investigators needed a scale measured in years. Olduvai's volcanic rocks made potassium–argon dating possible. Potassium forty decays into argon forty at a known rate. Under appropriate conditions, volcanic minerals can preserve a clock that begins when the mineral cooled or crystallized. Laboratories measure isotope ratios, evaluate potential contamination, and calculate dates with uncertainties.

In a 1961 paper, Louis Leakey, Jack Evernden, and Garniss Curtis reported potassium–argon results for Bed I. The dates supported an antiquity on the order of 1.75 million years for the relevant deposits. Evernden and Curtis contributed the geological dating expertise; Louis Leakey brought the archaeological and fossil problem and the sample context. This was a striking result in the history of paleoanthropology because it extended the timescale available for interpreting early tool-making and human relatives.

A geochemical age is not assigned directly to the skull as if an instrument reads its birthday. The laboratory dates suitable volcanic material. Geologists establish its position relative to the fossil-bearing layer. A reliable conclusion requires both the isotope measurement and the stratigraphic argument. If a tuff contains older reworked crystals, the measurement can be misleading. If a sample was mislabeled or the layer correlated incorrectly, a technically sound laboratory result can end up attached to the wrong event.

Those issues were real at Olduvai. Richard Hay's geological work refined the basin's stratigraphy and environmental history. His 1976 monograph assembled detailed sedimentological observations. Later reviews of the early potassium–argon results found substantial scatter among samples and clarified why some dates were not dependable. A 1991 study by Robert Walter, Paul Manega, Richard Hay, Robert Drake, Garniss Curtis, and colleagues used single-crystal argon methods to improve the chronology. A more precise clock did not erase the earlier discovery; it revised the dating framework with better measurements.

This is a useful model of reproducibility. The original claim was surprising, was tested and criticized, and prompted better sampling and measurement. Different methods, such as fission-track dating, also provided checks. No single date stands independently of geology. The credibility of the Olduvai chronology comes from field observation, multiple measurements, publication, reassessment, and the ability of later investigators to ask whether the ages appear in sensible stratigraphic order.

## Did someone butcher these animals?

The FLK Zinj locality and other Olduvai sites contain dense concentrations of stone artifacts and animal bones. Some bones bear cuts consistent with stone tools; others bear marks made by carnivore teeth. Stone flakes can have sharp edges. The physical evidence allows researchers to investigate the sequence of activities that produced an accumulation. But a heap of bones is not a written account of a camp, a hunting expedition, or a family dinner.

Imagine one long bone bearing a V-shaped mark. A human ancestor might have cut meat from it with a sharp flake. A carnivore's tooth, sediment abrasion, or trampling could also modify a surface, sometimes leaving superficially similar marks. Specialists examine the groove's cross-section, microscopic striations, orientation, relation to joints and muscles, and association with other damage. They compare experimentally cut and chewed bones. A single suggestive scratch is weaker evidence than a patterned assemblage showing multiple lines of support.

The field that studies how remains enter and change within archaeological deposits is often called taphonomy. Researchers such as Lewis Binford, Henry Bunn, Robert Blumenschine, Manuel Domínguez-Rodrigo, Rebeca Barba, and Charles Egeland have contributed to debates about early carcass acquisition and modification. The arguments concern whether hominins hunted large animals, scavenged remains left by carnivores, accessed meat early or late in a carcass's history, and used certain locations repeatedly. Different sites may have different histories, so there need not be one answer for all of Olduvai.

An older description of a 'home base' can sound persuasive: stones, bones, and fossil humans occur in the same place, so perhaps early people brought food there and shared it. A competing explanation might emphasize carnivore transport and later hominin use, or vice versa. Excavators test these ideas by looking at which skeletal parts are present, how bones were fractured, the order of tooth and cut marks, and the distribution of artifacts. If a carcass was used by several animals and hominins at different times, it may be impossible to assign every mark to one simple sequence.

Modern excavation and reanalysis have changed environmental interpretations as well. The Olduvai Paleoanthropological and Palaeoecological Project and collaborators have examined springs, vegetation indicators, and the paleolandscape around FLK Zinj. A concentration once interpreted as near a barren lake margin may have been close to freshwater and patches of woodland. This is a reminder that behavior depends on setting. A water source can draw animals, carnivores, and hominins to the same place, creating complicated deposits without a permanent settlement there.

There is also an attribution issue. Some of these projects take place in Tanzania and depend on Tanzanian permissions, researchers, collections staff, and fieldworkers. Published authorship lists do not always record the full labor behind an excavation. A responsible lecture gives the names that can be verified, while noting that the archive often identifies international investigators much more thoroughly than local contributors. It should not invent names to make the account feel complete.

## The Laetoli surface

Laetoli lies south of Olduvai in northern Tanzania. Its volcanic ash deposits preserve fossil animal tracks and hominin remains from the Pliocene. Mary Leakey and Richard Hay published early work there with a team including Garniss Curtis, Robert Drake, M. K. Jackes, and Tim White. In a 1976 paper, they reported fossil hominins and potassium–argon constraints placing the relevant deposits millions of years old.

The tracks emerged through several field seasons, and their discovery history is often oversimplified. Andrew Hill recognized animal tracks in the footprint-bearing tuff in 1976. Peter Jones and Philip Leakey identified an enigmatic sequence at what became Site A. In 1978 Paul Abell recognized an unmistakable hominin footprint at Site G, prompting excavation of the longer trackway under Mary Leakey's leadership. The sequence of individual contributions is important because different discoveries were later combined into the familiar phrase 'the Laetoli footprints.'

The principal Site G trackway showed repeated impressions of a foot with an aligned big toe and patterns suggesting upright walking. The sediment had originally been volcanic ash that was moistened, crossed by animals, and then preserved by later deposits. The ash did not begin as a cast made for a museum display. It was a real walking surface that recorded movement. An individual footprint can reveal the shape of a foot pressed into the ground; a sequence can reveal relative direction and spacing, but translating that into a precise gait is more difficult.

Mary Leakey and Richard Hay's 1979 *Nature* paper documented the footprint-bearing tuffs and associated animal tracks. Subsequent collaborative publications, including the major 1987 Laetoli volume edited by Mary Leakey and John Harris, assembled broader site evidence. Fossil anatomy and footprint morphology suggested that at least some of the tracks were made by an early hominin, commonly associated with Australopithecus afarensis. That species attribution is an inference from timing, anatomy, and the regional fossil record. The prints do not carry a species name.

The dates are essential. The track-bearing layer is approximately 3.66 million years old according to the geological framework commonly used today. This pushes clear evidence of bipedal locomotion far earlier than the evolution of a modern human brain or body. The tracks changed the order in which the public often imagined human attributes emerging. Upright walking has a deep history, and the fossil record shows that it did not require all the other features of ourselves to exist first.

## What exactly can a footprint tell us?

A track is a dynamic interaction between an animal and a surface. The depth of a heel impression depends on body weight, speed, local moisture, and the mechanical response of the sediment. A toe may appear long because of dragging. Two tracks made by the same individual can differ because of a small change in footing. A fossilized track is therefore not a perfectly faithful cast of the skeleton of the foot.

Researchers measure footprint length and width, toe orientation, step length, stride length, and the distribution of pressure-related deformation. They compare the trackway with those made experimentally by modern humans and other animals on known substrates. Such comparisons can estimate gait features, but the assumptions matter. A modern human walking on damp sand is not a controlled reproduction of volcanic ash, and bears or apes walking bipedally may behave differently from early hominins.

Consider a footprint with an apparently close-set big toe. That supports a foot adapted for a pattern of propulsion different from the grasping foot of a chimpanzee. But one impression cannot tell us every angle of the hip or knee during a stride. Likewise, two parallel trails show animals moving in roughly similar directions; they do not establish a family relationship, a conversation, or a particular social purpose. Popular illustrations showing a parent guiding a child are creative reconstructions, rather than direct readings of the ash.

There are also questions about the number of individuals whose footprints overlap. Tracks can be superimposed, partly eroded, or incompletely preserved. Analysts may disagree over which print belongs to which trackway. Better imaging and physical models can improve measurement without guaranteeing that every behavior is recoverable. A good interpretation describes the confidence of its specific claim: bipedal walking is supported more securely than a precise reconstruction of the walkers' thoughts or relationships.

The trackway itself requires conservation. After exposure, rain, roots, and erosion can destroy the evidence that excavation makes visible. The Getty Conservation Institute and Tanzanian collaborators undertook work to protect and conserve the Laetoli tracks. Documentation, reburial, and management were not side issues. They helped determine whether future researchers would be able to examine the same physical surface and challenge earlier interpretations.

## A forgotten trackway returns

The Site A footprints illustrate how scientific history can change when people revisit observations that fell out of favor. The five bipedal impressions discovered in the 1970s were not as readily interpretable as the Site G trail. Some researchers proposed that a bear or another animal walking on two legs might explain them. They received less attention while Site G became the iconic example of early hominin walking.

In 2019, a team revisited Site A, relocated the trackway, and documented it using modern imaging methods. Ellison McNutt, Kevin Hatala, Catherine Miller, Jeremy DeSilva, Charles Musiba, and many colleagues contributed to the study published in 2021. The researchers compared the impressions with experimental footprints from humans, chimpanzees, and bears. They argued that the Site A prints were more consistent with a bipedal hominin than with a bear, and that their differences from Site G might imply more than one kind of hominin locomotion at Laetoli.

The careful word here is 'might.' The 2021 study advanced a testable interpretation of a small trackway, not the discovery of a complete skeleton belonging to a new named species. Its argument depends on measurements, comparison samples, and how the original sediment recorded foot placement. The paper also documents the contributors who found and analyzed the resurfaced prints. We can credit the original Jones and Philip Leakey observation and the modern reanalysis without treating one as proof that the other had all the answers.

More finds can revise the count of trackmakers as well as their possible identity. At Site S, Fidelis Masao and Elgidius Ichumbaki of the University of Dar es Salaam, together with Marco Cherin and collaborators, reported additional tracks in 2016. These added another local record of bipedal activity and opened questions about the range of body sizes within an early hominin species. What makes these discoveries powerful is their capacity to challenge a neat, single-line picture of early human walking. The fossil record can contain several forms of bipedal anatomy at overlapping dates. Determining which species made which print is more demanding than detecting a bipedal gait.

Notice the chain of evidence behind that conclusion. The site was first recorded in field notes and maps. Decades later researchers found it again. New instruments produced three-dimensional measurements. Comparative experiments made predictions about different kinds of feet. The resulting scientific paper described the limits of the sample. Without the original field records, the modern team might not have located the tracks. Without new comparisons, the old interpretation might never have been revisited.

## Two sites, two kinds of inference

Let us imagine that we have been asked to write three sentences for a museum display. The first says: 'A hominin skull was found near early stone tools at Olduvai.' We can support this with the excavated fossil, site records, and artifact collection. The second says: 'That individual made the tools.' That requires evidence the association does not supply. A careful display would explain that more than one hominin taxon existed in the wider record and that attribution of tool-making requires separate arguments.

The third sentence says: 'By about 3.66 million years ago, hominins were walking on two legs at Laetoli.' This has strong support from the dated Site G tracks and their morphology. But if we change the sentence to 'Two members of a family walked arm in arm toward home,' it becomes a story invented around the evidence. Stories can be engaging, and artists may need to choose a scene. They should be clearly distinguished from conclusions that can be tested.

Olduvai asks how objects, bones, and fossil specimens came to be concentrated in a deposit, and how that assemblage sits within a dated geological history. Laetoli asks how the passage of a living body was impressed into a surface and what form of movement can be inferred. Both require geologists to relate a physical surface to volcanic events, but their interpretive challenges differ. A cut-marked bone is evidence of an action involving an object; a footprint is a deformation of a surface by a moving body. Neither is a photograph of an entire ancient society.

This comparison is also a lesson about the word 'discoverer.' Mary Leakey's identification and excavation of OH 5 were different achievements from the potassium–argon work by Evernden and Curtis, the detailed geological synthesis by Hay, and the anatomical descriptions by Tobias. At Laetoli, the track discoveries involved Hill, Jones, Philip Leakey, Abell, Mary Leakey's team, and later reanalysts. We should assign credit to each supported action, rather than allowing one famous surname to replace a chain of work.

## What the ground has taught us

Olduvai and Laetoli do not give us a complete evolutionary family tree. They give us specific observations: fossil anatomy, stone manufacturing traces, animal-bone modifications, stratigraphy, isotopic ages, and preserved footsteps. Their strength lies in the ability to ask new questions of old objects. A modern scanner can expose a measurement not made in the original excavation. A new dating method can refine a tuff age. A larger comparative sample can weaken an old species assignment or strengthen an interpretation of how an animal walked.

There is a danger in turning such research into a sequence of heroic discoveries in which each scientist reveals the truth and the next scientist simply adds another chapter. Real inquiry includes incorrect assumptions, disputed classifications, contaminated measurements, and multiple plausible explanations. What keeps it productive is the preservation of objects and contexts, and the habit of making claims that another investigator can test. That is why excavation notebooks and conservators are part of the scientific story.

It also requires institutional continuity. The fossils and traces are Tanzanian heritage, and decisions about access, conservation, and research involve Tanzanian institutions and communities. A textbook can record the contributions of Mary Leakey while also recognizing how earlier colonial scholarship neglected local collaborators. Modern coauthorship and heritage management do not fully repair missing records, but they can change whose expertise is visible and whose decisions govern the work.

We began with a footprint in ash. Its maker did not intend to leave a lesson for a distant future species. An animal walked across a wet surface, and geological processes preserved the impression. Millions of years later, the surface was recognized, mapped, dated, interpreted, protected, questioned, and interpreted again. Each step was a human action in our own time, grounded in a physical trace from another. The most interesting answer to 'How do we know?' is therefore a story with two histories: the ancient event that made the evidence, and the modern collaborations that taught us to understand it.

## Research and attribution notes (not spoken)

### Historical distinctions

- **OH 5 (1959):** Found by Mary Leakey at FLK Zinj. First named *Zinjanthropus boisei*; now generally *Paranthropus boisei*. Fossil association with Oldowan tools does not identify the toolmaker.
- **Olduvai dating:** Louis Leakey, Jack Evernden, and Garniss Curtis (1961) published initial K–Ar age measurements. Richard Hay provided major stratigraphic and environmental synthesis (1976); Walter, Manega, Hay, Drake, Curtis et al. (1991) refined dates with laser-fusion argon methods.
- **Laetoli chronology:** 1976 early track recognition by Andrew Hill and Site A work by Peter Jones and Philip Leakey; 1978 Site G hominin print identified by Paul Abell and excavated under Mary Leakey's direction. Source recollections differ over exact year of some preliminary observations; distinguish Site A and G.
- **Site G:** Fossilized walking surface dated near 3.66 Ma. Attribution to *A. afarensis* is widely accepted as a strong hypothesis, but the tracks themselves are not taxonomic specimens.
- **Site A:** 2021 analysis by McNutt, Hatala, and coauthors used renewed excavation, 3D measurements and extant animal comparisons; two-taxon conclusion is a proposal to test, not an identified skeleton.
- **Taphonomy:** Bone surface modification, transport, sedimentation, and repeated activity need independent tests. A cut mark does not automatically establish hunting or a permanent home base.

### Editorial cautions

- The three-sentence museum-label comparison is a hypothetical reasoning exercise.
- Avoid assigning tool manufacture to OH 5 by proximity alone.
- Distinguish footprints as evidence of motion from stories about kinship or destination.
- Do not omit the Tanzanian institutional and fieldwork context.
