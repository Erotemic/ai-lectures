# Three Kingdoms, Part XII: The Revenge Campaign and the Fires of Yiling

The oath in the peach garden promised that Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei would sustain one another even at the cost of their lives. When they made that promise, they were men gathering volunteers to save a dying dynasty. Now Liu Bei is an emperor in the west, Guan Yu has been killed by the forces of Sun Quan, and the strongest advisers in Liu Bei's court fear that personal grief will dictate the policy of a state. A brother may demand vengeance. An emperor must think about the safety of provinces, garrisons, and households that never took the oath. *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* brings these obligations into direct conflict.

The war that follows, culminating in the defeat at Yiling in 222, is historical. The novel's intimate conversations, duels, omens, and descriptions of personal revenge are narrative constructions built around that catastrophe. The story is one of the clearest places in the book to observe the difference between a man whose character inspires loyalty and a ruler capable of making strategic mistakes. We will follow Liu Bei's decision to attack Wu, the death of his remaining sworn brother, the rise of Lu Xun, and the extraordinary transfer of authority to Zhuge Liang at Baidi.

## A death before the march

Liu Bei resolves to campaign against Sun Quan's state. His advisers warn him that the north remains powerful, and that an exhausting war against Wu may abandon the broader goal of restoring the Han. Zhuge Liang's plan required cooperation with the southern kingdom to contain Cao Wei. Guan Yu's loss has damaged that arrangement, but a revenge campaign may destroy any prospect of repair. Liu Bei hears the advice, yet grief and obligation outweigh it.

Zhang Fei is determined to avenge Guan Yu as well. He demands extravagant preparation from his subordinates on an impossible schedule and responds with violence when they cannot comply. Two officers, fearing for their lives, kill him while he sleeps. The death is abrupt and bitterly suited to his character. The fierce warrior who could defy enemies at a bridge is killed not by a great champion but by his own men, whom his temper has made desperate. The novel is unsparing about the fact that courage in war does not excuse cruelty in command.

The murders also expose the operational cost of a commander's private impulses. Soldiers obey dangerous orders because discipline is necessary, but discipline becomes unstable when a leader treats subordinates as objects of rage. Zhang Fei's death removes one of Liu Bei's oldest companions before the revenge campaign has accomplished anything. A man fighting to punish an ally for killing one brother now loses another brother through the violence of his own military household.

## A brother killed before the campaign begins

Liu Bei's decision to punish the southern kingdom after Guan Yu's death is driven by more than a ruler's loss of territory. He has defined his public identity through a brotherhood whose origins precede all his titles. To ignore the death would seem to place the calculations of a king above the claims of the man who helped him endure years of defeat. His advisers warn that revenge may sacrifice the position they have spent those years constructing. But advice that asks a grieving person to rank political benefit above loyalty can sound like an invitation to abandon himself.

Zhang Fei shares the anger. His later death at the hands of officers he has abused makes the revenge campaign even more painful before it properly begins. The novel often portrays him as capable of generosity toward valiant enemies and ferocity toward subordinates who cannot protect themselves. Here the weakness has direct consequences. Men who expect unbearable punishment decide that murder and flight offer better odds of survival than obedience.

The episode does not suggest that every harsh commander is immediately killed by his own soldiers. It dramatizes how fear can corrode a hierarchy. In a functioning army, soldiers and officers must trust that an order will be intelligible and that effort will not be met by arbitrary violence. When punishment becomes unpredictable, subordinates begin to manage the ruler's moods rather than the military task. Zhang Fei's strength cannot save him while sleeping, when the men nearest him have ceased to believe they can live safely under his command.

The death removes the last of Liu Bei's famous original companions. What began as the promise of three men in a garden is reduced to one survivor who now rules an imperfect state. The story's private catastrophe intensifies the political folly that his counselors fear. Liu Bei's loyalty is genuine, but the campaign undertaken in its name will place thousands of people at risk who never made the Peach Garden oath.

## The emperor leaves his strongest ground

Liu Bei commands a substantial army and marches east against Wu. The early campaign brings successes, and the novel includes episodes in which Shu officers win duels or exact personal revenge. These victories confirm the emperor's belief that the cause is righteous and militarily feasible. Yet each step carries him farther from his base in the west and into territory where Wu's commanders can choose how and when to fight. A campaign may gain towns while becoming more vulnerable as it advances.

Sun Quan attempts to negotiate and may seek arrangements involving Wei to secure his position. From Wu's viewpoint the war is a struggle against invasion by a former ally whose claim to Jing threatens southern security. The novel's moral sympathies often lie with Liu Bei, but it gives Wu officers practical reasons to resist. Sun Quan has populations to defend and cannot simply return strategic territory because Liu Bei's grief is sincere. The dispute that began as a disagreement over the meaning of an alliance has become a war between two established regimes.

The Wu court chooses Lu Xun as commander. He is younger than some of the experienced officers, and his appointment creates resentment. Veterans doubt whether a man with a scholarly reputation and limited public record as supreme commander can manage the emergency. Lu Xun responds with discipline rather than theatrical displays of bravery. He resists provocations and allows Liu Bei's army to extend its position. His refusal to fight on the enemy's terms looks like weakness to those who judge leadership by immediate action.

## The army that follows a grieving emperor

When Liu Bei advances against Wu, his followers must turn an emperor's anger into a campaign. They need staging grounds, ships where rivers must be crossed, provisions sufficient for an extended advance, and arrangements to maintain contact between units moving through difficult terrain. A ruler may name revenge as his purpose, but his army cannot fight with a purpose alone. Every decision to move forward creates another line of wagons and another vulnerability for the enemy to examine.

The southern government does not face the invasion with complete internal harmony. Sun Quan must decide how to explain the death of Guan Yu and the seizure of Jing, and whether diplomatic overtures can avert a devastating war between states that still share a northern opponent. Liu Bei's demand for punishment is not easily satisfied, because the southern court cannot casually discard the commanders and decisions upon which its own survival depends. Reconciliation may be rational in the long run while politically intolerable in the immediate aftermath of war.

Lu Xun, entrusted with resisting the advance, confronts a different pressure. Senior officers may regard him as young or untested in command of such responsibility. They expect bold counterattacks and fear that withdrawing or holding positions will make them appear cowardly. Lu Xun must defend patience as a form of action. He cannot merely wait without a plan; he must understand why an advancing enemy becomes vulnerable when pushed farther from supply and when the conditions of his encampment worsen.

The novel places an older ruler driven by memory against a younger commander able to measure the present situation without being bound by the same personal grief. This is a compelling contrast, but it should not tempt us to turn Liu Bei into a fool who has forgotten everything he learned. His past contains real courage, tenacity, and strategic success. His defeat is tragic because the same commitment that once sustained him now distorts the scale of the decisions before him.

## The temptation to win today

Lu Xun's strategy requires his officers to endure uncertainty. A subordinate sees an opportunity and wants to attack. The commander orders restraint. Another fears that failing to fight will damage morale. Lu Xun accepts the criticism because the enemy's current position may become less favorable with time. Liu Bei's troops are camped along a difficult line. Summer conditions make exposed ground uncomfortable, and the arrangement of tents and stores may become vulnerable to fire.

This is the inverse of Zhang Fei's command problem. Zhang Fei imposes impossible demands and loses the confidence of subordinates. Lu Xun demands patience and wins a victory because he can keep his officers from spoiling the timing. Both exercise authority under pressure, but the nature of the pressure is different. The good commander must distinguish a real opportunity from a provocation designed to induce an error. The novel has often celebrated an attack made at exactly the right moment. Here it celebrates the harder preliminary skill of waiting until the moment exists.

Liu Bei's decision to camp in a series of positions becomes disastrous. The exact historical arrangements should not be reduced to the novel's dramatic picture of hundreds of connected camps, but the general strategic vulnerability is clear. A long, exposed deployment creates many points that can be attacked, and a fire assault may turn local disorder into a rout. Lu Xun eventually commits. Wu forces strike with fire and speed, and Shu's extended position collapses. The western army suffers immense losses in men, equipment, and confidence.

## The long camp and the short season

The catastrophe at Yiling develops from the way an army occupies ground. Liu Bei's forces extend their encampments through terrain that offers shade and relief from summer conditions. A commander who sees exhausted soldiers may understandably permit them to settle in more comfortable positions. But a chain of camps may become difficult to reinforce. Forest and dry vegetation can carry fire; a defender familiar with routes may strike several positions before their neighbors realize what is happening.

Lu Xun's decision to wait before launching the destructive attack is essential. He observes the enemy until its dispositions create an opportunity that cannot be recovered by a sudden change of orders. A hasty strike against an alert and concentrated force could fail. An attack against tired and separated troops may set off a retreat that the attackers can then pursue. The novel's fire scenes recall Red Cliffs, but the roles have changed: an army fighting for Liu Bei now suffers the kind of disaster that once preserved his future.

Fire in these stories is both a weapon and an image. It moves rapidly, obscures sight, produces panic, and turns an ordered camp into dangerous ground. It also creates the narrative sensation of a moral order reversing itself. Liu Bei once survived when Zhou Yu and his allies burned Cao Cao's vessels. Now the southern state burns the camps of Liu Bei. The audience cannot preserve a comfortable division between the side that uses clever stratagems and the side that is defeated by them.

The retreat is a political disaster as well as a military one. Veterans are lost, equipment is abandoned, and confidence in the emperor's judgment is shaken. A defeated force may still possess talented soldiers, but rebuilding it requires resources and time that its enemies may not grant. Liu Bei's western government survives, yet the dream of recovering Jing through immediate revenge is broken.

## The second fire and the reversed lesson

Yiling becomes an echo of Red Cliffs. At Red Cliffs, the southern alliance used fire to defeat Cao Cao, a northern invader unfamiliar with the environment. At Yiling, Wu uses fire against Liu Bei, once an ally in that earlier victory. The technique has no permanent moral owner. It can rescue a threatened state or destroy an invasion led by the novel's preferred hero. A narrator who equated victory with righteousness would have difficulty with the symmetry. The book allows it, and the contrast is among its most powerful structural choices.

The fires also reverse the reputation of the principals. Liu Bei has spent years growing from fugitive to emperor, advised by celebrated strategists. Lu Xun enters as a commander doubted by his own side. The younger man wins because he evaluates the actual disposition of forces. The older ruler loses because personal urgency has drawn him into a position his advisers warned against. The novel's affection for Liu Bei cannot erase the result. The fate of soldiers is determined by the ruler's choices, not by the moral attractiveness of his earlier career.

Liu Bei escapes with difficulty toward Baidi, commonly associated with White Emperor City. The campaign has damaged the army that secured his kingdom and worsened the political relation with Wu. Revenge has not restored Guan Yu or Zhang Fei. Nor has it retaken the lost strategic corridor. The living are left to bear the cost of a war undertaken partly on behalf of the dead.

## A maze of stones

The novel gives Zhuge Liang a remarkable intervention after the defeat: the Eight Arrays formation, associated with stones or an arrangement of terrain that confuses a pursuing commander. The episode magnifies the strategist's intellectual influence even when he is far from the battlefield. A Wu force that seems ready to finish the pursuit becomes trapped in an elaborate arrangement and must seek a way out. The scene belongs to the novel's increasingly marvelous portrayal of Zhuge Liang's knowledge.

There is a serious conceptual point beneath the marvel. A commander who arranges obstacles, channels movement, and conceals routes may turn terrain into an active military instrument. An army pursuing a defeated enemy can become careless precisely because victory seems assured. The novel exaggerates these possibilities into near-supernatural design. It also offers a consoling image after Liu Bei's failure: the cause still possesses an adviser capable of recognizing and restraining danger that the emperor himself could not control.

But the Eight Arrays story should not make us forget the scale of the loss. A clever defensive device cannot restore all the men and resources destroyed by the fire. Literature often presents a small brilliant trick after a great defeat, inviting the audience to admire the survivor's ingenuity. A careful reading keeps the costs in view. Liu Bei's kingdom has emerged from the campaign weakened, and no symbol of strategic genius makes the campaign wise in retrospect.

## Last words at Baidi

Liu Bei becomes gravely ill. He summons Zhuge Liang and entrusts his young heir Liu Shan to the minister's care. In the novel, he goes so far as to say that if the son proves unable to rule, Zhuge Liang should take the position himself. This is an extraordinary statement from a founder who has spent decades asserting dynastic legitimacy. It appears to elevate the competence of a trusted minister above the inheritance rights of the imperial family.

The scene can be read in several ways. Liu Bei may be expressing complete confidence in Zhuge Liang's integrity, knowing the minister will refuse to usurp the throne. He may be acknowledging that the realm needs effective government more than a particular family member's prestige. He may also be testing the minister with a declaration that obliges him publicly to affirm loyalty to the heir. The novel gives the exchange deep emotional power because Zhuge Liang has served the founder through victories and disasters and now must keep the cause alive without him.

Liu Bei dies in 223. His state passes to Liu Shan, whose capacity is uncertain, and Zhuge Liang becomes the chief figure responsible for its survival. The older generation of sworn brothers has vanished. Guan Yu fell at Maicheng, Zhang Fei was murdered by subordinates, and Liu Bei has died after a catastrophic revenge campaign. The novel's long celebration of personal brotherhood ends not in a jointly achieved empire but in grief, failure, and the transfer of obligations to people who did not participate in the original vow.

## The ruler who does not return home

Liu Bei retreats to Baidi, near the approaches to his western territory. The novel slows its pace because the consequences are no longer easily represented by a battlefield. A man who sought to avenge a brother has lost the campaign, and another brother has died before he could join it. His military and political legacy now depends on advisers who warned against the course he chose. The distance from the capital gives the deathbed scene a feeling of unfinished return.

Zhuge Liang comes to receive the ruler's final instructions. Their conversation concerns the young successor and the future of Shu. The novel depicts Liu Bei as granting extraordinary trust to the minister, even suggesting that Zhuge Liang should take responsibility if the heir cannot fulfill the office. The precise interpretation of this statement has been much discussed. It can be heard as an expression of confidence, a political test, or an effort to bind the minister to an especially demanding duty. In the story, it becomes the moral foundation of Zhuge Liang's subsequent regency.

The scene is moving because Liu Bei cannot solve his own succession problem through force. The child who was rescued in the retreat at Changban is now the adult heir upon whom the regime must depend. The founder has to entrust him to another man, accepting that loyalty after death cannot be enforced by personal supervision. All that remains is the network of character, institutions, and remembered obligations built during his life.

The deathbed also returns us to the problem of rulers who identify the state's future with their own immediate feelings. Liu Bei's final concern is the continuation of his cause, but his last campaign has diminished its resources. Zhuge Liang's next task is to repair the damage, not to celebrate the founder's anger. A minister may honor a dead ruler best by declining to repeat his mistakes.

## A last conversation between a ruler and a minister

At Baidi, Liu Bei's trust in Zhuge Liang is striking because it comes after the minister's strategic judgment has been vindicated against the ruler's. Zhuge Liang warned against the revenge campaign, and the defeat has reduced the state he must now defend. The dying ruler cannot pretend that this loss did not occur. He also cannot personally lead the recovery. The conversation therefore passes political responsibility from the man who created the state to the man whose task is to preserve it under a less capable successor.

The heir's position is difficult. Liu Shan inherits a name and an office, but much of the regime's authority is attached to the reputation of the founding emperor and his extraordinarily respected minister. If Zhuge Liang uses that reputation to displace the heir, he destroys the very dynastic continuity he has promised to protect. If he declines every decision out of excessive deference, the young sovereign's government may become incapable of responding to danger. His task is to exercise power in a way that keeps the institution around the heir functioning.

The novel makes this a question of exemplary service, but the constitutional problem is real. An officer who is more competent than the sovereign must decide how to use that competence without treating the state as his private possession. This obligation is demanding precisely because others may praise him for wielding power so effectively. Zhuge Liang's later reputation rests partly on the belief that he bore that responsibility without converting it into a new dynastic claim.

## Repairing a war of brothers

Zhuge Liang must address the consequences of Liu Bei's choices. Shu cannot endure a permanent war with Wu while Cao Wei remains stronger in the north. The old strategic logic therefore requires reconciliation. Sun Quan also has reasons to reduce tensions: his regime must defend northern frontiers and cannot assume Wei will remain a harmless partner. Diplomacy resumes, and a relationship badly damaged by the loss of Jing and the campaign of revenge is rebuilt on practical interests.

The novel gives diplomatic episodes that are sometimes playful, involving learned exchanges and contests of verbal intelligence. They show that politics has returned from the immediate violence of revenge to the work of maintaining arrangements. The diplomats do not need to love one another. They need to understand why another war would be costly and what promises each side can reasonably keep.

The repair does not undo history. Jing remains lost to Shu, and Liu Bei's followers cannot bring back their dead. But it restores the possibility of coordinated resistance against the northern power. If the Longzhong Plan depended on a stable eastern partnership, the state must now achieve part of that design without the territory once thought essential to it. The best available policy has changed because the world has changed.


## A diplomatic letter after the flames

Once Liu Bei is dead, Shu and Wu need a new arrangement. The northern state remains stronger than either of them in isolation, and the invasion has demonstrated how easily their rivalry can exhaust them. Diplomacy now requires the survivors to explain a reversal: the side recently attacked must be asked to cooperate again, and Shu must accept that the territories it hoped to recover will not simply be returned.

Zhuge Liang's approach to renewal is therefore neither sentimental nor effortless. He must persuade officials who lost family members or positions in the war that strategic survival demands a different emphasis. The Sun court must believe that Shu will not resume the invasion as soon as its army recovers. An agreement has to make future conduct more predictable even though neither partner can remove the motives that caused the earlier rupture.

Envoys become as important to the story as warriors. They must carry proposals, interpret tones, and respond to accusations without turning a single disrespectful phrase into a renewed crisis. The novel gives some of these exchanges the shape of contests in wit, but the work beneath them is a practical reorganization of interstate relations. Two governments must learn to coexist while both continue to consider themselves defenders of a broader political order.

The repair of the alliance is an example of statecraft that receives less applause than the fire at Yiling but may do more to preserve lives. Neither side obtains everything it wants; each is asked to accept an arrangement whose imperfections are preferable to immediate war. In a narrative crowded with vows made to be fulfilled at any cost, the capacity to revise a disastrous course is itself a form of courage.

## The surviving state and its burden of memory

By the close of this movement, the founding generation of Shu has nearly vanished. Guan Yu and Zhang Fei are dead. Liu Bei is buried. Zhuge Liang remains, together with senior officers and officials whose task is to transform a personal following into a state capable of surviving its founder. The novel invites melancholy because the reader remembers the early days when the three sworn brothers possessed little except their promise. The contrast with imperial ceremonies and elaborate governments measures both their achievement and their losses.

It is tempting to claim that the oath caused the disaster, but that would be too simple. Personal loyalty kept Liu Bei's cause alive through years when the opportunity to construct a government seemed remote. Without it, there may have been no Shu state to defend. The tragedy is that a commitment useful for building trust within a small band can later conflict with the obligations of a ruler responsible for an entire population.

This change of scale is among the novel's deepest political questions. What virtues enable a leader to gather companions? What different virtues enable him to govern citizens who never promised him affection? When are those virtues compatible, and when must one be restrained by another? The loss at Yiling does not make friendship worthless. It shows that the affection of a ruler can become destructive when no institution or trusted counsel can limit the public consequences of private grief.

The next generation inherits both the achievements and the damaged possibilities of the founders. The western basin, the northern frontier, and the southern alliance survive. Their survival gives Zhuge Liang a platform from which to attempt the ambition Liu Bei did not fulfill. But the form of that ambition will change. The great personality at the center of the early tale yields to a minister whose reputation is built on work, planning, restraint, and the willingness to bear responsibilities whose successful completion may be beyond his lifetime.


## What survives an oath

The peach-garden vow is usually remembered as a celebration of exemplary friendship. The Yiling campaign makes it difficult to admire without qualification. Liu Bei felt an obligation to avenge his brother. But the public consequences fell upon soldiers, farmers, and families throughout his territory. An emperor has special duties that cannot be discharged solely by honoring commitments made in private life. The novel's hero becomes most human at the point where those duties collide and he chooses badly.

Zhuge Liang inherits both the institutions and the moral burden. His loyalty is now owed to a state that may outlive its founder, and to a young ruler whose personal capacities are uncertain. He must preserve administration, restore alliances, and decide how much effort to expend on the larger dream of recovering the north. Those tasks require patience and repeated choices under constraints. The grand actions of the first generation have given way to the work of maintaining a vulnerable kingdom.

At Yiling, the novel has refused the easy consolation that honorable intention produces honorable results. The battle honors Lu Xun's discipline and reveals Liu Bei's misjudgment, even while the narrative retains its emotional allegiance to the fallen emperor. Baidi then asks whether a political project can survive the death of the man whose character originally gave it meaning. The answer will depend on Zhuge Liang, on the people he can trust, and on the practical institutions he can build. The realm remains divided, and the cause of Han restoration has become both more established and more remote.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 81–86. The deaths of Zhang Fei and Liu Bei, Lu Xun's victory at Yiling, and the Baidi guardianship of Liu Shan have historical bases. Evaluate the literary scale of the camp fire, Eight Arrays scene, and wording of the deathbed charge against the historiographic record; explanations of Liu Bei's motives should be identified as interpretation.
