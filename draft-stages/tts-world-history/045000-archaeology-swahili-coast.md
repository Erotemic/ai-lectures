# The Swahili Coast: Excavating a History Written in Houses, Pots, and Coins

Imagine arriving by sea at Kilwa Kisiwani, an island off the coast of what is now Tanzania, sometime in the fourteenth century. From the boat you might see coral-stone buildings and the outline of a mosque. You might hear Swahili spoken in the streets and encounter merchants whose business connects this coast to the African interior, Arabia, India, and beyond. But imagine that you are an archaeologist arriving centuries later. The buildings have partly collapsed. A pottery fragment has no label naming its owner. A copper coin bears a ruler's name, but it might have spent two hundred years moving from hand to hand. How would you decide whose city this was, where its wealth came from, and how it changed?

There is an old temptation to look at the imported goods, the mosques, and the Indian Ocean connections, and conclude that the cities must have been founded by outsiders. It is a bad inference. Goods move farther than the people who make them. Religions cross political boundaries. Local builders adopt, adapt, and invent architectural forms. The Swahili language is a Bantu language, and archaeological evidence places coastal urban development within a much longer East African history. The coast also received people and influences from overseas. The task is to reconstruct both processes without allowing either to erase the other.

This is the kind of question that changed as archaeologists began excavating houses and ordinary deposits rather than treating impressive buildings and imported porcelain as the entire history. We will follow some of the original researchers, including Neville Chittick at Kilwa, Mark Horton at Shanga, Stephanie Wynne-Jones and Jeffrey Fleisher at Kilwa and Songo Mnara, and the teams that developed new dating and environmental methods. We will examine how an urban history is assembled out of apparently modest clues: potsherds, building foundations, coins buried under floors, animal bones, soil chemistry, and the position of a mosque. Along the way, we will consider what archaeology adds to the written accounts and what it still cannot know.

## The coast before the stone towns

The expression Swahili coast commonly refers to a chain of communities along the eastern African shore and adjacent islands, from southern Somalia through Kenya and Tanzania toward northern Mozambique, though particular definitions vary by period and by historian. Their languages, political organizations, and relationships were not identical. Kiswahili grew from African Bantu roots and developed through centuries of contact. Calling the region a connected world should not suggest one kingdom governing every port or one moment when its history began.

Older European writing sometimes described stone-built Islamic cities as foreign colonies planted on an African coast supposedly incapable of producing such urban cultures. The claim acquired plausibility because medieval travel accounts referred to links with Arabia and Persia, and because elites at some towns told genealogical stories connecting themselves to ancestors across the sea. Yet a foundation story can assert prestige or a political claim. It does not automatically describe the origins of every household in a town. To understand that distinction, archaeologists must look beneath the monuments.

At Shanga, on Pate Island in the Lamu archipelago of Kenya, Mark Horton and collaborators excavated a long sequence of settlement. Horton's 1996 monograph, prepared with colleagues including Helen Brown and Nina Mudida, traces development from an eighth-century community with buildings made largely from perishable materials to a later town with mosques and stone architecture. The early inhabitants were already participating in coastal networks. The growth of monumental architecture was gradual and locally situated. There was no need to insert an entire imported population to explain each new building material.

A date such as the eighth century does not come from merely looking at a mosque and deciding that it looks old. Excavators relate walls, floors, pits, and refuse deposits to one another, then combine artifacts and scientific dating evidence. A ceramic type found in a sealed layer can narrow a sequence; radiocarbon dates from appropriate material can constrain it independently. Each conclusion depends on knowing whether an item was deposited when the floor was used or introduced by later disturbance. At a settled coastal site, rebuilding may have cut through older deposits many times.

The evidence from Shanga also includes locally made pottery. Imported wares are useful for identifying long-distance connections because their production centers can sometimes be recognized. But the bulk of everyday ceramic fragments at a settlement often came from nearer home. Looking only at the Chinese or Persian pieces would be like describing a modern city entirely from its imported electronics while ignoring the kitchens, street repairs, and local industries that make daily life possible.

Researchers studying other coastal settlements have similarly argued for important continuities between the coast and its hinterland. Stephanie Wynne-Jones's work on Kilwa, including her study of the town's early urban communities, uses pottery distributions and settlement patterns to bring inland connections into the picture. The coast cannot be reconstructed simply as the maritime edge of someone else's civilization. The people living there cultivated, fished, built, governed, and spoke in local traditions while participating in a very large trading world.

## Kilwa: the city and its chronologies

Kilwa Kisiwani is a particularly revealing case because its ruins are substantial and several kinds of written history refer to it. There are accounts collectively called the Kilwa Chronicles, known through different transmissions; medieval travelers, including Ibn Battuta, also described the coast. These sources are invaluable. They are also selective. A ruler's ancestry may matter greatly to a court chronicle while telling us little about the person who made a fishing net or cooked for a household.

Neville Chittick directed major excavations at Kilwa in the 1960s, and his published reports remain important records of architecture, stratigraphy, coins, and ceramics. His research helped establish the town's phases and relationships with Indian Ocean commerce. It also shows why archaeological knowledge is cumulative. Later teams can return to a trench location, inspect a different kind of material, and develop an improved chronology without implying that the earlier excavation was pointless.

Consider a copper coin carrying the name of a Kilwa ruler. Its inscription may identify an issuer. It cannot, by itself, establish exactly when it fell to the ground. Coins may circulate long after the reign they commemorate. A piece may be kept for ritual purposes, redeposited during construction, or arrive in fill taken from elsewhere. If we date a house simply by the name on the oldest coin found in it, we risk building a chronology on the object's manufacturing date rather than its final use.

In 2022, Mark Horton, Jesper Olsen, Jeffrey Fleisher, and Stephanie Wynne-Jones published a new high-resolution dating study for Kilwa Kisiwani. Their team analyzed a sequence sampled near an influential earlier excavation, combined radiocarbon determinations with archaeological stratigraphy, and modeled the possible timing of phases. The resulting chronology placed the earliest coral buildings and coin use toward the end of the tenth century and illuminated episodes of later change. This is a demonstration of how a chronology is improved: not by choosing a favorite written king list but by allowing several kinds of evidence to constrain each other.

An archaeologist's calibrated date is rarely a single year written on the material. Radiocarbon measurements come with uncertainty, and the conversion between radiocarbon years and calendar years is not always simple. If several samples occur in a known stratigraphic order, statistical modeling can use that order to estimate the timing of transitions more precisely. It is important that the order is established from excavation and not invented from the dates. The model becomes stronger when the assumptions are visible and can be challenged.

Kilwa's archaeological history also helps us read the Chronicles critically. The existence of dynastic coins supports the fact that rulers made public claims to authority. Agreement among ruler names, architecture, and dated deposits can support particular historical phases. But a sequence of coins cannot verify every ancestor named in a prestigious genealogy. Neither can a foreign visitor's praise or criticism stand in for the experience of everyone living in the town. The archaeology adds a wider population to the picture, although that population often remains anonymous.

## The house with coins in its foundations

Across the water from Kilwa Kisiwani lies Songo Mnara, another coral-stone town. Research there has produced one of the clearest examples of why an object's position is part of its meaning. Jeffrey Fleisher and Stephanie Wynne-Jones led work investigating houses, open areas, and the ways people used urban space. Their publications are collaborative: survey specialists, excavators, ceramics analysts, conservation workers, and Tanzanian partners made the evidence possible. A project website or journal article may name principal investigators, but a complete history of research must also recognize the work of field teams and local communities.

Archaeologists recovered a deposit of more than three hundred Kilwa-type copper coins in a defined context associated with the foundations of a stone house. John Perkins, Fleisher, and Wynne-Jones published the assemblage in detail. The discovery is valuable partly because the coins were not merely isolated objects whose original positions had been lost. Their association with the building gives an opportunity to ask what people were doing with money and metal at the moment a house took shape.

A quick interpretation might call this a merchant's savings, accidentally lost. But the relationship to the foundations suggests other possibilities. The coins may have been placed deliberately, building value into a house as a social or commemorative act. Associated materials, including a carnelian necklace, complicate the simple image of coins as interchangeable pieces of cash. They were objects used in social practices as well as instruments of exchange. A coin can carry a ruler's claim in its inscription and a household's action in the archaeological context in which it was deposited.

Here is the evidentiary argument in slow motion. First, excavation records where the coins were found relative to the construction layers. Second, their inscriptions, manufacturing traditions, and wear offer information about origin and circulation. Third, the building's archaeological sequence constrains when the deposit entered the ground. Only then can researchers propose why it was placed there. The final step is interpretive, and more than one explanation may remain possible. But without the preceding steps there would be little basis for even choosing among explanations.

This case also corrects a familiar economic assumption. Finding coins does not mean that every exchange was monetized or that each coin's purchasing power is obvious to us. People can use coins to pay, save, display, dedicate, remember, or establish claims. In a town tied to merchants, farmers, fisherfolk, and regional producers, different exchanges may use different standards of value. Coins give an unusually legible trace because someone intentionally marked them with names or symbols, yet they never give us a complete ledger of the local economy.

Wynne-Jones and Fleisher also studied how houses opened toward courtyards and public spaces. A spectacular mosque may dominate a visitor's view, but pathways and thresholds reveal another urban landscape: where people congregated, how domestic activities met public life, and how movement was organized. Archaeologists infer possible uses from architecture together with associated deposits. A courtyard is not automatically a market, and a room containing imported porcelain is not automatically the home of a foreign merchant. Those conclusions need multiple pieces of evidence.

## Soil chemistry, meals, and the invisible city

Not every archaeological result arrives in the form of a beautiful coin. At Songo Mnara, Jeffrey Fleisher and collaborators, including F. Sulas, used geophysical survey, soil chemistry, and the distribution of excavated artifacts to study spaces whose purposes were not clear from standing walls. A magnetometer may reveal a buried boundary or activity area. Soil chemical signatures can record concentrations of past materials, although they do not label themselves 'kitchen' or 'market.' Microbotanical evidence such as phytoliths can help identify plants that were processed or deposited.

Suppose an open area contains elevated phosphorus, ash, cooking debris, and certain plant traces. It is reasonable to investigate whether food preparation or communal eating occurred there. Yet phosphorus can also accumulate from animals or other refuse. A single chemical measurement is not a meal. Researchers compare patterns across space and against controls, then examine whether the traces make sense alongside pottery, bones, and architecture. This combination is sometimes called a multi-proxy argument: different evidence types address the same proposed activity.

We can learn a lot from studying what ordinary people ate and discarded. Imported luxury pottery may demonstrate a connection with the far end of the Indian Ocean. Fish bones, locally made pots, grinding equipment, and crop traces may reveal a town's day-to-day dependence on coastal ecosystems and inland producers. Archaeologists are constrained by preservation, however. Acidic soils can dissolve bones. Fishing gear made from organic fibers may perish. Changes in excavation methods can alter which tiny fragments enter an archaeological catalogue. A low count of one food resource may reflect the conditions of recovery as well as ancient consumption.

Marine archaeology broadens the question. Edward Pollard, Fleisher, Wynne-Jones, and others have investigated waterfront structures and maritime spaces around Songo Mnara. A city on the sea has a geography of landing places, channels, currents, and tidal flats, even if its surviving stone houses occupy the center of attention. Some parts of that geography change seasonally. Others change over centuries because sand moves and sea levels shift. Reconstructing a harbor requires connecting the architecture on land to the coastal environment in which boats actually operated.

The archaeology challenges the visual stereotype that a Swahili town should be understood only as a picturesque cluster of coral houses. There were people in earth-and-thatch houses as well as stone houses. In some places stone architecture became important relatively late. The buildings we see above ground are the portions of the city that lasted best, and their survival skews the histories we tell. An archaeologist who samples only the most durable monuments is selecting for the richest or most conspicuous parts of life.

## Imported ceramics and what they do not prove

Consider the famous Chinese ceramics recovered from medieval East African coastal sites. A piece of glazed porcelain may be recognizable as a product of a particular manufacturing tradition, allowing an archaeologist to say that an object made far away eventually reached the town. Such an object is strong evidence for a material connection. Yet it does not come with a complete biography. We rarely know the merchant who purchased it first, how many ports it passed through, or whether it was brought by a ship arriving directly from China. If it was cherished for generations, the date of its manufacture may be substantially earlier than the deposit in which it was found.

Researchers identify ceramic production traditions by comparing clay fabric, glaze, form, decorative techniques, and, where possible, compositional analysis. Geological and technological signatures can sometimes narrow likely sources. The argument becomes better when it uses many fragments, secure excavation contexts, and dated production sequences. A painted motif by itself is weaker evidence because decorative ideas can be copied. Even material made from the same raw clay can circulate through different commercial networks. The job is to build a distribution history, not to assign every attractive fragment to the merchant from the nearest familiar story.

There is an even more basic question about percentages. Suppose one excavation trench yields five imported potsherds and ninety-five local ones. Another yields twenty imported pieces and eighty local ones. Would we conclude that the second household had four times as much international trade? Not without knowing how the trenches were selected, how the pottery was counted, which periods the deposits represent, and whether a single broken vessel accounts for many sherds. A household that kept one imported bowl for fifty years might leave less imported pottery than a merchant's storeroom that broke several in one afternoon. The archaeological sample records loss and discard as well as ownership.

In the large 2023 study of medieval coastal ancestry, the authors point out that imported objects often represent a small minority of assemblages. That observation accords with archaeologists' emphasis on local production. But the minority status of imports does not make international trade economically negligible. A small quantity of high-value goods can sustain powerful relationships, and some exported goods, including organic products, may survive poorly. A measured proportion of ceramic fragments describes an excavated assemblage, not the share of a city that earned its living from international exchange.

This is where the Swahili case teaches a broader historical method. Evidence of imported things can be used to establish contact. Locally made things can help reconstruct everyday practice. Neither category mechanically establishes ancestry, language, or political loyalty. A better picture emerges by asking what the objects were used for, where they entered the archaeological record, how their distribution varies among households, and whether the same pattern occurs through time. An exceptional plate is interesting. A carefully documented town full of ordinary pottery is an archive.

## A language, a genealogy, and ancient DNA

Language and genetic ancestry answer different historical questions. Kiswahili's structure and vocabulary establish that it is a Bantu language shaped through contact, including major Arabic influence. That is linguistic evidence, not a DNA test. A gene cannot tell us which language someone used at home. Conversely, an imported word cannot tell us whether the person who first introduced it settled, married locally, or departed on the next ship.

In 2023, a large collaborative team including Chapurukha Kusimba and many geneticists, archaeologists, and historians published an ancient-DNA study of medieval and early modern individuals from several coastal sites. Their results indicated mixtures of ancestry associated, in their statistical models, with African and Asian populations, with important regional and temporal variation. The study drew attention because some medieval genealogical traditions claim origins across the Indian Ocean. But the genetic results cannot transform those narratives into literal population lists or identify the ancestors of every Swahili speaker.

Sampling matters. Burials recoverable for DNA analysis are not a random census. The published study itself notes that many sampled graves were prominent and could overrepresent particular social groups. The ancestry of an elite burial population may differ from that of craft workers or rural neighbors. Statistical models depend on comparison populations and the availability of ancient DNA from different places and periods. The vocabulary of 'Persian-related ancestry' describes relationships in those models, not a named medieval traveler whom researchers have identified by reading his diary.

More importantly, a community's identity is not settled by ancestry proportions. Archaeology, language, oral history, food, architecture, and social practice have to be interpreted together. A person born on the coast to parents of different origins could speak Swahili, inhabit a local house, practice Islam, and belong fully to that city's society. The question of where the Swahili cities came from is better answered by reconstructing the historical communities that built and sustained them, rather than awarding their achievements to a category defined elsewhere.

This is a case where later scientific methods add genuine evidence but also demand restraint. A sample can measure aspects of biological ancestry under explicit assumptions. It does not directly reveal a person's memories, political loyalties, occupations, or the social meanings attached to family origins. That distinction is essential when research is entangled with older arguments about who counts as the author of a civilization.

## Who gets named in the excavation report?

Archaeological attribution poses its own historical problem. We can name Chittick, Horton, Fleisher, Wynne-Jones, Olsen, Perkins, and many colleagues because their publications survive and are searchable. But field archaeology also depends on Tanzanian and Kenyan specialists, workers, residents, officials, and communities who help identify sites, conduct excavation, safeguard collections, and interpret landscapes. Their contributions have not always been recorded with equal care in the published literature.

This should change how we tell the history of the research. Credit for an original interpretation goes to its author or team. Credit for the material recovery must include the field operation that made the interpretation possible. And the authority to care for archaeological places is not supplied by a distant journal. Communities and heritage institutions in the countries where the sites stand have ongoing responsibilities and legitimate interests in what happens to excavated objects and places.

Wynne-Jones and Fleisher have published specifically on conservation and community archaeology at Songo Mnara. That work is part of the evidence history because archaeological projects can alter the structures they excavate, expose delicate remains to weather, and attract new tourism and development pressures. The best possible account of a city will be a poor outcome if the process of obtaining it accelerates the loss of the city itself.

It is worth remembering that an archaeologist's excavation is also an event in the site's history. After a trench is dug, the original layering cannot simply be put back. Field notes, samples, photographs, and collections become the evidence future researchers have to use. The careful recording of a coin deposit, or an overlooked sample of house-floor sediment, may be what allows a new question to be answered a century later.

## What we can now say

The Swahili coast was a set of African societies deeply engaged with the Indian Ocean. Archaeologists see local settlement histories, local pottery and production, varied housing traditions, Islamic religious institutions, imported goods, coinage, and shifting social practices. They see towns participating in overseas commerce without becoming mere appendages of overseas powers. They also see long-standing contacts and movements of people across the sea that cannot be reduced to decorative influences.

At Kilwa, excavated sequences and radiocarbon models help date phases of urban development. At Shanga, houses and mosques document a changing settlement over many generations. At Songo Mnara, coins placed in house foundations reveal a social practice that an isolated object in a museum case could barely suggest. Scientific work on soil, food remains, and ancestry gives us still other dimensions, each with its own interpretive limits.

The historical achievement is not that a single new discovery answered the question of Swahili origins. It is that careful research replaced an inherited assumption with an account detailed enough to include local continuity, international connection, political ambition, ordinary work, and historical change. A city tells us who made it through many small traces. The archaeologist's job is to connect them without erasing the people who left them, or the people who recovered them.

### Editorial cautions

- The Songo Mnara >300-coin deposit is distinct from smaller scattered coin finds; the deposit context supports an interpretation about foundation placement, not certainty about the participants' motives.
- Do not use genetic admixture components as ethnic labels for individual historical persons, and do not treat elite sampled burials as representative of everyone on the coast.
- Explicitly credit field and community contributors when identifiable in particular reports; do not invent names absent from the record.
