# Ancient DNA: Reading Molecules Without Inventing a People

A researcher holds a fragment of bone that has survived for centuries inside a burial. Under the right circumstances, some of the molecules inside it may still contain pieces of DNA. Most will be broken into short fragments. The majority of the material recovered may come from soil organisms rather than the person who was buried. Modern human DNA may have entered during excavation, handling, or laboratory work. If the researcher can distinguish the surviving ancient fragments from the rest, however, the sample may help answer questions no ancient text addresses.

Did individuals buried in different communities share recent ancestors? Did groups move between regions? Are genetic relationships consistent with a proposed migration, or do they point to a more complicated history? These questions make ancient DNA one of the most powerful tools available to archaeology. They also make it especially easy to overinterpret. A genome is not a diary. It cannot tell us what language someone spoke, what beliefs they held, or how they understood belonging to a community.

In this lecture, we will reconstruct the method from the beginning. We will meet the researchers who developed ways to retrieve old DNA, examine what laboratories do to identify contamination and molecular damage, and follow a worked example in which genetic data changed the understanding of an ancient population. We will then examine a study of medieval communities on the Swahili coast of Africa, where ancient DNA has been brought into conversation with archaeology, written accounts, and living communities. Our central theme is the difference between detecting biological ancestry and writing the history of people.

## Why an old bone is a difficult library

DNA is a molecule that carries genetic information. In living cells it is stored in long strands, supported by repair mechanisms. After death, the molecular environment changes. Enzymes and microbes break down tissues; DNA strands fragment; some chemical components change. Warm temperatures and water often accelerate decay. A bone may preserve its shape long after almost all usable DNA has gone. Two bones of similar archaeological age can yield dramatically different amounts of material.

This immediately explains why the simple instruction, sequence the skeleton, conceals a research program. A laboratory needs to distinguish human DNA from microbial DNA and from modern contamination. It needs to recover fragments short enough that ordinary approaches designed for fresh samples may not work well. It needs to record the sample's archaeological context. And it needs a way to decide whether the sequence data really originated from the ancient specimen.

One clue is the pattern of damage. Over time, certain DNA bases undergo chemical changes. Cytosine, for example, can be converted into uracil through deamination. In sequence data, these changes can create characteristic patterns of apparent substitutions, especially near the ends of short DNA fragments. Researchers can study fragment lengths and such damage signatures to assess whether a collection of molecules is consistent with age. None of these clues is infallible; samples and laboratory procedures differ. But a dataset containing appropriate damage patterns, supported by clean controls and independent measurements, makes an ancient origin more credible than a sequence printed without methodological explanation.

Contamination can be especially misleading in human studies because excavators, conservators, and geneticists are human. A stray modern skin cell may contain DNA that is easier to amplify than fragmented ancient molecules. For a time, early ancient-DNA studies generated excitement about extraordinary results later regarded as contamination or mistaken identification. The field became more reliable through increasingly careful clean-room procedures, authentication criteria, replication, and technological advances. Its history is partly the history of learning how to say, this apparent signal is probably not ancient.

## The beginnings of ancient DNA research

In 1984, a team led by Russell Higuchi reported retrieving DNA from a museum specimen of an extinct quagga, a relative of the zebra. In 1985, Svante Pääbo reported molecular cloning of DNA from an Egyptian mummy. These were important early experiments because they suggested that biological sequences might outlast the life of the organism that carried them. But early demonstrations were limited by fragment length, contamination, and the difficulty of knowing exactly what the cloned material represented. In a 2024 interview Pääbo himself reflected that his earliest mummy sequences were almost certainly contaminated by present-day DNA. That later self-correction is part of the science's history.

A landmark came in 1997 when Matthias Krings, Svante Pääbo, and collaborators published a sequence of mitochondrial DNA from a Neanderthal fossil. Mitochondrial DNA is present in many copies per cell, which made it an attractive early target, although it follows only one line of ancestry and cannot stand in for a full genome. The team compared the Neanderthal sequence with those of living humans and investigated the relationships among them. The result opened new questions while leaving most of the Neanderthal genome unexplored.

As sequencing technology developed, teams could recover far more fragments and compare them across the nuclear genome. A landmark 2010 Neanderthal genome draft, produced by the consortium led by Pääbo and colleagues including Richard Green, supplied evidence that present-day populations outside Africa generally have some Neanderthal ancestry. The analysis addressed relationships and past interbreeding using genetic patterns rather than relying only on anatomical comparisons of bones. The interpretation depended on statistical models of ancestry and on comparison with modern and ancient data, which later studies refined.

The field's progress was not simply a sequence of remarkable discoveries. It depended on chemical procedures, computational methods, reference databases, laboratory infrastructure, and the archaeology required to identify and date the specimens. The instrument reads fragments. Researchers then need to assemble a defensible argument about the history those fragments might reflect.

## From a bone to a sequence

Imagine a laboratory about to sample a tooth or part of a skull. Before anyone drills, the archaeological team must document the remains: location, associated artifacts, stratigraphic relationship, and dating evidence. Sampling destroys a small piece of material. The research therefore has to justify why the question is important, what sampling is permitted, and whether a less destructive method might answer it. Curators and descendant or associated communities may have specific interests or authority over the treatment of human remains.

Once a sample is authorized, personnel work to limit modern DNA contamination. Surfaces may be cleaned; specialized facilities are separated from spaces where amplified modern DNA is handled; protective clothing and equipment are used. Blank controls are processed alongside actual samples to detect contamination introduced by reagents or handling. Laboratory notebooks record what happened and when. A suspicious sequence discovered in the sample but also present in a blank control is not good evidence for an ancient genome.

The sample is powdered or otherwise processed, and DNA is chemically extracted. The surviving molecules are often short. Laboratory procedures attach known adapter sequences to the fragments so sequencing instruments can read them. Researchers may enrich a library for targeted genetic positions, or sequence fragments more broadly. Computational pipelines compare the reads against reference sequences, discard low-quality alignments, identify duplicates, assess damage patterns, and estimate the fraction of data that belongs to the target organism.

A laboratory report contains many numbers that should make the listener cautious about a simple headline. What proportion of reads aligned to the human genome? What fraction of the positions was covered? Were there enough data to estimate contamination? Was the specimen radiocarbon dated directly? Did the analysis rely on a few characteristic genetic sites or hundreds of thousands? How does a change in reference populations affect the interpretation? These questions can determine whether a proposed relationship is well supported or remains tentative.

## Why one part of the skull became important

The petrous portion of the temporal bone, which surrounds the inner ear, can preserve more endogenous ancient DNA than many other skeletal elements. In 2015, Ron Pinhasi, Daniel Fernandes, Kendra Sirak, and a large team reported a systematic comparison of parts of the petrous bone. They found especially high yields in the dense region of the inner ear capsule in their sampled archaeological specimens. Their article helped establish why researchers might prefer this material when trying to recover ancient genomes.

This methodological success creates a preservation dilemma. A highly promising part of a skeleton may also be culturally important, anatomically informative, and irreplaceable. If several laboratories want to drill into it, the specimen cannot be treated as a limitless supply of sample powder. There are strategies for limiting destruction and recording what was removed, but the core issue remains: information is extracted by consuming part of a human remain. Ethical responsibility and scientific opportunity have to be considered together.

Pinhasi and colleagues' result also has a technical limit. They compared samples under particular conditions; it would be incorrect to assume every petrous bone yields good DNA. Warm and humid burial environments remain difficult, preservation varies, and differences in laboratory treatment can affect results. The research gave scientists a better sampling strategy. It did not make old DNA universally recoverable.

## What can ancestry analysis actually establish?

Suppose a team sequences individuals buried at two sites separated by several centuries. The researchers compare genetic variants across many positions. If the later group shares patterns not common in the earlier sample but common in a different reference group, that can support a model involving ancestry from another population. Statistical tools can evaluate whether a proposed mixture of reference populations is consistent with the observed variation, within the assumptions of the model.

To make this concrete, imagine three population samples called A, B, and C. A set of ancient individuals from region C shows allele frequencies that fall between those of A and B at many sites. One possible explanation is ancestry from populations related to both A and B. But the words related to are important. A and B may be imperfect proxies for groups that actually existed at the time. Their own histories may involve admixture, drift, and movements. A fitted percentage can describe relationships in a particular model without being a literal count of ancestors crossing a political border.

Researchers test alternatives using shared genetic variation, statistical comparisons, and ancient samples from different periods. They also use family relationships, mitochondrial lineages, and Y-chromosome lineages when informative. Each type of data has limits. One individual's mitochondrial lineage follows a maternal line and does not represent every ancestor. Two genetically similar people could speak different languages, follow different religions, or belong to different political communities. Two genetically different people could share a culture and live in the same town. Population history and cultural history overlap, but they are different questions.

Sample size is a further constraint. The people whose remains are available may come from elite tombs, religious cemeteries, or sites with exceptional preservation. They may represent only certain generations or social groups. If archaeologists sample ten individuals from one burial ground, those ten genomes cannot automatically speak for every community across a huge region. More data can help, but only if sampling expands meaningfully across places, dates, and contexts.

## A worked example from Europe

In 2015, Wolfgang Haak, Iosif Lazaridis, Nick Patterson, David Reich, and a broad international team published genome-wide data from sixty-nine ancient Europeans who lived between roughly eight thousand and three thousand years ago. The study compared the genomes with other ancient and present-day samples. It found changes in the genetic ancestry represented in European populations over time, including a substantial contribution related to groups from the Eurasian steppe during the Late Neolithic and Bronze Age.

The important result was not that a genetic algorithm printed the word migration. It was that models relying only on continuity among earlier sampled populations were difficult to reconcile with the later genetic patterns. Introducing ancestry related to steppe populations provided a better explanation for the data. Archaeologists could then ask how that population change related to material cultures, settlement shifts, burial practices, and historical language hypotheses. The genetic result made some historical scenarios more plausible, but it did not independently reveal what each ancient person called their language.

The article's title linked the migration to a proposed source for Indo-European languages. That remains a question requiring linguistic evidence as well as genetics. A language may spread through intermarriage, elite institutions, political domination, or other processes, and no DNA marker labels a language. Genetic evidence can establish population connections that constrain the history of language spread; it cannot substitute for the study of words, grammar, and historical linguistic relationships.

The paper also illustrates why the reference panel matters. As additional ancient genomes become available, researchers can use populations closer to the time and place of the movement under study. Models can change as a result. A compelling early model may have described a real large-scale event but missed regional diversity or complex successive migrations. Scientific correction is expected when the sample of the past grows from dozens to thousands of individuals.

## The Swahili coast: genes, archives, and archaeology

Consider a second case where the sources are very different. Along the eastern African coast, Swahili-speaking communities built towns, mosques, households, and trading relationships across the Indian Ocean. Archaeology reveals local settlement histories, material production, and exchange. Medieval and later written accounts also preserve stories about origins and connections across the sea. Some historical interpretations treated those connections as evidence that coastal civilization must have been founded by outsiders. Archaeological research has shown the depth of African participation in the region's urban development.

In 2023, Esther Brielle, Jeffrey Fleisher, Stephanie Wynne-Jones, and a large team published ancient-DNA results from eighty individuals associated with medieval and early modern settlements along the Swahili coast, as well as an inland comparison. The sampled burials covered several towns and dates, broadly from the thirteenth through nineteenth centuries, with some comparisons from later periods. Their analysis found that many sampled coastal individuals had ancestry related both to African and to Asian populations. The inferred pattern of ancestry was not identical across people or towns.

The paper reported evidence that African female ancestors and Asian male ancestors contributed substantially to the ancestry of many sampled coastal individuals. This conclusion followed from genome-wide mixture models considered alongside patterns on sex-linked chromosomes and other genetic data. Such a result can inform questions about marriage, kinship, and the direction of earlier population movements. But it is not a record of any specific marriage. It does not identify the names, motives, legal status, or personal choices of individual ancestors hundreds of years before the sampled burials.

The study has particular significance because it can be compared with some Swahili oral and written traditions about connections across the Indian Ocean. Researchers can now test whether people in certain cemeteries had measurable ancestry linked to populations outside Africa. That strengthens some questions about historic mobility while leaving the interpretation of identity to a wider range of evidence. A person with mixed ancestry could be fully Swahili in language, religion, community, and self-understanding. Genetic similarity to a population elsewhere does not make them a stranger in the city where they lived.

The findings must also be kept in chronological perspective. The samples do not include every era in the formation of coastal towns. They do not tell us the genetic makeup of the earliest founders of each settlement. They cannot determine whether cultural practices were inherited, exchanged, or reinvented from the genetic patterns alone. Excavations of houses, mosques, ceramics, burials, and trade materials remain necessary to investigate the history of the coast.

This is also a story about research collaboration. The study brought together geneticists and archaeologists working on the African coast, including researchers with long experience excavating Swahili settlements. The details of burial selection, excavation histories, local permissions, and collaboration are part of the evidential record. We should consult who participated in sampling and who was included in decisions about the research. Modern genetic results can become politically charged when they are presented as a ranking of who belongs somewhere. Their historical value depends in part on resisting that misuse.

## What genes cannot tell us

A genome does not preserve a person's favorite story, occupation, religious observance, or political allegiance. It may contain information about biological relationships, some traits, and vulnerability to certain diseases. But those are not a substitute for a biography. Archaeologists have to integrate genetic data with graves, objects, settlement patterns, isotopic information, dates, and written or oral history where available. Genetic evidence is one witness among several.

There is also no genetic test for a timeless ethnic category. Names used for groups may change across centuries, and communities may form through shared language, residence, political allegiance, or other relationships rather than descent alone. The ancestry categories built into a model depend on the populations sampled and the questions asked. A computer can estimate an affinity with available reference groups, but that affinity should not be casually turned into a statement about an ancient individual's identity.

Imagine a skeleton buried in a town whose pottery, buildings, and inscriptions indicate one cultural setting. Its DNA resembles that of people from several regions. What should the historian conclude? The person had biological ancestors connected to those broader populations, within the resolution of the data. Beyond that, we need evidence about the person's life. A migrant, a locally born person with migrant grandparents, and someone whose ancestors arrived many generations earlier might have similar genome-wide ancestry profiles. The difference among those histories cannot always be recovered.

Equally, an absence of detected external ancestry is not proof of social isolation. Merchants could trade across great distances without having children together. Ideas can move without people, and people can adopt technologies without replacing their populations. The archaeological record of contact is not reducible to a migration model. Keeping that distinction clear helps genetics contribute to historical understanding rather than dominating it.

## Ethics are part of the method

Working with human remains raises responsibilities different from those involved in analyzing a pottery sherd. Some burials are sacred to living communities; some were excavated during colonial projects or under conditions of unequal power; some are held in museums far from where people were buried. Scientific permission from a curator may not settle the interests of descendant communities. Consultation, collaborative question-setting, and decisions about whether sampling should take place can change what responsible research looks like.

Sampling can also limit future science by consuming material from an irreplaceable specimen. A method that yields more DNA may tempt researchers to sample more aggressively, but high information yield is not an automatic ethical justification. Laboratories should document decisions and methods, and researchers should be clear about the limits of their authority. Questions about consent and stewardship should be discussed before a drill enters bone, rather than after results have already been published.

Interpretation brings another kind of responsibility. Ancestry estimates can be sensationalized as claims about racial purity, authentic ownership, or exclusive descent. Those inferences do not follow from the science. Human populations have complex, overlapping histories. Ancient DNA may reveal movement and mixture; it cannot justify ranking contemporary communities or replacing their own accounts of identity with a laboratory percentage.

An attentive researcher also credits the people behind the record. Laboratory scientists develop extraction and sequencing methods. Archaeologists establish the contexts. Local specialists and communities maintain site knowledge and permissions. Statistical analysts write and test models. Curators preserve remains and their documentation. A paper's author list offers one partial map of these contributions, but full field reports and ethical statements often explain more.

## Reconstructing a claim from beginning to end

Let's follow a single hypothetical result from an archaeological report to a historical conclusion. A burial is excavated and carefully dated. A small sample is authorized and collected with contamination controls. Genetic extraction yields damaged fragments consistent with age. A sequencing pipeline identifies hundreds of thousands of informative positions. Statistical tests suggest the individual has ancestry related to populations whose earliest available samples are found far away. Other individuals from the same burial ground show varying patterns.

What is now established? The individual has a genetic relationship, within the model's limits, to the comparison populations. That may support a history of mobility or admixture. What remains open? Whether the person personally traveled; which language they spoke; the social meaning of their ancestry; and the exact route and circumstances through which their ancestors moved. Isotope measurements from teeth might reveal childhood environments. Artifacts and inscriptions might supply clues about social life. Historical sources may describe relevant journeys or institutions. The strongest interpretation will show how these independent lines agree and where they remain in tension.

If a future genetic sample changes the model, the historical account should change with it. If a new archaeological date moves the burial centuries earlier, the chronology of a proposed migration may need revision. If descendant communities object to destructive analysis or dispute the project's framing, those concerns are part of evaluating the research practice, not a footnote unrelated to the evidence.

Ancient DNA has transformed historical inquiry because it allows some aspects of biological population history to be investigated directly. It has revealed relationships and movements that earlier researchers could only infer indirectly. Yet the molecules do not narrate their own past. People extract them, authenticate them, select models, and join them to archaeology. We know more because those methods can be tested, improved, and questioned. We understand people better when we remember that their histories were much larger than their genomes.

### Interpretation checks

- Ancestry modeled with available genetic reference populations is not the same thing as an individual's identity, language, or ethnicity.
- The 2023 Swahili study concerns sampled burials from particular places and periods; it is not a genetic census of all early Swahili communities.
- Sampling permissions, community consultation, provenance, and the destructive nature of DNA sampling are part of responsible method.
