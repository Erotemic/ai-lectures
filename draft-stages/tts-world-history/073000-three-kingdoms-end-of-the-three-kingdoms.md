# Three Kingdoms, Part XVI: The Fall of Shu and the End of the Three Kingdoms

The realm has been divided for decades. The men who first swore brotherhood in a peach garden are long dead. Their descendants and successors govern established states with court rituals, tax systems, garrisons, and histories of their own. The people born in the divided lands have no living memory of a unified Han empire. Yet the old promise still shapes decisions. In Shu, the westward kingdom founded by Liu Bei, Jiang Wei continues campaigns in the name of restoring the dynasty. In the north, the Sima family controls Wei and prepares to turn its resources toward ending the division. The last chapters of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* show the conquest of Shu in 263, the replacement of Wei by Jin in 265–266, and the fall of Wu in 280.

The end is not the triumph of the man whose moral claim the novel has most insistently favored. Liu Bei did not restore the Han empire. Zhuge Liang died without conquering the north. Jiang Wei's final schemes collapse amid mutiny and surrender. The eventual reunification comes under the descendants of Sima Yi, whose family gained control of the northern court by methods that resemble the rise of Cao Cao before him. The book's ending has a severity that a tale of heroic destiny might otherwise avoid. The state that survives is not necessarily the one whose cause has been most emotionally attractive.

## The last war begins before the armies cross the border

Shu's final crisis develops within a state that has survived for decades after Liu Bei and Zhuge Liang. Its soldiers have fought repeated northern campaigns, its officials have administered the western basin, and its ruler has grown old in office. The novel introduces court favorites and failures of judgment to explain why warnings are ignored. The familiar pattern is clear: a ruler believes reassuring messages, experienced advisers struggle to be heard, and the machinery of defense becomes weaker than its ceremonies suggest.

It would be inadequate to make one favorite the sole cause of a state's defeat. Shu faces a larger northern power with substantial resources, and the choice to continue aggressive campaigns has imposed costs. Yet court politics affects how limited resources are used. Commanders need accurate reports from the frontier, reliable appointments, and permission to prepare defenses before an invasion. A court in which access is controlled by men who fear bad news can make a small state's material disadvantages harder to manage.

Jiang Wei remains committed to the northern struggle and commands forces whose experience is valuable. But those forces cannot defend every road simultaneously. A successful state needs agreement between the frontier command and the central government on what must be protected first. The novel's tension comes from the widening gap between Jiang Wei's effort and the capital's confidence that danger can still be postponed.

The fall of Shu will therefore be told through armies and through the failures of political communication. One kind of weakness permits an enemy to advance. The other makes it hard for a government to recognize the moment when the advance becomes existential.

## The defenses of Hanzhong are tested

Shu's geographical strength lies partly in difficult approaches into the Sichuan basin. Hanzhong and the mountain passes can prevent a much larger northern army from moving quickly. But no defensive system is invulnerable simply because its terrain is famous. Command decisions determine which passes are held, how garrisons cooperate, and where reserves are placed. A line of forts can be undone if a crucial commander surrenders or if the attacker discovers a route the defenders have not adequately protected.

Sima Zhao, who controls Wei's government, orders a major attack on Shu. Zhong Hui leads a large force toward the established approaches, while Deng Ai and other commanders operate on different routes. Jiang Wei, the leading Shu commander, must avoid being cut off and concentrate troops where the main danger appears. The novel turns the invasion into a sequence of anxious reports, countermarches, and defenses by generals whose personal courage cannot cover every mountain path.

Jiang Wei withdraws toward the strong position of Jiange, where the terrain favors a defender. Zhong Hui's larger army has difficulty making progress. Supplies strain, and the northern commanders confront the possibility that holding the pass may exhaust the invasion. For a time the old strategic logic seems to work: Shu may lack the numbers to win a great battle on open ground, but it can make the entrance to its heartland too costly to force directly.

## The path nobody planned to defend

Deng Ai chooses a dangerous alternative. He leads a force through the Yinping route, across difficult mountains and tracks that the defenders regard as nearly impassable for an army. The novel presents the movement as a feat of endurance and audacity. Soldiers carry or improvise equipment, negotiate steep paths, and risk being trapped in terrain where a failure of supplies could destroy them before an enemy appears. The march succeeds, and the force emerges beyond the strongest defended approaches.

The event has a clear historical core. Deng Ai's exploitation of the Yinping route was decisive in the conquest. It illustrates a general danger in positional defense: a force can stop the enemy it expects at the place it expects, yet lose when another force arrives beyond the prepared line. Jiange does not fall because its defenders suddenly forget how to fight. Its strategic value is bypassed by a second operation that changes the meaning of the position.

The surprise throws Shu's court into crisis. The capital Chengdu has a population, an administration, and palace rituals, but it cannot summon an effective field army out of ceremony. Zhuge Zhan, son of Zhuge Liang, organizes resistance at Mianzhu. The novel gives him an inheritance heavy with emotional expectations: he bears the name of the great minister, and the audience wants him to protect the kingdom his father served. His forces are defeated, and he dies with other defenders. The loss is both military and symbolic. A family that stood for continuity in Shu can no longer prevent the end of the state.

## The mountain passage that defeats expectation

The northern invasion of 263 contains a strategic surprise. One invading column pressures Shu's established defenses, while Deng Ai undertakes a difficult advance through terrain thought too hard for a major force to traverse. The route is dangerous for the invaders themselves. Men must carry supplies and make their way across places where a single obstruction could prevent the movement of an entire column. The very difficulty that may lead a defender to discount the approach makes its successful crossing valuable.

A commander contemplating such an expedition must balance two risks. If the march fails, the force may be stranded or starved without ever meeting an enemy. If it succeeds, the defenders may have concentrated elsewhere and left an approach to the heartland exposed. The plan requires not a supernatural ability to ignore mountains, but detailed preparation and a willingness to accept a severe logistical gamble.

The novel makes the movement a dramatic reversal. The defenders have spent years thinking about northern threats along familiar corridors, only to discover an enemy close to their central territory by a path they did not expect to bear such an army. The event shows the limits of any defensive system constructed around a stable picture of the opponent's possible choices. A position can remain strong against the assault for which it was designed and become irrelevant when the enemy finds another way.

Deng Ai's achievement belongs to the historical record of Shu's destruction, although particular speeches and episodes in the novel must be read as literature. It is worth emphasizing that his success also depended on others fixing Shu's attention at established defenses. A daring march works best when the defender has good reasons to look somewhere else. This is a coordinated campaign with a spectacular component, not merely the triumph of a lone adventurer.

## The court decides whether to surrender

Liu Shan's advisers debate what remains possible. Some urge resistance, relocation, or further attempts to coordinate with Jiang Wei. Others argue that Chengdu cannot be defended without bringing destruction upon its people. The novel's portrait of the emperor often invites contempt; yet the decision is grave. To surrender ends the dynasty founded by his father and abandons the military cause to which Zhuge Liang devoted his life. To resist with insufficient forces may expose the capital to assault, massacre, and hunger.

Liu Shan chooses surrender. The western kingdom ceases to exist as an independent state. The decision is sometimes remembered simply as cowardice, particularly when later anecdotes portray Liu Shan as contented in captivity. A serious account must distinguish the court's moral language from the practical alternatives. The loss of field forces, the unexpected arrival of Deng Ai, and the failure of prepared defenses changed the options. It is possible to criticize the decisions that created the weakness while recognizing that continued resistance after the breach might not have restored the state.

Jiang Wei learns that the court has surrendered. He still commands troops capable of holding a pass, but the political center in whose name he fights has ordered submission. The dilemma is almost unbearable. A loyal commander cannot simply invent a new sovereign whenever an outcome disappoints him. Yet he has inherited the dream of restoration from Zhuge Liang and may believe surrender is a mistake from which the state could recover if he finds another opportunity.

## A surrender argued over in the capital

When the invaders threaten Chengdu, Liu Shan and his advisers face the final question of government: should they continue resisting when the capital itself may become a battlefield? A defender can invoke honor and ancestral duty, but he must also consider civilians who will suffer through siege, storming, or the destruction of crops. A surrender can preserve lives while extinguishing the state whose memory has sustained the army for decades.

The novel gives this decision a moral charge because Shu has so often been treated as the continuation of the legitimate Han cause. To give up the capital seems to abandon the founders' promise. Yet the living ruler is responsible for people who did not personally make that promise. If he orders a hopeless resistance, their suffering may serve the memory of the dead more than the welfare of the living. Neither description automatically decides the issue. The relevant military possibilities and political alternatives matter.

Liu Shan's surrender has attracted sharply negative judgments in later tradition. He is often portrayed as a ruler incapable of appreciating the sacrifices made on his behalf. But a historical evaluation should distinguish his long reign, the state's problems, and the specific conditions of the 263 campaign from later jokes about his character. Even a weak sovereign can make a decision that avoids slaughter; even a rational surrender can carry consequences that its defenders regret.

When the gates are opened, the novel's emotional scale changes. The grand program of Han restoration has survived battle, exile, the loss of two sworn brothers, and the death of an exemplary minister. It now ends through an administrative decision taken in a threatened city. The contrast is painful because a state may die without a final heroic encounter equal to the promises that founded it.

## One last attempt at restoring a lost kingdom

Jiang Wei submits to Zhong Hui, then seeks to exploit tensions among the victorious Wei commanders. Zhong Hui may suspect Deng Ai or desire authority greater than the rewards his current patron will grant. Jiang Wei offers cooperation that appears to serve those ambitions while preserving the possibility of restoring Shu. The scheme is elaborate and perilous. The defeated general is trying to make a conquering army turn against its own command before the victory can be consolidated.

The novel gives Jiang Wei's final plan the intensity of a man wagering his life and the remnants of a kingdom on one more stratagem. But a plot depends on the reliable conduct of others, and the circumstances are volatile. Soldiers fear punishment, commanders mistrust one another, and news can travel faster than a conspirator expects. The attempted revolt fails. Zhong Hui and Jiang Wei are killed, and a series of violent events follows the conquest. The state has ended, and the last strategy intended to recover it has added casualties without reversing the outcome.

Jiang Wei's career completes the transformation of the original oath into inherited duty. He never stood with Liu Bei in the peach garden. He knew the kingdom as an established institution and received its ideals through service to Zhuge Liang. He fought because the goal had become part of the state's identity and his own understanding of honorable conduct. His death is moving because the intention was sustained under increasingly unfavorable circumstances. The political consequences are harder to judge. Repeated campaigns and the final conspiracy may have further damaged the people and institutions he meant to save.

## A final plan built from defeated forces

Jiang Wei does not accept the end immediately. In the turmoil following Shu's surrender, he attempts to exploit rivalries among the victorious northern commanders, including the tension between Deng Ai and Zhong Hui. The plot offers the possibility that a defeated officer might turn the conquerors against each other and recover a political future for his own side. The plan is intricate, dangerous, and dependent upon other men choosing exactly the forms of ambition and distrust that Jiang Wei anticipates.

The conspiracy's tragic energy comes from the distance between its objective and its means. To restore Shu, Jiang Wei must maneuver within an occupation whose commanders he has reason to distrust. He must involve men whose political aims do not coincide with his devotion to the defeated dynasty. A strategy that once looked like loyal resourcefulness can come to resemble gambling with the lives of people already exposed to conquest.

The historical aftermath of Shu's fall did include conflict among the victors and the violent failure of the plans associated with Zhong Hui and Jiang Wei. The novel shapes those events into the final ordeal of the old regime's military virtue. Jiang Wei's persistence invites admiration; his lack of a reliable political foundation invites sorrow. An army's fidelity cannot resurrect a state whose central government has surrendered and whose resources are controlled by its enemy.

The destruction of competing northern commanders also shows that victory can open new dangers for the winner. A general who conquers a province acquires prestige, soldiers, and knowledge of a region far from the capital. The court must decide how to reward him without creating a new independent center. The problems of faction and succession that weakened earlier dynasties now arise within the victorious regime as well.

## The northern usurpation completed

The victory over Shu increases the prestige of Sima Zhao and the northern ruling household. But Sima Zhao dies before founding a new dynasty in his own name. His son Sima Yan succeeds to the family's position and compels the replacement of the Cao Wei emperor. In 265 he establishes the Jin dynasty; the reign period and ceremonial transition are commonly placed across 265 and 266 depending on the event being described. The same broad pattern has now occurred twice in the novel's historical span. A commanding minister becomes indispensable to the court; his family holds the armies; the sovereign loses practical freedom; and a formal abdication transfers the throne to the stronger house.

The recurring structure raises the question of whether a successful dynasty can secure its own succession. Cao Cao's family gained power by acting in the name of Han restoration. The Sima family rose while serving Cao Wei. The legal ceremony of transfer cannot hide the dependence of the emperor on the forces that control the government. Yet Jin is also a state with armies, administrators, and the ability to carry out policies across a wide territory. The novel's ending does not allow an easy choice between treating its rulers as mere villains and treating success as proof of virtue.

Sima Yan now governs the north and the newly conquered west. Wu, the southern kingdom, remains independent beyond the Yangtze. Its ruler and officials face a formidable new opponent. The famous river defenses that saved Sun Quan at Red Cliffs no longer confront a northern state exhausted by a recent civil war; they confront a dynasty that has absorbed the resources of two kingdoms and is preparing to coordinate a major invasion.

## A dynasty changes its name again

The Sima family's position in Wei eventually becomes sufficiently secure for its own formal accession. In 265, Sima Yan replaces the Wei dynasty with Jin. Once again, a court presents the transfer of sovereignty through ceremonies and claims of political legitimacy. The change is both an official beginning and the recognition of power that has been accumulating for years.

Readers who remember Cao Pi's reception of Emperor Xian's abdication can see the deliberate symmetry. A family that once occupied the throne through a transfer from Han now yields the throne to a family that has controlled its ministers and armies. The circle invites reflection on the difference between lawful form and independent consent. Rites and titles can create continuity in administrative life while concealing the coercive circumstances under which they are performed.

Jin's rulers inherit the northern military state built by generations of Cao and Sima commanders. They also inherit the strategic problem of Wu, a southern realm whose rivers and cities have resisted northern expansion for decades. Reunification will require ships, commanders, organization, and decisions made by officials who have lived their entire careers in a divided political world.

The novel's tendency to frame events as the fulfillment of a cosmic cycle can make the change seem destined. Yet from the viewpoint of the commanders stationed along the Yangtze, the final campaign remains uncertain. They must face ordinary hazards: wind, river currents, disease, fortifications, and the possibility that an assault will fail. A dynasty's confident claim to rule the whole realm does not put its armies on the opposite bank.

## Wu after the founders

Sun Quan's kingdom outlived its founder and many original commanders. It built institutions, defended the Yangtze, and participated in the long three-sided rivalry. Yet later rulers and ministers struggled with succession, faction, and the concentration of power. The novel's final Wu ruler, Sun Hao, is portrayed as oppressive and self-indulgent, a convenient moral explanation for why the dynasty deserves to fall. Historians must treat that portrait with care. Defeated sovereigns often fare poorly in the accounts preserved by their conquerors, and the state's strategic difficulties cannot be reduced to one ruler's personality.

The southern regime has real strengths. Its waterways are familiar to local troops, and its forces have experience defending their territory. The geography that favored Sun Quan's house at Red Cliffs still matters. But Jin can now concentrate resources in a way that the earlier Cao government could not. Its fleets and armies operate in coordinated campaigns, pressing different sections of Wu's defenses. A defensive line whose purpose is to deny passage to one threat may become impossible to hold when opponents attack from several directions with adequate supply.

The novel gives veteran officers and new leaders their final chances to act. Some offer sound advice and are ignored. Others execute daring maneuvers that cannot change the overall balance. The book's moral architecture encourages a reading in which the decline of discipline and virtue explains defeat. That reading can be illuminating when tied to particular administrative failures. It should be tested against the broader record of relative population, military resources, routes, and political institutions.

## The river is crossed

Jin's final campaign succeeds in 280. Its forces penetrate Wu's defenses, and Sun Hao surrenders. The last of the three kingdoms has fallen. The realm is reunited under Jin, at least for a time. The novel's famous opening reflection about division and reunion seems fulfilled. But if the reader has followed the whole narrative, the ending resists easy triumph. The people who created the most memorable ideals have not been rewarded by final victory. The families who fought to preserve the Han have disappeared or become subjects of a dynasty their founders did not foresee.

What does reunification mean for those people? It may end some of the constant warfare among rival states and permit administration across former frontiers. It may also bring new exactions, political purges, and the problems created by a conquering court. The text does not provide a detailed social history of the peace that followed. Nor does the fact of reunification establish that political disorder will never return. The story began by describing a world in which unity and division alternate. The close of one cycle is not a guarantee against another.

The last campaign also changes how we view Red Cliffs. That earlier battle prevented a northern conquest in 208 and preserved the existence of southern political alternatives for more than seventy years. From the perspective of 280, one might say that the eventual northern victory was inevitable. The novel's details make such a claim too easy. Different decisions at many points could have changed the timing, cost, and form of the result. The final outcome should not erase the contingency that made the earlier choices meaningful.


## The river that outlives the kingdoms

The final invasion of Wu in 280 brings the narrative back to the water that once preserved the southern realm. At Red Cliffs, the coalition used its knowledge of the river to deny Cao Cao a decisive conquest. Now a different northern government organizes a campaign designed to overcome the barrier. Generations of officers have developed methods of operating along the river, and the political situation within Wu has changed. A defensive advantage is still real, but it is not permanent.

The Jin offensive proceeds through several approaches and benefits from the combined strength of a state that controls the north and west. Wu's ruler and officials must judge whether continued resistance can hold together the remaining defenses. The military outcome is shaped by the condition of the southern court as well as by the attacking armies. A government weakened by mistrust may struggle to coordinate forces even when its fortifications are substantial.

The surrender of Wu closes the period conventionally known as the Three Kingdoms. It does not return the people of China to the conditions that existed before the late Han crisis. Populations have moved, cities have been destroyed or rebuilt, local families have acquired new roles, and the memory of decades of warfare has entered political culture. Reunification ends the division of sovereignty without erasing the effects of the division.

The novel's closing sense of recurrence is therefore powerful but incomplete as historical explanation. It suggests that what was united will divide and what was divided will unite. That formula captures a broad rhythm while leaving unanswered what particular institutions, conflicts, and decisions produced each transition. We should hear it as literary reflection on impermanence, not as a law that makes human choices irrelevant.

## What remains of the promise in the garden

At the beginning, Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei possessed a bond and a wish to serve a failing dynasty. Their promise expanded through the choices of hundreds of people, into campaigns, governments, and a state that lasted beyond their deaths. In the end that state is gone. The novel allows its final pages to turn from famous personalities toward the passage of time. The original men are distant memories; their virtues and errors survive in the accounts that later generations tell about them.

The same is true of Cao Cao's house and Sun Quan's. Each family created an organization formidable enough to shape a generation. Each organization depended on heirs and ministers capable of maintaining it after the original achievement. None could control all the conditions of succession, and none remained in command forever. The novel has spent a hundred and twenty chapters dramatizing the difference between winning authority and preserving it.

We can leave the narrative with a judgment more demanding than the simple claim that everything was futile. People in the story protected comrades, built institutions, defended cities, and also inflicted terrible suffering. Their decisions changed lives even though their states eventually disappeared. Historical impermanence does not make action meaningless; it makes the consequences of action worthy of more careful attention.

The final question is how we know the story we have just heard. Some events are preserved in third-century histories. Some come from later annotations, anecdotes, performance traditions, and printed novels. Others belong to the moral imagination of storytellers who lived many centuries after the wars. The closing lecture will follow those layers and explain why separating them makes this great work more interesting rather than reducing it to a collection of errors.

## The survivors of a hundred years

The original heroes have become subjects of memory, sometimes cult and legend. Guan Yu is remembered as an exemplar of loyalty and will later acquire religious power in the imagination of successive generations. Zhuge Liang becomes an icon of strategic genius and selfless service. Cao Cao becomes the model of a dangerous, capable, often morally compromised ruler. Liu Bei represents benevolent intention and dynastic fidelity, even though his own decisions sometimes produce disaster. These reputations do not all match the historical record in the same way, and the book has participated in shaping them.

The novel's final pages are therefore about more than chronology. They explain why people might continue telling a story whose most beloved characters failed to secure its outcome. One answer is that political history does not reward moral character in a straightforward manner. The fate of a state depends on resources, institutions, geography, accidents, and decisions made long after the founding generation is gone. Another is that a failed cause can preserve questions worth asking: what do rulers owe subjects, what does loyalty cost, when does strategy become manipulation, and when should an inherited promise be revised?

The ultimate victor, Jin, is powerful because it inherited the northern state's administrative and military capacities and then conquered its rivals. It did not win because its founder had been the most romantic hero in the opening chapters. The book began with a small oath and a failing empire. It ends with an empire reunited by a family whose ascent repeated the very process by which the Han court had been displaced. Between those moments lie the moral attractions and dangers of a century of divided rule.

One may admire the faithfulness of the brothers, the courage of the soldiers, the patience of the advisers, and the achievement of reunification without believing that any one of these resolves the others. The novel's greatness rests partly in its refusal to grant the preferred heroes the last word. Their intentions are preserved as stories even after their states have disappeared. China has been reunited under Jin; the argument about what makes a ruler legitimate, and what makes a life honorable, remains unfinished.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 116–120. Wei's conquest of Shu in 263, Deng Ai's Yinping maneuver, Jiange resistance, Liu Shan's surrender, Zhong Hui's failed revolt, Jin's founding in 265, and Wu's fall in 280 are historical. The 265/266 dynastic transition should be dated precisely when discussing particular ceremonies and era years. Do not take the final conquerors' portrait of Sun Hao as sufficient evidence for a complete explanation of Wu's defeat.
