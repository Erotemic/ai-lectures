# Shahnameh: Goshtasp, Zarir, and the Prince in Chains

When Kay Khosrow relinquishes power and disappears, the Iranian royal world enters a different age. The succession brings Lohrasp to the throne. Later his son Goshtasp will reign, and the story will place the arrival and protection of the Zoroastrian faith within the royal history. Yet the most gripping conflict in this part of the *Shahnameh* concerns a son of Goshtasp named Esfandiyar. He becomes a great defender of the kingdom and its religious commitments. The stronger he grows, the harder it becomes for his father to grant him the authority he has earned. The young man can overcome enemies abroad, but he cannot force a cautious and jealous parent to fulfill a promise.

The epic's attention changes here. The wars of Kay Khosrow centered on vengeance for a murdered father and the cost of defeating Afrasiyab. The Goshtasp stories are increasingly concerned with royal faith, obligations, succession, and the difference between justice and obedience. The traditions used by Ferdowsi had their own long history, including ancient religious narratives in which the champions of the faith occupy different places from those emphasized in later heroic poetry. One can enjoy the courtly drama without mistaking every episode for a dated record of the historical spread of Zoroastrianism.

## The succession is an argument

When Kay Khosrow withdraws, the kingdom does not become empty for long. He has appointed Lohrasp, whose claim is accepted despite misgivings among those accustomed to serving a more celebrated line. The choice matters because the poem has spent so long teaching us to admire lineage. It now makes the heroes accept a ruler whose authority rests partly on the judgment of a departing king. Old champions who remember ancient services are asked to surrender their expectation that heroic merit will always determine succession. The transition feels stable only once the court's most powerful men agree to let it be stable.

Lohrasp's son Goshtasp complicates that settlement. Ambitious and proud, he wants recognition that does not arrive quickly enough. Depending on the telling and episode, his movements include alienation from the Iranian court and adventures in foreign lands. There is a recurrent pattern here: a young prince leaves the center of power, experiences the hazards of being treated as an unknown man, and returns with a reputation that might secure his future. But his departure has a different tone from Kay Khosrow's. One king withdrew because he feared pride; the next generation contains a prince whose pride helps drive him away.

Goshtasp's adventures lead him into scenes in which physical excellence, marriage, and recognition are intertwined. The story is interested in how a royal identity can be concealed without erasing habits of command. A man who has been raised to expect an inheritance may try to make himself indispensable wherever he goes. A successful foreign adventure can demonstrate courage while leaving unresolved the question of whether the adventurer has the patience to rule. The epic has supplied enough failed kings to make that question urgent.

Once Goshtasp receives power, the story's moral tension does not vanish. He can promote a religious vision and defend his state while remaining a father who expects more obedience than he is prepared to reciprocate. That division is central to Esfandiyar's life. It keeps us from interpreting the king's public commitments as an automatic guarantee of wisdom in private decisions.

## A young prince beyond his father's court

Goshtasp is a son of Lohrasp. Before he becomes king, he quarrels with the limits placed on his prospects and travels away from the court, eventually moving into the western regions of the epic's imagined world. Disguised or living without the recognition his ancestry would command at home, he encounters strangers who judge him by his actions. He gains respect by strength and skill, and his story includes a marriage that crosses political and cultural boundaries. His experience resembles the journeys of other royal figures who seek a place outside the country that has defined them.

The wandering creates a useful tension. Goshtasp knows that he is the son of a king, yet his status abroad cannot be confirmed by a procession of attendants. He has to perform tasks, earn trust, and respond to adversity. A prince who complains about being denied his proper share of authority discovers what ordinary life feels like when lineage does not speak on one's behalf. He does not become an ordinary person; heroic romance gives him extraordinary power. But the journey loosens the assumption that a throne is sufficient evidence of worth.

Eventually a reconciliation brings him back into the royal succession. Lohrasp withdraws, and Goshtasp becomes king. One might expect that a man who suffered from the insecurity of an heir would be especially careful in his treatment of his own sons. The poem chooses a harsher possibility. Success in securing the crown can make a ruler more afraid of losing it. Goshtasp's later conduct toward Esfandiyar repeats, in more painful form, the struggle over recognition that once drove him away from home.

## A new allegiance at the royal court

The poem connects Goshtasp's reign with the appearance of Zoroaster and the acceptance of his teaching. Within the epic's legendary ordering, a king's support allows a new religious allegiance to become the concern of the realm. The choice reaches outward: neighboring kings may treat it as a provocation, and the battles that follow give religious change the force of political history. The storyteller does not pause to reconstruct a modern history of Zoroastrian doctrine. He places faith, kingship, military obligation, and family loyalty in the same dramatic room.

The dating must be handled carefully. The historical Zoroaster's life has been variously dated by scholars; the materials bearing his teaching and the later Pahlavi traditions cannot be converted into a single securely dated biography. Goshtasp in this cycle belongs to a legendary chronology, even though the story preserves a deep Iranian memory of religious change. If we imagine a court scribe writing down a precise transcript of a conversation between the prince and the prophet, we have gone beyond the evidence. The poem can show how one tradition understands its foundation without furnishing the kind of contemporary record a modern historian would seek.

The difference affects how we hear the scenes. When a king treats religious conversion as a reason for war, we are encountering a literary representation of how spiritual allegiance can bind a political community. We may ask why that representation appealed to its later audience and what anxieties it carries about neighboring powers. We should not infer that every depicted campaign happened as described. The poem's historical importance includes what it tells us about remembering the past under the constraints and expectations of a much later world.

## Zarir and a war about belief

The narrative places Zoroaster's teaching within Goshtasp's reign. The king accepts the new faith, and conflict arises with rulers who resist its expansion or the political claims associated with it. Arjasp, a ruler from the opposing world, becomes the adversary in a struggle that combines warfare with religious loyalty. The language of the story is morally emphatic: the defense of the faith becomes part of the identity of the royal house. The social and historical circumstances of Zoroastrian traditions are far more complex than one decisive war, but within the epic the conflict organizes a test of fidelity.

Zarir, Goshtasp's brother, is one of the great champions. He goes to war and meets an enemy whose weapons defeat him. His death is deeply felt in the royal world. The fallen brother is a figure of courage and family loyalty; the mourning asks the audience to see the war as more than a clash of abstract beliefs. A child and a household lose someone whose life had distinct relationships, hopes, and obligations. The poem gives the death the familiar combination of public honor and private devastation.

The story of Zarir has a significant source history. A Middle Persian work commonly called the *Memorial of Zarir* preserves a related heroic account. It does not simply provide the identical text that Ferdowsi copied; the traditions show changes of emphasis, characters, and poetic elaboration. The existence of that earlier material is important because it demonstrates that the Goshtasp cycle was not invented from nothing in the eleventh century. It also cautions against treating Ferdowsi's version as a verbatim transcript of an ancient original. A transmitted story can retain a recognizable core while being reshaped through new literary aims.

The earlier poet Daqiqi occupies an especially interesting place here. He had undertaken to versify the royal traditions before Ferdowsi. His life ended before the large project could be completed. Ferdowsi includes a substantial passage of Daqiqi's own verse, around a thousand couplets, in the account of Goshtasp and the new faith. He identifies his predecessor rather than presenting the lines as entirely his own composition. For a listener interested in how the book was made, this is a rare moment when the long epic shows a seam in its authorship openly. Another poet's work is being carried forward inside the poem that eventually became the dominant version of the tradition.

The embedded passage has also inspired differing judgments about style and religious emphasis. Ferdowsi can respect the predecessor's undertaking while implying that he would have handled certain matters differently. The surviving verses give textual critics evidence for what an earlier poetic treatment looked like, but they do not allow us to reconstruct all the work Daqiqi might have written. This distinction becomes important in the research lecture. For the narrative, the central point is that the struggles of Goshtasp, Zarir, and Esfandiyar already belonged to a world of remembered tales before Ferdowsi gave them their surviving epic form.

## Zarir is more than the first casualty

Zarir's role in the religious wars adds a family cost to the public cause that Goshtasp has embraced. A brother of the king and a celebrated warrior, Zarir fights in a conflict where devotion to the new faith and allegiance to the royal house have become entangled. His death is mourned as a heroic loss. The lament gives the royal cause a human register beyond proclamations about which ruler holds the rightful belief. Someone's brother and father has been killed.

The older Middle Persian *Ayadgar i Zareran*, the Memorial of Zarir, preserves a related heroic tradition centered on the battle and its consequences. Its relationship to the *Shahnameh* allows scholars to investigate how the narrative changed as it crossed languages and literary settings. A shared motif can show that the story circulated before Ferdowsi; differences in speech and episode organization reveal that he was composing within a living tradition. Neither source should simply be treated as a modern chronicle of a precisely dated religious war.

Zarir's son Bastwar becomes part of the story's response to bereavement. The young survivor must act under the immense pressure of his father's name. The relationship recalls Sohrab, but here the child knows which warrior he has lost. Recognition is no longer the central problem; inheritance is. The boy is asked to turn grief into courage before he has fully had the opportunity to be a son. The epic celebrates his response while leaving the loss itself visible.

The episode prepares us to hear Esfandiyar's later service with a sharper ear. A king whose relatives have already died for the realm may ask even more of his heir. Sacrifice can strengthen a ruler's sense of entitlement to further sacrifice, particularly when he interprets resistance as ingratitude. The story of Zarir demonstrates both the grandeur of public commitment and the danger of treating family bonds as an endless reservoir of obedience.

## Esfandiyar's growing authority

Esfandiyar emerges as the prince whose prowess can rescue the kingdom. He fights with courage and persistence, becomes associated with religious defense, and wins the confidence of warriors who have seen what he can do. His father needs him in dangerous circumstances. Yet the same accomplishments make him a possible rival. If armies praise the prince as the savior of the realm, the old king must decide whether to understand that praise as a sign of dynastic strength or as a threat to his own position.

Goshtasp repeatedly makes promises about succession. Esfandiyar is asked to perform great tasks, with the prospect that the throne will follow. The prince has reason to expect that fulfillment of difficult obligations will be rewarded. The relationship is no longer simply paternal. It resembles a political contract: do what the kingdom needs, and authority will pass in due course. The king gains the benefit of the service while controlling the conditions of the reward.

The story includes accusations against Esfandiyar, his imprisonment, and dangers that arise when the kingdom lacks his strength. At points he is bound in chains. The image is deliberately humiliating. A champion who can break enemies on the field is confined by the authority he protects. The constraints come from his own father's court, a place whose legitimacy he has defended. For the father, imprisonment seems to preserve control. For the kingdom, it risks the loss of the very person who might save it.

When fresh attacks threaten the royal family, Esfandiyar's abilities become indispensable again. A captive prince can be released because the emergency is too great to ignore him. He answers the need, and the king benefits from his successes. But the pattern repeats: the promised transfer of power recedes. Esfandiyar's loyalty is made into a resource the father can draw upon without relinquishing command. The cost to the prince accumulates. Every fulfillment makes the next demand seem harder to refuse, because he has already committed his identity to service and honor.

## The fortress and the seven trials

Arjasp's forces threaten the kingdom, and the prince undertakes a dangerous expedition to recover people held by the enemy. The path to the fortress involves a sequence of extraordinary perils, known in the tradition as Esfandiyar's seven labors. They echo the seven labors by which Rostam rescued Kay Kavus from the demons of Mazandaran. The repetition is deliberate enough to be meaningful. The epic creates two great champions whose heroic credentials can be compared through similar feats. But their political positions are very different.

Rostam belongs to a great but not royal house, and his independence allows him to confront kings with a degree of freedom. Esfandiyar is the king's son. He is more directly trapped by filial obedience, dynastic promise, and the desire to succeed lawfully. When he defeats monsters and moves through deadly country, we should therefore ask what the achievements will allow him to claim afterward. A champion may earn fame from a seven-labors cycle. An heir may reasonably expect the throne that was repeatedly promised as a reward.

The hazards include beasts, supernatural enemies, and treacherous ways. Esfandiyar survives by courage, planning, and a willingness to move forward where ordinary companions might lose hope. He eventually reaches the enemy stronghold. A disguise, often involving the role of a merchant, enables entry. This is a familiar heroic device: the warrior known for brute strength recognizes that a fortress cannot always be taken by announcing his name at the gate. He must use deception to approach those who are capable of imprisoning his kin.

Once the city is penetrated, the balance changes. Esfandiyar can employ his strength decisively. Captives are released and the enemy's power broken. In the royal story the victory seems to settle a long argument. The prince has defended the kingdom, rescued its people, and demonstrated that he is fit to command. Yet the father remains on the throne. The reward that seemed to await him is not placed in his hands. The epic allows the frustration to become an ethical issue rather than merely the impatience of an ambitious son.

## A prince who keeps winning

Esfandiyar grows through trials that seem calculated to remove every excuse for denying him the throne. He fights for Goshtasp, endures danger, and repeatedly proves that he can do what his father's position requires. His excellence should make him secure. Instead, each achievement invites another demand. The familiar reward is deferred. A young champion may have patience for one mission, but the pattern reveals a political transaction in which the king controls the promised prize and the son performs the risk.

One episode sends Esfandiyar into a formidable fortress, the Brazen Hold, where his sisters and other members of the royal household are captive. He enters under an assumed identity, observes the enemy's habits, and makes rescue possible through deception and assault. We know the storytelling pattern from Rostam's earlier feats: the powerful hero must sometimes submit to a plan, disguise, and waiting before he can use his strength. But the family meaning has shifted. Esfandiyar rescues people whom his father should have a strong reason to protect. Successful rescue becomes a demonstration that the son can bear the obligations of kingship. The father responds by finding further service to demand.

The Seven Labors of Esfandiyar deliberately recall Rostam's earlier ordeals. A journey through deadly obstacles, hostile creatures, and uncertainty produces a familiar heroic rhythm. An audience that has listened to Rostam's feats will understand the comparison even before the poem makes it explicit. There are now two champions whose strength seems superhuman. One is the old defender of the realm; the other is a prince who believes his deeds entitle him to rule. Their eventual conflict is prepared by this doubling. The tragedy requires the listener to admire both men before they meet.

Esfandiyar is often described as possessing a body extraordinarily resistant to weapons, with his eyes remaining vulnerable. The detail gives the later duel a narrative pressure that no ordinary contest of skill could produce. It would be too easy to regard his seeming invulnerability as proof of a divine right to conquer. The poem makes it a terrible predicament instead. A man who cannot easily be wounded may begin to believe that no ordinary obstacle should govern him. Yet he remains exposed to a father whose commands can destroy his future and to a moral dilemma that armor cannot solve.

## A new command with an impossible object

Goshtasp's next demand concerns Rostam. The great champion of Sistan has a long history of saving Iranian kings. But he has also retained a commanding independence, and the king can present that independence as a form of insubordination. He tells Esfandiyar to go and bring Rostam bound in chains to court. The prince is promised the succession if he completes the task. It sounds like the final requirement, yet it has been designed to put two of the country's greatest defenders in conflict.

The command is poisonous because it attacks both men through their virtues. Esfandiyar takes obedience to his father seriously. Rostam takes honor and independent service seriously. The king can frame the task as enforcing royal sovereignty, while also knowing that a confrontation might remove the young man whose claim to succeed him has become so hard to deny. We do not need to assume that every version of the tale presents Goshtasp's intentions in identical language. The structure of the conflict is evident. A monarch who benefits from the service of champions makes their continued service dependent on humiliating one another.

Esfandiyar is warned that the mission is dangerous. Counselors and relatives point out that Rostam's stature is not that of an ordinary provincial disobedient. The old champion's deeds helped preserve the very throne whose authority is now invoked against him. The prince feels the difficulty but believes he must fulfill his father's command and the conditions set for his own future. His tragedy begins before he ever reaches Sistan. He is choosing the route that seems to preserve his honor, even though it carries him toward an encounter in which the requirements of honor cannot all be satisfied.

A modern reader can observe the political trap without simplifying Esfandiyar into a fool. Within the moral world he inhabits, obedience to a father and rightful king has profound importance. Disobeying might seem to undermine the very principles he defended in religious war. At the same time, a command can be unjust even when issued from an accepted throne. The prince has to decide whether loyalty means doing what he is told or preserving the standards for which he has fought. The poem makes the decision painful by giving him admirable reasons for the course that will destroy him.

## The difference between honor and obedience

The king finally orders Esfandiyar to bring Rostam bound before him. We can hear the contradiction in the request. Rostam has fought for kings over a lifetime; his independence is part of his status as the greatest servant of Iran. To bind him like a criminal is to ask him to renounce the honor that has made him valuable. Esfandiyar is not sent merely to request a meeting. His instructions concern the form in which another hero must submit.

The son is caught in an argument he cannot win by strength. If he disobeys Goshtasp, he violates the royal and filial allegiance he has been trained to respect. If he obeys, he risks bringing disgrace and death to a man who has defended the same realm. The oath that organizes one kind of virtue presses against another. A person committed to honor may discover that keeping one promise makes another duty impossible.

Esfandiyar can protest the injustice of the command, and the conversation matters. He is not a mindless weapon sent by a cruel father. He knows that Rostam deserves respect. But he does not find a way to refuse the mission. Goshtasp's power lies in exploiting precisely that conscientiousness: the dutiful son is the instrument by which a ruler removes a rival. The poem lets us see the manipulation from outside the prince's loyalty even while we understand why he experiences refusal as nearly impossible.

The political question is painfully durable. A sovereign may frame a dangerous task as a test of commitment to the realm, while the person ordered to perform it understands that the task serves the sovereign's private insecurity. What distinguishes responsible public service from compliance with an unjust command? The epic does not deliver a legal code in reply. It lets the conflict play through the destruction of two admired lives, one directly and the other through the guilt that follows. In the next chapter of the heroic cycle, Rostam will have to answer the command that Esfandiyar could not reject.

## Two kinds of heroic service

Compare the champions before they meet. Rostam is strong enough to rescue kings, but that strength has never made him safe from grief. He killed Sohrab without knowing the boy was his son. He raised Siyavash only to lose him to a murderous court. Esfandiyar is brave enough to rescue captives and face monsters, but each act of service has increased his dependence on a father's promise. The two men embody different vulnerabilities. Rostam can defeat physical danger and still misunderstand a relationship. Esfandiyar can fulfill a royal duty and still misunderstand what obedience will cost.

The fate of Zarir supplies a third example. He dies in a war framed as defense of faith, leaving grief to his family. The heroic tradition gives him honor, but honor does not undo the death. These stories share an insistence that noble service is costly. None offers an automatic reward proportionate to the sacrifice. A king may be worth defending and still make a disastrous demand. A faith may be sincerely held and still become entangled with dynastic ambition. A prince may be brave and still be drawn into a conflict that courage cannot resolve.

The religious material deserves separate care. Ferdowsi writes in an Islamic Persian literary world while recounting a pre-Islamic royal past in which Zoroaster and the establishment of Zoroastrian belief are central. The work does not require us to imagine that everyone in Ferdowsi's day held exactly the same beliefs as the characters in his story. The transmission of these episodes helped preserve a narrative of earlier Iranian religious culture within a later Persian poem. That coexistence is part of the poem's historical significance. It is also a reminder that a literary work can honor a predecessor's world without being a direct document from that world.

The episode ends with Esfandiyar approaching the territory of Rostam. He has earned fame in war, faced beasts, liberated captives, and waited repeatedly for the throne. His father offers one last task, and the task demands that he bind a national champion whose earlier work made the kingdom possible. Two exceptional men now face a demand that ought never to have been issued. In the coming encounter, both will speak about law, service, fatherhood, rank, and dignity. The most terrible question is already before us: when a ruler makes loyalty to the state require dishonor toward a loyal servant, how can either servant escape without becoming the thing he condemns?
