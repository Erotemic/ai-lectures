# Three Kingdoms, Part XI: Guan Yu's Last Campaign and the End of Han

Guan Yu has spent much of his life displaying a kind of fidelity that survives defeat, separation, and great temptation. He left the protection of Cao Cao to return to Liu Bei. He guarded the households entrusted to him. He became a model of steadfast friendship in a world where alliances repeatedly collapsed. Now he commands Jing, the province contested by Liu Bei's western kingdom, Sun Quan's southern house, and Cao Cao's northern armies. His reputation is immense. His position is dangerous. The qualities that made him a legendary hero will not be sufficient to save him.

In this movement of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, one of the novel's central figures falls, and the political world changes irreversibly. Cao Cao dies soon afterward, his son Cao Pi takes the throne from the Han emperor, and Liu Bei responds by claiming imperial continuity for his own state. The last living representatives of the old heroic generation face a realm in which the Han is no longer an undisputed political institution. We will follow the campaign, the betrayal between allies, and the creation of rival dynastic claims, then ask why the novel's admiration for Guan Yu coexists with its account of his mistakes.

## A commander far from the court that appointed him

Guan Yu holds the exposed eastern position of Liu Bei's growing realm. His appointment recognizes years of fidelity and military reputation, but the distance from his lord is not a minor inconvenience. Messages between Jing and the western capital must travel through contested or difficult territory. Decisions about raids, alliances, and appointments may demand answers faster than the court can supply them. A frontier commander can acquire a degree of discretion that his central government has no practical way to eliminate.

Guan Yu's importance also makes him a symbol. A powerful officer who resists Cao Cao on the middle river demonstrates that Liu Bei can still threaten the north while maintaining western defenses. Yet the symbolic position may obscure his vulnerabilities. Jing's security depends upon not being attacked effectively from both directions. If Guan Yu advances north and leaves a thin rear guard, the Sun house can exploit the opportunity. If he remains wholly defensive, he may lose the chance to pressure Cao Cao while the western army is occupied elsewhere.

The novel associates his choices with pride, particularly in his dealings with emissaries from Sun Quan. His refusal of an offered marriage alliance is presented as an offense that deepens resentment. We should understand the gesture as more than a private insult. A marriage proposal from one ruling house to another can be a bid to regularize political relations. Rejecting it with contempt may persuade an uncertain partner that future cooperation will bring humiliation rather than respect.

It would be easy to make the entire downfall the inevitable result of one boastful warrior. The structural situation is more substantial. Liu Bei's western successes have altered Sun Quan's incentives. Jing is valuable to both parties. The northern campaign commits Guan Yu's best resources away from the rear. His character helps explain the pace and tone of the breakdown, but it does not create the underlying conflict from nothing.

## Orders given too far away

Guan Yu's last offensive is a reminder of the importance of communication in a divided command. The central government in Shu needs to know when the northern siege has become difficult, how many troops remain in the rear, and whether the southern alliance is still dependable. Guan Yu needs timely instruction about the acceptable limits of his campaign. Between those centers lie roads where messages can be delayed and events can change before a courier arrives.

A successful officer operating far from the ruler can become vulnerable to his own independence. He must make decisions in circumstances that cannot be submitted to the court for immediate approval, and his successes may lead the court to trust him without examining each new risk. This is a structural problem even for a commander without Guan Yu's pride. In the novel, the problem grows sharper because his prestige discourages those around him from challenging decisions they consider dangerous.

The collapse of Jing demonstrates that a frontier can be lost while the army defending it is busy winning victories elsewhere. The lesson is not that every offensive was mistaken. The strategic aim may be sound. A state must also ensure that one theater's success is protected against another theater's failure, and that personal confidence does not replace a reliable account of how much force remains available at home.

## The northern offensive from Jing

Liu Bei has secured Hanzhong and proclaimed himself a king. Guan Yu prepares an offensive from Jing toward Cao Cao's positions in the north. He advances against Fancheng, defended by Cao Ren, and confronts forces sent to relieve the garrison. The campaign offers a chance to pressure Cao Cao while Liu Bei's western state holds difficult mountain approaches. If both fronts succeed, Cao Cao's military position could become precarious.

The novel gives Guan Yu a formidable confidence. He has proven himself against outstanding warriors and expects his reputation to influence the conduct of enemies. Pang De, a commander on Cao Cao's side, enters the campaign prepared to fight to the death, famously bringing a coffin as a sign of his determination. The two figures meet in a contest of resolve. Pang De is not portrayed merely as a cowardly opponent destined to be defeated. His refusal to abandon his allegiance makes him, in some respects, a mirror of Guan Yu. Two officers who prize fidelity are locked into incompatible commitments.

Heavy rains change the campaign. Flood waters devastate forces sent to relieve Cao Ren. Yu Jin's army is overwhelmed, and Pang De is captured. Pang De refuses to submit and is executed. The novel gives Guan Yu command over a natural catastrophe whose strategic consequences are enormous. His victories create panic in the north and raise the prospect of further rebellion against Cao Cao's rule. Yet the weather is not a creation of personal courage. It is an environmental force that an able commander can exploit and that another army may be unable to withstand.

Guan Yu's prestige reaches a new height. A listener could almost imagine that the larger political conflict has become a personal biography: the hero whose loyalty survived captivity will now conquer his enemies. But the novel begins placing warnings around the triumph. His siege is prolonged. He has committed substantial troops northward. The region behind him remains disputed, and his treatment of potential allies is often arrogant. A military position can become most vulnerable just when its commander's reputation makes him least willing to admit weakness.

## The river becomes an enemy

Guan Yu's attack upon Fan Castle places him against northern defenders under severe pressure. The novel depicts heavy rains and flooding, the vulnerability of armies crowded into low ground, and the defeat of Yu Jin's forces. Water can erase the distinctions that commanders depend upon. A unit positioned for an ordinary encounter may find its retreat route submerged and its supplies inaccessible. Cavalry loses mobility; defenders cannot always distinguish safe ground from rising currents. What looked like a defensible formation becomes a collection of isolated men seeking a way out.

The famous drowning of the seven armies is one of the episodes through which Guan Yu acquires almost mythic prestige. But the flood is not simply a miracle produced by his will. Seasonal weather and river conditions matter, as do the northern commanders' dispositions. A serious military reading asks what the attacker could anticipate, how trapped troops communicated, and what options remained once the flood separated them. The novel brings these factors into a spectacle of destiny while allowing a skillful commander to exploit the resulting crisis.

Yu Jin's surrender and Pang De's refusal to submit are arranged as a contrast in reputations. Yu Jin has been a long-serving officer of Cao Cao, yet his capture becomes associated with shame. Pang De dies without transferring his allegiance and receives the moral dignity that martial romance gives to a defeated man who will not yield. The distinction is emotionally legible, but in an actual flooded battlefield surrender may be the only way to preserve surviving soldiers. An ethical judgment of an officer cannot be reduced to the fact that he lived or died.

The campaign's success produces a dangerous effect. Guan Yu's fame grows precisely when his geographic position becomes most exposed. He is now a commander feared by the north and resented by his former ally. The more he concentrates on completing the siege and exploiting the flood, the more tempting the undefended or insufficiently defended rear appears to the south.

## The wound and the physician

During the campaign Guan Yu suffers an injury from a poisoned arrow. The novel introduces Hua Tuo, the celebrated physician, to treat the wound. The treatment involves cutting away affected tissue while Guan Yu remains composed, occupied with a game and conversation. The contrast is constructed for maximum admiration. Men who have seen combat are horrified by the procedure; Guan Yu scarcely shows pain. His self-control becomes a measure of martial character.

The scene should not be repeated as secure medical history. The historical Hua Tuo is remembered in early sources, but the novel's placement of the physician with Guan Yu raises chronological problems. The episode belongs to a long tradition of heroic bodily anecdotes. Its purpose is to establish a kind of discipline distinct from physical strength. Guan Yu is formidable not only when attacking others but when refusing to let his own suffering determine his outward conduct.

The scene also has an ironic position. A hero capable of treating bodily pain with perfect composure may still be unable to control pride in diplomacy. Self-command is not a single skill transferred automatically to every human situation. The novel can praise Guan Yu's endurance while showing that the same man ignores political threats he ought to take seriously.

## A physician at the table

The account of Hua Tuo treating Guan Yu's wounded arm is told as a contest between pain and self-command. The physician works upon a serious injury while the patient continues a game, apparently untroubled by what would overwhelm an ordinary man. The scene adds medical knowledge to the display of martial virtue. Guan Yu can endure the treatment because his mind remains fixed upon the task and the reputation he wishes to preserve.

The figure of Hua Tuo belongs to the history of late Han medicine, but the novel's famous operation upon Guan Yu is part of the way later storytelling brings celebrated people into one another's lives. Chronology and the surviving medical anecdotes do not justify treating the whole dramatic encounter as a verified clinical procedure. Its literary work is to show Guan Yu's bravery in the one context where a sword cannot help him: he must submit to another person's skill and endure vulnerability before his own companions.

The surrounding soldiers' response is important. The scene teaches the group how to regard pain. A commander who bears a terrifying procedure without panic may strengthen the confidence of people who fear what will happen when the enemy attacks again. Yet the standard can also be cruel if treated as a requirement for every subordinate. Extraordinary fortitude should be admired without turning ordinary suffering into a sign of bad character.

## The ally on the other bank

Sun Quan has never accepted the loss of Jing as a permanent arrangement. His advisers see Guan Yu's northern campaign as an opportunity to recover what the southern house believes it is owed. Guan Yu's confidence and blunt treatment of messages from Sun Quan aggravate the situation. His refusal of a proposed marriage connection becomes another symbol of disrespect. The personal insult matters because it confirms a political fear: Guan Yu does not treat Wu as an equal partner whose claims require negotiation.

Lü Meng, a Wu commander, devises a plan to strike while Guan Yu's army is committed in the north. He is capable of careful study and organization, a contrast with any stereotype of the purely martial officer. In the novel he feigns illness or withdrawal, allowing the less threatening Lu Xun to appear in a position that may lower Guan Yu's guard. The deception encourages Guan Yu to reduce defenses at his rear. Then Wu forces advance under disguise, the famous white-clad crossing of the river. The garrisons are surprised, and the occupied towns fall.

The attack works partly because Wu's commanders arrange to treat residents and soldiers' families carefully. If Guan Yu's army believes its households have been protected, its soldiers may be less eager to retake the territory at terrible cost. The novel shows information about relatives becoming a weapon. A commander who has lost his rear bases may find his men surrendering because they are more concerned for their families than for an abstract claim to strategic land. That choice is not necessarily cowardly. It measures what soldiers believe a military campaign ought to protect.

The seizure exposes a contradiction in Guan Yu's situation. He holds Jing for Liu Bei, but Sun Quan regards it as territory withheld after a series of agreements. Guan Yu's personal superiority in combat cannot erase the unresolved dispute between the ruling houses. Even a perfect battlefield performance near Fancheng would not prevent an army from cutting his communications and occupying the places his troops call home.

## The men who appear to be merchants

Lü Meng, a leading commander of Sun Quan's house, has a different problem. He cannot defeat Guan Yu merely by announcing an assault upon Jing. A frontal attack would warn the garrisons and give Guan Yu time to turn back. The novel's solution is an operation based on concealment: men advance by river in boats that appear to carry ordinary merchants, and guards are surprised before they can organize a defense. The episode highlights a vulnerability in any territory that depends on commercial movement. Rivers move goods and news; they also move people whose purposes are not apparent from their clothes.

The strategy is more effective because it has a political counterpart. The southern forces do not simply burn every settlement they enter. By restraining soldiers and treating the households of Guan Yu's troops with care, they weaken the defenders' incentives to fight desperately. News travels to Guan Yu's soldiers that their relatives are safe under new occupation. Men who have followed him because they trust his protection may begin to ask why they should perish on a road back to a place whose inhabitants are being left in peace.

This is an important contrast to the heroic duel. Lü Meng's achievement lies in arranging a change of allegiance while reducing the need for open fighting. It also exposes the moral uncertainty of the novel's sympathies. We may regret the blow to Guan Yu while recognizing that a commander who can restrain his army and prevent the plunder of civilians has performed a political good. The success is not automatically dishonorable because the novel's favored hero is the victim.

When Guan Yu learns that Jing has fallen behind him, the campaign reverses its meaning. He was advancing toward northern glory; now he is an isolated commander trying to reach a base that no longer recognizes his authority. A supply line is more than a path for carts. It is a chain of people willing to guard and provision those carts. Once that willingness changes, a famous army can dissolve even before a decisive battlefield defeat.

## The narrowing road to Maicheng

Guan Yu retreats south in the hope of recovering his base. He discovers how completely the strategic situation has changed. Officers who should be waiting with supplies are gone or have surrendered. Requests for assistance fail. The enemy holds the routes. The novel gives him a final struggle near Maicheng, where his remaining followers must choose between desperate breakout and surrender. The great warrior has become a fugitive in territory whose population and officials he once commanded.

He is captured and executed by Sun Quan's forces in 219, together with his son Guan Ping in the novel's account. The historical defeat and execution are well attested, though the details differ across records and literary tradition. The ending is not a defeat in an equal duel. It is a failure of position, alliance management, and the capacity to keep an army supplied and loyal. The same distinction was visible at Lü Bu's death: exceptional martial ability cannot sustain a command once the political conditions supporting it have collapsed.

The novel's treatment of Guan Yu after death draws on supernatural and religious traditions. His spirit appears, and the tale links him with signs that trouble surviving enemies. These scenes belong to the growing culture of Guan Yu's veneration. Across later centuries the historical officer became Lord Guan, a major deity honored in varied religious and social settings. That transformation cannot be explained by biography alone. Stories of loyalty, death, vengeance, and protection made him available as a figure with moral force far beyond the actual campaigns of his lifetime.

## The warrior as a captive

The retreat toward Maicheng strips away the signs that have protected Guan Yu's identity. His reputation no longer guarantees obedience. Former allies block routes, former subordinates have lost confidence, and the distance between his surviving force and the western heartland seems to lengthen with every day. A lord who once accepted a dangerous gift from Cao Cao and rode toward Liu Bei through hostile gates is again a traveler separated from his companions. But the first journey was animated by hope of reunion; this one narrows toward capture.

The novel's representation of his death insists on continuity of character. Guan Yu does not renounce the allegiance by which he has defined himself. The same steadfastness that won admiration in the service of Cao Cao may now make compromise impossible. Readers are invited to mourn not only the defeat of a general but the extinction of a moral presence whose conduct once seemed capable of bending the political world around it.

Historical Guan Yu was captured and killed during Sun Quan's recovery of Jing, and the alliance's breakdown has a concrete military history. Later accounts gave the death an increasingly sacred dimension. His posthumous rise as a figure of loyalty and martial protection continued far beyond the novel, through religious practice, imperial recognition, and popular storytelling. The transformation should not be projected backward as though his contemporaries were already watching an immortal protector fall. It is part of the history of how people remembered him.

The damage to Liu Bei's regime is also material. It loses a strategic province, a valuable army, and a celebrated commander. The western state now faces a southern rival holding the very approaches through which it once hoped to cooperate against Cao Cao. No amount of admiration for Guan Yu can erase those consequences. The story's grief is powerful because private fidelity and public strategy have come apart in the worst possible way.

## Why this loss changes everything

The loss of Jing is not merely the death of one commander. It compromises Zhuge Liang's strategic design, which depended on access through both Jing and the western province. Liu Bei's kingdom retains Yi and Hanzhong, but the eastern route has become a frontier controlled by a former ally. Sun Quan gains a strategically important region and removes a rival whose pride made negotiation increasingly difficult. Cao Cao's northern position benefits from the division of his opponents.

The tragedy is intensified by the history of their alliance. Liu Bei and Sun Quan cooperated to prevent Cao Cao from conquering the south. Each needed the other at Red Cliffs. Now a dispute over the distribution of the defended provinces has produced a new alignment. Wu's recovery of Jing makes military sense from its own perspective. It is devastating to Shu's ambitions and to the moral language of brotherhood in which Liu Bei's followers understand their cause. The book refuses to let the victorious coalition remain a permanent alliance of the good.

Guan Yu's death will also force Liu Bei to decide how to understand his original oath. The peach-garden vow bound him to Guan Yu and Zhang Fei in a commitment deeper than convenience. Does the obligation of a sworn brother require vengeance even when war would jeopardize the people and territory he now rules? A private promise made when three men commanded a small band has become a possible cause of interstate conflict. The oath's grandeur is about to meet its political cost.

## The northern ruler's final illness

Cao Cao continues to wield tremendous power, but his health deteriorates. The novel presents illness, ominous signs, and episodes involving the physician Hua Tuo. Cao Cao's suspicions help produce another tragic encounter in which he treats a proposed medical intervention as a possible assassination and orders the physician punished. The scenes continue a long-standing portrait: Cao Cao can attract talent and see possibilities overlooked by rivals, yet he can also become cruel or destructive when fear of betrayal takes hold.

He dies in 220. His son Cao Pi inherits the political system his father constructed. The transition reveals the extraordinary scale of Cao Cao's achievement. He has built a northern command powerful enough to survive his own death. He never formally became emperor, yet his household now possesses armies, advisers, administrative centers, and claims of public authority that can be transferred to a successor. The distinction between ministerial service and a new dynasty has become precarious.

The novel includes the celebrated rivalry between Cao Pi and his talented brother Cao Zhi, framed through the motif of a poem composed under threat. Whether the famous seven-step poem is historical in the precise form readers know is doubtful. Its place in the novel is clear: even a house powerful enough to replace a dynasty may suffer from dangerous competition among its sons. Succession cannot be taken for granted simply because armies obey the family name.


## A northern ruler at the end of his strength

Cao Cao's death arrives near the collapse of the old Han political order. The novel surrounds his last illness with forebodings and memories that invite readers to measure the founder's extraordinary career. He began as one ambitious officer among many; he ends with a northern realm, a powerful administrative organization, and an emperor unable to act independently. That development required battlefield success, ruthless decisions, talent for administration, and an appetite for authority no earlier opponent could fully restrain.

The tension in his legacy is already visible. He preserved much of the machinery of Han government, yet his preservation placed the government under his household. He employed capable men without always sharing their principles. He could admire courage in an enemy and order extreme violence against civilians. The novel's judgment is often severe, but a historical account must be more particular about what he achieved and what his subjects endured.

His son Cao Pi inherits a regime that can operate after the founder's death. This durability is itself an achievement. The succession does not instantly dissolve Cao Cao's armies and offices. Yet the transition also supplies the conditions for the formal end of Han. Cao Pi can transform effective control into an imperial title, pressing Emperor Xian to abdicate through the language of orderly succession. The public act creates a new dynastic calendar. The long period in which the emperor had lost practical independence finally receives an official conclusion.

For Liu Bei, Cao Pi's accession changes the terms of the struggle. He is no longer opposing a minister who governs through a Han emperor. He is opposing a rival who claims that Han has ended and another dynasty has begun. Liu Bei's own claim to continue Han becomes easier to formulate and harder to reconcile with the fact that rival regimes possess substantial populations and administrations. The empire has become, formally as well as practically, a disputed inheritance.


## The abdication of Emperor Xian

Cao Pi compels or arranges the abdication of Emperor Xian, ending the Han dynasty and establishing the state later known as Cao Wei. Ceremonies and documents present the transfer as orderly, but the military imbalance behind them is enormous. The old emperor's formal consent does not mean that he freely chose between comparable options. The scene represents the completion of a long transformation: the general who once moved the court into safety has left his descendants in control of the throne itself.

Liu Bei and his supporters reject the legitimacy of this transfer. They claim to continue the Han line and eventually raise Liu Bei to the imperial title in the west. The name Shu-Han expresses a contested claim rather than an uncontested constitutional fact. Sun Quan remains a third major ruler whose formal status develops through negotiations with the other regimes. The historical period conventionally called the Three Kingdoms is now emerging from the earlier decades of factional crisis and regional war.

The novel's moral preference for Liu Bei should not prevent us from seeing the difficulties of all three claims. Cao Pi has inherited the strongest territorial state but has displaced the official dynastic line. Liu Bei invokes Han continuity while his own authority rests on conquest and the willingness of supporters to treat his descent as decisive. Sun Quan commands a durable southern regime but operates amid competing forms of recognition. No political title removes the need to rule in fact.

Guan Yu has died in the year before these ceremonies, and the two events belong together in the structure of the story. The hero whose fidelity was founded on personal brotherhood is gone. The empire whose legitimacy supposedly anchored that fidelity is formally ended by Cao Pi. Liu Bei must now decide whether his claim to restore the Han requires a new empire under his own name, and whether vengeance for his brother is more urgent than the restoration he has always proclaimed. The novel has moved from the promise in the peach garden to the demands of dynastic sovereignty. Its greatest tensions are no longer waiting to be discovered; they are arriving at once.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 73–80. Guan Yu's 219 defeat and execution, Lü Meng's campaign, Cao Cao's 220 death, Cao Pi's replacement of Han, and Liu Bei's later imperial claim are historical. The physician bone-scraping encounter with Hua Tuo contains serious chronological problems and should not be passed off as medical history. Guan Yu's apparitions and later cult should be treated as reception, with ter Haar as a critical guide.
