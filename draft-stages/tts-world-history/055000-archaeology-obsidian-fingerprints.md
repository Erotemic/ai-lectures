# The Obsidian Fingerprint: From Volcano to Village

Imagine an archaeologist kneeling in a cave in southern Greece and lifting a small, sharp fragment of black glass from an ancient occupation layer. It might once have been part of a blade. Someone struck it from a larger piece, perhaps used it, perhaps discarded it within minutes. The person left no name, and nobody wrote down where the glass came from. But the glass may still tell us something about a journey. In parts of the Aegean, obsidian was carried to settlements far from the volcanoes that produced it. A fragment recovered on the Greek mainland can preserve a material connection to an island across open water.

How can anyone tell which island? Black glass looks rather similar in many places. An archaeologist cannot normally point at a dark flake and declare that it came from one volcano. Instead, researchers measure minute differences in its elemental composition and compare those measurements with samples from geological sources. This is chemical provenance: reconstructing where an archaeological material originated from properties that survived its use and burial.

Our main case will be obsidian from the Aegean and the wider Near East. We will follow the development of the technique, beginning with work by Joseph Cann and Colin Renfrew in the 1960s, extending through Renfrew's collaborations with the geologists J. E. Dixon and J. R. Cann, and into modern portable instruments studied by archaeometrists including Robert Tykot and Michael Shackley. Along the way we will ask what a geological match establishes about ancient people, and how far it can take us toward reconstructing travel and exchange.

## A stone that begins as glass

Obsidian forms when silica-rich molten rock cools rapidly enough that extensive mineral crystals do not have time to grow. The result is a natural glass. It can fracture in a way that produces very sharp edges, which made it attractive for cutting tools. Obsidian is not distributed evenly across the landscape. Good deposits occur in particular volcanic regions, and access to them can be constrained by mountains, seas, and the choices of other communities.

That uneven distribution is the beginning of the archaeological opportunity. A stone blade made from local flint does not necessarily tell us much about connections beyond a settlement. If a settlement contains obsidian and the nearest suitable volcanic glass is many days away, movement has occurred. The glass or the people carrying it crossed some distance. But distance alone does not identify a route. A fragment may have passed through several settlements before it reached its final owner. A trader might carry it, but so might a family visiting relatives, someone exchanging gifts, or a traveler bringing a tool for personal use.

It also matters whether people moved the raw material or finished objects. If a village contains a few elegant blades and almost no production waste, perhaps the blades arrived ready to use. If it contains cores, flakes, unfinished pieces, and shattered fragments, its residents probably worked obsidian there. Comparing the stages of manufacture lets archaeologists ask whether communities imported glass as blocks, received prepared cores, or obtained completed implements. Chemical analysis gives one part of that story; the pattern of discarded waste gives another.

Now picture two volcanic flows. Both are black. Both can be chipped into blades. One contains a little more rubidium relative to strontium; the other contains more zirconium or yttrium. These elements appear in quantities far too small for the naked eye to register, but instruments can measure them. During a volcanic rock's formation, the composition of its magma and the minerals crystallizing from it influence which elements remain in the glass. The glass therefore carries a chemical pattern linked to the geological history of its formation.

The word fingerprint is useful as an analogy, but it has a limit. A human fingerprint can distinguish one person from billions under favorable conditions. A chemical pattern does not always distinguish every possible volcanic flow. Two deposits may overlap in composition, and the geological sampling may be incomplete. Researchers usually ask which known source or source group best explains an artifact's measurements, and how confidently alternatives can be excluded.

## Before the laboratory could answer

Long before modern sourcing studies, archaeologists recognized obsidian in places distant from obvious volcanic sources. They mapped distributions, compared colors and translucence, and reasoned about possible routes. But superficial resemblance was dangerous. Glass from separate volcanoes could look alike; variation within a single volcanic flow could make pieces from the same source look different. The field needed a way to characterize the material rather than rely on appearance.

In 1964, Joseph Cann and Colin Renfrew published *The Characterization of Obsidian and its Application to the Mediterranean Region* in the *Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society*. They explained how instrumental analysis could discriminate sources using trace-element composition. This was a genuine methodological change: instead of suggesting that an artifact resembled stone from an island, researchers could compare measured chemical properties. It also changed the questions archaeologists could ask. They could examine whether different settlements drew on the same source and whether the proportions changed through time.

Renfrew, J. E. Dixon, and Cann expanded the approach. Their 1966 study, *Obsidian and Early Cultural Contact in the Near East*, examined connections among early farming communities and the geological obsidian sources available to them. It is worth crediting the collaboration accurately. Chemical provenance required geological knowledge and instrument work as much as archaeological questions. Cann's mineralogical expertise and Dixon's contribution were not incidental technical services. The interpretation depended on defining meaningful source groups.

Consider a hypothetical study modeled on those early projects. A team first visits volcanic outcrops and collects reference samples from multiple locations within each deposit. They record coordinates, geological features, and the circumstances of collection. They then analyze archaeological flakes from several settlements whose excavation layers have been dated independently. The table of results might show one elemental cluster dominating the earliest layers of a village and another rising centuries later.

What would that result mean? It would establish a change in the geological source represented at that site, provided the measurements and sampling are reliable. It would invite questions about travel routes, resource access, or exchange partners. It would not reveal why the change happened. A volcanic flow may have become inaccessible. A new community may have arrived. People may have preferred the fracture properties of another glass. Political relations might have changed. Archaeologists need the rest of the settlement record to choose among those explanations.

## The long journey from Melos

One of the most striking cases comes from the Aegean. Obsidian from the island of Melos, also called Milos, has been identified at mainland sites including Franchthi Cave in the Argolid. The cave's long occupation sequence was excavated and studied by interdisciplinary teams, and archaeologists including Catherine Perlès developed detailed accounts of its chipped-stone assemblages. The stone's provenance and the archaeological chronology must be established separately. A geochemical result identifies material; an excavation sequence and dating evidence establish when people used it.

For some prehistoric periods represented at Franchthi, obtaining Melian obsidian would have required crossing water. This provides evidence for maritime movement before the invention of writing in the region. But stop just before turning that into a picture of a particular boat. The archaeological fragment does not tell us the vessel's design, whether it had paddles or sails, or whether a community based on the mainland made the voyage itself. The crossing could involve multiple stages. The object confirms that material traversed a marine barrier; the practical mechanism must be reconstructed from additional evidence.

In 1971, S. A. Durrani, H. A. Khan, M. Taj, Colin Renfrew, and colleagues published a *Nature* paper on fission-track analysis that also confirmed a Melian source for obsidian from Franchthi. Fission tracks are microscopic damage trails left by the spontaneous fission of uranium atoms in certain materials. Their density can provide information about an obsidian's geological history, subject to calibration and the effects of temperature. This method works differently from simple trace-element matching, making the confirmation valuable. Independent techniques that rest on different physical properties can make a source interpretation more robust.

What does Melian obsidian say about life around the Aegean? It shows that people operating in a world of caves, coastal settlements, and islands could acquire material across distances that maps might make appear forbidding. The same region later became densely connected through seafaring networks. But we should not fold all those periods into one continuous tradition without evidence. An early crossing does not prove a direct institutional ancestry linking prehistoric foragers to later Bronze Age merchants. The strength of the obsidian case is that it gives us a firmly material starting point for a question that otherwise invites speculation.

## How a machine reads a stone

Modern archaeologists can analyze obsidian using several instruments. One common approach is X-ray fluorescence, often abbreviated X-R-F. In simplified terms, an instrument irradiates a sample with X-rays. Atoms in the sample can emit secondary X-rays at energies related to the elements present. A detector measures those energies and intensities, and a calibrated analysis estimates elemental concentrations. Researchers may focus on elements such as rubidium, strontium, zirconium, yttrium, niobium, or iron, depending on the source region and instrument.

A portable X-R-F instrument can sometimes measure an artifact without taking a destructive sample. Robert Tykot reported extensive Mediterranean work using handheld instruments, including many thousands of artifacts and hundreds of geological samples. That scale makes it possible to ask population-level questions. Instead of choosing three beautiful blades for laboratory analysis, a team can sometimes characterize an entire excavated collection and discover that a common, unremarkable kind of flake traveled farther than expected.

But the instrument does not simply announce the name of an island. The measurement must be calibrated with standards of known composition. The beam may interrogate a surface altered by weathering or burial. A tiny, irregular flake can present geometry different from a flat reference sample. Some elements are easier to quantify accurately than others. Different laboratories and instruments may produce results requiring careful normalization before comparison. Michael Shackley and other specialists have emphasized that source assignment depends on analytical precision and the completeness of geological reference collections.

An instrument can give extremely precise measurements of the wrong thing. Suppose a flake has a coating of soil rich in iron, or its surface has weathered. If an analyst measures that coating as if it were pristine glass, the numbers may be repeatable and misleading. A sound study documents surface preparation, instrument settings, standards, repeated measurements, and the rationale for assigning an artifact to one source group. That record allows another laboratory to compare or question the result.

There are also techniques that examine the sample differently. Neutron activation analysis can measure elemental signatures with high sensitivity, although it requires specialized facilities and raises practical issues about sample handling. Laser ablation coupled to mass spectrometry removes a minute amount of material to analyze a broader elemental suite. These methods may be chosen when source groups overlap under more portable measurements. The goal is not to celebrate the most expensive instrument. It is to obtain data capable of discriminating the sources relevant to a historical question while preserving archaeological material as far as possible.

## From measurements to a source assignment

Imagine five candidate obsidian sources labeled A through E. The archaeologist has analyzed reference samples from each. Source A typically contains relatively high rubidium and low strontium. Source B shows the reverse pattern. Sources C and D overlap on those two elements but separate when zirconium and niobium are considered. Source E resembles C in one volcanic flow but differs in another.

The researcher then measures a blade from an archaeological site. On a scatterplot of rubidium against strontium, its measurement falls within the cloud for Source C, but close to Source D. Adding another element can improve the classification. Repeating the measurement assesses precision. A model can estimate the likelihood of the observed vector of measurements under each reference population. But statistical confidence in a classification is conditional on the sources in the reference database. If an unknown sixth source exists, a model trained only on five sources cannot discover that absence by producing a confident label.

Fieldwork therefore remains essential. Geological surveys may reveal previously unrecorded exposures or variation within a known flow. A quarry might lie near one location while transported cobbles from that same source occur along a river. If prehistoric people collected glass from secondary deposits, an artifact's geological origin and the place where someone picked it up may differ. This is a useful distinction: provenance means the origin of the material, not necessarily the last stop before it reached a settlement.

Now return to the village and consider four layers. In the earliest, almost all analyzed obsidian comes from Source A. In the next, A remains common but C appears. Later, C predominates. In the latest, both are scarce and local flint becomes common. It is tempting to translate this into a story about three successive trading alliances. But the layers might contain different numbers of artifacts, be separated by centuries of unequal duration, or have been excavated from different functional parts of the settlement. A large refuse pit can dominate a sample. We need counts, dates, excavation areas, and measures of uncertainty before we write a history of changing networks.

The analyst can also examine artifact size and manufacture. Cores of one source might appear in large pieces while finished blades of another arrive as small, carefully prepared objects. That difference could reflect how material was transported, its accessibility, or the skill and choices of the people using it. Refitting flakes from a single core can reveal sequences of manufacture within a site. Chemical sourcing helps link those technological activities to geological origins, but neither kind of evidence tells the whole story alone.

## Sourcing is not dating

A frequent misunderstanding follows from the fact that researchers can sometimes both characterize obsidian and study how long its surface has been exposed. These are separate problems. Source analysis compares the rock with geological reference samples. Dating asks when a particular human activity occurred. An artifact's source does not supply the age of the archaeological deposit in which it was found.

Obsidian hydration offered one proposed chronometric method. When a fresh surface of obsidian is exposed, water slowly enters the glass and may produce a hydrated zone near that surface. Irving Friedman and Robert Smith published influential work on this idea in 1960. In principle, measuring the thickness of the hydration band and knowing how fast water moves through that composition of glass could constrain the time since the surface was made. But the rate depends on temperature, glass chemistry, environmental history, and other conditions. A simple rule that a band of a certain thickness always means a certain number of years would be unreliable across regions.

This creates a useful worked problem. Suppose two blades from different volcanic sources were chipped on the same afternoon and buried together. Their chemical compositions may cause water to penetrate at different rates, so their hydration bands could become different thicknesses. If we used one universal conversion from thickness to age, we might conclude that the blades had been made hundreds of years apart. The error would arise from a poor physical model of the process, not from the excavator's record. In practice, calibration and independent dates are crucial, and the suitability of hydration dating varies between contexts.

Radiocarbon dating of charcoal associated with an artifact has its own complications. A charcoal fragment might come from wood that was already old when burned. It may have moved into a different deposit. A radiocarbon determination measures the carbon in that sample; excavation establishes the relationship between the sample and the stone tool. The resulting date can constrain the period of tool use only if that association is sound. For Franchthi, excavated sequences and other chronological evidence are therefore essential companions to the material-source studies.

A further distinction concerns fission-track analysis. Durrani and his collaborators studied microscopic radiation damage that reflects properties of the volcanic glass itself. Their results were used for geological characterization and sourcing. It would be a mistake to hear that a scientific method measures an age-related physical process and assume that it directly gives the date when a prehistoric person chipped the blade. Rocks are older than the tools made from them. A geological clock and an archaeological clock can answer different questions even when both involve the same object.

These distinctions are especially valuable when a headline announces the earliest sea journey. The conclusion depends jointly on identifying an island source and assigning the artifact to a sufficiently early occupation phase. A stronger source match does not automatically make the archaeological date more precise. Each evidentiary link must be evaluated on its own terms, and agreement among them is what makes a historical reconstruction persuasive.

## When does movement become trade?

The presence of distant material proves that a transfer occurred at some point in the object's history. Whether the transfer was commercial trade is another question. Prehistoric exchange need not resemble a modern market. A person may have brought stone as a gift, a marriage exchange, a personal possession, or a shared resource collected during a seasonal journey. Communities may have passed material along through repeated local interactions, with no one participant knowing its distant source.

Archaeologists sometimes study how the amount of material changes with distance from its source. If large quantities cluster near a quarry and smaller quantities occur farther away, that pattern is compatible with a declining cost-effective supply. But one curve is unlikely to distinguish direct procurement from down-the-line exchange. Mountains, water routes, community preferences, political restrictions, and the availability of alternative stone all change the pattern. The great value of sourcing is that it makes those models testable against actual materials rather than a map of hypothetical arrows.

How might an archaeologist test direct acquisition? A settlement with production debris and a boat-accessible coastal position might plausibly have sent expeditions. Repeated use of a distinctive source across generations may suggest stable links. Evidence for travel in the opposite direction, such as goods of mainland origin at an island site, may make reciprocal contact more likely. None of these observations alone proves that every piece of glass was carried by a resident from the settlement where it was found.

The people who moved the obsidian are themselves difficult to recover. Source analysis names a geological outcrop rather than a merchant, fisher, craftworker, or family. We can sometimes reconstruct what tasks their tools performed and how much effort was invested in obtaining a particular raw material. Their motives remain partly hidden. A good historical explanation acknowledges this gap without reducing a real material network to an empty abstraction. Every piece represents decisions and labor, even when the names of the people involved are gone.

## What survives the next discovery

Chemical provenance changes when new reference material arrives. If a previously unknown volcanic flow has the same elemental pattern as Source C, some old assignments may need to be reconsidered. If a new instrument resolves differences once hidden by measurement error, one broad source group may split into two. If excavation establishes that a flake came from an earlier layer than previously believed, the chronology of movement changes while its chemical match remains the same.

This is an important feature of cumulative research. Cann and Renfrew's 1964 method did not finish the study of obsidian exchange. It established a way of turning artifacts and rocks into comparable evidence. Dixon and later collaborators extended geographic coverage. Archaeologists excavating caves and settlements provided dated material. Laboratory specialists developed and standardized new methods. Geological surveyors built reference collections. More recent studies refine distributions and test increasingly detailed social questions.

It is also possible for improved data to weaken a familiar story. A dramatic map might show a single direct route from an island quarry to a distant city. Later finds might reveal intermediate settlements using the same glass, or show that multiple routes were possible. Chemical origin can remain secure while the transport narrative changes. That is why it helps to separate the measured fact, the source assignment, and the proposed account of social exchange.

Think again of the first obsidian flake in the cave. At first it is a sharp object recovered from a layer of earth. An excavation record locates it in a sequence of human activity. An instrument measures its composition. Geological samples offer a likely source. Dates establish the period in which it was used. Comparison with other artifacts reveals a distribution extending through settlements and across water. Each step adds a different kind of knowledge; no one step can replace the others.

The most satisfying result is not a confident label on a museum display. It is a more precise historical question. How did communities that left us almost no writing acquire material from across the sea? What changed when people began collecting from different sources? Who worked the glass and where? The volcano offers the beginning of the answer. Recovering the human connections requires the archaeological record around the stone.

### Editorial cautions

- Franchthi obsidian demonstrates material movement across sea, not the exact design of the boat or identity of the carrier.
- A trace-element match selects among characterized geological sources; unknown sources and within-flow variation limit assignments.
- A source identified from a geological signature should not be presented as a direct trading partner, let alone proof of market exchange.
