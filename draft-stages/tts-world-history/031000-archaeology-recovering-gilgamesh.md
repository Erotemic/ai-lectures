# Recovering Gilgamesh: Excavation, Decipherment, and Broken Tablets

Imagine reading a novel in which the pages have been shattered into small pieces, many chapters are missing, some surviving passages belong to different editions written centuries apart, and the language has had no living native speakers for thousands of years. Now imagine that the fragments are scattered among museums in several countries, along with many more fragments whose contents are unidentified. How could you possibly reconstruct the book?

That is part of what happened to the Epic of Gilgamesh. We can listen to a story of a king of Uruk, his companion Enkidu, the death that changes his life, and a journey in search of survival beyond the grave. But the modern story we hear does not come to us as an intact manuscript excavated from the king's bedroom. It is the outcome of ancient copying, destruction, excavation, language decipherment, modern collation, and centuries of editorial decisions. Knowing that history makes the poem more remarkable, because it lets us see how much work stands between a fragment of clay and a readable literary narrative.

We will follow the chain backward, beginning in the ancient world. At the end we should be able to answer several different questions. How do we know the sounds or meanings of cuneiform signs? How were the tablets found? Who first identified the flood story? How can scholars tell when two broken pieces belong together? And when a modern translation appears smooth, which parts reflect a preserved text and which reflect the editor's reconstruction?

## The libraries of the ancient world

Gilgamesh belongs to a literature that changed over time. Sumerian poems about Bilgames, the Sumerian form of the hero's name, circulated in the late third and early second millennia before the common era. Akkadian narratives developed their own arrangements and wording. The version many readers meet today, often called the Standard Babylonian Epic, is preserved especially in first-millennium copies. Mesopotamian scholarly tradition associated a major version with a learned figure called Sin-leqi-unninni. That attribution is worth preserving, but it does not make him the author of every episode in every surviving Gilgamesh text. The work had a long history before and after his supposed contribution.

A tablet was created by pressing a stylus into damp clay, leaving combinations of wedge-shaped impressions. The writing system could represent words, syllables, determinatives, and other conventions. A sign did not always have one fixed value. A scholar reading Akkadian must therefore understand the grammar and the scribal conventions as well as recognize the sign shapes. To complicate matters, some tablets preserve school exercises rather than authoritative library copies, and different scribes can make different errors.

One major source for the Standard Babylonian Epic is the royal library of Ashurbanipal, a seventh-century before-common-era Assyrian king, at Nineveh in what is now Iraq. Ashurbanipal's collection included scholarly, ritual, medical, lexical, and literary works. This was a library supported by royal power, not an unbiased sample of everything Mesopotamians ever wrote. The tablets reflect what scribes and collectors thought worth acquiring and preserving. Ancient attribution matters here too: the monarch's library has a name, but the many scribes who copied and organized its contents were essential to its existence, and most cannot now be individually identified.

Clay survives in circumstances that destroy papyrus and wood. Some tablets were fired accidentally by destruction fires; others survived as dried clay protected in buried deposits. But survival is irregular. Pieces can break along lines of writing. One face may be legible while the other has worn away. A tablet found in a palace mound may have been moved before burial. We must reconstruct the history of the artifacts before attempting to reconstruct the history that their texts describe.

## Nineveh and the people who brought its tablets to light

In the nineteenth century, European and local excavators investigated the great Assyrian centers of northern Mesopotamia. Austen Henry Layard became internationally famous for excavations at Nimrud and Nineveh. His colleague Hormuzd Rassam, an Iraqi-born Assyrian archaeologist from Mosul, had a particularly important role in the investigations, including work that recovered tablets from Nineveh. A summary that says the British discovered the library can erase the expertise of Rassam and the many local diggers, craftspeople, and transport workers whose names were often not recorded in published accounts.

The excavations were part of imperial-era collecting. Objects were removed from Ottoman territories and carried to European institutions. Large sculpted reliefs became museum attractions, and thousands of inscribed clay pieces entered storage and study. The transport and collecting made the texts available to a new scholarly community while separating much of the material from its landscape and from present-day Iraqi custodianship. An account of how we know the epic should include that history, because provenance is part of the evidence, and the cultural ownership of excavated objects remains a live question.

At first the tablets did not present a readable book. Many were unsorted, incomplete, and labelled by excavation or museum numbers rather than literary titles. It took people trained in the ancient languages to distinguish a military chronicle, an astronomical series, a school list, and a poem. The texts were not discovered once, on one spectacular day, and then translated without difficulty. Recovery depended on repeated examination and patient comparison over decades.

The most famous episode in the poem's modern rediscovery concerns George Smith, an English engraver who taught himself cuneiform and joined the British Museum's work on the tablets. In 1872 Smith identified a Mesopotamian flood narrative in material now recognized as Tablet Eleven of Gilgamesh. He presented the discovery to the Society of Biblical Archaeology in December of that year. The resemblance to the flood of Genesis drew public attention. Smith became a figure of popular legend; yet his finding rested on the earlier work of excavators, copyists, decipherers, and museum colleagues. Samuel Birch had supported his work, while the collections themselves owed much to Rassam and Layard's excavations.

The irony is that the initial public fascination was often directed less at Gilgamesh himself than at a story resembling the Bible. Smith's audience wanted to know whether the ancient tablets could illuminate Genesis. That question helped propel interest in Assyriology but could also narrow how the literature was read. The tablet speaks from a Mesopotamian tradition with its own complex development. It is possible to compare flood narratives without treating one as merely a footnote to the other.

## How the script became readable

Before Smith could identify a flood story, other scholars had to learn to read cuneiform. The process involved many people and several languages. At Persepolis, inscriptions written in multiple varieties of cuneiform invited comparison. Georg Friedrich Grotefend made crucial early progress in Old Persian by using patterns of royal names and titles. Henry Rawlinson later copied and analyzed the great multilingual inscription of Darius at Behistun, helping advance the reading of Old Persian and related written traditions. These were major contributions, but the history is sometimes compressed into the false impression that a single heroic decipherer solved every cuneiform language at once.

Edward Hincks, the Irish scholar, was particularly important for understanding the more complex writing used for Akkadian. Jules Oppert and Edwin Norris contributed to the wider decipherment effort; different researchers worked on Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian with varying degrees of success. In 1857, translations of the same Assyrian text prepared independently by Rawlinson, Hincks, Oppert, and William Henry Fox Talbot were compared by a scholarly committee. Their agreement on central features was strong evidence that the system could be read reliably. It was a comparative test, rather than a proclamation based on one expert's confidence.

There is a further correction to the familiar story. Old Persian inscriptions helped open the problem, but the Behistun inscription was not a single magical key that directly unlocked all Akkadian. The assyriologist and historian of writing Peter T. Daniels has discussed the limits of that simplified claim, and the Cuneiform Digital Library Journal provides a detailed history of early contributions. Progress came from bilingual texts, sign lists, royal names, grammar, comparison across inscriptions, and independent verification of readings. Scholarly histories of decipherment are themselves subject to revision as archives are studied more closely.

Consider an example without needing to know Akkadian. Suppose a sign appears in a list where surrounding signs identify a known ruler, and the same cluster appears in a second inscription beside a different title. A proposed sound value might make both passages grammatical. Another sign might mark the category of a divine name but not be read aloud. A syllabic spelling could alternate with a sign representing a whole word. The reading becomes credible through repeated agreement across examples. If it makes one sentence look elegant but breaks a hundred other sentences, it is probably wrong.

That is why the ancient lexical lists are so important. Scribes compiled lists of signs, professions, objects, animals, and words. They were teaching materials and intellectual systems in their own right, and they also supply modern philologists with comparisons. We can sometimes determine a difficult sign value because an ancient student copied a list whose pattern supplies information missing from a literary tablet. The ancient writers were already analyzing their written language. Modern decipherers benefit from the knowledge they preserved.

## Reconstructing a text from fragments

Imagine one broken tablet preserving the beginning of a line, another giving the ending, and a third tablet from a different city preserving the passage nearly intact. An editor must ask whether all three represent the same literary version. The words might match exactly; they might differ through copying; or they might belong to distinct recensions. Even a physically perfect join is only one kind of evidence. Sometimes two museum fragments fit together like pottery shards. Other times they share text without sharing a physical tablet. These cases must be distinguished.

The Standard Babylonian Epic is not a single manuscript with every word securely preserved. Scholars consult different copies, compare lines, and decide how to present damaged passages. Some editions mark missing lines with gaps. Others offer cautious restorations when parallel passages make a reconstruction likely. Translation adds another layer: an Akkadian word may have a wider semantic range than the English expression chosen to render it. The translator selects a readable phrase, which can obscure alternative readings if no notes are supplied.

Andrew George's major study *The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: Introduction, Critical Edition and Cuneiform Texts* is an example of how demanding this scholarship is. George works with numerous manuscripts and witnesses, documenting readings and the editorial basis for reconstructing passages. His *Epic of Gilgamesh* translation for general readers offers another route into the poem. When we say that a scene occurs in the epic, we ought to ask which textual witness and which edition support that scene. Translators and editors are intellectual contributors, and they deserve names in the history of the text.

The Sumerian Gilgamesh poems form a related but distinct problem. Samuel Noah Kramer translated and studied important Sumerian compositions in the twentieth century, including tales of Gilgamesh and Aga and the hero's death. Later scholarship refined individual readings and made broader comparison possible. At Oxford, Jeremy Black and colleagues including Graham Cunningham, Eleanor Robson, Jarle Ebeling, Esther Flückiger-Hawker, Jon Taylor, and Gábor Zólyomi helped produce the Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature. Its editors explicitly describe composite texts reconstructed from tablets, many written by apprentice scribes. The database makes it possible to see a text in transliteration and English while learning that a smooth paragraph can be built from multiple damaged witnesses.

This changes the way a historical lecture ought to sound. If one narrative says Gilgamesh acts alone and another places Enkidu beside him, we should not automatically merge them to repair a plot hole. We should identify the composition, its date range, and what its manuscripts actually preserve. The variation is evidence of literary history. The freedom to compare versions is more interesting than pretending that ancient Mesopotamia wrote a canonical novel once and then never changed it.

## What a museum label cannot say by itself

Suppose a museum displays a small rectangular object marked with wedge shapes, calls it a fragment of the Gilgamesh Epic, and dates it to the first millennium before the common era. What should we ask? First, what is the museum registration number? Without that identifier, we may not be able to tell which object an editor cited. Next, where was it excavated? Was it recovered in a secure archaeological context, acquired from a dealer, or inherited from a collection whose findspot is unknown? Those histories change what we can claim about how the text was used.

Then ask which script and language it records, and how the date was established. Its linguistic version need not be as old as the physical copy. A seventh-century tablet can preserve words composed centuries earlier; the composition itself may contain still older themes. A date printed beside a literary work can mean the approximate date of a particular manuscript, the period of its composition, or the legendary era in which the story is set. Confusing those categories is one of the easiest ways to distort ancient literature.

The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, or CDLI, was built to address some of these access problems. Researchers including Robert K. Englund and collaborators connected museum catalogues, transliterations, images, and publication data. An online record can let a reader move from a printed citation to an actual artifact number and image. The system cannot repair all losses of archaeological provenience or solve every disputed reading. It can make the evidence inspectable and connect scattered collections more effectively.

There is an ethical implication here. A nineteenth-century scholar might have become famous for translating a tablet excavated by workers who were never named, stored in a museum far from the findspot, and described without a full archaeological record. Today's historians can acknowledge both the importance of the translation and the uneven distribution of recognition. Crediting George Smith is right. Crediting Hormuzd Rassam and the ancient scribes is also right. Naming researchers who correct earlier readings does not diminish their predecessors; it explains why knowledge improved.

## Scribal classrooms as archaeological evidence

It is tempting to imagine a Mesopotamian poem traveling through history as one famous book in a palace library. The surviving record is much messier. Some of our witnesses to Sumerian literary works come from ancient school settings. Apprentice scribes practiced signs, copied word lists, and wrote exercises that sometimes preserved parts of myths and poems. Their mistakes or partial passages can help modern researchers identify the educational use of a composition. A manuscript need not be the most beautiful copy ever made to be invaluable to a scholar.

Imagine a teacher assigning a passage about a goddess's descent. One pupil copies the opening carefully, then stops before the end. Another misunderstands a difficult sign. A third leaves an incomplete exercise. Centuries later, an archaeologist may find the broken tablets in a domestic or school-related context. The scholar cannot simply treat every difference as evidence that the ancient story originally contained three alternative endings. The copyists' educational setting helps explain why the differences exist. On the other hand, a consistent alternate line found in several independent witnesses may support a genuine variant. This is a problem of textual criticism informed by archaeology.

Oxford's Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature is explicit about the difficulty. A reconstructed composition may draw on a number of tablets, many damaged, and some written by trainees. Its composite edition represents editorial scholarship applied to those witnesses. The team associated with Jeremy Black did a substantial service by exposing texts, translations, and metadata to readers who could not consult each museum collection in person. The online presentation should not make the composite look like a single artifact; knowing how the text was assembled is part of reading it correctly.

There is also a difference between a text's *presence* at a site and its *origin*. If a poem about Uruk was copied at Nippur, the archaeological fact is that a copy existed in that scribal setting. The poem may have been composed elsewhere and centuries earlier. A place in a poem is not necessarily its place of composition, and a tablet's findspot is not necessarily where its author lived. We can map the diffusion and copying of stories when witnesses are available, but we cannot infer every step of their transmission from one broken exercise.

## The damaged line and the translator's voice

Now consider a translator facing a sentence with missing signs. If a duplicate passage survives intact elsewhere, the editor may restore the absent words with confidence. If only a parallel myth shares a similar phrase, the restoration may be weaker. If the line has no parallel at all, the best edition may print a gap. Readers who have not seen the original may imagine that a translated paragraph is entirely preserved; critical editions make these differences explicit with brackets, ellipses, editorial symbols, and discussions of alternative readings.

Translation itself introduces choices. Does the hero address his companion formally? Is a goddess described in terms of political sovereignty, sexuality, or ritual status? Ancient terms are embedded in institutions and literary conventions that English words may represent only approximately. Two translations that differ can still be serious scholarly attempts to render the same witnesses. Conversely, a translator can produce an elegant phrase that projects an interpretation not compelled by the cuneiform. For a lecture that wishes to follow the evidence, naming the translator and edition is especially useful at contested points.

The issue is complicated by rights and access. Modern translations are authored scholarly and literary works, even when the ancient text is thousands of years old. A lecturer can narrate an original paraphrase of a scene and cite the edition used without treating the translation as free raw material to reproduce at length. If a particular ambiguity is central to interpretation, a short, properly attributed phrase may be appropriate. Otherwise, explaining the interpretive choice in our own words often teaches more than reciting an isolated translated line.

This is why I would like every substantial claim about a recovered ancient poem to have a path back to a scholarly edition. For the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic, Andrew George's critical edition is one such path. For Sumerian compositions, the Oxford corpus and the underlying specialist publications help us see the fragmentary manuscript record. Earlier scholarship by Samuel Noah Kramer remains important to the history of the field, even where particular readings have been corrected. An intellectual history should be willing to credit a scholar for opening a problem and another for improving its solution.

## A practical demonstration of historical knowledge

Take the claim that the ancient Mesopotamians told a flood story with striking parallels to Genesis. We can trace it step by step. One surviving witness is the flood narrative on Tablet Eleven in the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh sequence. The relevant material was recovered among the Nineveh tablets excavated in the nineteenth century. George Smith recognized and publicly discussed it in 1872. The writing system had been deciphered through the cumulative work of Grotefend, Hincks, Rawlinson, Oppert, Norris, and others. Modern specialists have collated the surviving manuscripts and compared the narrative with separate texts such as Atrahasis. Literary historians debate questions of transmission and relationship among these works. That is a chain of evidence and intellectual labor, not one mysterious act of recovery.

We can be equally careful about authorship. The ancient figure Sin-leqi-unninni is traditionally associated with the Standard Babylonian Epic. The Sumerian poems cannot simply be credited to him. Ashurbanipal's royal library preserved crucial copies but did not create all their content. George Smith was a pioneering modern interpreter, not the author of the epic. Andrew George and the Oxford editors reconstructed and translated texts for modern audiences, but they did not excavate every tablet they edited. The contributions fit together because they solve different problems.

The most instructive result may be what cannot be reconstructed. Some lines remain missing. Some copies disagree. Some artifacts have poor findspot records. A polished modern translation may supply connective words to make a passage intelligible. When we hear the story told without interruption, we should enjoy it while retaining a mental map of these different sources. The gaps do not make the ancient work less worthy of reading. They remind us that literature can survive through many fragile acts of preservation.

Imagine holding the fragment again. We now have a better sense of what would be required to read it responsibly. We need the object's recorded history, the script and language, the specific text or version, comparisons with other fragments, a published edition, and a clear account of how the translator reached the proposed reading. We also need to remember the ancient author or scribe where an attribution survives, the excavation team where it can be identified, and the modern scholars whose arguments we are using. Behind every line we quote, there is an archaeologically recovered object and a history of people learning to read it.

Gilgamesh asks what survives a human life. The epic's modern recovery gives a surprisingly literal answer. Words can outlast their writers by thousands of years when people copy them, bury them, find them, study them, and make them available again. We know the king's story because a long chain of identifiable human actions carried the text forward, often imperfectly, from one world into another.
