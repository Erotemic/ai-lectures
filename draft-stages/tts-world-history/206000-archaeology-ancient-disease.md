# Ancient Disease: What a Skeleton Cannot Tell You

In a medieval cemetery in London, archaeologists uncover rows of graves filled during the catastrophe that swept across Europe in the middle of the fourteenth century. A chronicler might call the event a pestilence. Modern history books call it the Black Death. The buried individuals cannot tell us what they felt during their last days. Many of their bones bear no distinctive mark of disease. Yet scientists have recovered fragments of a bacterium's genetic material from some of their teeth, reconstructed much of its genome, and compared it with the genomes of related bacteria living today.

A very different excavation might find an ancient skeleton with damaged vertebrae. The pattern could be consistent with tuberculosis. But more than one disease can damage bones, and much of the evidence needed to identify the infection has disappeared. Elsewhere, an ancient latrine preserves the eggs of parasitic worms; those eggs tell a story about disease in people who may have left no recognizable skeletal lesions at all.

These three encounters with the past show why archaeological medicine is an exercise in combining incomplete records. Bones record some of the body's responses to disease. Teeth may protect traces of pathogens. Sediments can preserve eggs, molecules, and waste. Ancient writers describe symptoms, sometimes through theories of illness very different from ours. Each record has its own clock, its own biases, and its own ways of being misread.

We will follow the evidence from the cemetery to the microscope and the sequencing laboratory. Along the way, we will meet researchers who made particular findings possible, and we will ask precisely which claims the measurements support: that a pathogen existed, that a person carried it, that it caused illness, or that it contributed to a historical epidemic. Those claims require progressively more evidence.

## The marks that disease leaves, and the marks it does not

Imagine two people who both die from an acute infection. One was healthy until a short illness; the other lived for years with recurring problems. If their bodies were buried in similar conditions, the bones of both might look ordinary. Many infections primarily affect soft tissues and kill too quickly to produce recognizable changes in the skeleton. The absence of a bony lesion therefore gives little assurance that a person was healthy.

Chronic infections and repeated physiological stresses sometimes leave traces. Bone is living tissue. It remodels in response to injury, inflammation, strain, and changes in its blood supply. A long-standing infection may cause bone destruction or new bone formation. Some infections can damage joints or vertebrae. But that process is shared by several diseases and sometimes by noninfectious disorders. A swollen bone is a finding; it is not automatically a diagnosis.

Paleopathologists build a differential diagnosis: a reasoned comparison of several possible causes. The term comes from clinical medicine. A physician examining a living patient can ask about pain, order blood tests, and observe changes over time. The archaeologist has a partial skeleton, knowledge of its burial context, and perhaps microscopic or molecular samples. A defensible diagnosis makes the limits of those observations explicit.

Suppose adjacent vertebrae have collapsed and fused. Tuberculosis is one possibility, especially when destruction is concentrated in characteristic regions. Other bacterial infections, trauma, and degenerative conditions can produce overlapping appearances. The distribution of lesions in the entire skeleton matters. So do radiographs or scans, surrounding sediments, and any molecular evidence. The same lesion in a single isolated bone may be far harder to interpret.

The history of paleopathology involves generations of researchers who learned to describe such patterns without assuming that every familiar-looking lesion belongs to a familiar disease. Charlotte Roberts and Jane Buikstra's work on the bioarchaeology of tuberculosis is a significant example of synthesizing skeletal cases with microbiology and historical context. Their approach helps distinguish a diagnosis based on characteristic damage from a diagnosis supported by the bacterium itself.

## Why a cemetery is a sample, not a census

Consider what selection occurs before an archaeologist even opens a trench. An infected person must die, be buried where remains will survive, and eventually be located and excavated. The condition must leave a recoverable trace. The burial must be identified and dated sufficiently well to enter a study. Every stage filters the population.

This creates a famous difficulty in the archaeology of health. People with slowly developing skeletal lesions may have survived long enough for bones to respond to disease. People who died quickly from a severe infection may have left no lesions. Finding a high proportion of visibly affected bones in one cemetery does not necessarily mean its inhabitants were sicker than those in another. The difference could reflect who survived, who was buried there, and which traces preserved.

A burial ground associated with a hospital may have a different population from an ordinary village cemetery. A plague pit concentrates mortality during a particular episode. A privileged burial with elaborate preservation practices selects for wealth or status. A medieval mass grave can therefore tell us a great deal about an event while representing a restricted slice of society.

The same problem appears in counting injuries. Twenty percent of excavated individuals with a particular lesion does not automatically mean twenty percent of the living population had the disease. Some people could be affected without a visible trace; others might never reach the cemetery. Responsible studies give the number of individuals examined, how well their bones were preserved, and how the diagnosis was reached.

Age is another complication. Disease can change the chance of reaching old age, while bone conditions become more common with age. A population of many young adults will display a different lesion profile from a population with many elderly burials, even before differences in infection are considered. Archaeologists therefore compare like with like when possible and qualify estimates when preservation or sampling prevents that comparison.

## A pathogen preserved inside a tooth

Now return to the Black Death cemetery. In the fourteenth century, the disease moved through communities with catastrophic speed. Historians have debated its epidemiology, transmission, and mortality using chroniclers, tax records, burial evidence, and later medical knowledge. For a long time, identifying the precise pathogen behind medieval plague remained a question for which the written sources could not supply a laboratory answer.

In 2011, a team led by Kirsten Bos and Verena Schuenemann, working with Johannes Krause, Hendrik Poinar, and other collaborators, reported a draft genome of the bacterium Yersinia pestis recovered from Black Death victims at London's East Smithfield burial ground. That cemetery is dated to the epidemic years of 1348 through 1350. The researchers did more than discover a short sequence that vaguely resembled a modern bacterium. They reconstructed a substantial ancient genome and examined its relationships to later strains.

The crucial word is ancient. A modern contaminant can appear in an archaeological sample, and bacteria living in the soil may be plentiful. To identify authentic pathogen DNA, researchers inspect the lengths and damage patterns of recovered fragments, their distribution across the reference genome, and the results of negative laboratory controls. They look for consistent evidence across a substantial part of the organism's sequence.

A tooth is useful partly because its interior can protect material from the environment. If a pathogen circulated in a person's blood near death, its genetic material may survive within tissues that formed part of the tooth. Such a finding can establish the presence of infection in that individual. It still does not recover the person's final conversation, particular symptoms, or complete medical history.

An earlier 2011 project by Verena Schuenemann and collaborators screened many East Smithfield remains and enriched the recovered DNA for a plague-associated plasmid. Targeted enrichment means using molecular methods to recover fragments related to a selected organism more efficiently than if every fragment in the sample were sequenced indiscriminately. This is especially useful when ancient pathogen DNA is scarce among overwhelming amounts of microbial and human DNA.

The genome results gave a biological counterpart to the historical identification of the Black Death. They also allowed an evolutionary comparison. A bacterial family tree is reconstructed from genetic differences among sampled strains; researchers estimate how different branches relate and when divergence may have occurred. Such a tree is a model inferred from data, and its historical interpretation depends on geography, dating, and the number of lineages sampled.

## Finding a possible beginning in central Eurasia

A later study shows the value of combining sources that answer different questions. In 2022, Maria Spyrou and colleagues examined burials at Kara-Djigach and Burana, near Issyk-Kul in present-day Kyrgyzstan. Tombstone inscriptions dated to 1338 and 1339 mentioned pestilence. Ancient DNA recovered from some of the individuals included Yersinia pestis. The team reconstructed genomes positioned near a major branching point associated with the second plague pandemic.

The inscriptions alone establish that a community recorded an epidemic. The human burials give the physical context. The bacterial genomes show that plague infection was present among sampled individuals and offer information about the lineage's evolutionary position. Comparative data from modern reservoirs around the extended Tian Shan region informed the team's proposal of a source region for the pandemic's emergence.

This is a considerably more specific claim than saying that plague came from somewhere in Asia. It remains a historical reconstruction assembled from samples that survive, with a geographical range and uncertainties determined by what has been studied. If researchers later recover closely related, securely dated older genomes elsewhere, they may refine the proposed emergence geography or the timing of the branching event.

Notice the separation between finding the organism and explaining how it traveled. A grave tells us where an infected person was buried. To reconstruct spread we also need patterns of human movement, trade connections, animal reservoirs, ecological circumstances, and the timing of disease at multiple sites. Medieval documents and archaeological geography become partners to molecular biology.

It is tempting to draw a single arrow from a supposed point of origin to the entire world. Epidemics involve multiple transmission events and complex local circumstances. The genome can sharply constrain parts of the story while leaving routes, social responses, and differences in suffering unresolved.

## Diseases before written epidemics

Ancient DNA has also pushed evidence of some pathogens thousands of years before the earliest recognizable written descriptions. Research teams studying human teeth from prehistoric Eurasia have recovered early lineages of Yersinia pestis. In 2015, Simon Rasmussen and colleagues reported genomes from individuals dating roughly 2,800 to 5,000 years ago. Subsequent studies added further prehistoric sequences and refined the evolutionary picture.

That finding raises a subtle question. If a bacterial lineage existed five thousand years ago, did it cause an epidemic like the Black Death? Genetic ancestry alone cannot answer that. Older strains may have differed in genes involved in transmission and virulence. The ecological setting, population density, and available animal hosts also differed. A molecular finding can date the presence of a lineage without measuring how many people it killed.

The same caution applies when a pathogen's DNA is recovered from one burial. A person may carry an infection without dying from it. A skeleton might indicate other causes of death, or reveal nothing decisive. To infer an outbreak, researchers look for clusters of similarly dated cases, historical evidence, and broader mortality patterns. Ancient disease history contains evidence at several scales: a molecule, a person, a cemetery, a settlement, and a region. Moving between scales takes additional argument.

Even confident identifications have limits. Ancient pathogens sometimes exchange genetic material with related organisms. Short, damaged fragments may align ambiguously. Reference databases change. A candidate sequence that looked specific when few comparative genomes were available may later prove less diagnostic. Publishing raw sequence data and analytical procedures makes reassessment possible.

## Disease in a place with no skeletons

A latrine can preserve evidence of intestinal parasites even when its users left no named burial. Parasitic worms often produce eggs that pass from the body in feces. Under favorable conditions, eggs survive in sediments for centuries. If archaeologists recognize those eggs under a microscope, they can identify infection-related organisms in a particular deposit.

One widely studied example is the Roman world. Archaeologist and biological anthropologist Piers Mitchell reviewed evidence for parasite species across Roman settlements and burials. Large cities had aqueducts, drains, and public latrines. Yet researchers continued to find evidence for roundworm and whipworm, which are associated with fecal contamination. The remains indicate that building impressive water infrastructure did not guarantee interruption of every parasite life cycle.

Consider a concrete latrine investigation. At the Roman baths in Sagalassos, in what is now Turkey, a team including F. Williams, T. Arnold-Foster, Hyejin Yeh, Marissa Ledger, and collaborators studied sediment samples. Chemical fecal biomarkers helped establish that the deposits contained human waste. Microscopy detected roundworm eggs in all five samples examined. An immunological assay also detected evidence interpreted as Giardia in one sample. Different methods answered different parts of the question: did the material contain human fecal residues, and which infectious organisms appeared within it?

We should not turn five sediment samples into a census of all residents of Sagalassos. Latrine deposits may accumulate waste from many users over time. Eggs can be transported, mixed, or unevenly preserved. The result establishes a local record of parasites and informs the ecology of disease; it cannot reveal every user's age or symptoms.

Another example comes from the early farming settlement of Çatalhöyük in central Anatolia. Research combining lipid biomarkers with microscopic examination identified human coprolites—preserved feces—and whipworm eggs in some of them. The investigators, including Piers Mitchell and colleagues from archaeology and chemistry, used those complementary methods to connect waste to human activity and investigate intestinal infection. That is a different kind of historical evidence from damage to bones.

## The hardest part: deciding what the signal means

Let's combine the three methods in a hypothetical excavation. A settlement layer contains animal and human refuse. A burial in that layer has destructive vertebral lesions. Another individual yields a few DNA fragments matching a bacterial species. Nearby, a drain contains parasite eggs. A careless account could announce that the inhabitants suffered an epidemic caused by a particular bacterium and aggravated by poor sanitation.

That conclusion goes far beyond the evidence. The vertebral lesions need a differential diagnosis. The sequences require authentication and enough coverage to identify the organism. The drain could have been used over decades. The samples might not date to the same short episode. Several independent illnesses could have affected the community without any single epidemic connecting them.

Archaeologists therefore record the precise findspot and stratigraphic relationships, date the samples, and ask what each test actually measures. A radiocarbon date on human bone applies to the sampled material, with adjustments and uncertainties. A coin within a layer may supply a terminus after which the deposit was formed, but coins can circulate for generations. A written chronicle can describe a catastrophe without using modern diagnostic categories. These sources become powerful when they constrain one another.

The interpretation of ancient diseases can also change with improvements in laboratory methods. An older report identifying an organism from a short DNA fragment may be reassessed using more genomic positions and better contamination controls. Work on parasites now combines microscopy, protein assays, and ancient sedimentary DNA to detect different organisms. Each added method changes the information available and introduces new validation requirements.

A useful standard is to state conclusions in degrees. The observed lesion is compatible with several diseases. The molecular fragments support a particular pathogen, with specified authentication criteria. Multiple affected individuals occur in the same dated burial episode. Historical evidence independently describes heightened mortality. Together, these may justify a strong historical explanation. No single dramatic result substitutes for the chain.

## Who receives credit for a discovery?

The answer is rarely just one person. A cemetery may be discovered and excavated by a local archaeological service. Specialists document the skeletons and burial sequence. Historians date the event through archives and inscriptions. Laboratory teams extract molecules and sequence them. Bioinformaticians reconstruct genomes and compare them. Other researchers challenge assumptions, replicate parts of the work, or add new cases.

Naming Bos, Schuenemann, Krause, and Poinar in the Black Death work gives a listener access to a specific publication. It does not erase the excavators, site specialists, or earlier scholars who established East Smithfield's context. Naming Spyrou's team in the Kyrgyzstan study places the molecular finding within an international research collaboration that also depended on previously recorded epitaphs and collections. Likewise, Mitchell's syntheses depend on parasite studies from many excavation teams.

The history of medicine also belongs to the people who suffered the illnesses, cared for the sick, maintained water systems, and left written or oral accounts. Archaeology often preserves far fewer names from their side of the story. The fact that an anonymous person has become a scientific sample is a reason to treat their remains with respect, not to reduce them to data points in a chart.

Destructive sampling is especially consequential. A tooth removed for sequencing cannot simply be put back into its original condition. Permissions, collection policies, community consultation, and the retention of samples and records are part of responsible archaeological research. The scientific value of the result must be weighed against an irreversible intervention in human remains.

## Returning to the cemetery

We began with a medieval grave whose bones could not disclose the cause of death. By combining a dated burial ground with authenticated bacterial DNA, researchers established that Yersinia pestis infected individuals buried during the Black Death. Comparisons with other ancient genomes and historical inscriptions then offered more specific hypotheses about the epidemic's wider history.

The same inquiry also taught us why skeletal lesions may identify a possibility without confirming its cause, why parasite eggs can preserve evidence missing from bones, and why an ancient genome cannot reconstruct an entire epidemic by itself. We learned that selection affects every cemetery sample and that the absence of evidence in one tissue says little about disease in another.

Ancient disease becomes knowable through the agreements—and sometimes the disagreements—between bones, sediments, molecules, dates, and written testimony. The most informative answer is one that allows us to trace each historical claim back to a particular kind of observation, and to the researchers who showed how that observation could be read.


## A fever written down and a fever preserved

Historical descriptions can enrich the material record, but translating an ancient symptom into a modern diagnosis is difficult. A chronicler may describe fever, swelling, rash, and widespread death. Those observations are valuable. They do not always distinguish among infections that modern medicine recognizes as separate diseases. Texts may survive because they were copied by a literate institution, while the sickest members of society left no account in their own words.

The problem becomes even more acute when a physician's description belongs to a medical theory that interpreted illness through bodily humors or divine action. A careful historian can translate the described symptoms while preserving the original author's explanation. A direct quotation from a medieval writer establishes what that writer reported; whether the reported event was caused by a particular bacterium is a different question.

This is why the study of past disease increasingly benefits from collaborations among historians of medicine, archaeologists, paleopathologists, and microbiologists. A molecular result can make one ancient diagnosis more credible, but it does not turn every old account of 'pestilence' into plague. Distinct outbreaks may involve different causes; the same word may have covered many illnesses.

A 2024 study of early medieval remains at La Olmeda in northern Iberia provides another illustration of interdisciplinary diagnosis. Researchers including L. Coppola Bove and C. L. Kirkpatrick examined skeletal lesions among individuals from the cemetery and screened selected remains for pathogen DNA. Genetic fragments assigned to malaria parasites were recovered in several sampled children. The team considered malaria alongside other possible infections when interpreting the skeletal changes. The findings demonstrate the power of combining evidence while also showing why a sequence assignment should be judged alongside symptoms that can actually be preserved and the possible presence of more than one disease.

Historical records may add context to those findings, but they rarely allow us to identify named sufferers. The bones, texts, and molecules should inform one another without pretending they answer identical questions.

## Why a negative test may change little

Suppose a research team samples thirty graves and detects a pathogen in three. It is tempting to announce that ten percent of the cemetery was infected. The calculation is straightforward; its interpretation is not. Ancient DNA preservation varies by burial conditions, tissue, climate, and laboratory procedure. Infected individuals may have had too little pathogen DNA to be detected. Other graves may never have been sampled. A positive result can be quite specific; a negative result may indicate only that this test did not recover the target sequence.

To estimate infection prevalence, researchers need a sample that represents the population of interest and a validated understanding of detection probability. Ancient cemeteries almost never meet every condition of a modern epidemiological survey. The most informative report may be that a pathogen was present in a dated group, with the proportion of positive tests clearly stated and no population-wide rate inferred.

This distinction also changes how scientists discuss the apparent absence of a disease before a particular date. The earliest recovered genome is the earliest identified evidence currently in hand. It is not necessarily the organism's first appearance in the world. More ancient remains and better recovery methods can push that boundary backward, as has happened repeatedly in pathogen research.
