# Saving a Shipwreck: What Happens After the Excavation

In October 1982, the surviving hull of a Tudor warship rose slowly out of the Solent near Portsmouth, England. The ship was the Mary Rose, which had sunk in 1545 during the reign of Henry the Eighth. For centuries, much of its surviving wood had rested in waterlogged sediment. Underwater, the remains had been protected from some forces that destroy wood on land. Above water, a new danger began immediately. If the timbers were simply left to dry in the open air, they could shrink, crack, and lose the shapes that made them valuable archaeological evidence.

The recovery of a shipwreck is often imagined as the triumphal end of a search. For conservation scientists, it is frequently the beginning of a much longer problem. Bringing a fragile object from one chemical environment to another may unleash the processes that destroy it. A piece of wood can be strong enough to retain its shape underwater yet too weak to survive drying. A metal fitting can corrode rapidly when exposed to oxygen and changing humidity. Salt held inside ceramics may crystallize and break their surfaces. The scientist's task is to understand what the object has become during burial and how to keep it from falling apart after excavation.

We will follow the Mary Rose and the Swedish warship Vasa, excavated and raised in different decades, to explore how this work is done. These are early modern warships rather than ancient vessels, but their preservation problems apply directly to much older maritime archaeology. We will examine waterlogged wood, polyethylene glycol treatment, corrosion, sulfur chemistry, three-dimensional recording, and the tradeoffs involved in removing artifacts from their underwater contexts. We'll also follow the researchers who documented these problems: Alexander McKee, Margaret Rule and the Mary Rose teams; the Vasa conservators; and analytical chemists including Magnus Sandstrom and Farideh Jalilehvand.

## A ship preserved by the conditions that trapped it

When a wooden ship sinks, it does not immediately become a fossil. Wood is a biological material. Its cell walls contain cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, and microorganisms can attack parts of that structure. Marine borers may destroy exposed timber quickly. Other parts of the vessel may become buried in sediment where oxygen is scarce and particular forms of decay proceed more slowly. The history of a wreck often includes both processes at once: one side erodes away, while another is buried and survives.

The Mary Rose had served for decades before sinking in 1545. It lay in the Solent between the Isle of Wight and the English mainland. Alexander McKee helped organize the modern search, and Margaret Rule became central to its archaeological investigation. The Mary Rose Trust credits many divers, surveyors, conservation specialists, engineers, and volunteers with the long campaign. The remains were located in the 1970s, excavated, recorded, and eventually raised on a specially constructed support system in 1982. Recovering the hull meant planning for its structure, its weight, the forces exerted by the surrounding sediment, and the hazards of the sea.

An excavation underwater faces a choice that an excavation on land also faces, but with different consequences. If you lift a loose timber before recording exactly where it belongs, you may never know whether it was part of the ship's original frame, a repair, or a later intrusion. If you leave a delicate object in exposed water while documenting it, currents or organisms may damage it. The Mary Rose project developed recording and lifting practices that sought to preserve the relationships among objects, timbers, and deposits while managing the physical risks of excavation.

Many artifacts recovered from the vessel were associated with particular places inside it. A chest found in one location, with its contents recorded carefully, is more useful for reconstructing life on board than an identical chest whose position was lost. A shipwreck can preserve a sudden assemblage of equipment, possessions, and supplies. But it is not a perfect photograph of the moment of sinking. Some materials floated away, others were washed out, and still others were moved in centuries of seabed activity. The careful record is what allows a later historian to distinguish a plausible scene from a fictional one.

The preservation of the hull was itself selective. The surviving portion of the Mary Rose was protected by sediments that reduced exposure to marine wood borers, while other sections disappeared. As a result, reconstructing the full vessel requires combining recovered timbers, structural evidence, contemporary shipbuilding knowledge, and cautious geometric inference. A museum visitor sees a physical ship, but also a product of decisions about restoration, support, and display. The gaps in the hull are part of the evidence rather than merely damage to be concealed.

## Why wet wood cannot simply be dried

Imagine a sponge whose walls have gradually weakened while every pore remains full of water. If it dries slowly enough, ordinary kitchen sponge material usually retains a workable shape. Waterlogged archaeological wood is different. Decay can remove or weaken structural parts of the cell walls while leaving water occupying the spaces. The wood may retain its external form in burial because the environment supports it mechanically. When water leaves, capillary forces and weakened cellular structure can cause collapse, distortion, or cracking.

Conservators use a range of methods depending on wood species, condition, size, and the intended outcome. One well-established treatment uses polyethylene glycol, usually abbreviated P-E-G. This is a water-soluble polymer available in different molecular weights. Solutions can penetrate porous, waterlogged wood and help support the weakened structure as the water is removed. The polymer does not transform old wood back into living timber. It adds material that helps retain dimensions and reduces the risks of drying.

For the Mary Rose, the conservation team had to treat an immense surviving hull, too large for easy immersion in a tank. After recovery the timbers were kept wet. The Trust describes a period of controlled freshwater spraying that helped prevent uncontrolled drying and remove some contaminants. Starting in 1994, the team began spraying the hull with polyethylene glycol solutions. Different polymer sizes were used during the long treatment in stages, chosen to penetrate and stabilize different parts of the deteriorated wood.

The spraying did not end in a few days or a single season. It continued until 2013, after which carefully controlled drying began. The museum's conservation history records that the major drying phase concluded in 2016, with climate control and monitoring continuing afterward. The result was possible because scientists and engineers designed pumps, pipes, humidity control, sensors, structural supports, and a building that could function as part of the treatment. Conserving a large ship is partly chemical science and partly a problem of long-term infrastructure.

Why not simply apply more polymer until the wood is safe? A treatment must actually penetrate the areas that need it. Different parts of a hull have different densities and degrees of decay. The process must not introduce damaging stresses or produce unintended chemical effects. Added materials may change an object's mass, appearance, and future response to humidity. Conservators measure how far treatment has reached, how much water remains, and whether components are shrinking or shifting. They compare cores and controlled tests rather than assuming that enough calendar time has passed.

The museum now monitors the Mary Rose with instruments that detect small movements. One system measures distances to points on the hull repeatedly; other markers track cracks. A slowly changing joint can become important years before a visitor notices a problem. Conservation is therefore not just an action performed between excavation and exhibition. It is ongoing maintenance informed by continuing observation. Even a successfully treated vessel remains an artifact whose condition depends on its environment.

## The Vasa and an unexpected chemical threat

Another enormous shipwreck reveals a different danger. The Vasa was a Swedish warship that sank in Stockholm harbor in 1628. It was raised in 1961 after more than three centuries underwater. Like the Mary Rose, it underwent extensive stabilization and became a major museum object. Yet decades after recovery, conservators noticed acidic salts appearing on some of the timbers. The question was troubling: how could a supposedly conserved ship develop a chemical threat from within?

In 2002, Magnus Sandstrom, Farideh Jalilehvand, Ingmar Persson, Ulrik Gelius, Patrick Frank, Ingrid Hall-Roth, and collaborators published an influential investigation in the journal Nature. Using techniques including X-ray absorption spectroscopy, they studied sulfur compounds present in the wood. Their results showed that substantial amounts of sulfur had accumulated during burial, including forms capable of oxidizing into sulfuric acid after exposure to oxygen. Iron compounds from corroded metal fittings could promote parts of this chemistry.

The proposed process can be described in several stages. In low-oxygen marine sediments, sulfur-containing substances may enter wood through microbial and chemical processes. Some sulfur remains in reduced chemical forms. When the environment changes after salvage, oxygen can support conversion toward more oxidized forms. If sulfuric acid accumulates, the resulting acidity can attack the wood's cellulose and related components. A ship may thus bring chemical material from the seabed into the museum that becomes dangerous only under new conditions.

To demonstrate this, researchers needed more than a patch of white powder on a surface. The powder might be a symptom, but it does not by itself tell us which substances are present deep inside the oak. X-ray spectroscopy can probe how sulfur atoms are chemically bound. Other tests measure total sulfur, iron distributions, pH, and the condition of wood polymers. Comparing surface and interior samples reveals whether a treatment that cleans the outside could plausibly address the underlying problem.

The research by Sandstrom and colleagues also gives a lesson in scientific chronology. The sulfur accumulated during the centuries underwater; the acidic deterioration became conspicuous after the ship entered a different environment. If researchers simply blamed the most recent museum treatment, they might overlook the long chemical history of the wood. On the other hand, the presence of sulfur is not sufficient to predict the same rate of deterioration everywhere. Humidity, temperature, oxygen access, iron chemistry, and wood condition all matter.

Follow-up work on the Mary Rose has used related analytical techniques. One investigation published in 2005 examined sulfur in its marine archaeological oak. The team used synchrotron-based X-ray microspectroscopy to locate different sulfur compounds and iron-bearing particles. It found that sulfur chemistry in the wood is spatially complex. A treatment designed to remove one form of contamination must not be assumed to remove every chemically relevant substance. Conservators have to decide which reactions threaten the object, which can be slowed, and which interventions may create additional risks.

## The long chemical life of a recovered object

Wood may be the most dramatic conservation challenge, but marine finds are diverse. Think of an iron nail inside a ship's wooden hull. On the seabed, corrosion can convert much of the original iron into other substances, often preserving an outer form while weakening what is inside. The metal may also retain chloride ions from the saltwater environment. If it is recovered and placed in damp air, chemical reactions can continue. Expanding corrosion products can fracture the object or detach fragile surfaces. A nail that looked stable inside a waterlogged beam may deteriorate once it has been separated from its protective context.

Conservators may use controlled washing, appropriate chemical treatment, or carefully regulated storage to reduce those risks. Yet no one method suits every metal object. An iron tool, bronze fitting, lead weight, and silver coin differ in composition and corrosion chemistry. Even pieces made from the same alloy may have experienced different burial conditions. The conservator first documents the object's condition, construction, and surface deposits. Radiography can reveal a surviving metal core or hidden features that would be damaged by aggressive cleaning. Treatment decisions then follow the diagnosis.

Consider a ceramic jar from a cargo of wine, oil, or grain. Fired pottery may be mechanically stronger than waterlogged wood, but pores and cracks can hold dissolved salts. When the jar dries, salts may crystallize in those spaces. Growing crystals exert pressure that can cause flaking or spalling. Slow desalination, repeated measurements of water conductivity, and careful drying may be needed. If residues remain inside the vessel, sampling for organic chemistry should be considered before a conservation treatment introduces compounds that later analyses might mistake for ancient contents.

Now imagine a rope fragment found beside that jar. Its fibers might retain information about plant species and manufacturing methods. They might also be too fragile to lift individually. A field conservator may consolidate the surrounding sediment or raise the fragment with support, allowing laboratory excavation under controlled conditions. A sample of the fibers might be used for microscopy or other analysis. But every sample is a small, destructive decision. The team balances what can be learned now against the possibility that a better method will be developed later.

This is why conservation is closely related to experimental science. Researchers need reference materials and controlled comparisons. How much did an untreated piece shrink? Did a proposed treatment reduce collapse? Did it change the sample's mass, appearance, or future chemical stability? How deeply did the consolidant penetrate? These questions can be investigated on small test samples before applying a treatment to an irreplaceable hull. Records of the test are part of the evidence of why one method was chosen over another.

At the Mary Rose, conservators tested different polyethylene glycol procedures before treating the hull. Long-term application was chosen in light of experiments, assessments of deterioration, and the engineering limits of treating such a large object. Subsequent monitoring is effectively another experiment, though one in which the object must be protected rather than exposed to deliberately harmful conditions. Measuring shifts in timber geometry or chemistry helps determine whether the chosen environment remains suitable.

An artifact may also carry marks that are evidence, not dirt. Tool scratches, residues of repair, deposits from its use, and traces of pigment may be precisely the features historians want to examine. Cleaning a surface until it looks attractive can destroy those clues. Modern conservation therefore values documentation before treatment, minimal necessary intervention, and clear records of every material introduced. The scientific question is not merely, 'Can we make it look new?' It is, 'Can we keep the historically informative object stable while changing it as little as the situation permits?' 

## From an image of a ship to a measured model

The chemistry is only one part of conservation. The shape and location of every recoverable component also matter. Modern underwater archaeological teams increasingly use photogrammetry, in which many overlapping photographs are combined into a three-dimensional surface model. Divers may photograph a wreck in a planned pattern, ensuring that distinctive features appear in multiple views. Researchers establish scale through measured control points and compare the resulting model to direct observations.

Photogrammetry can capture complicated surfaces faster than some traditional techniques and can preserve a record before a wreck is disturbed by excavation. But the process does not eliminate field interpretation. The camera sees the surface available at that moment. Sediment may hide timbers beneath it. A dark hole may be deep or simply poorly illuminated. If images lack sufficient overlap, software may invent a plausible but incorrect geometry. Control measurements and independent checking remain essential.

A three-dimensional model can be used to calculate dimensions, study how pieces fit, compare different seasons, or create a virtual representation for public access. It can also help conservation scientists monitor physical movement after recovery. Yet a digital scan cannot tell a future researcher what was inside a layer of sediment that has already been removed. Images, context sheets, laboratory samples, and excavation diaries preserve different kinds of knowledge.

The Mary Rose's excavation and recovery involved hundreds of people across professions. There is a danger in narrating the entire project through one name, however prominent. McKee's search, Rule's archaeological leadership, the diving teams' recording, the engineers' lifting apparatus, and the conservators' decades of chemical treatment were different achievements. The ship's survival in a museum is a collective technical accomplishment, built from decisions whose consequences continue to be evaluated.

## A careful chain of custody

Consider one piece of timber removed from a shipwreck during excavation. Before it is touched, the divers photograph its location, record its relationship to neighboring pieces, and assign a durable identifier. While lifting it, the team supports the object to avoid bending or fracture. The timber enters wet storage, where its condition and temperature can be controlled. A conservator documents dimensions, surface features, damage, and any earlier repairs. Samples, if taken, receive their own labels that connect them to the parent object and the exact sampled location.

This chain sounds bureaucratic, but every step protects a different historical question. The original position may help reconstruct the ship's construction. The direction of a tool mark may reveal how the timber was shaped. A sample from near an iron fitting may show local corrosion chemistry, while a sample from a different area provides a comparison. Without that spatial documentation, two laboratory results could be reported as properties of 'the wood' even when they represent different chemical environments.

Records also help when treatment histories become complicated. A curator should be able to find out whether a piece was washed, impregnated with polymer, freeze-dried, glued, or coated with another substance. Each intervention may change later measurements. A researcher studying residues must know whether a modern consolidant contains the same kinds of organic molecules as the ancient material being sought. A future conservator must know whether an old adhesive is compatible with a proposed repair. Identification numbers and treatment logs keep those questions answerable.

This can produce a surprisingly large archive for a single wreck: plans, photographs, diver notes, laboratory results, conservation records, and condition monitoring over years or decades. These records should themselves be preserved and made usable. A photograph with no scale, a sample named only 'wood A,' or a spreadsheet whose column meanings are forgotten may be difficult to interpret later. Archaeological preservation includes the preservation of the documentation by which a scientific claim can be checked.

There is a parallel with the original ship's voyage. The crew depended on chains of practical knowledge: where the vessel was repaired, what it carried, which components could be trusted, and which needed attention. The modern conservation team develops a new chain of knowledge about the same physical structure. It is possible to know much more about the chemistry of the recovered wood than about the names of the sailors who worked on it. That is a difference in surviving evidence, and careful record keeping is how we avoid losing still more.

## What should be raised at all?

Because recovery creates conservation problems, archaeologists must sometimes ask whether raising an object is the right choice. An undisturbed underwater site may be threatened by currents, fishing, looting, or construction. It may also be comparatively stable if left protected and monitored. Raising an object makes it accessible for detailed study, but it introduces cost and risks that can continue for decades. The value of information that can be obtained has to be weighed against the loss of context and the responsibility to care for the object afterward.

The tradeoffs differ by material. A ceramic bowl can hold salt within its pores and require desalination. Iron objects can suffer chloride-driven corrosion after recovery, requiring specialized stabilization and storage. Textiles and rope may be too fragile to handle without support. Organic residues can provide evidence about what a container held, but contamination from conservation chemicals may complicate later chemical analysis. Every intervention should be documented so later scientists know what substances are ancient and what was introduced in the laboratory.

This last point is easy to underestimate. Imagine a researcher decades from now trying to identify traces of food or cargo in a wooden vessel. If the object was treated with a resin whose formula is no longer documented, the analysis may be ambiguous. If a sample was taken before treatment and archived, the future researcher has a better comparison. Good conservation preserves the physical artifact and also preserves the ability to ask questions of it.

Conservation methods change. A treatment considered best practice in one decade may later turn out to create problems, or new scientific techniques may reveal risks that were previously undetectable. That is a reason to document decisions, choose compatible and reversible treatments where possible, preserve samples, and avoid interventions that foreclose future analysis. Not every treatment can be fully reversed; acknowledging that limitation is part of responsible planning.

The work also has an ethical dimension. Commercial salvage can remove valuable objects rapidly, while leaving inadequate records of their original placement. A scientifically excavated wreck may also become a public attraction, raising questions about ownership, display, colonial collecting histories, and whose maritime past is represented. No chemistry test can settle those questions alone. But the scientific record makes it possible to discuss what was recovered, what was lost, and what care is still required without relying on a romantic image of treasure hauled from the seabed.

## The evidence after recovery

A wreck is often called a time capsule. The image is powerful but incomplete. Ships are working objects. They change over decades of repair, and the sinking itself rearranges their contents. Afterward, the environment continues to select what survives. Then excavators, conservators, and curators make choices that determine what future researchers can study. The history of the evidence continues long after the historical ship has disappeared.

The Mary Rose shows the enormous effort needed to preserve waterlogged timber without destroying its shape. The Vasa shows that burial chemistry can remain active after recovery and that sophisticated materials analysis can expose threats invisible to the naked eye. Photogrammetry and careful recording show how the location and geometry of objects can survive the removal of sediment, though never as a complete substitute for the original site. The decision to conserve a ship is a commitment to future generations of research as much as to a museum display.

When we ask how we know the past from a shipwreck, the answer includes the sailors who used the vessel, the environmental processes that preserved portions of it, the excavators who documented it, and the chemists and engineers who keep it intact. Their contributions have different roles, and they deserve different kinds of credit. The object is historical evidence because of what it survived, what researchers recorded, and what careful stewardship may still allow us to learn.

### Editorial cautions

- Chemical processes differ between wrecks; the Vasa's documented acidification does not prove identical rates or remedies for other ships.
- Environmental stabilization after 2016 is ongoing; treatment completion should not be described as an end of conservation responsibilities.
- PEG protects waterlogged timber against collapse but does not restore all original cellulose or guarantee immunity from later oxidation.
