# Searching for Troy: What Excavation Can Tell Us About Homer

The city is burning in a poem. Hector is dead, Achilles has accepted the pleading of Priam, and a generation of warriors has fought under Troy's walls. We know the Iliad as a story of grief, rage, honor, and the terrible costs of war. But what sort of historical evidence would establish that its city existed? How could an archaeologist recognize the world behind a poem composed long after the events it claims to describe? And if an excavation discovers a burned city, does that prove that Achilles stood at its gates?

These questions have a history of their own. In northwestern Turkey, near the Dardanelles, a mound called Hisarlik contains the remains of many settlements built one on top of another. The site is widely identified with ancient Troy, or Ilion. That conclusion does not require us to believe that every event in Homer's Iliad happened as narrated. We can recover walls, pottery, trade contacts, human remains, and episodes of destruction. We cannot excavate the motivation of a character or a conversation in a poem. Archaeology and literature answer different questions, and the most interesting discoveries happen when we keep both kinds of evidence in view.

This lecture follows the researchers who investigated the site, including several whose credit was obscured by a familiar heroic discovery story. It also follows the evidence: successive settlement layers, competing destruction dates, Hittite documents, and the modern controversy over how important Troy was in the Late Bronze Age. By the end, we should understand both why the archaeological Troy matters to readers of Homer and why its ruins do not amount to a literal record of the Trojan War.

## Before the famous excavator

A popular account begins with Heinrich Schliemann, a wealthy nineteenth-century businessman who became determined to locate the city described in Greek epic. But the story starts earlier. Frank Calvert, who lived in the region and owned part of Hisarlik, had investigated the mound and argued that it was the location of Troy before Schliemann began his major excavation. Calvert undertook trial excavations in the 1860s and shared his hypothesis with Schliemann in 1868. Schliemann's later fame overshadowed Calvert's contribution.

Historian of archaeology Susan Heuck Allen documented this relationship through correspondence and other archival sources in *Finding the Walls of Troy*, published in 1999. The correction is an example of the problem we're studying. Our knowledge of an archaeological discovery comes partly from the excavated soil and partly from documents about the excavation. A person can change the history of archaeology without receiving fair credit in the story told afterward. Reading letters, notebooks, and publications may be necessary to reconstruct who actually identified a site or developed an interpretation.

Schliemann's work nevertheless transformed the public visibility of Hisarlik. He undertook large excavations beginning in the 1870s, dug deep into the mound, and found impressive early Bronze Age material. He announced a cache of precious objects as Priam's Treasure, associating the finds with the legendary king. But the treasure belonged to a settlement much earlier than the most plausible Late Bronze Age setting of the Homeric war. The seductive label outran the chronology.

The excavation method contributed to the problem. Schliemann's great trench cut through later layers as he sought a deep, supposedly original Troy. His approach exposed ancient architecture but destroyed parts of the stratigraphic sequence that later researchers needed. Excavation is irreversible: a trench can reveal a stone wall while erasing the relationships that might have dated it securely. The price of a spectacular discovery may be the loss of evidence for a more accurate interpretation.

None of this is an argument that Schliemann accomplished nothing. He helped demonstrate the archaeological richness of Hisarlik, made the site internationally prominent, and assembled substantial documentation. But a history that credits only him misses Calvert, his collaborators, the Ottoman authorities and workers involved, and the later archaeologists who had to correct his chronological claims. The sequence of research is more informative than the legend of a single individual finding Troy by believing in Homer.

## More than one city under the same name

The first critical insight is that Troy is not just one city. People built and rebuilt settlements at Hisarlik through different periods. Archaeologists describe a succession of major phases conventionally numbered Troy One through Troy Nine, with smaller subdivisions. The numbering summarizes an archaeological sequence; it does not imply that a new civilization arrived every time a numeral changed. Walls were repaired, houses altered, materials reused, and practices changed at different rates.

An architect and excavator, Wilhelm Dörpfeld, worked with Schliemann and took a leading role in later nineteenth-century investigations. He recognized that powerful fortifications associated with Troy Six belonged to a much later period than the material Schliemann had called Priam's treasure. Dörpfeld considered Troy Six a promising candidate for the city behind the legend. His achievement was in the stratigraphic distinction: if the poem points toward a Bronze Age city, we should examine the phase that belongs to the appropriate historical period, not simply the deepest impressive walls.

In the 1930s, Carl Blegen led University of Cincinnati excavations that refined the sequence still further. Blegen's team included scholars such as Marion Rawson, John Caskey, and John Boulter, whose technical and publication contributions belong in the history of the project. The resulting multi-volume excavation report, *Troy: Excavations Conducted by the University of Cincinnati, 1932–1938*, provided stratigraphic descriptions and material evidence that other researchers could test. Reports are not glamorous compared with the gold, but they are how an interpretation becomes accountable.

Blegen argued that the end of Troy Six could be explained by an earthquake, and that a later settlement, Troy Seven A, was a stronger candidate for a war-related destruction. Houses crowded within repaired fortifications, storage installations, and evidence of destruction suggested a community facing danger. Other researchers have revised particular dates and the interpretation of individual finds. A crowded city could result from several social pressures. Fire and collapse can result from conflict, accident, or natural processes. A destruction layer does not identify the army that caused it.

Archaeologist Donald Easton later re-examined Schliemann's notebooks and reconstructed aspects of the early excavations. This work is a valuable demonstration of historical method applied to archaeology itself. By comparing old plans, find descriptions, and later stratigraphic frameworks, Easton could recover some of the context that appeared lost in nineteenth-century reports. The reconstruction remained limited by gaps in the original records. We learn from both its successes and its boundaries. The notes of an excavation are evidence, and the way they were produced affects their reliability.

## How to interpret a destruction layer

Imagine excavating a room with fallen masonry, charcoal, and smashed vessels. One possible explanation is that attackers burned the building. Another is that a roof caught fire accidentally. A third is that an earthquake damaged the building and occupants later burned debris. The evidence might support one explanation more strongly than the others, but a single broken pot cannot decide the case.

An earthquake can produce distinctive patterns of collapse and structural damage. Warfare might leave weapon points, trauma on human bones, and signs of rapid abandonment. Yet arrowheads can be carried and reused, and human bodies can be buried or moved long after fighting. Even a thick destruction layer can represent more than one episode. To interpret it, archaeologists map where objects were found, examine whether floors are sealed, compare different parts of the settlement, and establish the chronology of rebuilding. A dramatic narrative should emerge from that analysis, rather than determine in advance what the excavator must see.

The most difficult inference is the identity of the attackers. Even if Troy Seven A was destroyed by an organized assault, a date and a burned wall do not prove the presence of Agamemnon, Achilles, or Hector. The Iliad's characters inhabit a poetic narrative shaped by a long oral tradition and later textual transmission. The poem provides precious evidence about Greek ideas of heroism, warfare, hospitality, and suffering. It does not function as a day-by-day military dispatch from a specific year in the Late Bronze Age.

That distinction may feel disappointing if one begins with the question, Did the Trojan War happen exactly as Homer tells it? But the archaeological questions are larger. Was there a fortified settlement on the route between the Aegean and the Black Sea? How did its inhabitants trade, store food, build defenses, and respond to political pressure? Were they linked to Anatolian powers and Mycenaean Greeks? Did their city experience violence or major destruction during the centuries in which the legend is sometimes placed? These questions can be investigated with evidence. The answers may support a historical setting for later storytelling without identifying a single war behind every verse.

## The lower city and the scale of Troy

Another major phase of research began in 1988 under a collaboration involving the University of Tübingen and the University of Cincinnati. Archaeologist Manfred Korfmann led renewed work on the Bronze Age remains, while Charles Brian Rose worked on the Greek and Roman phases. Their teams examined the settlement and its landscape with methods that included geophysical survey and broader regional study. The project was increasingly interested in the lower town outside the monumental hilltop citadel.

Why does that change the picture? If we see only the surviving citadel, Troy may appear to have been a relatively small fortified complex. Evidence for habitation and infrastructure outside its walls could imply a larger settlement with a wider range of activities and a bigger population. Geophysics and excavation can reveal structures and landscape features invisible from the surface. But estimates of a lower city's extent depend on which features really belong to the Bronze Age, how continuously the area was occupied, and how well survey anomalies can be interpreted. A ditch detected in the ground is evidence of a ditch, not an automatic head count for a metropolis.

Korfmann's interpretations prompted debate about Troy's size, trading role, and regional importance. Researchers including Donald Easton, J. David Hawkins, Andrew Sherratt, and Susan Sherratt weighed the archaeological and textual evidence in published assessments. The controversy was more productive when particular claims—such as the date of a defensive ditch or the significance of a trade route—could be separated from sweeping judgements about whether the excavator had exaggerated the whole site. Archaeology advances through this kind of argument when the disputed observations remain available for review.

A city does not have to be enormous to be historically consequential. Troy's position near routes connecting the Aegean and the straits made it an interesting place to study regardless of its precise population. But geography creates possibilities, not proof of a particular political or commercial role. Imported pottery can establish contact without telling us exactly who transported it or whether it was purchased, exchanged as a gift, or carried through multiple intermediaries. Archaeologists build a regional history from patterns of finds, not from one intuitive map arrow.

## Letters from a different world

There is another source of evidence for Late Bronze Age western Anatolia: Hittite records. The Hittite kingdom's administrative and diplomatic tablets discuss places and political relationships whose names may overlap with the geography known later from Greek tradition. Many scholars identify Wilusa in Hittite texts with Ilios or Troy, and Ahhiyawa with a Mycenaean Greek power or powers. These identifications are historically significant but have been developed through linguistic and geographical arguments, not a surviving document labelled 'The Trojan War.'

Hittitologist J. David Hawkins and other specialists have contributed to reconstructing the political geography behind these references. If Wilusa corresponds to Troy, written records place the region within diplomatic relationships and disputes beyond the horizon of the Greek poem. That helps explain why the settlement could have mattered to larger powers. But the existence of Wilusa does not validate Achilles's personal history. Even the strongest identification of a place name leaves the literary narrative to be studied as literature.

The evidence also has different dates. The oldest surviving Greek texts of Homer come from the first millennium before the common era and later manuscript traditions, although their poems preserve material from earlier oral performance. Hittite administrative texts belong to the Late Bronze Age. Excavated Troy Six and Seven structures have physical archaeological chronologies. These are three different archives, each requiring its own methods. The best historical synthesis asks where they overlap and where they remain independent.

We can use the comparison to avoid a familiar mistake: treating the absence of a named Homeric hero in Hittite records as conclusive disproof of all conflict at Troy, or treating a possible place-name match as conclusive proof of every heroic scene. Neither inference follows. The tablets were written for the Hittite state's purposes. Their selection of events reflects diplomacy and administration. The Homeric poems were shaped for audiences interested in heroic action and its consequences. Archaeological layers record physical events, which may be silent on their political causes. Distinct sources can illuminate a historical landscape without collapsing into one supposedly complete account.

## How an archaeological interpretation changes

Let's compare the principal nineteenth- and twentieth-century proposals. Schliemann's earliest dramatic identification attached an impressive treasure to Homer's Priam. Dörpfeld shifted attention toward Troy Six because its fortifications and period better fit a Late Bronze Age setting. Blegen argued that Troy Seven A's destruction made it a stronger candidate for a siege. Korfmann's team expanded investigation beyond the citadel and emphasized a larger Anatolian context. Later scholarship revisited excavation records and debated the city's scale and international role.

There is no embarrassment in this sequence. It shows how a question changes when better evidence becomes available. The history of the scholarship is not merely a list of wrong men followed by right men. Each phase brought discoveries and biases; later researchers could correct chronologies while retaining useful field observations. In particular, the recovery of Calvert's contribution by Susan Heuck Allen and the recovery of contextual information through Donald Easton's archival studies show how historians can improve archaeological history without turning a spade in the ground.

We should also remember where the site is. Hisarlik is in modern Turkey. Excavations took place within the laws, political circumstances, and cultural heritage institutions of the Ottoman Empire and then the Turkish Republic. Research projects relied on local expertise and labor. Objects excavated there have complex collection histories. The gold Schliemann removed became part of international disputes about ownership and restitution. Archaeology is conducted in a living landscape, under changing political authority. Its material and its records have histories after excavation as well as before it.

A good museum exhibit can tell this layered story. It can show the architectural phases, the chronology of ceramics, the evidence for damage, and the literary images that made the site famous. It can explain what belongs to Schliemann, Calvert, Dörpfeld, Blegen, Korfmann, Rose, and other teams. It can show where a hypothesis is popular but disputed. It can present the poetic tragedy of Hector without implying that the burial scene in Book Twenty-Four can be excavated from the earth.

## How archaeologists test a literary map

There is an appealing temptation to stand above the ruins and match every landmark to Homer's language. A river in the poem must be that stream. A battlefield must be that plain. A nearby ridge must be the place from which a god watched the fighting. The temptation is understandable: geography is tangible, and the poem is vivid. But ancient landscapes shift. Rivers change course, sediments accumulate, coastlines move, and later roads and fields redraw the surface. A useful historical map must explain what the landscape probably looked like in the period under investigation, rather than assume that today's topography is identical to the poet's.

Survey archaeologists and geomorphologists investigate these changes through sediment cores, landforms, geological mapping, and the distribution of archaeological sites. A prehistoric shoreline may be traced through marine deposits. An ancient harbor can become dry land. A river crossing significant to one century can lose its importance in another. At Troy, understanding the surrounding plain and the routes through the straits informs questions about contact, transport, and settlement. Yet the existence of a plausible route is only an opportunity for a particular event to occur, not proof that it did.

This provides a useful way to distinguish reconstruction from illustration. A museum reconstruction of Bronze Age Troy may draw walls and gates based on excavated foundations and show roofs, upper floors, and activity beyond what survives. Some of those details are supported by comparable architecture and building materials; others are visual guesses needed to make a readable scene. A responsible exhibit can explain which features were measured and which were restored for visualization. The same principle should govern a historical lecture: vividness is valuable, but the narrator should know where the image comes from.

## The problem of matching an epic to one event

The Iliad is the product of a long history of Greek oral poetry, and scholars of Homeric composition have shown that formulaic language and recurring scenes were part of how these poems worked. Milman Parry and Albert Lord are among the scholars associated with analyzing oral-formulaic epic through comparison with living traditions of oral performance. Their research helps explain how stories may carry older themes while changing as they are performed and transmitted. The historical settings of a poem need not all belong to one century. A later storyteller can describe an early Bronze Age object, a Late Bronze Age fortified city, and social behavior drawn from much later audiences in the same narrative world.

Archaeology can sometimes identify an object or practice that resembles something in the poem. But we must be careful about the direction of inference. If Homer mentions a weapon that appears in Bronze Age tombs, it may reflect some memory or inherited description of older material culture. It might also be a feature transmitted in poetry independently of a specific war narrative. We should ask how specific the match is, how securely the artifact is dated, and whether the object existed over a long enough period to weaken its chronological significance.

Consider the famous shield of Achilles. The poem offers an elaborate description of scenes of agriculture, law, dancing, conflict, and cosmic order. An archaeologist can compare the artistic techniques and motifs to surviving metalwork, but that does not make the shield a catalogued artifact we should expect to excavate. The literary description is part of the poem's meditation on society and violence. It can draw on material culture without being an inventory. The same principle applies to architecture, ritual, and battlefield organization. A poem's realistic detail is evidence about its poetic imagination and cultural knowledge; each proposed historical correspondence needs separate evaluation.

A different comparison is possible with Hittite diplomatic documents. They need not describe the characters of the Iliad to tell us about political conflicts in western Anatolia. Their interest lies in contemporary relations among states and places, as understood by the people who wrote them. If Wilusa is Troy, we gain an external view of a settlement whose Greek literary identity became famous later. That external view constrains some historical possibilities but leaves many narrative details unverified. It is more valuable for what it actually says than for how easily we can turn its names into characters in a familiar drama.

## A scholar can be wrong and still contribute evidence

Frank Calvert's observations at Hisarlik helped direct major investigations. Heinrich Schliemann's deep trench revealed material and damaged contexts. Wilhelm Dörpfeld's phase distinctions corrected the identification of Priam's treasure. Carl Blegen's excavations refined the sequence of Late Bronze Age occupation. Manfred Korfmann's regional research changed the questions asked about the settlement's scale. Susan Heuck Allen's archival work revised the story of who deserved early credit. Donald Easton's close study of field notebooks recovered context from old excavations. Their contributions cannot be collapsed into a series in which each researcher merely replaced the previous one.

This becomes clearer if we compare the kinds of evidence they produced. A dated pottery sequence and a measured wall have continuing value even if the excavator's preferred historical explanation changes. An old notebook may record the position of a feature accurately while the text surrounding it contains confident but mistaken interpretations. A later historian can separate the observation from the claim. At the same time, some nineteenth-century contexts were recorded so poorly that no modern reanalysis can recover what has been lost. Credit and criticism belong together in a responsible account.

There is also a distinction between the person who signs a report and the people whose labor made the report possible. Excavation teams require diggers, conservators, surveyors, ceramic specialists, illustrators, local guides, and archives staff. Many older publications provide only partial lists, and sometimes a name has disappeared altogether. We can correct a record where names survive, and we can describe the structural limitation when they do not. The absence of a worker's name in a famous monograph is evidence about the publication's social world, not evidence that the worker contributed nothing.

## What have we actually learned?

Let us return to the question that began this lecture. Is Troy real? There is an excavated settlement at Hisarlik with a long history and substantial Late Bronze Age fortifications. That is not in doubt. The identification of Hisarlik with Troy or Ilion has strong historical and archaeological support. Multiple phases of damage and rebuilding reveal episodes of real human upheaval. Diplomatic texts may connect the settlement to political geography known from Hittite records. Those are meaningful historical results.

Does that prove the Iliad? The Iliad is a poem about a particular phase of a legendary war and its human costs. It does not even narrate the entire fall of the city. Its narrative took shape through traditions whose relationship to any single Bronze Age event cannot be demonstrated by a layer of burned debris. The archaeology provides a setting, constraints, possible historical memories, and new questions. It does not turn poetic dialogue into a transcript.

There is a deeper lesson in how this knowledge was made. Frank Calvert argued for Hisarlik before Schliemann became the face of its discovery. Schliemann brought energy and attention while damaging parts of the evidence. Dörpfeld and Blegen developed more precise stratigraphic interpretations. Korfmann, Rose, and later collaborators investigated a wider urban and regional landscape. Susan Heuck Allen and Donald Easton recovered information through archival research, while Hittite specialists established a separate documentary context. Each person or team addressed a different problem. Crediting them allows us to see the process, including its corrections.

The ruins are therefore more than an answer to whether a favorite story is true. They provide an opportunity to see how material evidence is converted into historical knowledge. We begin with walls and objects in a layered mound. We compare dates, literary traditions, and documents written for very different purposes. We test reconstructions against what the ground and archives preserve. And we end with a richer, more constrained picture of a real place whose name also belongs to one of the great works of world literature.
