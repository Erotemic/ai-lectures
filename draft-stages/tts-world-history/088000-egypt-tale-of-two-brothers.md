# The Tale of Two Brothers: Betrayal, the Hidden Heart, and the Returning King

Imagine that someone has hidden his heart in the flower of a tree. He is alive as long as the flower survives, but if the tree is cut down he will fall dead wherever he happens to be. Imagine, further, that his enemies can kill him, change his shape, and try to destroy the next shape too, only for him to return in a form they cannot recognize. This seems to belong to a world of folktale and fantasy. In ancient Egypt it also belongs to the world of kingship. The man whose heart is hidden in the tree will eventually become Pharaoh.

His name is Bata. He is the younger of two brothers, and at the beginning he has none of the power we associate with kings. He works in the fields, looks after cattle, and lives in the household of his older brother Anubis. Their bond seems secure until an accusation turns it into a deadly pursuit. What follows is one of the most elaborate surviving stories from ancient Egypt: a household tragedy, an escape across supernatural water, a marriage made by the gods, a series of transformations, and an ending in which a persecuted farm worker takes the throne.

We know the composition from a manuscript called Papyrus D'Orbiney, in the British Museum, copied in the late Nineteenth Dynasty, around the end of the thirteenth century BCE. The language is Late Egyptian, written in hieratic, the flowing handwriting Egyptians used for many documents and stories. The named copyist, Inena, left a trace of himself at the end. We do not know who first invented the tale. The story we can actually read is the one preserved in this papyrus. Its unusual changes of direction are part of its design, and they deserve to be followed closely.

## A household that appears complete

Bata is an indispensable member of his brother's household. Anubis has a wife and a farm; Bata prepares clothing, leads the animals out, plows, harvests, and brings back food from the fields. The brothers are introduced as sons of the same parents, but they do not occupy equal positions. Anubis is the established householder. Bata, in some translations, is treated almost as his son. Bata's labor contributes to the older man's prosperity. The arrangement includes affection and hierarchy at the same time.

Egyptian farming was organized around the rhythms of the Nile flood, irrigation, animal labor, and fields that demanded sustained attention. A household could depend upon the reliability of someone who knew the animals and the agricultural calendar. The tale's beginning works because its audience can recognize that dependence. Bata is more than a decorative hero. The household functions through his work. He is depicted as vigorous, skilled, and faithful to his responsibilities. The cattle themselves seem to understand him, an early hint that ordinary agricultural life is about to give way to the marvelous.

One day, when seed is needed in the field, Bata returns to the house. Anubis's wife is there. She propositions him. Bata refuses, arguing that she stands toward him like a mother, because she is his older brother's wife. He promises to keep the encounter private. His refusal shows the limits of what a younger man owes a household: he serves the family, but he will not violate its relationships to please someone who has authority within it.

The wife fears that Bata will disclose what happened, or resents the rejection; the story does not provide a sustained interior portrait of her motives. Instead it shows what she does. She arranges to appear assaulted and tells Anubis that Bata tried to force himself upon her. The accusation converts the man whose labor sustains the home into an apparent enemy inside it. Anubis believes her and prepares to kill his brother when he returns. A family built on trust now becomes a trap.

The pattern can sound familiar to readers of the biblical Joseph story, where a powerful man's wife also accuses a younger man who has rejected her advance. Scholars, among them Miriam Lichtheim and Susan Tower Hollis, have discussed the comparison. Similar narrative patterns may travel through cultural contacts, and they may also arise independently. We cannot use the resemblance to prove that one surviving text was copied from the other. For the Egyptian tale itself, the accusation creates a particular crisis: Bata has no human witness to prove his innocence, and the person who should protect him has become his pursuer.

## The cattle speak, and the river divides

Bata begins to return with his cattle. The animals warn him that Anubis is waiting with a weapon. Their speech does more than add a charming supernatural touch. It reverses the normal hierarchy of trust. Animals recognize the danger while the older brother has failed to recognize the truth. Bata looks back, sees the threat, and flees.

Anubis chases him. Bata appeals to the sun god, usually called Ra or Re in English. A body of water appears between the brothers, filled with crocodiles. The river prevents the older brother from completing the murder. It creates the distance needed for a conversation that violence had made impossible. Across this barrier, Bata can tell his side of the story without coming within reach of the blade.

The scene is striking because the divine intervention does not settle the dispute with an announcement from heaven. It makes a hearing possible. Bata explains the encounter in the house. He denounces the betrayal and asserts his loyalty. He then performs a severe act of self-mutilation, cutting off his genitals and throwing them into the water. The episode is disturbing, and the story intends us to feel its force. Bata makes his rejection of the sexual accusation bodily and irrevocable. His claim of innocence becomes a wound that will affect how we interpret his future transformations.

One could read the act as an impossible attempt to remove every basis for suspicion. Yet the cost falls on the person falsely accused, and that asymmetry should remain visible. The story does not distribute suffering according to a simple moral ledger. Bata must leave the household to survive. Anubis, on the other bank, realizes that he has acted upon a false accusation. He will return home and punish his wife, but the brothers' life together has already ended.

Bata says that he will travel to a place called the Valley of the Pine, or Valley of the Cedar in other translations. The Egyptian expression and the tree's identification have been rendered differently, and the specific botanical species is less important here than what the story makes the tree do. Bata will place his heart in its uppermost flower. He tells Anubis how to recognize when disaster has struck. If a sign appears in the older brother's drink, Anubis must seek the heart and restore his brother to life. A quarrel in the fields has become an agreement about resurrection.

This is the first great change in the story's logic. Bata's innocence cannot secure him a place at home. Instead he relocates his life into a vulnerable object far away. He will live, but another person's knowledge and future loyalty may be required to save him. The separation brings a new form of dependence: a heart hidden in a tree that can be felled by someone who knows nothing of the man attached to it.

## A new home made by the gods

In his distant valley, Bata builds a life alone. He hunts; he occupies a landscape unlike the cultivated fields he left behind. The gods take notice. They decide he should have a companion, and a divine craftsperson fashions a woman for him. The account presents her beauty as extraordinary. It also presents a difficulty that will shape the second half of the tale: someone has created a partner for Bata without giving that partner a voice in the arrangement.

The story puts marriage at the center of both of its major disasters. The first began when Anubis's wife acted deceptively and Anubis believed her. The second begins with Bata's newly created wife and the desire of the king of Egypt. These women are given great power to redirect events, but their motives are filtered through the narrator's judgment, often in terms of danger and betrayal. It is possible to observe this pattern without endorsing every assumption the story makes about women. Ancient narrative can tell us how a particular writer built suspense and moral judgment; it does not provide a census of Egyptian women's conduct.

Bata warns his wife about the vulnerability of his heart and the danger associated with the sea. The tale now makes its hero extraordinarily strong in some ways and mortally exposed in another. A man who can survive exile and attract divine attention is still dependent on a flower high in a tree. The weakness has an exact location. Suspense enters because the audience knows it before Bata's enemies do.

A lock of the wife's hair reaches the Nile and eventually the royal court. The king's attendants detect the fragrance associated with it. The scent is explained as belonging to a woman of remarkable divine beauty. Pharaoh wants to possess her. The court sends people to find her. Bata initially resists, and the king's first attempt does not achieve its goal. But the court returns with greater force and persuasive offerings; the woman is brought to Egypt.

The sequence is a compressed story about imperial reach. A household in a remote valley cannot simply declare itself beyond the king's power. People and objects travel from a distant place to the royal center, where desire becomes an order, and an order becomes an expedition. The strange route of a strand of hair is a narrative device, but the assumption behind the device is political: a king can mobilize others to obtain what he wants.

At court, Bata's wife explains where his hidden heart can be found. The tree is cut down, the flower destroyed, and Bata falls lifeless. The king gains the woman, and the audience witnesses the deadly consequence of exposing a secret. What seemed like an elaborate precaution against mortality has made the location of Bata's life discoverable and exploitable.

## Anubis searches for a heart

The story has prepared this moment long before the tree falls. Anubis receives the sign that his brother is in danger. He sets out to find him. The search is difficult. The heart is not sitting in a recognizable chest; it has been displaced into the landscape. Anubis must look among the remains of the tree and continue searching rather than abandon the effort after one failure.

Eventually he finds the heart. He places it in water, and Bata is restored. The episode reverses the earlier river scene. At first, water separated the brothers and saved Bata from Anubis. Now water enables the elder brother to help return the younger to life. We cannot reduce the change to a tidy formula in which every wrong has been paid back. Anubis had intended murder; he now performs the labor of repair. His search gives us action rather than merely an apology.

The recovery also changes the story's treatment of the body. Bata's life can be stored outside him. A tree can become the site of death; water can participate in restoration. These are narrative possibilities connected to a religious culture fascinated by bodily integrity, transformation, and the continuation of life after death. But the tale is not a funerary manual. It takes motifs that can carry religious associations and turns them into the machinery of a gripping plot.

Bata understands that he has a new problem. Simply returning as the man he was would expose him again to the court that had destroyed his first body. He needs a form that can enter the royal world while concealing his identity. He becomes a magnificent bull, and Anubis takes him to the king. The ruler admires the animal and brings it into the palace. The man whom the court thought it had eliminated now occupies a place close to power, disguised as a possession.

## The bull and the trees

At court the bull speaks to the woman who had been Bata's wife. The revelation terrifies or infuriates her: the person she thought destroyed has returned. She persuades the king to slaughter the bull. The request is not an incidental cruelty. It is another attempt to make Bata's death permanent by destroying the new form in which he survives.

The animal is killed, but drops of its blood reach the ground and produce two remarkable trees. Egyptian stories often give creative force to drops, fragments, and the transformed residue of bodies. Here the storyteller turns an intended final destruction into another beginning. Bata is no longer one body that a hostile power can identify and seize. He is a succession of forms that makes the king's control look increasingly fragile.

The trees stand in the royal precinct. They too speak and reveal themselves to the woman. She again asks the king to remove the source of her fear. The trees are felled. Yet during the cutting, a splinter flies into her mouth. She swallows it and conceives. In the story's strange reproductive logic, Bata survives by entering the body of the very person who has been plotting his destruction.

This is the climax of the transformations. A man becomes a bull, blood becomes trees, wood enters a woman, and a child is born. Each change answers an act of violence while also escalating the spectacle. The tale does not offer a scientific mechanism, and the attraction of the episode does not depend on inventing one. Its artistic mechanism is exact: every effort to destroy Bata provides the material of his return.

The child is acknowledged in the royal household. He grows into the role of heir. Eventually he becomes king. The younger brother who began as an agricultural worker under another man's authority now occupies the highest position in the country. The story has crossed every visible boundary between field and palace, human and divine, death and life, male hero and child born from a hostile mother's body.

As ruler, Bata summons the woman who had betrayed him and has the accusations and acts reviewed. She receives punishment. The tale ends with Bata ruling for a period and with Anubis, the older brother, succeeding him. The brotherly relationship that first seemed destroyed becomes part of the royal succession. It is a final turn more surprising than a simple reunion, because the family drama has been expanded to explain a chain of kings.

## What kind of story have we heard?

It is tempting to classify the tale at once. It contains animals that speak, divine beings, an external soul, magical transformations, and a persecuted figure who wins a crown. It therefore resembles what later collectors would call a folktale or fairy tale. It also contains Egyptian gods and motifs recognizable from religious literature. It is set in a pharaonic world where royal power organizes action. It may have served several interests for its ancient listeners: entertainment, reflection on betrayal, courtly legitimacy, and pleasure in narrative reversal.

The modern scholar Susan Tower Hollis made this complexity a central concern of her study *The Ancient Egyptian Tale of Two Brothers*. She examined the story as literature while investigating its mythological relationships. Other Egyptologists have proposed more specific political readings. Wolfgang Wettengel, for example, argued for connections with the Ramesside royal house and the incorporation of a Near Eastern divine tradition. Such interpretations can illuminate features that a simple folktale label would miss, though they require their own arguments. We should distinguish an interpretation of the story's political meaning from the events the papyrus actually narrates.

The names Anubis and Bata are especially suggestive. Anubis is also the familiar name of an Egyptian god involved with death and embalming. Bata has associations with a divine figure in Egyptian tradition. A fictional brother called Anubis is therefore not merely a man with a neutral name. The tale plays in a world where names can evoke religious characters and where a man's restoration from death may carry more than one meaning. Still, one should resist assuming that every action in the story corresponds directly to a fixed myth. Literary authors can combine inherited motifs and create new dramatic connections.

The tale's two halves also deserve to be read together. The first moves from domestic order to the complete collapse of trust. The second moves through repeated violent attempts to prevent Bata's return. In each half, the dangerous act is supported by someone who has access to authority: a wife whose accusation Anubis believes, and a woman whose requests the king obeys. Bata survives because animals, a god, and ultimately his repentant brother give him forms of help that official power does not provide. The narrative rewards patience in a peculiar way. Waiting alone would not save him. Others must recognize danger, keep faith, search, and act.

We can test a familiar interpretation: is this simply a story about the triumph of innocence? Bata does ultimately become king, so the ending offers that satisfaction. Yet the price of vindication is extraordinary. His first home is lost; he wounds himself; he dies and returns through forms exposed to further attack. Innocence gives him moral standing but does not spare him suffering. The story's emotional intensity comes from the gap between what a just world might permit and what its hero must endure before justice is publicly recognized.

Another interpretation emphasizes kingship. A worker from a household becomes a king through divine favor and repeated rebirth. A royal heir is born in circumstances ordinary biology cannot explain. Anubis then succeeds him. A listener living under the Ramesside kings might hear in such an ending a provocative way of presenting political legitimacy as a transformation whose true origins are hidden. Yet we should not pretend that the papyrus explicitly names the particular historical kings whom every episode allegedly represents. It gives a story. The historical reading is a proposal built from the story's details and context.

## A story that survived in someone's handwriting

The surviving papyrus adds another remarkable fact. Unlike many ancient narratives, this copy has a named scribe. The modern reading of the name is often given as Inena or Ennana. The colophon, an end-note placed after the story, identifies the copyist. A further memorandum helps date the manuscript to a period when Seti, later Seti the Second, was a royal heir. These clues provide a view of the people who physically transmitted the literature, even though they do not identify the first creator of the tale.

The papyrus was purchased for the British Museum in 1857 from Elizabeth d'Orbiney. That is why the manuscript bears a modern collector's name rather than the name of the Egyptian scribe. Scholars have connected it with other Ramesside literary manuscripts, including items associated with the Sallier and Anastasi collections. The connections are informative, but its original archaeological findspot is not securely documented. A museum can possess an ancient object and still lack the excavation record that would tell us exactly where it was lying when recovered.

That distinction is valuable because this work otherwise invites such vivid imagination. We can narrate the crocodile-filled water and the changing bodies with confidence that these events belong to the Egyptian story. We cannot report the events as things that happened in Egyptian history. We can attribute the surviving handwriting to an ancient scribe while leaving the first act of composition open. We can date the manuscript approximately without pretending that this supplies the birthday of every motif in it.

The earliest modern editions and translations involved scholars such as Emmanuel de Rougé, Charles Wycliffe Goodwin, Samuel Birch, and P. Le Page Renouf. Later editions and interpretations include Alan Gardiner, Miriam Lichtheim, Edward Wente, and Hollis. Their work lets us recognize a chain between a physical papyrus and the English sentences available today: collecting, preserving, reading hieratic, resolving Late Egyptian grammar, publishing signs, translating, and interpreting narrative structure.

The chain helps us appreciate how much of this story depends upon real people doing exacting work. At the same time, the tale has survived with enough of its own energy that the manuscript's scholarly history cannot exhaust it. There are still the two brothers by the river, the missing heart in the tree, the bull whispering at court, and the splinter that brings a king into being.

## The end of the brothers' story

At the beginning, Anubis's household seemed secure because one man worked and another man possessed the house. That arrangement failed when trust broke down. At the end, both brothers are associated with kingship, but only after the younger has passed through exile, death, multiple bodies, and a second birth. The distance between those two states is the story's true measure of its ambition.

*The Tale of Two Brothers* is worth knowing because it gives us an ancient Egyptian narrative that refuses to stay within one genre. It begins in fields and ends on a throne. It treats intimate betrayal with the scale of cosmic metamorphosis. It uses spectacular magic to ask what can survive when someone powerful wants another person erased. And because a particular scribe's copy has endured, we can encounter that narrative across more than three thousand years. We do not need to turn its events into historical facts to take them seriously as part of human literary history.
