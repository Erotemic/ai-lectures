# The Indus Cities: History Without a Deciphered Script

Stand in a ruined city of straight streets, carefully laid bricks, wells, drainage channels, and workshops. Imagine walking past a small carved seal showing an animal and a few signs in a script you cannot read. You know that the builders could organize substantial projects and maintain networks of exchange. You can inspect their houses and objects. But you cannot read a king's proclamation, a merchant's letter, or a historical chronicle written in their own language. How much history could you reconstruct?

This is the problem of the Indus civilization, also called the Harappan civilization. Its urban centers flourished across parts of what are now Pakistan and northwestern India in the third millennium before the common era. Some were contemporary with Egyptian kingdoms and the cities of Mesopotamia. Their built environments and material culture survive on a remarkable scale, yet their short inscriptions have not yielded an accepted decipherment. The Indus case asks us to investigate what archaeology can establish, what remains unknown, and why an inscription that looks legible may not actually be readable.

There is another history inside the investigation: how the Indus cities were first recognized by modern archaeologists, whose work made them visible, and how old assumptions about cities, kings, religion, and conquest have been revised. The surviving evidence does not need to become a complete text to tell us important things. But we must learn to stop exactly where the evidence stops.

## Ancient walls and a modern surprise

The ruins of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro did not spring into existence in the 1920s. Local people knew the mounds, reused materials, and lived in landscapes where ancient remains were visible. British colonial officers and travelers reported on parts of the sites in the nineteenth century. One early observer, Charles Masson, described Harappa. Railway construction and other development removed bricks from archaeological mounds, destroying context before systematic excavation.

In the 1920s the Archaeological Survey of India undertook major investigations. Daya Ram Sahni worked at Harappa, while Rakhaldas Bandyopadhyay, often rendered R. D. Banerji in older English accounts, investigated Mohenjo-daro. John Marshall, then director-general of the survey, brought the findings to international attention in 1924. These roles should be distinguished: documenting a site, excavating significant layers, recognizing cultural relationships between locations, and announcing the historical significance are different achievements.

The chronology of discovery has sometimes been simplified in ways that inflate the contribution of the director while reducing the visibility of Indian excavators. Archaeologist B. B. Lal's retrospective account identifies Sahni and Banerji as central to the recognition of the major sites. Other researchers, including M. S. Vats and Ernest Mackay, conducted important excavations and publications. Later excavations by George Dales, Richard Meadow, Jonathan Mark Kenoyer, and collaborators changed the questions scholars could ask.

The initial realization was consequential: an extensive urban society existed in South Asia more than four thousand years ago. Its material culture was comparable in complexity to other early urban traditions, yet it did not fit the story of Indian history built around later textual and political records. Archaeology had supplied an additional historical foundation.

## The city as an archive

Mohenjo-daro, in modern Sindh, Pakistan, contains streets and structures that reveal planning on an impressive scale. Houses incorporate wells, bathing areas, drains, and brickwork. The Great Bath is among the city's best-known structures. We can describe its construction and waterproofing; we cannot securely name the specific ritual or social ceremonies that occurred there. The name 'Great Bath' records a modern archaeological description, not an ancient label.

A city can be read without written language through patterns of construction. Repeated brick dimensions may suggest shared technical conventions. Streets intersecting at planned angles reveal decisions about the movement of people, water, and goods. Drains crossing property boundaries imply some capacity for coordinating works beyond one household. Workshops and debris can identify bead making, pottery production, shell working, and other specialized activities.

But this evidence has limits. A drainage system does not automatically prove a modern municipal government. A large building is not necessarily a palace. A platform is not evidence of a priestly caste merely because an excavator gives it a ceremonial-sounding name. Early scholarship sometimes imposed familiar categories from Mesopotamia or Egypt onto the Indus cities. Current work tries to identify the actual material signatures of authority before assigning an institution.

UNESCO's description of Mohenjo-daro emphasizes the surviving plan, water systems, and the need to protect fragile structures. It also notes that only a fraction of the site has been excavated. This is an important reminder: our image of the city is shaped by where excavators dug, what was exposed, and which structures survived better than their neighbors.

## The problem of standardized things

Small cubical stone weights are among the most informative finds in the Indus world. Some follow strikingly consistent ratios, permitting comparisons across sites. Seals bearing animal imagery and short inscriptions recur. Certain proportions in brick construction appear across settlements. This material evidence suggests shared conventions of measurement, craft, and perhaps transactions.

Jonathan Mark Kenoyer and Richard Meadow's Harappa Archaeological Research Project examined these patterns through more recent stratified excavation. Kenoyer's work on Indus measurement shows both the reach of shared standards and significant local variation. He cautions that standardized object types should not automatically be taken as evidence for one centralized imperial bureaucracy. Different settlements can adopt shared conventions through trade, imitation, training, or institutions without being ruled by a single monarch.

Imagine a set of weights found in several towns. You can test their masses, plot variation, and compare wear. You might infer that exchanging goods across the region required mutually intelligible standards. But you cannot immediately infer who enforced the standard, whether markets were taxed, or the name of a merchant guild. Material pattern and political explanation are separated by a gap that requires additional evidence.

We can also test whether seal imagery and shapes vary systematically by region and period. Kenoyer's research emphasizes that seals were made objects, not abstract letters. Their production involved choices of stone, firing, polishing, engraving, and sometimes handles. The distribution of seals and sealings may help reconstruct exchange practices even when we cannot read the signs.

## The script that refuses to speak

Indus inscriptions appear on seals, tablets, pottery, and other objects. They are generally short, often only a handful of signs. Scholar Asko Parpola, who has devoted decades to the script, explains several basic difficulties: the inscriptions are brief; the underlying language remains unidentified; and no known bilingual inscription offers the kind of parallel that helped with Egyptian hieroglyphs. Different researchers also disagree about whether certain visual variants count as distinct signs.

Think about the difference between finding a long letter and finding a stamp with five characters. If you know neither the language nor the sign values, the long text may reveal repeated word endings, sentence structure, and grammatical patterns. A collection of five-character stamps gives you far fewer clues. A sign that repeats at the end of many inscriptions might mark a title, a grammatical element, a personal name, or simply a convention of seal design. Without external anchors, these interpretations can all be made to fit some patterns.

The language problem is difficult. Some scholars propose an early Dravidian language, drawing on later linguistic evidence and comparisons. Others have suggested different linguistic affiliations, or even that the signs did not encode spoken language in the familiar sense. Those proposals can be evaluated for internal consistency and empirical predictions, but a complete decipherment must do more than assign plausible meanings to attractive signs. It should yield readings that work across inscriptions and explain why the signs appear in the observed patterns.

Parpola's work has given the field extensive catalogs, systematic comparisons, and detailed arguments. One need not agree with a proposed reading to appreciate that work. Similarly, the debate over whether the signs constitute a full writing system raises testable questions about sequence patterns, medium, context, and linguistic structure. Calling the script 'undeciphered' is not a claim that scholars have done nothing. It is a claim about the current state of demonstrable interpretation.

## Why a proposed translation can be seductive

Suppose someone says a seal depicts a bull beneath a line of five signs and offers the translation, 'The great lord of the sacred bull.' The words sound plausible. Many ancient cultures connected cattle with wealth, ritual, or power. But where did the reading come from? Can the scholar explain which sign means 'great,' which means 'lord,' and why the same assignments work on hundreds of other seals? Can they predict what a similar inscription would say in a different archaeological context?

If every contradiction can be explained away by inventing a new sign value, the decipherment is unfalsifiable. If a researcher decides the language before analyzing the inscriptions and then chooses readings that confirm the decision, the procedure is circular. A convincing result needs independent constraints. A bilingual inscription would be especially valuable. A long text with repeated grammatical structure would also help. At present neither has provided a secure breakthrough.

Computers can analyze symbol frequencies and sequences. They can detect repeating clusters, positional constraints, and differences among sites. But computational regularities are not semantic translations. An algorithm that predicts the next sign may model the internal order of a notation system without discovering the words spoken by its users. The archaeological context of the objects remains necessary.

This is a useful lesson for all historical interpretation: technical sophistication cannot replace missing anchors. We should welcome new analysis without confusing an attractive model with a demonstrated reading.

## Trade beyond the cities

Even without reading the Indus signs, archaeologists can trace objects and materials across regions. Beads, ornaments, seal impressions, and specialized crafts suggest links with areas to the west. Mesopotamian texts mention a trading region called Meluhha, commonly associated by scholars with the Indus world during parts of the third millennium. The identification is based on multiple lines of evidence, including geographic and commercial context; it does not mean that every mention of Meluhha can be mapped to one particular Indus city.

Trade studies introduce new kinds of measurement. A stone bead may be examined for its mineral composition. A shell object can be compared to species from different coasts. Pottery styles and manufacturing techniques can be mapped. The shape of a container may be widespread because an object was transported, because craftspeople migrated, or because local workshops copied an admired design. Those explanations can sometimes be distinguished through material analysis and excavation context.

The lesson is that a script is only one archive among several. A trade seal that cannot be read may still reveal how standardized imagery circulated. A broken bead may preserve traces of a drilling technology. A dump behind a workshop may explain more about daily labor than an ornate object displayed alone in a museum.

We should also resist seeing the Indus civilization solely through its largest cities. Villages, pastoral settlements, smaller craft centers, and regional traditions formed the broader world. Later research emphasizes the diversity hidden behind the label 'Harappan.' A single cultural name can make a geographically extensive network look more uniform than it was.

## Why the cities changed

The major urban configuration of the mature Indus period did not last indefinitely. Large settlements changed, some were abandoned or reorganized, and many populations continued living in different settlement patterns. Archaeological explanations have considered shifts in river systems, climate, trade, land use, and other social or environmental changes. It is inadequate to infer a single sudden catastrophe from the fact that monumental urban phases ended.

Earlier popular accounts sometimes invoked a violent invading people as the explanation. That story was tied to speculative readings of skeletal remains and much later literary traditions. A few human remains in an archaeological setting cannot on their own demonstrate a civilization-wide massacre, and the chronological distance to the later texts is a serious problem. Researchers now examine settlement sequences, environmental data, and regional variation instead of expecting one uniform collapse.

Even the word 'collapse' can conceal different outcomes. An urban center may diminish while nearby rural settlements remain occupied. People can carry technologies, languages, and customs into new social settings without keeping a city intact. A changed street plan is archaeological evidence. The disappearance of an entire people would require much more, and such a claim should never be made casually.

The evidence also continues to be threatened. Rising groundwater, salinity, floods, and development damage ancient brick structures. Conservation teams face difficult decisions about how much to expose and how much to leave buried. Archaeologists can sometimes learn from future methods only if part of the site remains protected.

## Comparing Uruk and the Indus

Consider the contrast with early Mesopotamia. At Uruk and later cities, inscriptions developed into writing traditions that can now be read, though their origins were also complicated. We can combine excavation with administrative tablets, royal inscriptions, and literature. In the Indus cities, seals remain unreadable, but architecture, manufacturing debris, standardized weights, and settlement surveys are informative in their own right.

This means the narratives we tell may differ in texture. We can sometimes quote a Mesopotamian ruler saying what he claims to have done. We cannot yet quote an Indus ruler's proclamation, if such proclamations existed at all. It would be easy to compensate by importing kings and priesthoods from better-documented societies. But that would hide the actual character of the evidence.

It is possible that the Indus political system used forms of authority different from the highly visible kingship of Egypt or Mesopotamia. It is also possible that institutions and markers of elite power existed in forms archaeology has not yet identified. The absence of a named palace is informative, yet not conclusive evidence for a particular political philosophy. We should formulate multiple hypotheses and ask what finds would support or undermine each one.

## The people who made the evidence possible

Credit should move across generations. Local people had long known the ruins. Masson recorded a site before the twentieth-century excavations. Sahni and Bandyopadhyay excavated crucial urban evidence. Marshall helped publicize its historical implications. Vats, Mackay, and later researchers recorded architecture and objects. Kenoyer, Meadow, Dales, Parpola, Lal, and many other scholars developed specialized interpretations and disagreements. Work by Pakistani and Indian archaeologists and heritage staff continues, although older international summaries often give too much attention to visiting Western researchers.

The ancient artisans deserve attention too, even though most are unnamed. They made seals, measured weights, drilled hard stone beads, built drains, and maintained complex urban systems. Their products are not just illustrations for modern theories. They are experimental traces of skills, choices, and institutions. Archaeologists can reconstruct workshop procedures by examining waste, tools, partly finished objects, and wear patterns.

The original owners of the seals may not have expected their stamps to become our best clues to an undeciphered writing tradition. A well used for household water may survive longer than an inscription intended for posterity. Survival rearranges what a future historian can know. That is why careful excavation, local stewardship, and interdisciplinary analysis are essential.

## Another excavation can change the picture

Our account has concentrated on Harappa and Mohenjo-daro because these are large, famous sites. But the greater Indus world included settlements with different histories and environments. Dholavira, in what is now Gujarat, India, is especially instructive because its reservoirs and water-management structures show engineering responses to a different landscape. Other sites along rivers, coasts, and inland routes provide contrasting patterns of housing, crafts, and agriculture.

A researcher cannot simply average these sites and declare that every Indus settlement had the same plan. Geography changes what people need to build. A coastal town may invest in exchange facilities and water access differently from an inland agricultural town. A settlement near a shifting river channel may accumulate deposits and lose buildings in ways that make its surviving record unlike that of a site on more stable terrain.

To compare the evidence fairly, archaeologists must establish local sequences. They study which structures cut into earlier ones, which pottery types occur in dated layers, and whether radiocarbon samples belong to the construction or abandonment phases under investigation. The dates of the mature urban phenomenon are approximate ranges rather than a single birth certificate for every city. A city may have earlier occupations and later communities on the same ground.

This is where broad narratives about a civilization become vulnerable. A textbook map colored uniformly across northwestern South Asia can make hundreds of communities seem to have entered and left history simultaneously. The excavation record reveals phases, continuities, and variations. Meadow and Kenoyer's work at Harappa emphasizes that even widely shared material conventions appeared against a background of local practices that cannot be collapsed into one standardized culture.

## Seals are objects with lives of their own

Imagine holding a small square steatite seal. One face is carefully engraved with an animal and a short inscription; the reverse has a projection that might help with handling or suspension. An archaeologist examines its wear, the depth of the engraved lines, and its position in a layer. A materials specialist considers how the stone was fired or treated. A researcher studies impressions in clay from related contexts.

If we cannot translate the signs, we can still ask whether the seal was pressed repeatedly into another material, whether objects of similar design were manufactured in the same workshop, and whether certain symbols cluster with certain animal motifs. If seals served administrative functions, patterns of wear and find locations might help identify those uses. But even then, we should not presume all seals were used identically. Some might be marks of identity, control over goods, prestige objects, or several things at once.

The 'unicorn' motif, a stylized creature with a single visible horn in many seal images, is a good example of interpretive restraint. Scholars can describe its frequency, design, and variation. That does not establish that ancient Indus people believed in the same mythical unicorn familiar from later European art. Nor does the motif by itself tell us whether it represented a deity, social group, institution, or workshop. Comparison within the ancient visual corpus must come before a translated cultural label.

A seal may also have traveled far from where it was manufactured. Its archaeological context could be a trade site, a house, a disturbed deposit, or a secondary reuse. A seal found in Mesopotamia is evidence for movement or connection, but the object's life history could include several transfers between people. The point is to make stronger claims from material analysis while leaving its unreadable inscription unreadable.

## Testing competing explanations for authority

One of the most interesting puzzles is the relation between remarkable urban coordination and the comparatively weak evidence for grand royal monuments. Suppose someone proposes that the Indus world was a single centrally governed state. What would we expect? Perhaps a shared administrative archive, clear concentrations of elite wealth, monumental palaces, or consistent symbols of rulership. Some shared material standards exist, but other familiar indicators of a single state are not securely identified.

Now suppose someone proposes a loose network of autonomous cities sharing commercial and ritual conventions. That model might better explain local variation, but it too would require evidence of local institutions and interactions. A third possibility is regional political systems that changed over time. Archaeologists can test these proposals through settlement hierarchy, storage facilities, craft control, burials, and the distribution of specialized goods.

The absence of a clear palace is a real observation. Its interpretation is tricky. A palace might have been built of perishable materials, incorporated into a structure not recognized as elite, or located in an unexcavated portion of a site. The lesson is not that we must refuse all political inference. It is that an inference should include an account of the evidence expected under alternatives.

This open comparison can be intellectually more valuable than a false certainty. We learn how societies coordinate public works and exchange, how power might manifest materially, and how scholars reason when the text record is poor. The Indus world remains highly visible in archaeology even while its political language stays beyond our reach.

## An honest ending without a translation

We end without unveiling the secret message. There is no established sentence from an Indus seal to recite as a triumph of decipherment. We have a rich archaeological record and unanswered questions about language, authority, ritual, trade, and the transformation of cities. That combination is intellectually productive.

The lesson is to let different evidence types do different work. Brick dimensions can speak about construction conventions. Drains can reveal investment in managing water. Workshop debris can reveal craft organization. Seals can tell us about images, objects, and the distribution of signs even while their exact words remain inaccessible. Comparisons with Mesopotamia can identify contacts without supplying a complete internal history.

There is a final archaeological problem in the very act of conserving the cities. When bricks are exposed to water and salt they can deteriorate faster than they would have under protective burial. UNESCO identifies rising groundwater and salinity at Mohenjo-daro as significant preservation threats. An excavation can create knowledge and simultaneously create new obligations to the site. Leaving some areas undisturbed for future generations can be a responsible scientific choice, especially when new analytical methods may extract information from preserved context later.

The Indus civilization is an invitation to practice historical imagination under discipline. We can picture the effort involved in building a street, drilling a bead, or preparing a seal for use. But when we want to name a ruler, translate a line, or declare a single cause for urban change, we must ask for evidence of the appropriate kind. This is how archaeology can reveal an ancient world without pretending to know its every word.
