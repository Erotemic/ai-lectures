# Ancient literature and storytelling: World History roadmap

A literary strand inside `world-history`, separate from the mathematical
curricula. The intention is to learn ancient societies through complete,
substantive accounts of what their stories say, how they survive, and what
scholars can infer from their historical setting.

## Possible further lectures

**Greek epic and the Trojan tradition**
- The *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are covered through all 24 books of each work in five independently listenable lectures. Further Homeric topics could examine the ancient epic cycle, contrasting receptions, and the archaeological setting without claiming the poems are documentary history.

**Other ancient literary worlds**
- *The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor*: Egyptian adventure and the speaking
  serpent; distinguish its shorter primary narrative from surrounding context.
- *The Baal Cycle*: Ugaritic divine kingship and sea/death conflict, with
  attention to substantial gaps in the tablets.
- *The Ramayana*: exile, Sita, Hanuman, Ravana, and transformations of the
  tradition; identify the edition or regional tradition being told.
- *Mahabharata*: dynastic conflict, the dice game, ethical dilemmas, war,
  and aftermath; divide into coherent listening units.
- *Classic of Poetry* (Shijing): selected poems in the social worlds in
  which they were gathered and interpreted; do not pretend one long plot.
- *Zhuangzi*: linked narrative arguments and thought experiments, with
  careful distinctions among chapters and transmission histories.
- *Popol Wuj*: K'iche' Maya creation and Hero Twins, transmitted in a
  colonial-period manuscript containing precolonial traditions.
- The Hebrew *Book of Job*: suffering, justice, the friends' arguments,
  and literary form.

**Later traditions to incorporate when the ancient strand grows:**
Sundiata, the *Shahnameh*, and Norse poetry. They are not all ancient works
in their surviving literary forms; that chronology should be stated plainly.

## Editorial design

1. Tell the primary story with enough scene and character detail to stand on
   its own. Analyze the decisions and effects rather than only enumerating
   plot points.
2. Name the source version used. Compare variants after the listener has a
   coherent version in mind. Do not invent missing manuscript content.
3. Make cultural, political, and religious context part of the explanation
   without presenting the text as a literal historical chronicle.
4. Treat different traditions on their own terms before attempting
   cross-cultural comparisons. Similar motifs alone do not establish
   transmission.
5. Use a substantial audio-first length supported by the material, generally
   30-50 minutes, with readable prose and no production or authoring remarks
   inside the spoken markers.
6. Keep links, source metadata, pronunciation notes, and proposed extensions
   outside the spoken region. Existing mathematics files remain untouched.
7. Avoid spoken episode-order references so any lecture can be selected first.

## Comparative lectures to consider after the works are understood

- The land of the dead: Inanna, Gilgamesh, Egyptian funerary literature,
  and the Greek underworld, with contrasts given as much care as parallels.
- Flood narratives: *Atrahasis*, *Gilgamesh*, Genesis, and the evidence for
  their separate composition and transmission.
- Heroic fame and mortality: Gilgamesh and Achilles, starting from the
  specific scenes and values in each literature.
- The story of creation and legitimate rule: *Enuma Elish* and other ancient
  accounts, without forcing every tradition into a uniform framework.
