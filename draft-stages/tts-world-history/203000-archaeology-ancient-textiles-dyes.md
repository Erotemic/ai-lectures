# Threads, Colors, and Invisible Labor: How Archaeologists Read Ancient Textiles

A piece of blue cotton survives in an ancient mound on the coast of Peru. It is small enough to fit in a hand. Nobody has written a label identifying the person who spun the thread, collected the plant that supplied the color, or chose the pattern. But an archaeologist can examine how the threads cross. A chemist can remove a tiny sample and identify a molecule whose structure still survives. The excavation can establish that the cloth came from a layer placed there six thousand years ago. Together these observations turn a fragment of fabric into evidence of ancient technology.

That sounds straightforward, until we ask what any one observation really means. A molecular match tells us that a dye was present. It does not name the person who prepared it. A radiocarbon date may help establish the age of a deposit, while leaving questions about when an object was made and how it reached that deposit. A weaving pattern reveals a sequence of decisions; the maker's intentions remain harder to establish. And there is a much larger difficulty: for most places and periods, nearly every piece of cloth has vanished.

We are going to examine the archaeology of fabric through three cases. First, cotton and blue dye at Huaca Prieta in Peru. Second, dyed fibers found in the copper-working landscape of Timna in the southern Levant. Third, textiles preserved by salt at Hallstatt in the Austrian Alps. Along the way we will ask what spinning, weaving, dyes, wear marks, experimental reconstructions, and laboratory instruments can reveal. We will credit the researchers responsible for these discoveries and, as far as the evidence allows, the people whose hands made the cloth.

## The missing majority

Textiles matter to historical interpretation in a way that is easy to overlook. Clothing regulates temperature, marks status, enables work, and communicates social relationships. Fiber is also used in bags, sails, ropes, fishing nets, carrying straps, bedding, curtains, bandages, and wrappings for the dead. Ancient economies depended on growing, collecting, tending, preparing, spinning, dyeing, and weaving fibers. Many of these tasks consumed enormous amounts of time. A palace may survive as masonry while the fabric that furnished it is reduced to dust. An ancient port can leave stone quays and bronze fittings while the sails have disappeared. If we reconstruct a society from durable materials alone, we see a distorted version of its productive life.

Plant fibers and wool are usually broken down by microbes, insects, chemical reactions, and mechanical damage. Survival is more likely in exceptionally dry places, waterlogged environments with limited oxygen, frozen deposits, and some salt-rich contexts. Burning may sometimes leave a charred impression. Corroding metal can preserve the impression of a textile touching it, even after the original fibers have been replaced by mineral deposits. Pottery can retain impressions made by cords or baskets against wet clay. Archaeologists therefore investigate direct textile fragments and indirect traces: spindle whorls, loom weights, needles, shears, stains, impressions, and images of dressed people.

Each class of evidence answers a different question. A spindle whorl suggests the production of twisted thread, but its presence does not establish what fiber was spun or how much was produced. A loom weight may help reconstruct how threads were held under tension; it cannot tell us the pattern of every finished cloth. A textile impression may preserve weave structure but not color. Depictions on painted pottery may emphasize ceremonial clothing, while everyday work clothes are scarcely shown. The historical picture emerges through comparison, rather than one artifact being allowed to stand for an entire industry.

## How thread becomes a technical record

Start with a single fiber. Cotton grows as seed hairs. Flax yields long fibers from its stem. Wool comes from animals, and other plant fibers require different harvesting and preparation. A laboratory may identify material by characteristic cellular structure under a microscope. Sometimes degraded fibers only permit a broader identification. Conservators must decide whether sampling will damage a rare object; even the preparation of a microscope slide is an intervention in the evidence.

Spinning aligns fibers and twists them into yarn. The direction of twist can be recorded conventionally as S or Z, named for the way a diagonal resembles the middle stroke of those letters. Two or more yarns can be plied together, sometimes with an opposite twist. These observations can survive when color and softness do not. If you have ever untwisted a rope, you know why this is informative: the arrangement of fibers reflects the mechanical decisions that made the thread stable. A piece of spun yarn is an artifact of motion.

Weaving introduces another set of choices. Warp threads run lengthwise in a woven fabric, while weft threads pass across them. In a basic plain weave the weft alternates over and under the warp. A twill shifts the crossings to produce a diagonal pattern. But some ancient fabrics were made by twining: elements are twisted around other threads rather than passed through a conventional weave. The construction is visible in the crossings, and that construction matters when identifying a tradition of manufacture. Calling every ancient fabric simply woven can erase distinctions between skilled techniques.

An archaeologist can count threads per unit of length, measure yarn diameter, describe crossings and edges, and look for repairs. They may distinguish an original border from a tear made by excavation. They may find a repeated pattern suggesting planned manufacture rather than a piece of random cordage. None of these measurements requires knowing a maker's name. They allow different objects to be compared as products of techniques learned, practiced, and modified across generations.

## Blue at Huaca Prieta

The site of Huaca Prieta lies on Peru's northern coast. Its long occupational history includes people who gathered, fished, farmed, built, and deposited materials in a changing coastal environment. Early research at the site was carried out in the twentieth century; later investigations by teams including Tom Dillehay and collaborators examined its deep sequence. Textile fragments recovered from the site were especially important because the coastal preservation conditions allowed evidence that would usually have decayed to survive.

In 2016, Jeffrey Splitstoser, Tom Dillehay, Jan Wouters, and Ana Claro published a study called *Early pre-Hispanic use of indigo blue in Peru*. Their analysis concerns cotton textile fragments, including examples dating to about six thousand years ago. The team described twined structures and blue-colored yarns in these fragments. The physical construction could be studied directly, but the identification of the color required chemistry. Blue-looking fibers do not by themselves identify a particular dye. Light, dirt, conservation, and aging can change apparent color, and different substances may produce similar shades.

The researchers used high-performance liquid chromatography with photodiode-array detection, abbreviated HPLC-PDA. Imagine first dissolving a minute sample of colored material under controlled laboratory conditions. The instrument moves the extracted mixture through a column. Different compounds interact with the column and travel through it at different rates; a detector then records how strongly separated components absorb light across different wavelengths. The result is a series of peaks, each with a retention time and spectral characteristics. A peak becomes meaningful when compared with appropriate reference materials and known dye chemistry.

In the Peruvian samples the team identified indigotin, a key component of indigo blue. They interpreted this as the oldest chemically confirmed example of indigo dye then known. The determination is stronger than saying the thread looks blue. It connects a surviving molecule to a class of dyeing practice. But a finding of indigotin does not automatically identify the exact plant species used, the precise preparation recipe, or the location where dyeing occurred. Those further questions require other evidence. The paper suggested native South American plants capable of producing indigo as possible sources; the chemical result itself established the dye component.

Dating deserves similar care. The researchers connected the pieces to stratigraphic contexts and radiocarbon determinations from the excavated sequence. When an archaeologist says a textile is approximately six thousand years old, the statement condenses excavation records, laboratory dating, and an argument linking the sample to those dated deposits. A date is not stamped on the cloth. We should ask whether the dated material came from the object, a secure associated deposit, or an older piece reused later. In this case the site's documented contexts are part of the basis for the claim.

What can we infer about the people who made the cloth? They cultivated or obtained cotton, prepared fibers, made yarn, organized a twined structure, selected color placement, and acquired practical knowledge of dyeing. Their technology required sustained skill. Their exact identities, social standing, and reasons for depositing the pieces remain beyond the reach of the chemical chromatogram. It would be a mistake to call the surviving textiles mere decoration. They are records of time spent learning and producing complex material objects.

## Why making a dye is difficult

A useful experiment is to imagine trying to color a length of cotton. Simply rubbing blue plant leaves onto the surface may stain it briefly without producing a durable blue. Indigo dyeing commonly requires a sequence of chemical transformations. The colored compound is not readily soluble in water in its familiar form. Dye makers use chemical conditions that can convert the material into a soluble reduced form, allowing it to penetrate fibers; exposure to air can then restore the blue pigment. Actual ancient procedures varied, and the exact Huaca Prieta recipe is unknown.

This detail changes our interpretation of the artifact. Identification of a durable dye can indicate familiarity with a multistep process, even when we cannot reconstruct every step. Chemical knowledge need not have been expressed in modern vocabulary. Repeated experimentation, apprenticeship, and careful observation can produce effective procedures without a theory of electrons. A group that knew how to maintain a dye bath had technical expertise. Historical accounts that reserve technology for stone construction or metallurgy have left out important forms of practiced chemistry.

Researchers often reproduce ancient techniques experimentally. They may compare yarn twist, fiber damage, or color compounds after dyeing with candidate plants and treatments. Experiments test whether a proposed process is feasible and what traces it leaves. They do not by themselves establish that ancient craftspeople used exactly that recipe. More than one method can yield a similar chemical signature. Archaeology can reveal the range of plausible processes and sometimes eliminate alternatives, even when it cannot identify one unique sequence of actions.

## Purple beside the copper furnaces

Now travel thousands of miles and several millennia forward to Timna, a region of ancient copper production in the southern Levant. Its landscape contains mining features, smelting debris, workshops, and camps associated with intensive metallurgical activity. A visitor might reasonably expect an archaeological lecture about ore, furnaces, and slag. Yet this industrial landscape also preserved scraps of textile. The dryness of the environment protected materials that would normally disappear.

In a 2021 paper, Naama Sukenik, David Iluz, Zohar Amar, Alexander Varvak, Orit Shamir, and Erez Ben-Yosef reported identifying fragments of fibers dyed with genuine purple derived from marine snails. Their samples came from excavations of a copper-smelting site at Timna dated to the late eleventh and early tenth centuries BCE. The find was significant because dyed fibers of this type had rarely survived from that period in the southern Levant. But the underlying scientific result is more specific than a broad claim about kings, kingdoms, or who owned the mines.

The investigators applied chromatography to colored fibers and identified brominated indigo compounds. Two markers were particularly important: monobromoindigotin and dibromoindigotin. These are characteristic of dyes made from certain marine snails. The team compared the archaeological chemical patterns with controlled modern dyeing experiments. Because the molecules were identifiable, this was a much more secure assessment than naming purple from the appearance of aged fibers. In principle one could dye a thread purple using other materials. The molecular combination points to a particular category of source.

Think about the journey implied by such a fragment. Copper smelting at Timna took place inland, in a desert landscape. Snails capable of supplying the dye were marine animals, obtained at the coast. The presence of shellfish-derived dye at the industrial site indicates a link, direct or indirect, between coastal resources and people in the mining region. It does not establish a single trade route or identify the clothing's original wearer. The fabric may have reached the site already dyed, and many exchanges could intervene between harvest and final deposition.

The investigators considered the quality of the textiles and their context in relation to questions about social organization. Prestigious dye was a costly product in many ancient settings, and finding it among the materials of an industrial site challenges a picture of mine workers as a population without access to valued goods. But three fibers cannot tell us the distribution of luxury throughout a society. A rare scrap could come from an elite visitor, a worker with a special role, a transported garment, or a piece reused in a different task. Interpreting status requires comparing the textile with settlement organization, other artifacts, and the broader regional context.

Notice the difference between two kinds of identification. Chemistry can support a strong answer to the question, what dye did these fibers contain? Social history asks, who wore them, who produced them, who paid for them, and why did they end up here? Those latter questions require more than a chromatogram. The technical identification creates a valuable starting point. It does not eliminate the work of interpretation.

## Cloth preserved inside salt

Our third case takes us to the Hallstatt salt mines in Austria. Salt can preserve organic materials in ways that ordinary soil does not. Mining debris sealed in the workings contains tools and other objects associated with past mining, including textile fragments. The association with salt extraction offers opportunities to study clothing, practical fabrics, and working conditions rather than only ceremonial textiles recovered from burials.

Research by Karina Grömer, Hans Reschreiter, Margarita Gleba, and collaborators has examined the construction and chronology of textiles associated with the salt mines. Their work combines fiber identification, weave analysis, archaeological context, and dating. In a 2020 study, Grömer, Gleba, Mathieu Boudin, and Reschreiter revisited the dates of linen twills from Hallstatt. That research is a reminder that a label assigned decades ago may change when more precise questions are asked about the fabric and its context.

Picture a recovered strip with diagonal crossings. Identifying a twill can tell us something about the technology required to make the cloth. Looking for edges, wear, and repairs may suggest reuse or the sorts of activities to which fabric was exposed. If a textile has been repurposed as a tie or protective covering, its last archaeological use may differ from the purpose for which it was originally woven. These successive lives matter. The object found in a mine need not have been manufactured specifically for mining.

Dating can be complicated by reuse and by the relation between the textile and a mining deposit. A radiocarbon date on a fiber informs us about when the plant or animal was alive, not necessarily the last day the fabric was worn. In a long-lived mining complex, objects can be moved, discarded, and buried in different phases. Direct dating, associated finds, and stratigraphy should be compared. If two lines disagree, the disagreement is a reason to reexamine assumptions about deposition and reuse.

Hallstatt also shows the value of comparing scientific measurements with the grammar of craft. A person skilled at textile analysis may recognize a pattern of interlacing that a general excavator misses. A conservator may understand how salts affected fibers and the treatment required to prevent deterioration. A radiocarbon specialist may identify a chronological problem. The resulting interpretation has many authors, even when an exhibition label names only the excavation director.

## How a fragment enters an argument

Let us build a simplified evidence chain. An excavator documents a fragment in a secure deposit. The context is dated using suitable material, and the relationship of the dates to the object is examined. A textile specialist describes the fibers, the yarn twist, the structure, and any repairs. A chemist analyzes an appropriately small sample and identifies a dye compound using reference standards and instrumental measurements. Conservators record the object's condition and the effects of treatment. A historian compares the result with other objects and with written or visual evidence where such sources exist.

At each step we can ask what might go wrong. A textile could be intrusive in a disturbed layer. Modern handling could introduce contamination. A dying dye molecule might be difficult to distinguish from a related compound. A comparison with a reference plant might identify a family of possible sources rather than one species. The absence of similar cloth elsewhere may reflect preservation and excavation practices rather than genuine rarity. Different specialists must confront different sources of error.

The tests can be surprisingly concrete. If a chromatogram shows a dye marker, repeat the analysis of a second tiny fragment, when sampling policy permits. Compare a blank processed alongside the artifact. Check that the spectrum and retention time agree with known compounds. If a proposed weave reconstruction fails when tensioned on a loom, reconsider the arrangement of the threads. If associated dates conflict, examine how the layers accumulated. The reason for confidence in a claim is the chain of independently inspectable work behind it.

There is also a problem of scale. A single dyed strand establishes that somebody used a certain technology at some time and place. A hundred fragments distributed among households can help reveal how common the technology was. Repeated finds across many settlements may illuminate distribution and exchange. But preservation varies so much that the number of surviving pieces is not a direct count of original production. Archaeologists need explicitly comparative sampling before arguing that one society wore more colorful cloth than another.

## Credit and the history of interpretation

Historical attribution in textile archaeology is especially difficult because the original makers' names rarely survive. We can name some of the researchers: Jeffrey Splitstoser, Tom Dillehay, Jan Wouters, and Ana Claro for the Peruvian dye study; Naama Sukenik, David Iluz, Zohar Amar, Alexander Varvak, Orit Shamir, and Erez Ben-Yosef for the Timna purple identification; Karina Grömer and colleagues for the analysis and redating of Hallstatt textiles. Their articles also depend on field staff, conservators, and technical specialists whose contributions should be consulted in full research reports.

Ancient women and men may have divided textile tasks in ways that varied across communities. Evidence from some historical settings associates spinning and weaving strongly with women's labor, but the division cannot be generalized mechanically to every prehistoric site. An unidentified piece of cloth cannot prove the sex, age, or social identity of its maker. Crediting ancient labor therefore means describing the skills and material processes that the object demonstrates, and resisting the urge to invent a biography of an anonymous craftsperson.

Consider how different this is from the history of stone monuments. A royal inscription may claim that a ruler built a temple, even though countless workers made it possible. A textile fragment often has no ruler's name at all. We encounter technical achievement directly, before we encounter its official sponsor. A thread was drawn out, a spindle turned, a pattern was maintained, a dye bath was prepared. Those actions remain legible even when personal names do not.

## What the colors tell us

Return to the blue cotton from Huaca Prieta. The investigators can describe its construction, identify indigotin, and establish a credible ancient context. We can say that people on Peru's coast possessed sophisticated textile and dyeing technology long before European contact. We cannot say that they used a particular modern recipe, wore the cloth at a specified ceremony, or learned the technique from a faraway civilization merely because similar colors appear elsewhere.

Return to Timna. The presence of brominated dye compounds on fibers supports the identification of murex-derived purple. The coastal origin of the source material gives us a reason to investigate movement and exchange. The social meaning of the fabric requires separate arguments about context and comparison. Return to Hallstatt. The preserved structure of cloth and its archaeological setting reveal technical practices, repair, reuse, and the long lives of everyday objects, even as redating may alter the chronology assigned to some pieces.

All three cases recover a part of history usually lost with the decomposition of organic matter. A textile is an intricate record of labor. Its surviving molecules and threads tell us what materials were handled, what techniques were understood, and which decisions can still be reconstructed. They offer an important corrective to histories composed entirely of battles, palaces, and named rulers. Archaeology can recover the achievements of people whose names no surviving document records. And it can explain precisely how those achievements came to be known.
