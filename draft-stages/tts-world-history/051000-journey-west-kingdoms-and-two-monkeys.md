# Journey to the West, Part IV: The Kingdoms of Desire and the Two Monkey Kings

Two monkeys stand before the Buddha. Each is a perfect likeness of Sun Wukong, the stone-born hero whose strength and cunning have made him the most formidable member of a Buddhist pilgrimage. Each claims to be the true disciple. Each accuses the other of stealing his identity. Both wield the same staff, know the same tricks, and display the same outrage. If the wrong one returns to the scripture monk, the expedition may continue under the control of an impostor. If the real one is condemned, a loyal guardian may be destroyed while the deceiver escapes.

Before that moment, the case has passed through other courts and realms. The two Monkeys have been too alike for ordinary investigators to distinguish. Their dispute is at once a puzzle of supernatural identification and an unsettling vision of what it means for a person to be split into rival versions. The Buddha eventually identifies the false figure as a six-eared macaque. The true Monkey kills him. The solution closes the public dispute, but it leaves readers with an image that can scarcely be forgotten: the most dangerous opponent of the Monkey King looks exactly like the Monkey King.

This episode comes late in the stretch of *Journey to the West* that we will explore here, chapters forty-three through sixty-one of the hundred-chapter Ming Chinese novel. The pilgrimage party consists of Tripitaka, a human Buddhist monk charged with obtaining scriptures; Sun Wukong, his immensely powerful monkey guardian; Zhu Bajie, a piglike companion prone to appetite and complaint; Sha Wujing, a steadier attendant; and a dragon prince transformed into the monk's white horse. The historical Xuanzang really did travel toward India in the seventh century. The episodes of magic, impersonation, and impossible kingdoms grew in storytelling traditions that culminated in a late-sixteenth-century printed novel.

The road through these chapters is full of things that seem to promise safety or satisfaction: a kingdom where religious leaders hold power, a river community eager for its children to live, a woman who wishes the monk to marry her, a magical duplicate who claims to be the rightful disciple, and a fan that will cool a mountain of flame. Underneath the adventure runs a persistent question: how can the travelers act wisely when their desires and their ability to recognize the world are being tested at the same time?

## Rivers that exact a price

In the Black Water River, a demon seizes the monk. The attacker is connected to the aquatic dragon world, where kinship, official standing, and the consequences of earlier offenses complicate the distinction between monster and divine servant. Monkey seeks help from a dragon prince, and the enemy is brought under control. The episode is comparatively brief, but its setting is characteristic. A river that looks like a physical obstacle belongs to an inhabited supernatural jurisdiction. Its residents have relatives and patrons, and the solution may require negotiation with their network rather than simply beating an anonymous beast.

The travelers later come to the River of Heaven, also called the Sky-Spanning River in translations. The people living beside it face a terrible demand. A powerful spirit who occupies the waters requires the sacrifice of children. The community has been drawn into a recurring bargain under which the lives of the young are offered in the hope of protection or relief. The pilgrims arrive at a moment when the cost is about to fall on a particular family.

Monkey and Pigsy take the places of the threatened children through transformation. The story has room for jokes about their disguises, but the danger to the children remains serious. The spirit is a fishlike being with supernatural powers. He tries to capture Tripitaka and make use of the monk's body, and the contest finally requires Guanyin's intervention. The bodhisattva appears with a basket and recovers the spirit, who is associated with her own sphere.

The rescue is satisfying from the point of view of the children and their families. The cause of the terror, however, raises another uncomfortable question. Why did a being connected with a compassionate sacred figure become a predator demanding human offerings? The novel does not provide a modern inquiry into administrative responsibility. It presents a cosmology in which divine households are sufficiently vast that creatures can leave them and cause disasters. That detail helps explain the plot while also providing material for satire of officials who fail to supervise their own dependents.

The river itself becomes a prolonged ordeal. Water, cold, and the difficulty of moving the human monk matter even when his companions can work wonders. In some tellings the ice tempts the group to make a crossing that turns dangerous. The need to protect the vulnerable traveler prevents the party's powerful members from treating the landscape as a sequence of obstacles that can be vaulted with a single somersault.

## A kingdom that persecutes monks

In the Kingdom of Cart Slow, usually called Chechi, the conflict is openly religious and political. Daoist masters have gained extraordinary influence at court and Buddhist monks have been forced into humiliating labor. The pilgrims encounter these suffering monks and become involved in contests staged under royal supervision. The three principal opponents are often called Tiger Strength, Deer Strength, and Antelope Strength. They represent a courtly form of religious power supported by miraculous performance and institutional favor.

Monkey first plays pranks at a Daoist temple, mocking the authorities' ritual dignity. Later the competition becomes a series of spectacular public demonstrations. The contestants seek rain, test meditative or magical abilities, and submit to terrifying trials involving bodily injury and restoration. Monkey repeatedly exposes the rivals' limitations while using his own shape-changing to tilt the contests in his favor. The outcome destroys the false masters' authority and frees the oppressed Buddhist monks.

To reduce the episode to a declaration that Buddhism defeats Daoism would miss the novel's broader texture. The hero himself learned techniques from a master bearing Daoist associations. Guanyin, the Buddha, Laozi, and celestial officials occupy an interconnected story world. The targets in Chechi are specific religious specialists who have acquired power through the king and used it to persecute others. The novel can participate in religious competition while also making fun of the political arrangements that turn rulers into patrons of cruelty.

The king's role deserves attention. He grants prestige to those who promise rain and protection. When their power appears effective, he allows them authority over rival practitioners. Religious claims become instruments of government. The contest exposes how easily rulers can confuse a convincing display with moral reliability. The masters' defeat is possible because Monkey can rival or exceed their demonstrations. Yet the larger harm was enabled by the decision to let favored specialists decide how other people should live.

## The ring that collects every weapon

In another mountain region the party encounters a dangerous creature who possesses an implement capable of drawing away weapons and magical treasures. Monkeys, gods, and other allies find their equipment useless as it is seized. The enemy is associated with a blue or green ox, and the controlling ring is connected with Laozi's household. Monkey discovers that ordinary escalation will not work. Calling stronger allies only supplies the monster with more things to steal.

This episode is amusing partly because it punctures the logic of the grand rescue. When a hero cannot defeat an opponent, he summons an even greater figure; the figure arrives armed with a miraculous device; the device is promptly taken. The mountain becomes a warehouse of captured advantages. The solution depends on tracing the implement's origin and recovering the right kind of authority over it.

The story again makes objects a source of power that can migrate away from their intended users. A sacred treasure can threaten the world when separated from the discipline or ownership that once controlled it. The ring also supplies a formal lesson about adaptation: an enemy who defeats equipment by the same method each time cannot be overcome simply by supplying more of that equipment. Monkey must revise the kind of solution he is attempting.

These episodes provide a rhythm between satire and spiritual pressure. Courts are vulnerable to charlatans. Monsters use prestigious relics. The pilgrimage party must sometimes ask for help from the very heavens whose negligent attendants created the crisis. None of this erases the reality of cruelty experienced by ordinary people in the story. The false Daoist masters' forced labor and the river spirit's demand for children make the joke about officials morally consequential.

## The river of pregnancy

The travelers enter a region where a peculiar river can cause pregnancy in those who drink its water. Tripitaka and Pigsy become afflicted, an event that produces comic panic as well as bodily fear. They seek water from another source that will reverse the condition. The scene turns an experience conventionally associated with women into an ordeal for male pilgrims. Pigsy, whose desires frequently center on eating and marriage, discovers that bodily consequences can arise in ways he has never anticipated.

Nearby is a kingdom governed by women, with a queen who wishes to marry Tripitaka. The proposal is apparently attractive: a throne, comfort, safety, and a spouse who sincerely wants the monk to stay. It also directly conflicts with the vow of celibacy and the purpose of the pilgrimage. Monkey engineers an escape, making use of the court's own desires and expectations.

One of the strengths of this sequence is that the ruler's proposal has emotional credibility. The queen is more than a mechanical seductress standing at the side of the road. She governs a state, recognizes the monk's beauty or worth, and imagines a future in which he remains. Tripitaka must refuse the apparent rewards of social life without treating the human feelings behind them as meaningless. A reader may sympathize with the queen even while understanding why the monk departs.

The portrayal also raises questions about gender in the novel's world. A kingdom governed by women becomes an occasion for fantasy, anxiety, and jokes about marriage. It reflects assumptions and possibilities circulating in the literary culture that produced the work. It is valuable to ask how the women's political authority is represented and whether their community is granted a comparable interior life to the male travelers. The story is entertaining, but its structure gives a modern reader reasons to think about which kinds of desire are rendered comic and whose freedom the plot protects.

An even more dangerous temptation follows in the form of a scorpion spirit who abducts the monk and tries to force a union. She can injure powerful beings with her venomous sting. The conflict moves from a political offer the monk must decline to a coercive assault against which he needs protection. The two stories should not be treated as identical. A voluntary marriage proposal that conflicts with a vow and an abduction are morally different situations, even if both are organized around the question of whether the monk will remain celibate.

The sequence makes the meaning of a religious commitment more concrete. Renunciation is not merely a private feeling of detachment. It requires a continuing practice in circumstances where other people may have sincere hopes, unreasonable demands, or violent intentions. The monk is not the strongest person on the road. Keeping his vow often depends on others recognizing its value and helping him preserve the conditions under which he can act freely.

## A proposal, an abduction, and the meaning of a vow

The Women's Kingdom is one of the episodes most likely to change its tone depending on the reteller. It can be played as comedy about male travelers baffled by a society that does not organize itself around men. It can be made into a romance in which a ruler recognizes the monk's sincerity and offers him a future that almost persuades him to remain. It can be emphasized as an exercise in religious renunciation. The hundred-chapter novel supplies all these possibilities, but it keeps the pilgrimage moving toward the monk's refusal of marriage.

The political arrangement is as interesting as the romance. The queen possesses real authority and can offer a throne. The proposal is therefore not simply an invitation from one private person to another. To accept it would be to take a place in a state with subjects and duties, and to abandon the mission commissioned by the Tang ruler and the higher religious powers. A human institution presents a legitimate form of life that conflicts with the chosen monastic one. The difficulty is genuine because neither kingship nor marriage is inherently monstrous.

Monkey's schemes for departure can make the monk's vow seem effortless from the outside. He finds a way to move the party past the court before the queen can hold them. But the prince or queen left behind has feelings and expectations, and a return to the road may impose suffering on others. The novel usually follows the pilgrims' fortunes rather than tracing the long emotional aftermath for such characters. Readers can recognize that narrative choice without assigning to the scene a moral certainty it does not state.

The scorpion spirit then supplies a more openly predatory counterpart. In her domain, the monk's refusal is treated as something to be overcome by force. The struggle against her is therefore not the same conflict as his refusal of a ruler's sincere proposal. The juxtaposition allows the novelist to test the boundary between desire, persuasion, and coercion. The monk's vulnerability makes that boundary consequential. His guardians may be able to resist someone capable of imprisoning him, but he cannot always secure that freedom on his own.

The pregnancy river introduces a different set of bodily anxieties. Tripitaka and Pigsy have to endure a condition they had not anticipated and seek relief from a rare source of water. The humor can be broad, yet it asks the male travelers to experience vulnerability in bodies they had assumed were organized in a predictable way. Pigsy's endless interest in what can be consumed has led to a form of consequence he cannot digest away. The party's usual distinctions between the heroic guardian, the holy monk, and the glutton are interrupted by a shared need for help.

Taken together, the episodes concern more than temptation by women. They ask how a vow fits into a world where other people have projects, bodies have consequences, and power over another person's future can be exercised through love, authority, or force. The story's comic handling is often rooted in the gender conventions of its literary culture. Those conventions deserve notice, especially where the male protagonists' freedom is given more sustained attention than the women who confront them.

## When Monkey leaves for the second time

A renewed confrontation with bandits puts the monk and Monkey at odds. Wukong kills men who threaten them. Tripitaka recoils from the loss of life, and their quarrel ends in another dismissal. The conflict recalls the White Bone Demon episode, but the circumstances are different. A human bandit does not become a supernatural monster merely because he is dangerous. Monkey's ease in destroying enemies raises the moral stakes of being his master.

Then the situation becomes stranger. Someone looking exactly like Monkey appears and assaults the monk, takes possessions associated with the journey, and claims to be the rightful pilgrim. The genuine Monkey seeks to establish his innocence. The impostor has imitated his appearance, his magical equipment, and his intentions so thoroughly that ordinary recognition fails.

The pair travel among authorities in search of judgment. Even figures accustomed to supervising spirits cannot easily distinguish them. The contest dramatizes the breakdown of a simple criterion for identity. If two beings look alike, speak alike, fight alike, and each claims the same memories, which one is the companion who made the original vow? In an ordinary legal case a person might seek witnesses, physical traces, or a history that cannot be copied. The novel deprives its investigators of these tools to intensify the puzzle.

At last the Buddha identifies the other as the six-eared macaque, a being with powers of comprehension and imitation. The true Wukong kills the false one. Afterward the pilgrimage continues with the original guardian restored. The religious interpretation suggested by the episode's chapter titles is especially rich. Two minds have arisen where one person should possess an undivided resolve. A rebellious, clever, violent Monkey must confront a rival whose most frightening quality is that he embodies the same capacities without a trustworthy relation to the pilgrimage.

It would be too simple to declare that the false Monkey is merely the hero's evil side and stop there. Within the plot he is an independent supernatural adversary whose deception harms the party. The symbolic reading and the literal adventure reinforce one another without canceling each other. The scene asks how to separate a legitimate self from an imitation when the distinction cannot be read off outward behavior alone. It also reopens the question of how the monk can know whom he trusts. The person capable of saving him may be indistinguishable from someone capable of attacking him.

The Buddha's judgment resolves the fictional trial through a kind of superior knowledge. In real human disputes, such knowledge is rarely available. The force of the story comes partly from making the investigators' failure intelligible. Their powers are formidable, but their criteria are insufficient. A hierarchy of supernatural perception can eventually settle the case, while the reader is left remembering the terrifying resemblance.

## The mountain that burns

The travelers now face Flaming Mountain. Its fires block the road. The landscape is not merely hot or inconvenient; the flames make passage impossible by ordinary means and threaten everyone who lives nearby. To cool it, Monkey must obtain the plantain fan possessed by Princess Iron Fan, also called Rakshasi in many translations. She has reason to dislike him, because he helped subdue Red Boy, her son. He comes seeking a favor from someone whose family has already suffered because of his intervention.

The story sets up a struggle of negotiation, deceit, and transformation. Monkey asks for the fan and is refused. He tries to force the issue, discovers that the fan's powers can throw him far away, and eventually uses disguises to obtain something that may or may not be the genuine object. The fan is not simply a magical key whose owner can be robbed in one scene. Its power becomes entangled with family relations and the reputation of the hero himself.

The Bull Demon King, Red Boy's father and Iron Fan's husband, enters the conflict. He and Monkey have a history of association from the monkey's earlier days, when he cultivated powerful sworn brothers. That older bond no longer guarantees friendly conduct. The Bull Demon King has his own desires, relationships, and grievances. The parties change shape, spy on one another, recover the fan, lose it, and fight again. Pigsy contributes to the struggle rather than merely standing aside. The conflict eventually draws in other powerful forces and concludes with the means to put out the flames.

This is one of the novel's greatest extended action episodes, but it has a strong moral architecture. The mountain is an impersonal hazard for the people who inhabit the region. The solution depends on a private family whose grievance against Monkey is real. The mission may be holy, yet the travelers are not free of responsibility for how their previous actions have affected others. Monkey's relationship with Red Boy matters when he comes asking Red Boy's mother for help. An earlier victory has become a later obstacle.

The fan itself embodies the problem of proportion. A small object can govern a vast landscape. A single person may therefore hold power over the safety of many people who have never met her. Ownership, resentment, and communal need are drawn into conflict. Monkey can fight, but fighting his way to the fan risks turning the owners' motives still more hostile. He must understand the people who control what he needs.

## Three attempts to borrow a fan

The Flaming Mountain story rewards a closer account of the repeated negotiations. Monkey's first approach is to ask Princess Iron Fan for the object that can cool the flames. She knows who he is and recalls what happened to her son, Red Boy. His plea for the safety of the road does not remove her grievance. The refusal is therefore tied to an earlier incident in the same large novel, and her magic can send the visitor hurtling away. The hero has to learn that an old family relation can be a stronger obstacle than the physical mountain.

He then turns to deception. The novel lets the fan change hands through imitation, cunning, and contests of recognition. Monkey adopts a disguise suited to gaining access, while the family catches on and answers with tricks of its own. The sequence is sometimes described as the three borrowings of the plantain fan. The word borrowing becomes comic because the owner is resisting, the visitor is deceitful, and everyone understands that a request made by an invincible warrior may conceal coercion.

The Bull Demon King complicates the exchange. He was among Monkey's former associates before the pilgrimage altered Monkey's loyalties. That history gives both men a claim to remembered friendship, but neither can make the old relationship govern the present conflict. Red Boy's fate, the Bull Demon King's marital relationships, and the importance of the fan to the surrounding region all enter into the dispute. The fight is consequently a test of whether Monkey can resolve a problem that has been made political and familial by the consequences of his past.

The region's inhabitants provide the moral urgency. They did not take part in the family quarrel. They need the fire extinguished so that life can continue. The spectacular conflict between shape-changing warriors might otherwise become an entertaining private feud. The burning landscape keeps reminding us that a useful resolution must extend beyond deciding which fighter has won. The final cooling of the mountain makes possible a public benefit, not merely a tally of defeated enemies.

The repeated attempt is also a storytelling technique. The audience knows the object that can solve the problem from the beginning. Suspense depends on how the hero will obtain it when a simple request fails. Each later attempt can revise the terms: from direct appeal to trickery, from trickery to battle, from a personal encounter to a conflict involving a family and powerful allies. The object stays the same while the social problem becomes increasingly visible. This is one of the reasons the Flaming Mountain episode can sustain a long dramatic telling without needing an entirely new monster for every scene.

## The road cannot be crossed by force alone

Look back over the sequence from the river offerings to Flaming Mountain. At the River of Heaven, a community makes a terrible bargain with a supernatural predator. In Chechi, religious specialists gain political authority by performing miracles and use it against others. In the Women's Kingdom, a ruler's desire conflicts with a monk's commitment. The false Monkey becomes an enemy who cannot be identified by appearance. At Flaming Mountain, the key to public safety is held by a family nursing a private grievance. Each challenge has a different structure. A single technique would fail because the interests, evidence, and relationships differ.

Monkey's ingenuity becomes most valuable when he recognizes those differences. He must know when to fight, when to disguise himself, when to find the true owner of a magical instrument, when to bring a case to a higher authority, and when to confront the consequences of his former actions. Tripitaka's principles give the expedition a purpose, but they do not supply a ready-made procedure for every decision. Bajie's complaints sometimes reveal genuine difficulty even when they are selfish. Sandy's steadiness carries the group through arguments that could otherwise end the journey.

The novel's religious language often describes cultivation in terms of collecting scattered faculties, disciplining desire, and distinguishing true insight from seductive appearances. These ideas can illuminate the episode of two Monkeys, but the broader sequence resists a purely private psychology. Villagers face sacrifice; monks are enslaved; a queen makes a serious offer; an angry mother refuses a request. The characters act in social worlds with consequences for others. Any interpretation of personal enlightenment that forgets those worlds would miss much of the novel's force.

The historical record adds a further distinction. Xuanzang's seventh-century journey involved real rivers, mountains, political boundaries, religious institutions, and negotiations with patrons. Its history can be studied from near-contemporary travel and biographical materials. The Hundred-Chapter *Journey* is evidence for a much later imagination of travel, power, and religious learning. The burning mountain and the two Monkeys belong to that imagination. We can ask what landscapes, religious debates, and popular stories supplied material to its makers without equating a fictional kingdom with a securely identified stop on Xuanzang's route.

After the fan cools the flames, the party resumes its movement west. The travelers have faced monsters who possess the treasures of the gods and a demon who possessed the face of their closest companion. They have encountered rulers who want their help and rulers whose authority has enabled oppression. They have survived because they were sometimes strong, sometimes clever, and sometimes able to ask for aid. More painfully, they have survived while knowing that they cannot always distinguish loyalty from imitation or compassion from a cleverly prepared trap.

The image of the two Monkeys remains with us. The novel identifies the impostor and allows the hero to continue. But the deeper danger of that resemblance is not resolved by the death of one magical animal. A person can have a name, a reputation, and an extraordinary capacity for action without possessing a settled moral direction. Monkey is learning to commit himself to a task that constrains his appetites and asks him to protect beings weaker than himself. The struggle against his likeness brings that unfinished work into the open. The road west gives him more chances to finish it.

## Source and editorial notes

Narrative boundary: chapters 43–61. Principal episodes include Black Water River, Chechi/Cart Slow Kingdom, the River of Heaven and its fish spirit, the ox's capturing ring, the pregnancy river and Women's Kingdom, the scorpion spirit, the true and false Monkey Kings, and Flaming Mountain with Princess Iron Fan and the Bull Demon King. Meanings of religious allegories are interpretive; the actual six-eared macaque is identified within the narrative as a particular opponent. Place-names follow common English renderings where spellings vary.
