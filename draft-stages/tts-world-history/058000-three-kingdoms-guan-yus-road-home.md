# Three Kingdoms, Part IV: Guan Yu's Road Home and the Succession in Jiangdong

A warrior sits at the table of a powerful lord who has given him shelter, a fine horse, honors, and costly gifts. He knows his host has enemies. He also knows that the person he considers his elder brother may be among them. To accept a reward is easy. To accept it without surrendering a prior obligation is far more difficult. Guan Yu, whose long beard and great blade have already earned him a reputation, will have to decide when gratitude ends and allegiance begins. His decision creates one of the enduring moral set pieces in *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*.

The novel is a later telling of the civil wars that brought down the Han dynasty. We can use it to follow a historical world, but we should not mistake its imagined conversations or road adventures for stenographic records. The story of Guan Yu's journey through a sequence of guarded passes is famous precisely because it turns a complex conflict of loyalties into action that a listener can remember. We will follow that journey, the conspiracy at the imperial court, and the death of Sun Ce in the south. The connecting problem is what gives personal loyalty its authority when the state can no longer guarantee agreements.

## The court's hidden resistance

Emperor Xian has been moved under Cao Cao's protection. The court still performs official acts, but Cao Cao is the commanding power behind them. A group of officials bound by an imperial message seeks to eliminate him. The plot involves Dong Cheng and eventually draws in other men who have reasons to fear Cao Cao's position. Some conspirators are moved by loyalty to the Han; others may be hoping to recover power from a rival faction. The novel stages their danger through secrecy and urgent promises. A command concealed in fabric is a strange substitute for a court that can issue orders freely.

The conspiracy is betrayed. Cao Cao discovers evidence, investigates participants, and executes those he identifies as enemies. In the story a physician attempts to poison him, and an imperial consort connected to the plot is killed. These scenes sharpen the sense that the emperor lives in his own household without control over it. Officials who claim to obey his commands can be treated as traitors by the man who administers the realm. Whatever practical order Cao Cao is restoring, the political center is divided against itself.

Liu Bei's involvement becomes dangerous. He has received imperial recognition and has a claim to belong to the Han family. Cao Cao has already treated him as a potential rival during the famous wine-table conversation. Liu Bei takes an opportunity to leave the court, moving into Xu Province under the pretext of military duties. He then turns against Cao Cao's arrangements in the area. The venture does not hold. Cao Cao marches against him, his forces scatter, and Liu Bei flees toward Yuan Shao. Zhang Fei is separated from his brothers. Guan Yu is left defending a city where members of Liu Bei's household are present.

The story has brought the peach-garden brothers to a test more difficult than fighting together. They cannot consult or coordinate. One has fled to a northern rival, one has disappeared amid the defeat, and one must protect vulnerable relatives while facing overwhelming force. The oath that bound them has no organization capable of enforcing it. Each man must infer what fidelity requires from the information available to him.

## An enemy whose offer must be heard

Cao Cao wants Guan Yu captured but also wants his service. He recognizes the warrior's ability and calculates that generosity may succeed where intimidation would fail. Zhang Liao, an accomplished commander associated with Cao Cao's army, approaches Guan Yu and communicates possible terms. Guan Yu will not simply change sides. The novel gives him three conditions: his formal submission is to the Han emperor rather than to Cao Cao personally; the women in Liu Bei's household must be protected; and when he learns where Liu Bei is, he must be free to go to him.

The conditions are a lawyerly effort to separate two forms of obligation that Cao Cao's political arrangement has deliberately merged. Cao Cao speaks in the name of the Han, but Guan Yu refuses to interpret that fact as a command to abandon his sworn brother. He will recognize the emperor's authority without converting the emperor's most powerful minister into his permanent master. Cao Cao accepts the bargain. From his perspective it is not entirely foolish. If kindness wins a great warrior's attachment, he may gain a servant worth many regiments. If it fails, he will at least have protected the households and demonstrated a reputation for generosity.

Guan Yu enters Cao Cao's camp. The novel enjoys the detail of their relationship. Fine garments are offered. Guan Yu keeps the older clothing associated with Liu Bei beneath or close to the gifts, a visible hierarchy of commitments. An excellent horse is provided, the Red Hare once associated with Lü Bu. Guan Yu admires it, not chiefly because it confirms his status, but because it will allow him to reach his elder brother more quickly when the way opens. Cao Cao begins to understand that his own gifts may be strengthening the intention he hoped to overcome.

This is a more interesting picture of loyalty than blind refusal. Guan Yu accepts genuine obligations to a host who has treated him well. He will later return favors. But he insists that new benefits cannot cancel old promises without his consent. The novel makes the issue difficult because both men behave with forms of honor within the limits of their positions. Cao Cao has spared a formidable opponent and honored his family obligations; Guan Yu does not pretend that this hospitality was meaningless. Their disagreement concerns what consideration can rightly purchase.

## Two obligations at one table

The problem of Guan Yu's service under Cao Cao is easiest to understand if we resist describing it as a simple choice between loyalty and treachery. Guan Yu has surrendered in a situation in which Liu Bei is absent and his household has become vulnerable. Cao Cao offers protection, titles, gifts, and recognition. A person who accepts food and safety from a patron incurs obligations that cannot be dismissed merely because the patron is an enemy. Yet Guan Yu also considers himself bound to the elder brother with whom he made an earlier promise. The narrative asks whether those obligations can be fulfilled in sequence rather than allowed to cancel one another.

Cao Cao sees the difficulty. He understands that a warrior motivated only by reward can be purchased, but a warrior who considers loyalty the chief measure of his life may be more valuable even if he will eventually depart. That is why the relationship is full of gestures that could be either diplomacy or genuine admiration. Gifts of clothing and horses display wealth. Rank communicates public confidence. Feasts place two men at a table where an exchange of courtesy can temporarily obscure the fact that they may soon command opposing armies.

The famous gift of Red Hare, the horse associated with Lü Bu, is especially apt. A great horse is both a practical military asset and a symbol of the warrior's identity. Guan Yu's delight exposes the limit of Cao Cao's understanding. Instead of being won over by the horse's splendor, Guan Yu imagines how quickly it will carry him back to Liu Bei when he learns where his brother is. The gift succeeds as a measure of Guan Yu's strength and fails as a purchase of his future. Cao Cao hears that failure because Guan Yu has not disguised it.

A less generous ruler might decide to destroy a retainer whose departure is certain. Cao Cao refrains. The novel makes the act a crucial point in his moral characterization. He can be dangerous, calculating, and ruthless, but he is capable of recognizing a virtue whose immediate effect is to frustrate his ambitions. If he spared Guan Yu only because he expected a later advantage, the scene would become a calculation without residue. The narrative retains a more complicated possibility: in admiring a man who will not remain, Cao Cao is briefly moved by a standard beyond his own possession of power.

## A gift can create a debt without buying a person

Cao Cao's many gifts raise a question that remains important long after the journey ends. If an enemy treats you well while holding the power to keep you from those you love, what do you owe him? Guan Yu refuses to allow gratitude to cancel the earlier promise to Liu Bei. But he does not deny that Cao Cao has behaved generously in specific respects. The novel presents these as simultaneous judgments.

Cao Cao's act of letting Guan Yu depart is equally complex. It protects his reputation as a ruler capable of appreciating talent, and it may arise from genuine admiration. A person can have more than one reason for a decision, and the reasons can reinforce one another. Cao Cao need not possess a saint's complete selflessness to perform an act that the narrative praises. His decision is powerful because it grants an immediate advantage to a potential enemy.

The remembered debt will return at a moment when Cao Cao's life appears to lie in Guan Yu's hands. The tale makes gratitude consequential across political divisions. That design can be questioned as military policy, but its emotional claim is clear: the obligations created through humane conduct do not disappear because armies move to opposite sides of a battlefield. The conflict between such obligations and a commander's public duty is the source of the later episode's force.

## White Horse and the enemy banners

A new campaign interrupts the negotiation. Yuan Shao threatens Cao Cao in the north. His generals Yan Liang and Wen Chou are formidable opponents, and the balance of forces makes Cao Cao's position dangerous. Guan Yu is given a chance to serve in battle. At White Horse he rides against Yan Liang and kills him with extraordinary speed, a feat celebrated in both literary tradition and the historical record in differing forms. Later the novel attributes another spectacular killing to him. Cao Cao rewards his service lavishly.

The issue is now sharper. Guan Yu has accepted protection and gifts; he has also performed a dangerous service that helps repay the obligation. But he recognizes that the adversary he serves may be fighting the army sheltering Liu Bei. The noble bonds of friendship are caught inside a much larger military contest. The novel exploits the dramatic possibility that Guan Yu might kill the very people through whom news of his brother could reach him. It is an example of how local honor and strategic alignment can pull in opposite directions without either being a mere fiction.

When Guan Yu finally learns where Liu Bei has gone, he prepares to leave. Cao Cao does not wish to be confronted with an explicit demand, and his attendants delay a farewell audience. Guan Yu takes his decision into his own hands. He seals or leaves the rewards and gives up the official position. He takes the women of Liu Bei's household and sets out toward his brother. The departure is not a theft of a patron's property; in the novel it is a return of benefits coupled with an insistence that the original agreement be honored.

The journey becomes a sequence of confrontations at checkpoints. Officers demand documents and refuse passage, perhaps because of orders, perhaps because they fear responsibility if a valuable general escapes. Guan Yu defeats them one after another. The set of five passes and six slain commanders is highly structured literary material. It is not a route we can trace on a military map as an independently established itinerary. Its function is to test the same obligation under different local circumstances. At each barrier the warrior must decide whether to surrender the mission, negotiate indefinitely, or use force against men who are performing an official duty.

## The battlefield whose banners conceal a name

At White Horse, the northern armies are massive, and the field is crowded with reputation. Yuan Shao possesses resources and celebrated commanders. Cao Cao must find a way to break an opponent who has every reason to expect victory. Guan Yu, serving temporarily under Cao Cao, sees a renowned enemy general and advances with such force that the opposing formation is thrown into confusion. The novel stages the encounter as a decisive demonstration of martial supremacy, but its political consequences are more important than the physical feat.

After the enemy commander falls, news of the event travels beyond the battlefield. Liu Bei, currently associated with Yuan Shao's side, learns that the warrior responsible is Guan Yu. Suddenly one man's triumph threatens another man's security. Yuan Shao's camp can interpret the death as an injury inflicted by an officer related to a guest they are harboring. Liu Bei must explain a relationship in which bloodless brotherhood carries more weight than present command. The same event that earns Guan Yu honor with Cao Cao exposes Liu Bei to danger among Cao Cao's rivals.

The episode captures something essential about civil war: identities overlap in ways that military banners cannot simplify. A man may be an officer of one lord, sworn kin to another, and a valued enemy of a third. Personal bonds survive the transfer of armies from one patron to the next, but those bonds become suspicious whenever political camps harden. The novel turns this ambiguity into suspense. Guan Yu's excellence at arms creates a crisis of interpretation elsewhere, in a tent where Liu Bei cannot control what the observers will believe.

We should also be careful about the division between historical combat and narrative expansion. Guan Yu's role in killing Yan Liang at White Horse is preserved in early historical accounts; the broader itinerary of gates, passes, and dramatic reunions was elaborated through the story tradition. These do not make the historical action less interesting. A single attested military exploit can grow, over centuries, into an extended moral adventure because listeners want to see the same principle tested repeatedly: will Guan Yu accept danger rather than surrender his chosen allegiance?

## The cost hidden inside a heroic journey

The passage is glorious from Guan Yu's point of view. He remains true to his sworn brother, protects the household entrusted to him, and breaks through every attempt to detain him. Yet a listener can also see the moral difficulty that the novel's admiration tends to subordinate. The officers at the passes may have no personal quarrel with him. They have orders, families, and loyalties of their own. Guan Yu's ability to dispatch them does not establish that each death was necessary. The fiction converts a problem of conflicting obligations into a succession of contests that are easy to win emotionally. Its construction can be admired while its ethical simplification is examined.

The road also isolates Guan Yu from the institutional politics in which Cao Cao excels. Guan Yu cannot appeal to a higher court that will enforce a letter of safe conduct. He must persuade or fight the person immediately before him. The same empire that can ceremonially recognize the emperor has no reliable neutral authority to protect one man traveling between rival military households. The very form of the road narrative expresses a fragmented political order.

Eventually Guan Yu hears of Zhang Fei and approaches him. The reunion is not immediate celebration. Zhang Fei suspects that his brother's service under Cao Cao has made him a traitor. Guan Yu must prove that he has not deserted Liu Bei, and another armed encounter helps resolve the suspicion. The brothers are reunited and then find Liu Bei. The novel allows suspicion because it takes the oath seriously enough to acknowledge how badly betrayal would hurt. Trust is stronger when it survives the possibility that another person has chosen differently.

The presence of the two women, for whom Guan Yu has accepted responsibility, also gives the journey a purpose beyond reuniting warriors. An oath of brotherhood can be a masculine ideal that excludes most of the people affected by war. Here the warrior must move slowly enough to protect a household. His obligations limit the freedom that his military power might otherwise grant. At least part of the novel's admiration rests on his refusal to treat those dependents as expendable cargo.

## The household that moves with the warrior

Guan Yu's journey is often remembered as one man on a famous horse passing through a chain of dangerous gates. But he is also charged with safeguarding members of Liu Bei's household. Their presence changes the nature of his task. He cannot seek every contest that offers glory, and he cannot simply ride through uncertain ground at the pace of an unencumbered fighter. His promise includes keeping vulnerable people safe while armies and political borders move around them.

This is a more substantial test of loyalty than the refusal of a generous title. To reject a gift is a momentary act. To protect a family across days of uncertainty requires decisions about food, lodging, routes, and whom to trust when local officers are suspicious. Guan Yu is being asked to function as guardian rather than conqueror. The story's admiration for him arises partly from the fact that his power is placed in the service of people who cannot repay it with political office.

The novel also reveals its own hierarchy of attention. Guan Yu's duels are named and retold; the fear and fatigue of the women traveling with him receive less space. This does not erase their role. Liu Bei's household is part of the continuity of the cause he wishes to build. Its survival rests on networks of care and cooperation that the epic tends to represent through the deeds of its armed protector. The listener can recover the wider human situation from the martial arrangement of the story.

## The officers who guard a road

On Guan Yu's fictional journey, each gate becomes a miniature political order. A pass has an officer who controls movement; his superior has issued standing instructions; a stranger claiming urgent business presents himself with impressive credentials, but perhaps without documents sufficient to satisfy those instructions. The officer must decide whether honoring a personal reputation is worth disobeying his immediate command. The novel usually resolves this problem through confrontation. Guan Yu insists on passage, an official refuses, and blood follows.

A listener may respond with delight when the red-faced warrior wins again. That pleasure is the mechanism of martial romance. Yet the defeated guards can also be read as functionaries trapped between the reputation of a celebrated man and orders they fear to ignore. We are not meant to transform the gates into a legal hearing. We can, however, recognize the fiction's preference for moral clarity over procedural uncertainty. Guan Yu's bond to Liu Bei matters more to the narrative than the necessity of every provincial checkpoint to maintain a chain of command.

This makes the road a revealing contrast to Cao Cao's larger political project. Cao Cao is creating a state in which routine offices and appointments matter. Guan Yu moves as though an outstanding personal obligation should supersede ordinary administration. The tale wishes to honor both orderly rule and the hero who breaks through its gates. That tension helps account for the durability of the episode. A government needs guards who follow instructions. An epic needs a traveler whom no guard can keep from his promise.

The sequence also gives Guan Yu a geography of separation. Each gate crossed is another step away from material comfort and closer to an uncertain reunion. By the time he encounters the men he seeks, the narrative has made his devotion visible through distance and risk. The journey earns its emotional resolution not by providing a new argument for loyalty but by letting the audience watch loyalty accumulate cost.

## Why Cao Cao lets him go

Cao Cao learns of Guan Yu's departure. Some officers want pursuit. Cao Cao decides instead to honor his word and the integrity of his former guest. The choice may be read as noble admiration, but it also makes sense as a calculation of political reputation. A lord who promises favorable terms to a captured enemy and then violates them will find future defections and surrenders harder to negotiate. Guan Yu is a single valuable fighter. Cao Cao's credibility before many other potential recruits may be worth more than securing him by force.

The scene therefore complicates the contrast between the characters. The ruler whom the novel often regards as morally compromised behaves with recognizable restraint. The warrior whom it praises for loyalty kills officers whose duties obstruct his own. Each act may have causes that cannot be reduced to praise or blame without losing what makes the story interesting. Cao Cao's decision also demonstrates an aspect of statecraft that Lü Bu lacked: the capacity to absorb a personal disappointment for the sake of a longer-term advantage.

The novel's Guan Yu will later become the subject of enormous religious veneration. The historical officer was eventually killed after a failed campaign. His literary and religious afterlives should not be read back as proof that every dramatic scene in this journey occurred. The essential historical relationship with Liu Bei is documented; the precise adventures of the passes belong to a developing legend of fidelity. Distinguishing these levels allows us to understand both history and the making of an exemplary hero.

## Sun Ce against the spirit world

While the northern rivals move toward major war, the southern house faces a different crisis. Sun Ce has established power through audacity and quick campaigns. The novel now adds a story involving the Daoist religious figure Yu Ji. Sun Ce treats the figure as a threat to political authority or a deceiver who attracts a dangerous following. He orders him executed despite warnings and appeals. Afterward visions and signs torment the young commander. These scenes can be read as a supernatural punishment for pride, or as a literary expression of guilt and fear. They belong to the novel's moral atmosphere rather than a medical record of Sun Ce's final illness.

Sun Ce is wounded in an attack and deteriorates. The energetic founder dies young. Before his death he entrusts the house to Sun Quan, who has not yet demonstrated the same battlefield prowess. The counsel surrounding the succession has a striking practical sense. Sun Ce's strengths were conquest, rapid decisions, and bold attacks. Sun Quan will need to employ administrators, listen to experienced men, and hold a coalition of families and officers together. Zhou Yu and the civil official Zhang Zhao become indispensable advisers for the new ruler.

The transition marks a crucial difference from Lü Bu's career. Lü Bu's power could scarcely survive his personal failures. Sun Ce has gathered an organization that may survive his death. It is still vulnerable, but the ability to transfer command is one measure of whether a military house has become more than a personal following. Sun Quan must learn how to balance military prowess against administration and how to use elders whose achievements precede his own. That task is less spectacular than his brother's campaigns, but without it the southern realm would not endure.


## The south loses its conqueror

While Guan Yu travels, the lower Yangtze faces a different test. Sun Ce has turned a series of conquests into a formidable regional power, but his personal energy remains central to it. The novel places his death amid an ominous confrontation with religious authority, including visions of the dead and the haunting presence of a man he ordered killed. The supernatural episode is dramatic because Sun Ce's strength, the very thing that protected his house, seems suddenly unable to protect him from his own conscience or fate.

The historical tradition includes conflict with Xu Gong and the assassination by men connected with Xu Gong. The novel elaborates the event through the figure of Yu Ji and encounters with the spirit world. These versions need to be held separately. The ghost story tells us how an audience might interpret the moral vulnerability of a violent young conqueror. It does not establish a supernatural cause of death. The underlying political event is the sudden loss of a leader whose reputation had made others confident that expansion would continue.

Sun Quan inherits an organization before he has established a comparable personal name. His first task is to prevent the organization from fracturing. Commanders who served his brother must accept him. Administrative families must believe that their interests will still be protected. Advisers such as Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu become vital because they can supply continuity between the conquering generation and the governing one. Where Sun Ce could resolve uncertainties by riding into battle, Sun Quan must learn to resolve many of them by listening, appointing, negotiating, and sometimes refusing reckless proposals.

The two stories—Guan Yu's reunion and Sun Quan's succession—therefore ask different questions of loyalty. Guan Yu remains faithful to a particular person even while serving a rival. Sun Ce's followers must transfer their fidelity from a dead founder to a surviving institution and heir. Both forms will shape the approaching struggle for the river. A bond that can survive separation and a household that can survive bereavement are different political achievements, and neither is secured by valor alone.


## A historical man becomes a moral pattern

What makes Guan Yu's road so memorable? One answer is the sequence of obstacles, each presenting a recognizable question in simpler form. A checkpoint officer asks for authorization. Guan Yu explains his obligation. The officer refuses, and force follows. The next pass presents a variation. The repeated structure gives the listener a sense of forward progress, while the protected household, the famous horse, and the anticipated reunion provide emotional continuity. The tale is built to be narrated.

Another answer is the character's apparent constancy. In a novel full of sudden betrayals, Guan Yu tells us what he intends and then follows through. He can be severe, proud, and violent. But he refuses to make loyalty a short-term bargain. This is attractive because the surrounding political world is unstable. People need agreements whose terms cannot be revised every time one partner receives a better offer. The problem is that one man's constancy may also become rigidity when the circumstances genuinely require change. The story will eventually test that danger.

Cao Cao's encounter with Guan Yu creates an unexpected counterpart. Cao Cao too has a principle that can guide a costly choice: a promise publicly made may be worth keeping, even when immediate interest points elsewhere. His own long-term judgment can lead him to honor an agreement with someone who will become an enemy. The book does not require us to grant him innocence in other matters to recognize the force of this decision.

Sun Quan faces a version of the same problem at the level of a ruling house. His brother's trusted officers will not remain loyal merely because he possesses the correct family name. He must conduct himself in ways that make service intelligible and worthwhile. A dynasty is renewed by acts that persuade living people, not only by possession of a title. The dying Sun Ce leaves his brother a political achievement and a debt to those who made it possible.

In the northern war Cao Cao's opponent Yuan Shao still possesses greater forces. Guan Yu has returned to Liu Bei, whose independence remains precarious. Sun Quan has inherited the south. The novel has distributed several different models of leadership across the map: the formidable administrator, the legitimate but vulnerable claimant, and the young successor of a conquering family. The road across five passes tells us how one warrior holds to a personal bond. The coming battles will ask whether any of these larger political communities can hold to a coherent purpose under pressure.

## Editorial and source notes

Narrative baseline: chapters 23–29. The five-passes/six-generals sequence is literary; it should not be plotted as verified history. Guan Yu's killing of Yan Liang is attested in Chen Shou, whereas the novel's wider martial embroidery is not. Sun Ce's death and Sun Quan's succession are historical; the Yu Ji supernatural scenes involve disputed anecdotal traditions shaped by later fiction. See ter Haar for the growth of Guan Yu's religious cult.
