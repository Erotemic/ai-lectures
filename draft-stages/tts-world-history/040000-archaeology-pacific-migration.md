# Finding Voyagers: How Archaeologists Reconstruct the Settlement of the Pacific

Imagine arriving on an island in the western Pacific about three thousand years ago. You have crossed water beyond the sight of land. The canoe carries people, food plants, tools, and perhaps animals. The landing beach is unfamiliar. Behind it is a forest; beyond the forest, a reef and a place to shelter. You do not know whether the next island can be reached, or whether anyone has lived on this one before. To begin a settlement, you need a great deal more than the ability to survive a voyage. You need a supply of freshwater, plant knowledge, shelter, relationships, and enough people and resources to make a community viable.

No written passenger list survives from those voyages. How can archaeologists know they happened? How could they tell whether two islands were settled by related people, by people traveling in opposite directions, or by groups meeting after long separation? And if modern people share languages and ancestry, how do we determine whether that reflects one migration or several?

One place to begin is a cemetery discovered at Teouma on the island of Efate in Vanuatu. Workers quarrying land in 2003 exposed archaeological remains. Excavations by a team including Stuart Bedford, Matthew Spriggs, and Ralph Regenvanu revealed Lapita pottery and human burials. The site provided a rare opportunity to connect the objects associated with early settlement to the bodies of people who lived there. Those remains later became part of a larger conversation among archaeology, linguistics, and ancient DNA studies. The results complicated some confident accounts of Pacific origins.

This lecture follows the evidence outward from a piece of decorated pottery to an ocean-wide history. We will ask what can be dated, what can be compared, and what experimental voyages can demonstrate. We will also credit the people whose navigational traditions made the journeys possible and whose communities preserve the relevant knowledge now. The history begins with Indigenous Pacific people, rather than with the European scholars who later tried to reconstruct their movements.

## Several Pacifics, several histories

The Pacific is immense, but it is not one undifferentiated empty space. People reached parts of Near Oceania, including New Guinea and the Bismarck Archipelago, tens of thousands of years ago. Later movements across Remote Oceania took place in a world already inhabited to the west. Different routes connected different populations, and their descendants developed distinct languages and societies. A recent archaeological map can make these processes look like a single arrow, but the people moved through settlements, encounters, return journeys, and changing coastlines.

Archaeologists often use the terms Near Oceania and Remote Oceania to mark a useful difference in the challenges of reaching islands. In parts of Near Oceania, intervisible islands and shorter crossings are more common. Much of Remote Oceania demands longer open-water passages. This distinction helps explain why the settlement of some islands occurs vastly earlier than the settlement of others. It does not divide populations into capable and incapable sailors. The question concerns the technological and social conditions under which particular crossings became practical and desirable.

In the twentieth century, scholars such as Roger Green and Patrick Kirch developed integrated models of Pacific settlement that considered archaeological cultures and historical linguistics together. They were not the first people to know about voyaging routes; Indigenous genealogies, oral histories, and navigational traditions had long described travel and relationships between islands. Modern archaeological models sometimes used those sources and sometimes ignored them. Their contribution was to formulate hypotheses testable against excavation and chronology.

Imagine a pottery sherd recovered from an archaeological layer. Its decoration resembles sherds from another island hundreds of kilometers away. That is an observation. The inference that the potters were the same people requires additional reasoning. A style can travel through trade, gift exchange, marriage, imitation, or movement of craftspeople. A pot can be carried by a visitor without the visitor founding a settlement. To identify migration, archaeologists seek repeated patterns: settlement structures, tool manufacture, food remains, pottery, dates, and environmental changes. Even then, material culture does not translate directly into ethnicity or language.

## The Lapita clue

Lapita pottery is known for intricate geometric motifs, often impressed with a dentate, or tooth-like, stamping technique. The name comes from a site in New Caledonia, and the archaeological term now describes a broader material tradition found across parts of the western Pacific. Early Lapita sites appear in the Bismarck Archipelago and later on islands farther east, including Vanuatu, Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa. The spread was rapid on an archaeological timescale, but it was not one continuous sailing trip.

Why does the pottery matter? Because it has recognizable manufacture and decoration, and because sherds can be recovered in stratified contexts with other evidence. A decorative motif alone might be a fashion. A package of similar ceramic techniques, settlement locations, stone tools, and transported plants or animals can point to sustained contact or movement. Archaeologists compare the oldest secure occurrences across a chain of islands and ask whether the pattern is consistent with populations expanding eastward over generations.

A site needs more than a typological label. Suppose a decorated pot is found in a layer containing charcoal. A radiocarbon date on the charcoal may help date that layer. But if the charcoal came from the center of a tree that had lived for centuries, its carbon may be older than the activity that placed it beside the pot. If the layer was disturbed, the association may be weak. If a shell is used, marine reservoir effects can complicate the apparent age. The age of a sherd is therefore constrained by archaeological context and appropriate dating material, not simply by proximity to any laboratory result.

Matthew Spriggs, Stuart Bedford, and other Pacific archaeologists have invested considerable work in establishing dependable chronologies. At Teouma, work with local collaborators and Vanuatu heritage authorities documented a cemetery as well as ceramic deposits. The burials and decorated pottery provided unusually rich contextual information for early Remote Oceanian settlement. The discovery is sometimes narrated as though two archaeologists found an untouched prehistoric community on their own. The more accurate record involves quarry workers, Vanuatu researchers, land access, excavation crews, osteologists, dating specialists, and community decisions about the handling of human remains.

Teouma demonstrates why a named site is more informative than an abstract migration arrow. We can ask about pottery placed near bodies, burial sequence, diet, and the relation of human remains to the first centuries of settlement. The answers require excavation records and direct measurement. A bone in an early cemetery may identify one individual. It cannot, without further evidence, represent every group who arrived on every island. The site gives us a detailed sample from one place in a changing network.

## What a date can and cannot date

Let's give the dating problem a concrete form. Imagine two island sites. At the first, a burned nutshell from a sealed hearth yields a calibrated age that falls around three thousand years before the present. At the second, a piece of old driftwood in a mixed beach deposit appears older by several centuries. Should we conclude that the second island was settled first? Not yet. The old wood could predate human use. The beach layer may have mixed materials. A settlement chronology needs short-lived samples securely associated with human activity and enough determinations to recognize outliers.

Radiocarbon dating is probabilistic. Laboratories measure the remaining carbon fourteen in once-living material. Researchers convert that measurement into a radiocarbon age and calibrate it using independent records of past atmospheric carbon. The resulting calendar age is generally a probability distribution, sometimes with more than one peak. A publication that displays a clean single year for first settlement can conceal a much more complicated argument about samples, calibration, and archaeological association.

Researchers such as Janet Wilmshurst, Atholl Anderson, and Thomas Higham have used stringent selection and recalibration of dates to investigate the timing of settlement in East Polynesia and Aotearoa New Zealand. Their analyses helped challenge older chronologies based on less secure samples. At Wairau Bar in New Zealand, Higham, Anderson, and Chris Jacomb studied carefully chosen archaeological materials; the resulting chronology places occupation toward the end of the thirteenth century of the common era. That is evidence about a site, and part of a broader argument about settlement. It is not the exact birthday of the first person to make landfall.

Suppose a midden contains bird bones with human cut marks and shell ornaments found in an early settlement layer. If the bones and ornaments agree with several well-chosen dates, we have stronger evidence for human occupation at that time. If a much older date comes from unassociated charcoal, it should not automatically push back the arrival of everyone in the archipelago. This is how a spectacular early claim can be revised without discrediting the archaeology as a whole. Later researchers are asking a more constrained question with better evidence.

The pattern of a species' disappearance can offer another clue. Some birds evolved on islands without mammalian predators and suffered after human arrival and introduced animals. Their extinction can help bracket the chronology of environmental change. But extinction is not always immediate, and not every population decline is caused by a single factor. Climate, habitat change, human hunting, and introduced species may interact. Dating an ecological impact is related to dating settlement, but the two should not be treated as interchangeable.

## Tools, food, and people crossing water

Migration leaves more than pottery. Food plants and animals moved with voyagers, sometimes becoming the basis of new agriculture and settlement economies. Archaeobotanists study charred seeds, starch residues, phytoliths, and the distribution of cultivated plants. Zooarchaeologists identify bones from introduced animals and changes in local species. Obsidian sourcing can help identify exchanges because volcanic glass carries a geochemical signature tied to its geological source.

Consider two sites separated by open sea. A piece of obsidian found at the second site matches the elemental chemistry of a source near the first. That is evidence the material moved, and may help reconstruct a network of exchange. It does not reveal whether the obsidian was carried on one journey, exchanged through intermediaries, or transported by people who settled permanently. Larger assemblages, dating, and comparison of production debris help distinguish these possibilities. The source has to be sampled well enough to make the chemical match meaningful.

A 2022 study by researchers of early Lapita interactions in southern New Guinea reported transported obsidian, animal introductions, and turtle-bone technology in contexts dating roughly 3,480 to 3,060 years before present. Their findings suggest relationships with Indigenous communities in New Guinea alongside the developing Lapita sphere. That complicates a picture in which a new population simply sailed past earlier residents without meaningful contact. The evidence is of exchange and interaction; it does not on its own provide a census of people aboard canoes.

We must also be careful about reconstruction from missing objects. Boats built from perishable materials rarely survive for thousands of years in ordinary tropical archaeological conditions. A site can preserve stone adzes and pottery without preserving the canoe that made its occupation possible. Researchers infer voyaging capacity from distances crossed, settlement sequences, materials moved, language, and surviving traditions of construction and navigation. A detailed illustration of a prehistoric boat is an interpretation unless directly supported by archaeological remains or a documented historical tradition.

This is where the distinction between possibility and historical occurrence becomes important. A modern canoe may prove that a certain route is navigable with a particular technique. It does not tell us exactly which people followed that route in a particular century. The archaeology supplies the evidence for where and when populations lived; experimental voyaging tests whether a proposed means of travel is technically feasible.

## A family of languages across the sea

Austronesian languages are spoken across an extraordinary range, from Island Southeast Asia through large parts of the Pacific and also Madagascar. Historical linguists compare systematic sound correspondences, shared grammatical forms, and inherited vocabulary. They reconstruct earlier language relationships and propose branching histories. A shared word for a canoe component, for example, can suggest inherited maritime terminology, but borrowing is possible and the actual history of the technology needs further evidence.

Roger Green and other scholars linked linguistic reconstruction to archaeological questions about settlement. A language family does not itself give us a dated map of migrations. A present-day language might have spread through trade, intermarriage, political power, or replacement of another language. Some communities can change language while retaining much of their ancestry; other communities can retain a language while gaining ancestors from elsewhere. Archaeology, linguistics, and genetics measure different aspects of history. Their answers can reinforce one another, but they are not three methods for calculating exactly the same thing.

For a listener accustomed to a family tree of languages, consider what happens when two islands exchange marriage partners for centuries. Their spoken languages might grow more alike through borrowing. Their people may acquire common ancestry. Their ceramics may show similar motifs. Those three observations can be compatible with intense contact without requiring that one island's entire population descended from a single migration from the other. To distinguish the possibilities, we ask whether the timing and geographic distribution of changes match an independent model.

The same reasoning applies to the word 'Polynesian.' It denotes a family of related cultures and languages with shared histories, not a single unchanged population existing for millennia. East Polynesian settlement came much later than initial settlement of parts of western Remote Oceania. Distinguishing these phases explains why a single map caption reading 'Austronesian expansion, 3000 BCE to 1200 CE' is too coarse for a serious account.

## Ancient DNA changes the map

Genetic research provides evidence that earlier archaeology could not produce. A 2016 study led by Pontus Skoglund and collaborators analyzed ancient genomes from early individuals in Vanuatu and Tonga. Those individuals had strong ancestry ties to populations of East Asian origin. This was important because many present-day populations of Remote Oceania also have substantial Papuan-related ancestry. If one assumed that the present distribution had remained constant since first settlement, one might infer a heavily mixed founding population. The early ancient samples revealed a more complicated sequence.

Subsequent work, including 2018 studies of ancient individuals from Vanuatu, found evidence for substantial later movement of Papuan-related ancestry into parts of Remote Oceania. In Vanuatu, that ancestry became prominent over time while Austronesian languages continued to be spoken. The combination is striking: a change in ancestry need not produce a corresponding replacement of language. New work can refine how many movements occurred, when they happened, and where the contributing populations originated.

Ancient DNA is powerful but has limits. An individual buried at Teouma is a person, not a complete snapshot of all island inhabitants. Preservation conditions determine which remains can be sampled; burial practices determine whose remains are available. Laboratory contamination, reference populations, genetic drift, and statistical modeling affect analysis. Models describe ancestry relationships between sampled populations, not exact tribal identities or the language spoken by any one skeleton.

Researchers also have ethical obligations. Human remains are ancestors, and living communities may have specific expectations about consultation, research access, and return. Consent and governance are parts of present-day scientific practice. A genetic sequence cannot substitute for the histories, expertise, or preferences of the communities connected to the burial. The names and contributions of local heritage professionals deserve as much visibility as the institutions that perform the sequencing.

There are further studies of Polynesian population history, including work using modern genetic data to propose sequences of settlement among island groups. Those models are useful hypotheses to compare with radiocarbon dates and linguistic evidence. They should not be turned into unquestioned sailing itineraries. A branching statistical relationship between populations is related to, but is not identical with, a logbook of voyages.

## Could people navigate deliberately?

One of the most persistent outside misunderstandings of Pacific history is that distant islands must have been settled by accident because navigation without European instruments was assumed impossible. This ignores Indigenous navigational expertise and the cumulative evidence of repeated travel, inter-island relationships, and deliberate settlement. Yet simply declaring that navigators were skilled does not explain how they worked. We should ask what methods can be documented and tested.

Traditional navigators use stars, sun, swell patterns, winds, cloud shapes, birds, and mental models of island locations. Navigation is a learned practice requiring training, memory, and judgment. Techniques vary across Oceanic communities, and a method documented on one atoll should not be assumed identical to every historical practice in Polynesia. Anthropologists such as David Lewis recorded and compared navigational traditions, while people practicing the traditions supplied the knowledge that made the research possible.

In 1976, the voyaging canoe Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti with the Satawalese master navigator Mau Piailug using non-instrument methods. Ben Finney and colleagues in the Polynesian Voyaging Society documented the project, but the navigator's expertise was central to its success. Crew members and Hawaiian organizers were not merely assistants to an academic experiment. The voyage was also a cultural act of recovery and teaching. Subsequent work by Nainoa Thompson and many others developed and transmitted navigational practice in Hawaiʻi and across Oceania.

What did the voyage demonstrate? That a trained navigator and suitable canoe can make a purposeful crossing without a compass or satellite receiver, under the conditions of that journey. It did not prove that the exact 1976 route was the one used by every prehistoric voyager. It did not determine the precise date of initial settlement in Hawaiʻi. Experimental archaeology and living knowledge show feasibility and technique; excavated contexts and dating address historical occupation. They ask related questions and become more persuasive when considered together.

The broader significance is also methodological. If a theory assumes that prehistoric voyagers could not navigate deliberately, a successful traditionally navigated crossing exposes a bad assumption. But if a theory predicts a particular prehistoric landing date, a modern voyage cannot verify the date. We should be willing to learn from the experiment without asking it to answer questions it was not designed to test.

## A worked case: from sherd to settlement history

Imagine we excavate an early coastal village on an island east of Vanuatu. We recover dentate-stamped sherds, basalt adzes, food remains, and charcoal from hearths. We document which objects lie together before moving them. Several short-lived samples date to a narrow period. Pottery appears in the earliest secure layers and changes in later ones. We compare its manufacturing methods with published Lapita collections and find strong similarities.

The first inference is that a community at that place used a Lapita-related material tradition during the dated interval. To infer migration, we compare settlement patterns across neighboring islands. To infer trade, we source stone and other imported materials. To infer diet, we examine residues, bones, and plant remains. To discuss ancestry, we would need ethically governed biological evidence and appropriate comparative samples. To discuss language, we must use linguistic history, recognizing that we cannot identify a person's spoken language from pottery decoration.

Now suppose the site yields a single date centuries older than the others. We re-examine the sample. Did it come from long-lived wood? Was its layer disturbed? Is the calibration appropriate? Was it measured at a laboratory with controls and reported uncertainty? A credible new early settlement date can transform the regional chronology, but the evidentiary burden rises when one result conflicts with an otherwise coherent pattern. Inquiry should begin with the sample, not with loyalty to a favorite migration map.

Suppose genetic evidence later shows that a buried individual belonged to an ancestry profile differing from most present-day residents. That finding could reveal demographic change over centuries. It would not make the present residents less indigenous to their home or establish that cultural traditions were interrupted. Descent, political belonging, language, and material culture have connected but distinct histories. The evidence can enrich the history without deciding modern identity.

## What remains recoverable

We can know that people crossed immense distances, occupied islands at particular periods, carried material traditions and living resources, and interacted with communities they encountered. We know this through archaeological sequences, securely dated objects, comparative languages, biological evidence, and navigational knowledge. We can sometimes identify several movements where older summaries imagined one. We can revise settlement dates when better samples are collected and calibrated.

There are still questions the archaeological record cannot settle on its own: the names of the first crews to reach a particular island, the full routes of individual voyages, the contents of every canoe, and the decisions people made when weather or politics changed. Oral traditions can preserve important historical knowledge, but their interpretation requires attention to language, genre, community, and changes over time. Written European accounts are later and often carry the observers' assumptions. Neither set of sources should be treated as a universal master key.

Credit belongs to ancient navigators and settlers; to descendants and living practitioners such as Mau Piailug and the many crews of Hōkūleʻa; to Vanuatu field collaborators including Ralph Regenvanu and the excavators at Teouma; to researchers such as Spriggs, Bedford, Kirch, Green, Wilmshurst, Anderson, Higham, Skoglund, and their colleagues; and to the communities who govern sites and sustain the knowledge required to understand them. Each contributed something different.

When we see a line of arrows crossing a map of the Pacific, we are seeing the result of countless observations and arguments. A pot has a manufacturing technique; a hearth has a dated sample; a body has a burial context; a language has a history of sounds; a sailor has knowledge of stars and swell. The arrows become credible when those independent clues can be brought into a consistent account, and remain open to revision when a new excavation changes the evidence. The people who crossed that ocean made the history. Archaeology is the work of learning, carefully, how much of their achievement we can now reconstruct.

## Research and attribution notes (not spoken)

### Evidence distinctions

- Lapita is an archaeological tradition, not a genetic haplogroup, a single ethnic category, or proof that each artifact was carried by a colonizing migrant.
- Secure radiocarbon chronology depends on short-lived associated samples, calibration, stratigraphy, and accounting for marine reservoir or old-wood effects.
- Teouma excavations included Vanuatu heritage personnel and named researchers Bedford, Spriggs, and Regenvanu; attribute discovery and research to the team and local context.
- 2016 and 2018 ancient-DNA analyses distinguish early sampled ancestry from later population histories. The specific sample does not imply the identity of every islander.
- The 1976 Hōkūleʻa voyage documents navigational capacity with named Satawalese and Hawaiian experts; it does not date ancient landfalls.

### Editorial cautions

- Do not treat a reconstructed modern canoe as the direct archaeological type of a given ancient voyage.
- Distinguish genetic ancestry from language, ethnicity, and cultural identity.
- The worked island excavation is explicitly hypothetical.
