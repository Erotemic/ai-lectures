# Popol Wuj, Part I: Before the Sun, Before the People

Imagine a world in which the sky is above, but the sun has not yet risen. The land is submerged, and there are no human voices to name the hills or call across a field. In the stillness, beings capable of thought and speech confer about how a world could come into existence, and about what sort of inhabitants it ought to have. Before the first successful people are formed, there will be attempts that fail. Some beings will walk and speak without remembering those who made them. Others will claim to shine like the sun before a true dawn has come. This is the world of the *Popol Wuj*, a major work of K'iche' Maya literature from highland Guatemala.

You may know the title through the spelling *Popol Vuh*. The form *Popol Wuj* follows a different convention for rendering K'iche', a Mayan language that is still spoken. The name is often understood as a book of community or council, with an association between the council mat and collective authority. This is a living Indigenous tradition with a history of interpretation, performance, and scholarship. It should not be treated as an archaeological curiosity from a vanished people. The surviving manuscript was copied in the early eighteenth century, after Spanish conquest, from an earlier K'iche' text now lost. Its stories and forms draw on older Maya intellectual and religious traditions. That means we are listening to a work whose written preservation is colonial-era, while many of the ideas it communicates have much deeper roots.

The book is not solely a creation myth. It includes the adventures of the Hero Twins, the shaping of the first people from maize, the emergence and migrations of K'iche' lineages, and histories that establish their social world. In the first part of our account, we will stay with the making of the world and the strange rivalry between real and false sources of light. Along the way we will meet gods who plan, beings who refuse to remember, objects that turn on their owners, and a brilliant but boastful bird whose feathers become his downfall. The emphasis will be on following the narrative in its own terms before drawing comparisons with other traditions.

## The silence and the council

At the opening, the earth has not yet emerged from water, and the sky rests above an undifferentiated expanse. The scene is presented in deliberate, paired language. Things are described through repeated but varied phrases, as though the act of naming must circle an idea before it can be brought into being. This is an important feature of K'iche' poetics, not merely an ornamental repetition that an English summary can discard without loss. The creators speak and deliberate. Among the names attached to them are Heart of Sky and the Plumed Serpent, and their relationships are rendered differently in translations that must navigate a densely structured original.

The creators decide that there should be land. Their words summon mountains, valleys, and trees from the water. We are not watching a modern physical explanation of continental formation. We are witnessing the establishment of an inhabitable and meaningful world. A valley gives space for animals. Trees give shelter. The division between water and land makes paths and territories possible. In a story about a society whose lives are connected to mountains, fields, and changing seasons, the creation of features of the landscape is a way of arranging relationships, not just adding objects to a blank stage.

Animals are made to occupy these places: birds, deer, and other creatures given the forests and valleys as their homes. But a problem arises. The makers wish to be remembered and acknowledged. The animals cannot speak the kind of language expected of them. They cry out according to their different natures, yet their voices do not fulfill the creators' aim. In many retellings the creatures are assigned to their habitats and are to serve as food or companions in the world that will come. Their creation is not simply erased because they do not provide the right response. Instead their place in a larger ecology is established while the makers seek a different sort of being.

This gives the story a distinctive motivation. The creators are seeking people capable of recognition, speech, memory, and gratitude. That aim does not make the ensuing attempts easy. A body can be shaped and still fail to sustain a community with its makers and its environment. The epic will return to this distinction between being alive and being the kind of human being the world requires. It is a profound question expressed through a story of trial, failure, and revision.

## The failed people of mud

The makers first try forming a human being from earth or mud. The result lacks coherence and stability. The figure is wet and soft. It cannot support itself properly; it cannot see clearly or speak in a sustained way. It dissolves or fails to endure. The failure is physical as well as relational. The creators have attempted to make something that resembles a person, but it cannot function as one. The story lets the material of the body explain what goes wrong. Mud is fertile and shapable, but a poorly made figure of mud lacks the strength to stand.

The attempt is abandoned, and the makers confer again. Instead of treating the first failure as the final word, they search for advice and a better material. This deliberative pattern is a thread through the narrative. Creation is presented through thought, testing, and correction. The text's creators are powerful, but their project unfolds through episodes whose results must be evaluated. A listener who expects one instantaneous command followed by a perfectly completed world may be surprised. Here part of the drama lies in discovering what human life actually needs.

The next experiment makes people from wood. These wooden figures can move and reproduce. They occupy houses and populate the world, so they appear to have solved the problem that ruined the figures of mud. Yet they are deficient in a different way. They do not remember their makers or speak with proper acknowledgment. Their existence has the shape of social life without the mutual recognition that would give it an ethical center. What looked like success becomes a more troubling kind of failure because the beings have acquired the capacity to mistreat other inhabitants and possessions.

In the story the wooden people are destroyed through a terrible upheaval. Dark forces and a flood-like rain overtake them. Their own household objects and the animals they have used turn against them. Grinding stones, cooking implements, dogs, and other creatures acquire voices to speak of the injury they have endured. It is one of the most arresting scenes in world mythology: the domestic tools of an ordinary life become witnesses for the prosecution. Things normally handled without thought testify that they have been worn, struck, or exploited.

We can hear this scene in two ways at once. It is a fantastical reversal, a world in which the kitchen fights back. It is also an indictment of a community that uses living beings and material things without regard for the relationships sustaining it. The wooden people fail to remember the gods, but their heedlessness has consequences closer to home. They have not learned to inhabit a world with respect. Their possessions become agents of the judgment they thought belonged only to those who held them.

The account is often summarized as a sequence of unsuccessful creations, but that summary can flatten the range of questions it contains. The people of mud lacked endurance. The people of wood had bodies and society but lacked right relationship. Physical vitality, collective reproduction, speech, and ethical recognition are different achievements. The story distinguishes them rather than assuming one automatically brings the others. The eventual people of maize will answer the problem in another way, connecting human flesh directly to the food and cultivation on which life depends.

## A false sun appears

Before the proper sun and moon have risen, a magnificently adorned being named Seven Macaw boasts that he is the sun. His name is sometimes represented in older spellings as Vucub Caquix, and his story is told differently in English depending on the translator's choices. He is a bird splendid with precious ornaments, shining eyes, bright teeth, and a display of luxury that seems to demand admiration. He proclaims a brightness that he does not truly possess. The world is still awaiting its proper heavenly order, yet he claims the prestige of its greatest visible source of light.

His false claim is not simply that he is wealthy. He mistakes ornament for the substance that ornament imitates. Jewels glint; gold shines; a brilliant feather catches light. But those are signs of magnificence, not the power to illuminate the world. Seven Macaw treats his decorated body as evidence of cosmic authority. The narrative makes vanity a cosmological problem. If the wrong being occupies the place of the sun, the world's order is misrecognized before it has even fully begun.

Two young brothers, Hunahpu and Xbalanque, are sent or act to bring down the false claimant. They will become known as the Hero Twins, and their adventures are central to the *Popol Wuj*. For now they are not introduced through a formal military title or a palace genealogy. Their strengths include agility, planning, and the ability to exploit an opponent's weaknesses. The contrast with Seven Macaw is deliberate. He advertises what he owns; the twins understand how an apparent advantage can conceal a vulnerability.

The twins use blowguns to attack Seven Macaw when he comes to a tree for food. One shot strikes his mouth or jaw, seriously injuring him, but Seven Macaw manages to wrench away one twin's arm. This is not a frictionless victory by unbeatable heroes. The opponent fights back and temporarily holds something that belongs to his attackers. The twins must recover the arm and finish the task by means other than another direct assault.

They recruit an elderly pair who pass themselves off as humble healers. The figures approach Seven Macaw and convince him that his damaged teeth can be repaired. But the treatment removes the brilliant teeth that are central to his pride and replaces them with an ordinary material. His splendid eyes and ornaments are similarly undone. Deprived of the display that supported his false identity, he loses the force of his claim and dies. The scene is cunning, a little grotesque, and more revealing than a simple duel. The hero's success depends on knowing what the enemy values enough to surrender his defenses for it.

The story does not declare that beauty itself is bad. Rather it asks what occurs when a person confuses a beautiful appearance with a right to rule. Seven Macaw's jewels are not a true dawn. The twins expose a false hierarchy by dismantling the signs through which it announces itself. This makes the episode meaningful in a creation narrative. Before the world can have a true source of light, someone who has usurped that symbolic position must be removed.

## The sons of Seven Macaw

The twins' contest extends to the false sun's sons, Zipacna and Cabrakan. Each is linked to extraordinary strength and the earth's violent forms. Zipacna is associated with moving mountains and tremendous physical ability. In one episode he encounters a group of four hundred youths who are moving a great log. He offers to help, and his power alarms them. They plot to kill him; he survives their trap and later destroys them. The episode is not simply a story in which an innocent giant is killed by clever children. There are layers of fear and retaliation, and the young men themselves attempt deception before becoming its victims.

The twins in turn devise a trap for Zipacna, using his appetite and attention to a creature represented as a crab. They create the appearance of prey in a place where the ground itself can become dangerous. Zipacna is lured into a mountain setting and overcome. With Cabrakan, the challenge concerns his power to break mountains. The twins deceive him with food that weakens him and then bury him. Different translations preserve different nuances of the cooking, the creatures, and the mountains, but the overall movement is consistent: overwhelming strength is undone by a carefully constructed situation.

Here the *Popol Wuj* develops a recognizable style of heroic action. Its successful protagonists rarely solve everything by being larger than their enemies. They observe desire, pride, appetite, and expectation, then construct a test that converts the opponent's advantage into a liability. Their victories can be unsettling. The young heroes are capable of trickery, and the narrative does not pause to make every death comfortable for a modern listener. But the sequence prepares us for their greatest contest, where the opponents will be rulers of the underworld who have already defeated an earlier generation.

## Houses, tools, and the right to speak

The judgment of the wooden people deserves a second hearing because of how carefully the narrative selects its witnesses. A grinding stone is not an incidental object in a household where maize is prepared. It is part of the work by which grain becomes food. Cooking vessels hold what families eat. A dog lives near people and depends upon their treatment. The revolt of these beings makes visible the domestic labor and interdependence that people can take for granted. In a reversal of normal speech, the things that were used without being consulted now have their opportunity to accuse those who used them.

The episode is sometimes compared to modern stories in which machines rebel against their makers. The comparison catches the humor of the reversal, but it may miss the agricultural world that gives it force. Food preparation takes effort, and a community's continued life depends on tools, animals, and human work. The wooden people have been made, but they behave as though everything around them exists only to be consumed. Their failure is therefore more than a failure to repeat a sacred name. They have not learned the kinds of relationship that make shared life possible.

There is an interesting tension in the scene. Objects speak because the makers have granted them a voice at the moment of judgment. Humans, meanwhile, are being judged partly for a failure of speech and remembrance. The text has inverted the presumed hierarchy between a person who commands language and an object that cannot reply. A creature that can reproduce and fill houses may be less fitting for life than a grinding stone that remembers how it was treated. The story uses impossibility to ask listeners to reconsider something possible and ordinary: what our ways of using the world reveal about us.

## An older generation of ballplayers

The poem now gives a family history that reaches back before the twins' own exploits. Two brothers, One Hunahpu and Seven Hunahpu, play the ballgame. Their activity is important enough to disturb the lords of Xibalba, the underworld. The sound of play reaches powers below who are offended by it. They summon the players through messengers, presenting an invitation that is actually a trap. The underworld's rulers want to control the encounter on their own terrain.

The ballgame needs context. Historical Maya ballcourts survive in many places, and ballgames had social, political, and ritual significance. The exact rules, equipment, and significance varied over time and region. We should not imagine that the *Popol Wuj* supplies a modern sports regulation book. It uses the game as a setting where skill, prestige, conflict, and supernatural power come together. When someone is invited to play in Xibalba, he is entering a contest in which the hosts control far more than the court.

The summoned brothers descend through dangerous places and make critical mistakes. They encounter a council of apparent lords, but some are decoys. They misunderstand the arrangement and are humiliated. They then fail a test involving a house of darkness and objects that must remain burning without being consumed. Their failure leads to death. The rulers display One Hunahpu's severed head in a tree. The image is horrifying, but the story is not finished with it. In this literary world, a severed head can still become the bearer of life and the source of a new generation.

A young woman called Blood Woman, often named Xquic in English traditions, comes to the tree. The head speaks and produces saliva that reaches her hand. Through this extraordinary contact she conceives the Hero Twins. The scene is not a biological explanation offered by an ancient scientist. It is a mythic account of transmission across death. A father's body has been destroyed, yet something from him will survive and return to challenge those who killed him.

The woman faces danger because her pregnancy provokes suspicion among the lords of Xibalba. Ordered to be killed, she persuades the agents sent against her to spare her and to substitute another sign of compliance. She escapes to the household of the grandmother, Xmucane. There she is not immediately believed or welcomed. She must establish that her story is true. In one memorable episode involving maize, an impossible task becomes evidence that she belongs to the family she claims. This is another recognition scene: kinship exists before the household accepts it, but a test must bridge the gap between a stranger's words and collective trust.

## Children born into an unfinished conflict

Hunahpu and Xbalanque are born into a family marked by old defeat. Their father and uncle remain associated with the underworld, their mother has fled it, and their grandmother's household is divided by competing claims. Other boys in the family, often known in translation as One Monkey and One Artisan, resent the twins and are eventually transformed into monkeys. The episode joins sibling rivalry to the story's larger concern with skill and recognition. The elder figures claim position by age and accomplishment; the younger ones succeed through wit and their relationship with forces that others fail to understand.

The future contest with Xibalba thus begins long before the twins descend. It is inherited as a family injury, but the children do not simply repeat their fathers' failed response. They will learn what the earlier players did wrong: how to recognize false rulers, how to manage impossible tests, and how to use performance itself as a weapon. This shift from the defeated generation to the victorious one is crucial to the narrative architecture. It changes a story about death into a story about knowledge acquired from death.

## The sky awaiting its proper light

Seven Macaw's false radiance has a special place in the order of events. A world without a true sun is difficult to imagine from everyday experience. Most of our perceptions of direction and time depend on the regular movement of light. The creature's claim therefore concerns the very conditions under which a world becomes knowable. He insists that his body can stand in for the celestial order before that order has been established. The twins' challenge prepares a distinction between the glimmer of owned valuables and the light by which every creature can find its place.

The episode is also memorable because of the bodily details. Seven Macaw's teeth and eyes are not detachable costume pieces in the story's logic. They are part of how he sees himself. The supposed healers gain access to his vanity through a wound, offering to restore his beauty while taking away the features that sustain his boast. This is a form of dramatic irony: he believes his visitors will repair the appearance that makes him superior, and the audience recognizes that their intervention will deprive him of it. The scene works as theater even when we know its ending in advance.

The sons' stories extend the image from the sky to the earth. A being who moves mountains seems to possess absolute strength because the landscape itself responds to him. Yet if a clever adversary can make him pursue an imagined crab or eat what weakens him, strength becomes insufficient. The narrative thus repeatedly tests claims to authority in relation to something more basic: the ability to discern the situation one is actually in. These are stories about making cosmic order, but they are structured so that audiences can recognize everyday errors of pride, appetite, and misplaced confidence.

The work's imaginative extravagance should not obscure its social observations. People who possess resources may claim a right to rule because the resources glitter. People who can compel others may mistake that compulsion for wisdom. Families may sustain grudges long enough for children to inherit them. The narratives build these recognizable tendencies into enormous birds, mountain-moving figures, and deadly underworld contests. Their scale makes the patterns vivid enough to remember, and their humor allows difficult questions to be carried by compelling stories.

## What kind of creation story is this?

A creation narrative might be expected to move from emptiness to finished order in a straight line. The *Popol Wuj* moves through rehearsals, rivalries, genealogies, and performances before it reaches the successful making of people. The delay is productive. It teaches the listener what the new world needs to overcome: bodies without stability, communities without gratitude, false claims of light, and rulers who mistake control over a testing ground for true superiority. The desired humans cannot simply be added to a world whose moral and cosmic relationships remain disordered.

The surviving text is also a piece of literary art. Its parallel phrases, paired names, reversals, and nested stories are integral to the way it creates meaning. Translators must decide how much of that pattern to preserve and how to make names pronounceable for new audiences. A single English sentence sometimes condenses several lines of K'iche' expression. That is why a source-based lecture should tell the narrative in fresh language while keeping access to the original and carefully annotated translations in its reading references. A polished English paraphrase is a guide into the work, not a replacement for it.

There is a temptation to compare every episode immediately with the traditions we may already know. The unsuccessful creations may recall other accounts of human origins. The flood associated with the wooden people may recall Noah or Mesopotamian narratives. A group of heroic brothers may evoke familiar twins from Greece or elsewhere. Those associations can help memory, but they do not establish that the traditions share a historical chain of borrowing. Similar storytelling problems can arise in different societies. We first need to know what this particular narrative does within K'iche' intellectual life before asking how it compares with distant texts.

The first movement of the *Popol Wuj* thus leaves us with a world prepared but still incomplete. Mountains stand above the water, animals have their places, the failures of mud and wood have been exposed, and a false sun has been cast down. The children who can challenge death itself have been born, but the lords of Xibalba still await them. Far beneath the surface, those rulers have turned the ballgame into a fatal trap. The twins' task is to enter that trap knowingly, and to leave it transformed. Only after this contest will the narrative turn fully to the people whose bodies are made from the food at the center of the highland Maya world.


## How a book survived a conquest

The physical survival of this story is itself a history of choices made under pressure. Spanish conquest in the sixteenth century reorganized the political and religious life of highland Guatemala. Indigenous communities endured warfare, epidemics, dispossession, and the imposition of colonial institutions. Yet K'iche' speakers continued to preserve histories and sacred accounts. People educated in alphabetic writing could use Latin letters to record narratives in their own language. A text produced in that setting carries older knowledge through a new technology of writing, rather than showing a community that had simply abandoned its intellectual past.

Francisco Ximenez, a Dominican friar working in the region around the beginning of the eighteenth century, copied a K'iche' version and placed a Spanish translation beside it. His copy is the main manuscript through which modern readers encounter the work. The earlier text from which he worked has not survived in an available form. The surviving manuscript is now held by the Newberry Library in Chicago. These facts create a difficult but productive relationship between preservation and colonial power. A colonial cleric helped transmit words that the community had reason to guard, while the conditions of conquest shaped who could record them and how they later circulated. The K'iche' authors and keepers of the tradition should remain visible in that story.

The work itself reaches beyond cosmology to record ancestral migrations and claims to authority among K'iche' lineages. That combination helps explain why a narrative about the earliest creation would be treasured by a particular community. A story of origins can answer a question about today's relationships: who belongs with whom, where did our forebears travel, and what responsibilities connect us to the land and to one another? The episode of talking grinding stones is easier to appreciate when we remember that a household's food, labor, tools, and obligations have a place in this wider historical understanding.

We also need to resist a convenient but misleading division between precolonial tradition and contemporary Maya life. People who speak K'iche' today are not artifacts of an ancient culture. They interpret their own texts and histories in a modern world. Historians, linguists, and translators can study the manuscript; they do not supersede the living communities for whom its language and stories remain meaningful.

## Research and editorial notes (not spoken)

Narrative based on the K'iche' *Popol Wuj*, emphasizing opening creation cycles, Seven Macaw and his sons, and the background of the Hero Twins. Spellings differ by translator (Popol Vuh/Popol Wuj, Xbalanque/Xb'alanke, Xquic/Blood Woman). No claim that all Maya communities hold an identical creation account. Some narrative details are transmitted in late copies; the Newberry manuscript is an early eighteenth-century transcription of an older K'iche' account, not a surviving preconquest autograph.
