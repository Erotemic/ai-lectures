# Reading Egyptian Stories: From Papyrus to Translation

On a museum table lies a long sheet of brown material, divided into columns of flowing black handwriting with occasional marks in red. A scribe copied these marks more than three thousand years ago. They recount a man who stored his heart in a tree, died when the tree was cut down, and returned as a bull, then as trees, then as a child who became a king. How do we move from those physical marks to a story that can be told aloud today? And, more pointedly, how do we know where our reconstruction is secure and where it depends upon a choice made by a modern editor or translator?

The object is Papyrus D'Orbiney, the surviving manuscript of *The Tale of Two Brothers*. It is a good place to learn the methods of literary archaeology because it supplies both an enticing story and a traceable chain of evidence. We have the papyrus as a material object, its handwriting, a named ancient scribe, several nineteenth-century reports and editions, modern grammatical study, and translations that can disagree about specific words. We also have gaps. Its archaeological discovery was not documented to modern excavation standards. Its original author is unknown. The existence of this copy does not tell us how many earlier performances or manuscripts may have circulated.

There are three conclusions to establish. First, the survival of an ancient story depends heavily on where and how its physical copies were deposited. Second, reading a manuscript requires several distinct tasks, each of which can be checked. Third, an ancient literary composition may have a history of variants and copying that is more complicated than a single identifiable author writing one final text. To see the conclusions at work, we will follow Egyptian literature from the plant that supplied its writing surface through the work of scribes, excavators, philologists, and translators.

## Why a written story can vanish

The Egyptians made writing surfaces from papyrus, a tall wetland plant growing especially in the Nile environment. Workers prepared strips of the stem, laid them in crossing layers, pressed and dried them, and joined the sheets into rolls. The resulting material was relatively light, flexible, and suited to ink. It was excellent for accounting, letters, teachings, legal documents, and literary stories. It was far more vulnerable to water, mold, insects, fire, and careless handling than an inscription carved into stone.

A piece of papyrus found in a dry sealed context may survive for thousands of years. A similar piece left in a damp settlement deposit may disappear almost completely. In other words, the number of manuscripts we have today is controlled partly by ancient literary activity and partly by a long series of accidents of preservation. A town with many readers could leave fewer surviving texts than a dry tomb in which a few rolls happened to be stored. Treating the archive as a representative sample of everything people read would therefore be a serious mistake.

The University College London project *Digital Egypt for Universities*, drawing on work by Stephen Quirke and others, emphasizes how small the surviving corpus is when measured against the length of Egyptian history. Its discussion of literary survival lists only a handful of Middle Kingdom tales preserved complete. Yet fragments recovered at Lahun include compositions otherwise unknown. The contrast is telling: an archaeological fragment proves that someone once possessed a text, even if no complete version survives. Every preserved title may stand beside many lost ones.

The survival problem is shaped by usage as well as climate. A roll read repeatedly may fray or break. A practice passage written on a shard of pottery or flake of limestone might be discarded in a heap where it remains dry. A carefully stored roll may survive with its columns in order, but the room containing it might be robbed centuries later. A museum collection might preserve an entire manuscript without recording its original position in the ground. These outcomes provide different amounts of information. Archaeology asks what physical circumstances produced the evidence we now hold, not only what the visible text says.

Papyrus was not the only surface. Egyptians wrote and practiced writing on ostraca, the convenient fragments of pottery and limestone that could receive ink. They wrote on wooden tablets. They used hieroglyphs in monumental contexts and cursive scripts for many everyday and literary purposes. The archive is thus dispersed across materials with different chances of survival. A school exercise on limestone, a literary roll in a tomb, and a royal inscription on a temple wall cannot be assumed to represent the same audience or purpose.

## What a scribe actually wrote

People often imagine ancient Egyptian writing as elaborate carved pictures: birds, reeds, eyes, and people in profile. That is hieroglyphic writing, especially familiar from monuments. But many Egyptian literary manuscripts were copied in hieratic, a more cursive way of writing signs with a brush or reed pen and ink. The relationship between hieroglyphic and hieratic writing is comparable to the relationship between formal and cursive writing systems in other cultures, though the signs and conventions have their own history.

To read a hieratic story, a scholar must identify the marks as signs belonging to a script and language. This task is called paleography when it concentrates on the forms and development of the handwriting. It is not always as easy as recognizing a modern printed letter. Signs change over time. A quick stroke can compress several elements of a formal sign. Adjacent signs can touch. Ink can fade or flake away. A modern editor should distinguish a fully visible sign, a probable reading, and a restoration supplied because the material is damaged.

The task involves grammar too. Egyptian changed across centuries, and the literary works we call Middle Egyptian and Late Egyptian differ in vocabulary and grammatical habits. A story's date of copying, its language register, and the probable time of its composition need not all coincide. A scribe in a later century could copy an earlier classic. Egyptian writing also ordinarily omitted the vowels in ways that make the familiar modern forms of many names conventional approximations. When we pronounce Anubis, Bata, or Sinuhe in English, we are speaking a modern rendering of an ancient written name.

Imagine three adjacent groups of marks. One is intact, one partly smudged, and one lost where the edge of the roll has broken. The editor might read the first directly, identify most of the second from visible strokes, and propose a restoration for the third from grammar and context. Those are three different evidentiary claims. A responsible printed edition marks them differently. If a translator converts all three into seamless English sentences, the reader may hear a confident story while missing the difference between what was present and what was supplied.

Egyptologists have developed conventions for showing these degrees of security in editions. Transcription represents the characters read from the manuscript, sometimes converting hieratic into more legible hieroglyphic equivalents. Transliteration represents the signs with a scholarly alphabet so their linguistic values can be compared. Translation expresses the resulting sense in another language. Each stage can be challenged independently. A disagreement about a word's English connotations is different from a disagreement about which signs appear on the original roll.

## Why red ink is evidence too

On many Egyptian manuscripts the main writing is black, while particular words or marks appear in red. Modern editors often call these red-written elements rubrics. They may help divide sections, mark headings, emphasize words, or signal a special role within a composition. Their meaning must be determined from the particular text rather than inferred from the color alone.

An edition that copies the words but ignores the red ink can lose part of the ancient organization of the page. Imagine listening to a long story without being told where a chapter begins, where a speech changes, or where a scribe has made a deliberate break. The words may still be correct, yet the performance structure changes. In some manuscripts we can study how the original reader's eye was guided through a text. That structure can inform how we divide a modern translation into paragraphs or scenes.

The physical roll contains other information that vanishes easily in continuous modern prose. Sheets have joins. Writing can be crowded near an edge. Columns can be corrected, erased, or overwritten. A scribe might leave a colophon after the final line, and a later hand might add a note. These features are evidence of production and use. The roll has a biography as an object, even before we begin the story it carries.

## One manuscript, one named scribe

Papyrus D'Orbiney is a particularly vivid example. The British Museum holds it under the registration EA 10183. It contains *The Tale of Two Brothers*, in nineteen pages of Late Egyptian hieratic. The collection record gives an approximate Nineteenth Dynasty date and identifies a scribe named Inena, often written Ennana in modern discussions. A memorandum mentioning a royal heir, later Seti the Second, provides an additional historical clue to when this copy was produced.

Here is the first attribution problem. Saying that Inena copied the manuscript is supported by a named scribal record. Saying that he invented the story is a different claim. We lack independent evidence identifying the original composer. A copyist might copy closely, rearrange an inherited account, repair wording he regarded as defective, or work from a model already altered by generations of transmission. The name at the end secures a human link in the chain. It does not automatically identify the beginning of that chain.

There is a second attribution problem. The papyrus entered the British Museum through an 1857 purchase from Elizabeth d'Orbiney. Its modern name comes from that collector. University College London's study of Ramesside book groups discusses links among the D'Orbiney, Sallier, and Anastasi manuscripts, including names of scribes and textual relationships suggesting a shared scribal milieu associated with Memphis or Saqqara. The manuscripts might have been deposited in related circumstances. But without a documented original excavation, the exact circumstances cannot now be established securely.

Suppose we had been given a photograph of the roll beside a labeled pottery jar in a particular tomb chamber. We could ask whether the roll belonged to someone buried there, whether other texts were stored with it, and how the deposition relates to the date of the manuscript. Instead we possess a valuable copy whose path from the ground to the nineteenth-century collector is incompletely known. The difference affects what we can say about the story's readers. It does not prevent us from reading the text. It limits a particular class of archaeological inference.

In this case the most reliable positive claims concern the manuscript's identity, language, handwriting, date range, and the story's preserved sequence. Questions about its original owner's social identity, the precise place where it was deposited, or how widely the story circulated in oral form remain open. That is a practical example of assigning confidence by claim rather than declaring an entire object either reliable or unreliable.

## A second manuscript whose ending names a writer

Consider the *Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor*, another ancient Egyptian narrative. Its main witness is Papyrus Hermitage 1115, a Middle Kingdom hieratic manuscript. The story concerns a failed voyage, a mysterious island, and a speaking serpent. It ends with a colophon identifying a writer, Amenaa, son of Ameny, and describing the text as complete from beginning to end as found in writing.

The formula is fascinating because it shows a concern with the integrity of the written composition. Richard Parkinson and other scholars have studied the role of such closing statements. A scribe could represent the work as an exact copy or a complete account. This does not guarantee that every surviving manuscript is free from error. It does show that Egyptian writers could care about reproducing an identifiable work, and could make that concern visible to readers.

Notice the comparison with *Two Brothers*. Both manuscripts provide us with a particular ancient copy. Both offer a name connected to copying. Neither name, by itself, solves every question about the first telling. The distinction lets us credit ancient scribes accurately, rather than denying them recognition or granting them an authorship that the surviving evidence does not establish.

Names can carry prestige too. Some Egyptian teachings present themselves as the words of a famous official or ruler from an earlier time. The text may take a learned figure as its speaker. In such a case it is essential to ask whether we have a historical author's signature, a literary frame, or a later attribution. Stephen Quirke's discussion of authorship in Egyptian literature provides examples of why the modern expectation of one author producing one fixed text can be misleading.

## What multiple copies can teach us

Now compare a different situation: *The Story of Sinuhe*. In this Middle Kingdom narrative, an Egyptian courtier flees abroad after the death of a king, lives for years in foreign lands, and seeks permission to return and be buried in Egypt. Its literary power has kept it prominent in modern anthologies. It also survives through a much more complicated manuscript tradition than *The Tale of Two Brothers*.

James P. Allen's treatment in *Middle Egyptian Literature* describes five Middle Kingdom papyrus witnesses, two New Kingdom papyri, and numerous New Kingdom ostraca, many fragmentary. No one manuscript supplies a perfectly complete, transparent text from which every feature can be read without judgment. Scholars compare the witnesses, identify where one preserves a passage absent in another, and assess variants in wording and scribal practice.

Take a hypothetical sentence in Sinuhe's return story. One manuscript preserves a verb with a clear subject. Another has a similar phrase but a damaged ending. A third, later exercise copy omits the line altogether. Which version should a translator use? The answer requires more than counting surviving copies. A late school copy might be short because a student copied only a selected passage. A variant might arise from an error, but it might also reflect a deliberate adaptation. Editors examine manuscript date, context, grammar, and the relationships among readings. They can sometimes reconstruct a strong probable text, but the result remains a scholarly edition rather than a newly discovered autograph.

In this situation, we gain something that *Two Brothers* does not offer in the same way: evidence of repeated copying across periods. The variations allow us to study circulation and reception. Repeated exercise copies suggest that certain literary passages mattered to scribal education. They do not prove that every Egyptian child read them. Literacy, institutional training, and access to texts were uneven. The educational evidence is strongest for the people and settings actually represented by the surviving copies.

This is also why a text with many surviving witnesses can be harder to edit than a single-witness tale. A single roll preserves a concrete sequence and often makes editorial choices less numerous, but it gives us few independent checks on whether a puzzling line has been altered in transmission. Multiple copies can reveal where a reading differs, yet they introduce the problem of deciding among those differences. Evidence becomes richer and interpretation becomes more demanding at the same time.

## From manuscript to edition to spoken English

The modern recovery of Egyptian literature required the decipherment of Egyptian writing and the patient work of later generations. Thomas Young and Jean-François Champollion made different contributions to understanding the scripts. The Rosetta Stone was crucial to the wider decipherment story, but recognizing signs on a damaged hieratic roll is an additional task. Reading an Egyptian story involves knowing the historical grammar and its literary conventions as well as recognizing the writing system.

In the nineteenth century, Emmanuel de Rougé examined *Two Brothers*, Charles Wycliffe Goodwin wrote about hieratic papyri, and Samuel Birch helped bring Egyptian manuscripts into publication. P. Le Page Renouf produced an early English treatment. Their editions and translations expanded access, and later Egyptologists corrected and extended the readings. Alan Gardiner's *Late-Egyptian Stories* supplied an influential scholarly transcription. Miriam Lichtheim, Edward Wente, Susan Tower Hollis, and others translated or interpreted the work in the context of Egyptian literature and mythology.

The sequence of work is important. A photograph or facsimile lets scholars inspect the physical marks. A transcription states how the editor reads the signs. A grammatical analysis explains why the words yield a proposed sentence. A translation chooses an English expression suitable for its readership. A literary interpretation asks what the sentences accomplish in the story. These are distinct achievements, often involving different researchers. Naming them makes it easier to identify whose claim is being discussed.

Consider the choice of translating a place name as the Valley of the Pine or the Valley of the Cedar in *Two Brothers*. The different English trees may reflect decisions about how to identify an Egyptian word and its cultural setting. The core plot is secure: Bata's heart is hidden in a tree, and the cutting of the tree threatens his life. An exact botanical identification would require further linguistic and historical analysis. In an audio narration we can tell the plot clearly while letting the supplementary research notes record the translation issue.

Another example is the name of the older brother: Anubis in familiar English, or Anpu in closer Egyptological forms. The difference does not create a second character. It reminds us that names pass through scholarly conventions before reaching our ears. A lecture can choose one form and explain it once; a serious reader of translations should be able to recognize the alternatives.

A translation can also impose a tone. Archaic English may make all Egyptian characters sound ceremonial. Highly colloquial English may remove the distance between a modern reader and an ancient court. Translators must decide how to render wordplay, titles, speech patterns, and repetitions. A comparison of two competent translations can therefore be informative even when their readings of the Egyptian signs agree. The difference may reflect a decision about style rather than a historical disagreement.

## The damage that acquisition history can leave

The path through modern collecting is part of the evidence history. Nineteenth-century antiquities markets moved manuscripts from Egypt into European museums and private collections, often before systematic archaeological recording was standard. A purchase date may be well documented even when the original findspot is not. Some collections preserve old labels, letters, and account books that assist later provenance research; others leave substantial gaps.

This is a reason to credit excavators, antiquities workers, sellers, collectors, museum conservators, and the ancient Egyptian people who created the texts according to their actual roles. A celebrated modern editor may have contributed an indispensable reading. The scribe who copied the papyrus made its survival possible in a different way. A person who recovered or preserved the roll contributed to its material history. Combining all those roles into one discoverer's name conceals the chain on which our knowledge depends.

The physical object can continue to yield evidence after publication. Conservators record material condition, support fragile sheets, and use imaging to make faint traces easier to examine. But imaging cannot conjure lost writing from a region of papyrus that no longer exists. A new photograph may correct an editor's sign reading. It cannot automatically recover an earlier oral version for which no trace survives. The limits of the method are part of its usefulness.

## A worked test of a historical claim

Let's apply the method to a sentence someone might say about Egyptian literature: “The Tale of Two Brothers was written by Inena at Saqqara for the royal court.” It sounds admirably precise. But it combines several claims whose evidentiary basis differs.

First, Inena is named as the scribe of the surviving manuscript. That part is supported by the papyrus. Second, the approximate date of the copy follows from manuscript analysis and associated historical clues. Third, Saqqara belongs to a scholarly reconstruction of relationships among Ramesside manuscript groups; the exact recovery context of D'Orbiney was not documented. Fourth, royal-court performance is an appealing possibility given the text's interest in kingship, but no surviving program tells us precisely who heard this copy read aloud. Fifth, the claim that Inena composed the first version is not established by his copyist's name.

A more faithful account would say: a late Nineteenth Dynasty manuscript of *The Tale of Two Brothers* survives in the hand of a scribe named Inena, and scholars have linked it to a wider group of Ramesside literary papyri. This is still informative. In fact, it permits productive next questions: what supports the scribal grouping, how are scripts dated, and which manuscript features point toward particular audiences? Precision comes from separating the assertions before joining them into a history.

Try the same exercise with Sinuhe. We know the story was copied many times because we possess multiple physical witnesses. We know that some copies are associated with scribal training because they survive on educational writing surfaces and in relevant contexts. We do not know every person who heard the story, how it sounded in every performance, or whether all episodes had exactly one form throughout its life. The surviving evidence supports a history of transmission and popularity within particular learned settings. It cannot supply a complete census of readers.

## What the papyrus lets us hear

It is easy to end a study of evidence by focusing only on losses: missing excavation records, damaged columns, unknown first authors, unrecorded performances. Those limits are important, but the positive achievements are substantial. A scribe in Egypt placed signs on a plant-based surface. The object survived. Scholars learned to identify its script and language. They published transcriptions, checked words against other Egyptian sources, and created translations that invite further debate. We can now recognize narrative structures and cultural references that remained inaccessible to most readers for centuries.

We can credit Inena for the copy of *Two Brothers* while withholding an invented attribution of its first composition. We can credit Amenaa for the physical witness to the *Shipwrecked Sailor*. We can credit the researchers who traced *Sinuhe* through papyri and ostraca and explained its grammatical variations. We can also acknowledge that the Egyptian authors and audiences whose names are lost were real people participating in literary traditions far larger than the handful of rolls that happen to survive.

The result is more than the ability to repeat an ancient plot. We can explain why this particular plot is available, what kind of object carried it, which people turned it into a readable edition, how a proposed English sentence can be checked, and why a manuscript date differs from the date when a story was invented. These are transferable skills. They help us ask better questions of a Mesopotamian tablet, a Maya manuscript, a Chinese bamboo slip, or a medieval codex.

The next time an ancient story reaches us as polished English narration, there is a worthwhile question behind the pleasure of listening. Whose handwriting survived, where did the object come from, and which readings make this version possible? For Papyrus D'Orbiney we can answer much of that question, and we can identify precisely where the evidence runs out. That is how an ancient work becomes both a story to enjoy and a document whose history can be investigated.
