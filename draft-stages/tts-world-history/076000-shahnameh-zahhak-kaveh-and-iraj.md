# 076000 — Shahnameh: Zahhak, the Blacksmith, and the Murder of Iraj

# Shahnameh: Zahhak, the Blacksmith, and the Murder of Iraj

A king sits on a throne with two snakes growing from his shoulders. Every day the snakes must be fed, and the food they demand is human brains. Far from the palace, a blacksmith hears that another of his children is to be taken for the king's kitchens. He has suffered too much already. He will enter the royal court and demand justice. That moment is one of the great turning points in Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*: the encounter between a ruler who has made terror the foundation of his reign and a working man who will no longer pretend to respect him.

Before that encounter, there is a history of the king's corruption. Zahhak is a prince whose father has a kingdom and who is susceptible to the attentions of an evil counselor, identified in the epic with the demonic power Ahriman. The counselor approaches him in disguise, first making betrayal seem reasonable, then offering pleasures and skills as rewards for obedience. The prince is drawn into the murder of his father. Later the same corrupting power enters his household in another guise, wins Zahhak's gratitude, and asks to kiss his shoulders. From the places touched by those kisses grow the two serpents. The appearance of the snakes is grotesque, but their purpose is precise. Zahhak's political wrongdoing becomes a bodily burden that can be sustained only by constant injury to other people.

When court physicians cannot remove the serpents, a demonic adviser returns with a remedy: feed them the brains of young people. The king's appetite has become an institution. Families receive demands they cannot refuse. The kitchens require victims; officers must find them; an entire society learns to live with an atrocity repeated so frequently that it becomes part of the working calendar. The evil is no longer concentrated in the intentions of one man. It depends on obedient attendants, fearful subjects, and a routine of selection and delivery. This is why the kitchen is nearly as frightening as the snakes. The danger has acquired an administrative form.

Two compassionate men obtain positions among the cooks. They cannot openly end the killings, but they find a way to save some of the condemned. One captive is spared while an animal's brain is substituted for the required food. The survivor is sent into the mountains to hide. The story then connects these rescued young people with communities living beyond the king's immediate reach. This is a remarkable answer to the scale of the horror. The cooks cannot overthrow the tyrant, and the narrative does not ask us to mistake their partial resistance for complete justice. Yet the small act matters to the person who survives. The kingdom has places where mercy persists, and the survival of those places makes rebellion imaginable.

## The dream of a boy yet unborn

Zahhak does not feel secure. A prophecy and a nightmare disturb him. He sees a young challenger who will rise against him. Court interpreters explain that the coming hero, Fereydun, will bring about his fall. The king responds as frightened rulers in epics often do: he tries to prevent the future by making the present more brutal. Agents hunt for the child and for those who shelter him. The result is an inversion of sovereignty. The king claims to protect a realm, yet uses the machinery of the realm to pursue a child.

Fereydun's father falls victim to the terror. His mother, Faranak, protects the infant. For a time the child is fed by an extraordinary cow, whose milk becomes the means of preserving him. When danger approaches, his mother moves him again, seeking refuge with a religious protector away from the king's men. The cow is found and killed. The image is painful because it enlarges the circle of Zahhak's violence: a creature whose role was nourishment becomes one more victim of a frightened ruler. Fereydun survives through the combined care of his mother, animal protectors, and people who risk sheltering him. He is the opposite of the man who sits on the throne. Zahhak receives life by consuming the young; the future king receives life because others feed and defend him.

When Fereydun reaches maturity, his mother tells him who he is and what happened to his father. The young man is eager to fight immediately. Faranak counsels patience. Her advice prevents a simple heroic formula in which rage itself is sufficient qualification to rule. A just grievance can become an unwise campaign if the injured person has no supporters or plan. Fereydun must learn how power works before confronting it. The child saved by a cow will eventually carry a weapon, often described as a bull-headed mace, whose shape keeps the story's imagery of protection and animal life close to the image of his kingship.

Meanwhile Zahhak fears the growing rumor of an avenger. He attempts to secure the appearance of popular assent. Officials are asked to certify the king's justice. It is a scene of coercive public theater: the ruler wants his subjects to affirm that a system built on murder is lawful and good. Here the blacksmith enters the story. His name is Kaveh. One of his sons is about to be taken for the snakes; others have already been lost. The court expects him to bow and accept the king's version of events. Instead he cries out against Zahhak before the assembled notables.

## The kitchens and the cost of limited resistance

The two cooks who save some of Zahhak's intended victims deserve more than a passing mention. Their position is morally desperate. They stand inside the machinery of killing. A ruler whose serpents demand human brains has created a steady supply of victims, and the men have access to those victims only because they work in the very place where the deaths are organized. To refuse all participation openly might get them killed and replaced. To obey in full would mean becoming agents of a repeated atrocity. They devise a partial deception that saves one person at a time and allows that person to escape into the mountains.

The ethical difficulty cannot be solved by imagining that the cooks have unlimited options. They are not the ruler; they do not command an army. Their act is meaningful because it preserves actual lives under immediate danger. But the act also leaves the larger system running. Every rescued youth is a real success, and every person still taken to the kitchen is a real failure of the social order. The story is more moving if we refuse to turn the men into triumphant liberators before the liberation has occurred. Theirs is a smaller, perilous form of resistance whose effects are measured in people who can grow old because they were spared.

The movement of survivors into the mountains also reverses the expected meaning of wilderness. In the story of the first kings, the mountains were dangerous because society had not yet been made secure. Under Zahhak, the royal center has become dangerous, and the wilderness is where threatened people find a chance to live. The tyrant's palace contains all the splendors of government but offers less safety than a remote shelter. The physical geography registers a political inversion: civilization's center has ceased to perform the protective work that justified its construction.

Fereydun's childhood belongs to this pattern. He survives by being moved beyond the places where the ruler's power is strongest, and by receiving care that cannot be commanded by the royal officers. The cow that feeds him is another symbol of nonroyal sustenance. Zahhak can order the animal killed, but he has already lost the contest for the child's survival. His fear makes him powerful enough to murder and too blind to prevent the future. In literary terms, every attempt to secure his reign by violence produces more reasons for people to wish it ended.

## Kaveh turns his apron into a standard

The blacksmith's accusation is direct because it comes from experience. He does not begin by discussing royal genealogy or the proper distribution of provinces. He asks why his children must die. When he is told to endorse a document praising the king, he refuses and tears up the false testimony. His defiance breaks the ceremonial order of the court. A person whose work has made him a maker of useful objects is suddenly an accuser standing before the highest power. Zahhak, facing a public refusal he cannot easily turn into assent, lets the surviving son go. Kaveh leaves the palace still furious and calls the people to resist.

He fixes his leather blacksmith's apron to a spear and lifts it as a banner. This is the beginning of the standard later celebrated as the Kaveh banner, or Derafsh-e Kaviani. An apron is a working garment, worn to keep hot fragments and sparks from striking the smith's body. It becomes a visible sign that the revolt is grounded in ordinary labor and ordinary households. Over time the banner is adorned with precious things and made a royal emblem, but its origin remains a protest. The poem imagines the legitimacy of kingship as something that can be rekindled outside the court by someone who has no crown.

Kaveh and those who join him seek Fereydun. The young challenger now possesses not just a hereditary claim and a prophecy but a political constituency. Zahhak's tyranny has supplied the cause that gathers people around him. Fereydun advances, crosses difficult country, and enters the great center of Zahhak's rule while the tyrant is absent. He confronts the women and captives of the household, including figures linked to the old royal order. The tale does not make these people mere ornaments. Their captivity and release are signs of how Zahhak controlled the previous world's dignity, and how the new regime seeks to present itself as a restoration.

The final struggle takes place after Zahhak hears that his palace has been seized. He rushes back and attacks. Fereydun strikes him, and the triumph might seem to call for immediate execution. Instead a divine command intervenes: Zahhak is to be bound and confined beneath Mount Damavand. The story's prison is as memorable as the throne. The tyrant who once occupied the center of political life remains alive but unable to rule. The mountain holds him away from the people whose lives he consumed.

Why imprison him? An epic might answer that certain evils cannot be erased at a stroke; they must be restrained. Another answer is literary. Zahhak becomes a continuing figure in the imagination, a warning that tyranny can return when vigilance fails. The poem does not supply a modern political constitution. It offers a visible distinction between the defeat of one ruler and the more difficult work of preserving a just order after him. Fereydun will discover that even a legitimate reign cannot secure permanent peace in a family.

## The forged testimony

Kaveh's act in the royal court is powerful partly because Zahhak is trying to manipulate the form of justice. He is not merely asking the blacksmith to remain silent. He wants a public sign that his rule is legitimate and fair. A written or witnessed declaration by leading men might appear to future observers as evidence that the king enjoyed respect. The ceremony tries to manufacture consent and leave a documentary trace of it. Kaveh refuses to let his name help turn an obvious wrong into an official truth.

Imagine what it means for a bereaved father to stand in that court. He has no illusion that his children are alive elsewhere. He knows the routine through which they were taken. He can see the officers and dignitaries who benefit from the system. His accusation does not need the prestige of a learned adviser. It rests on the gulf between what the king demands that the court say and what has actually happened to families. The tearing of the document transforms that gulf into a public act. A piece of paper can be destroyed; the testimony of the harmed person cannot be so easily restored to obedience.

The symbol of the apron grows from the same logic. A banner is normally a sign of organized military power or a ruler's household. Kaveh takes an object already associated with his daily occupation and places it where a royal emblem would be. The alteration is deliberate. The state had asked the worker to add legitimacy to an unjust order. He creates a rival symbol that derives its authority from refusing the request. When precious stones and ornaments are later attached to the banner, the new dynasty claims continuity with that act. The most important material in the standard remains the leather beneath the decorations, because without it the new royal emblem would lack its story.

A historian can ask what early Iranian banner traditions contributed to this narrative and when particular images of Kaveh gained their later political significance. Those are valuable questions about transmission and reception. They should not obscure the narrative scene itself, where the man confronts the king because he wants his child released. The public emblem is made from a private demand for life. The poem holds the two scales together: a father refuses another loss, and a kingdom begins to revolt.

## The kingdom divided three ways

Fereydun's kingship begins with a profound relief. He is the victor over an enemy whose appetite for young lives had seemed endless. He has three sons, and he seeks to distribute the world among them. Their names are Salm, Tur, and Iraj. To test their natures he confronts them in disguise, and their responses display different kinds of courage and temper. He then divides the realms. Iraj, the youngest and the gentlest in this telling, receives the central Iranian land. His brothers receive other parts of the world. The distribution gives the brothers territories, but it also exposes them to comparison. Each can look at the other's inheritance and wonder whether love and status have been apportioned fairly.

Salm and Tur grow jealous. They believe Iraj has received the best portion, and their resentment hardens into a plan. Their father is old; the brothers can imagine a future in which they no longer need to disguise their hostility. Iraj, learning of their anger, offers conciliation. He is willing to renounce the advantages of kingship rather than turn his brothers into enemies. His response is not a foolish failure to notice danger. He knows that the quarrel could lead to bloodshed, and he places the unity of the family above the possession of a crown. This willingness makes his death more painful.

The brothers murder Iraj. Their action gives the epic another father grieving a son, but the circumstances differ from Siyamak's death in the first reign. Iraj is killed by the very people with whom he shares a household and ancestry. The enemy now lies within the family. Fereydun receives the news and is overwhelmed. Victory over Zahhak did not create a world in which wickedness can never occur again. One tyrant has been imprisoned, while jealousy and ambition emerge in the heirs of the liberator. The poem moves from public terror to intimate betrayal.

The later avenger is Manuchehr, descended from Iraj. He grows into a warrior and eventually takes up the obligation that Fereydun cannot fulfill in his old age. The battles against Salm and Tur become the founding conflict of a much longer division between Iran and Turan. We should not map these legendary brothers onto a modern ethnic census or assume that the geography of every retelling corresponds to a fixed ancient border. The poem tells an origin story for a political enmity. Through that story, whole realms inherit a wound from a family argument.

When Manuchehr defeats the murderers, one can understand the relief of the injured household. Yet what has been solved? Iraj does not come back. The sons of Salm and Tur will remember the victors differently from the descendants of Iraj. The story has opened a channel through which loyalty and revenge will flow for generations. A warrior may believe he is keeping an ancient promise without considering whether a particular enemy has committed the act that made the promise necessary. That tension will grow more acute when we encounter Turanian figures such as Piran, whose generosity and judgment complicate any inherited division between righteous Iran and wicked Turan.

## A division before a murder

The story of Fereydun's sons sometimes passes too quickly from a distribution of provinces to a fatal act. The division is worth lingering over. A father hopes that giving each son a kingdom will reduce competition. Each will possess land, people, and authority. Yet the arrangement requires the sons to accept both the value of what they have received and the father's judgment of their characters. Salm and Tur look at Iraj's portion through the eyes of wounded comparison. They do not simply desire territory; they believe that the distribution reveals a preference. A dispute over inheritance becomes a dispute over love.

Iraj's response has the moral quality of a peace offering. He is prepared to give up his claim if that can restore fraternity. One might see a danger in his generosity: a conciliatory person can make himself defenseless against those who read concession as an opportunity. But the poem gives the gesture its value even when it fails. The brothers' act is wrong because they murder someone who is trying to avoid their injury, not because Iraj wins a better bargain. A refusal to fight can be noble even when a more ruthless opponent exploits it.

The subsequent violence is not a simple repetition of Zahhak's crime. The old tyrant consumed young lives through an institutional appetite. Salm and Tur kill a brother through envy and anger. The political consequences are nevertheless enormous. When Manuchehr avenges Iraj, a family wrong becomes a justification for states to fight across generations. The connection can help us understand how a personal grievance acquires the appearance of an unalterable public duty. The original people die, but the names of their houses remain available for later kings to summon.

A prince born long after Iraj's murder will not have witnessed the act. He may know it as a sacred story taught from childhood, and that story can influence whom he trusts on a frontier. In the later epic, wars with Turan repeatedly invoke inherited enmity even as individual Turanians behave with fairness and individual Iranians commit wrongs. That is why the first fratricide must be remembered without making its moral conclusion universal. The murderers are responsible for murder. Their descendants and subjects must still be judged by what they do.

The stories of Zahhak and Iraj give different forms to the same warning. A tyrant can require people to ratify an injustice; a family can require descendants to remember an injury forever. Both forms of authority ask the living to accept a story that shapes their choices. Kaveh refuses the story the king wants certified. Iraj offers another story, one in which brotherhood matters more than division. The subsequent epic will keep asking which inherited story a person is prepared to challenge.

## Fathers, sons, and the claims of justice

Set Kaveh beside Fereydun. The blacksmith acts because the state has taken his sons; the rightful challenger acts because a tyrant has murdered his father and threatened him as an infant. Their causes reinforce each other, but they are not identical. A dynastic claim alone could replace Zahhak with another ruler whose first concern is his own house. Kaveh's protest insists that the lives of ordinary people are also part of the measure of justice. The banner that begins as an apron ensures that the epic's memory of liberation includes labor and loss outside the royal family.

Set Fereydun beside his own sons. The king overcomes a monstrous appetite for children, then must confront the murder of one child by others. There is a cruel symmetry. Under Zahhak, sons are destroyed because they are food for the ruler's snakes. Under Fereydun's heirs, a son is destroyed because his brothers cannot bear his favored place. Tyranny at the level of the state and violence within a household share a willingness to make other people's lives expendable for one's own advantage. The relationship between public order and family morality is one of the epic's deepest concerns.

Fereydun is a great king, but greatness does not mean that he can arrange a succession without unintended consequences. By dividing the realm he hopes to provide for all three heirs. The division becomes the object of jealousy. By preferring the gentle son he may seem morally discerning; to the brothers it becomes evidence of a slight. Iraj's generosity does not disarm men committed to entitlement. And Manuchehr's victorious retaliation, though approved as justice within the tale, leaves a divided world. No character possesses the power to ensure that an act meant as repair will end the history of injury.

The result is more complex than a fable in which good conquers evil and everyone prospers. There is a clear moral difference between saving children and feeding them to serpents. There is also a tragic recognition that righteous victories occur within histories that keep producing new quarrels. In this epic, the past is inherited. Sometimes inheritance means protection, craft, a festival, or the knowledge of writing. Sometimes it means a demand for blood. The characters who are born generations after the first murder must choose what kind of inheritance they will carry forward.

## A standard that outlives the revolt

The apron raised by Kaveh becomes a royal emblem, and that change is worth dwelling on. An object associated with ordinary labor is taken from the working life of one man and turned into a sign under which armed men can assemble. The blacksmith does not begin with an inherited palace or a genealogical claim. His authority comes from refusing to certify the murder of his children. The standard makes that refusal visible to others who might have suffered separately and remained afraid.

After Feraydun's victory, the banner acquires the decoration and prestige of royalty. A sign born in resistance can become an emblem used by kings themselves. That transformation opens a difficult question: does the new monarchy retain the ethical force of the protest, or does it merely inherit a celebrated symbol? The epic's later rulers will sometimes demand public obedience while violating the duties that made Kaveh's rebellion justified. The banner can remind them, and us, that royal legitimacy is supposed to answer to something beyond royal appetite.

The story also explains why the leather apron remains more affecting than a jeweled standard invented for display. A laborer has put his own livelihood and his own grief into the act of defiance. We need not suppose that a named blacksmith can be verified in a prehistoric royal chronology to understand the political imagination carried by the image. It allows the people who bear the burdens of a kingdom to appear as moral agents capable of judging its ruler.

## What kind of past is this?

We can ask about the historical basis of these events without confusing different questions. Zahhak's snakes, Fereydun's miraculous preservation, and the familial partition of the world belong to legendary narrative. The names and motifs have deep connections with older Iranian traditions, including religious and mythological accounts that differ in details from Ferdowsi's poem. Scholars can compare those traditions and trace continuities of names, story patterns, and the language of royal legitimacy. Such comparisons do not establish that Zahhak literally maintained a kitchen for serpents or that a blacksmith's apron can be dated as the first royal banner.

The material can still illuminate social thought. Zahhak is most frightening when oppression has become habitual administration. Kaveh is most compelling when he rejects a public lie imposed by the state. Fereydun is most moving when the victory that made his kingdom possible cannot protect his own child. The stories hold together political authority, kinship, craft labor, and the meaning of justice. They became a shared vocabulary through which later people could praise resistance or condemn tyranny. The poem's written form belongs to Ferdowsi's time, but the concerns are not confined to one period.

Notice also how it distributes attention. Zahhak is made monstrous; his court contains people with consciences. Fereydun is noble; his household produces murderers. A legendary enemy can be wholly evil without every person who serves under his roof being so. A rightful king can be worthy without every descendant deserving the same respect. These distinctions protect the epic from collapsing into a list of royal labels. Its judgments arise from conduct, and conduct can diverge sharply within a single family or state.

The blacksmith's banner passes from an act of protest into a symbol of kingship. The path is ambiguous. Perhaps it means that just sovereignty must remember the common people who restored it. Perhaps it also warns how easily a ruler can absorb a popular emblem into the decorations of a new court. The narrative does not announce a settled answer; it gives the listener both images. We see the leather lifted in anger by a grieving father, and we see the same banner made splendid as the story of kings continues.

Fereydun's age ends with a kingdom restored and a family divided. The original oppressor is confined beneath a mountain. The blacksmith's demand for justice has helped bring a new ruler to the throne. Yet the newly established world has acquired a feud that will outlast the people who began it. The descendants of Iraj and Tur will meet as princes, warriors, lovers, and enemies, often unable to free themselves from this inheritance. A story that began with a father's struggle to save his children has become a question about whether people can stop asking their children to avenge them.

## Editorial and source notes

Retelling follows the Zahhak, Kaveh and Fereydun stories through Iraj, Salm, Tur and Manuchehr. Core motifs have Iranian pre-Islamic parallels but differ across texts. Avoid literalizing regional or ethnic labels. Kaveh's rebellion, Zahhak's shoulder-serpents, the bull-mace, Fereydun's apportionment and the murder of Iraj are narrative features, not datable political events.
