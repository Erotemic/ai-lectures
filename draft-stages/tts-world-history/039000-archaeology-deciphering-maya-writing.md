# Deciphering Maya Writing: How a Calendar Became a History

Imagine standing in front of a stone monument in a Maya city. A ruler appears in elaborate clothing. Around the figure, rectangular blocks contain carved heads, dots and bars, curls, and shapes that look like pieces of a picture. Some blocks may record dates separated by centuries. Others seem to name people. For a long time, outside scholars could identify some of the numbers, and yet could not read what the people themselves were saying. The monument was plainly deliberate, expensive, and public. Its message remained mostly closed to readers who did not inherit the writing tradition.

Now imagine a different kind of discovery. You already know how to read the dates. You line up monuments from one city and notice that their dates cluster into spans about as long as a human life. The first in a group seems to announce a beginning, and a later one an ending. You propose that these are the births, accessions, and deaths of named rulers. Then the glyphs around those dates start making sense as history. The carvings cease to be an abstract calendar and become records of people who governed, fought, negotiated, and died.

That second discovery is associated with Tatiana Proskouriakoff, whose 1960 paper transformed the study of Classic Maya inscriptions. It was not the only breakthrough. Yuri Knorozov had already argued that the writing represented sounds as well as words. Heinrich Berlin had recognized distinctive signs associated with particular kingdoms. Together with decades of subsequent work by Maya and non-Maya scholars, these approaches brought a largely unread script back into conversation.

This is the history of that decipherment, and a demonstration of how decipherment works. We will look at the objects, the mistakes, the useful mistakes, and the tests that made a proposed reading more than an attractive guess. We should also begin by acknowledging the people who produced the original writing: ancient Maya scribes, sculptors, patrons, and readers. They made the texts for their own communities. Modern decipherers are reconstructing an intellectual system that those communities had already built.

## An archive carved in public

Maya writing developed in Mesoamerica. In its most extensive surviving Classic-period use, roughly from the third to the ninth centuries of the common era, it appeared on stone monuments, buildings, ceramics, shell, bone, and other objects. Books made from bark paper were another medium. Most books did not survive the centuries and the destruction associated with colonization. Only a few pre-Hispanic screenfold codices are preserved, and their contents and dates differ from the stone inscriptions we are going to study.

The writing system is usually described as logosyllabic. Some signs represent whole words, often called logograms. Others represent syllables, commonly consonant followed by vowel. A scribe could write a word with a logogram, with a sequence of syllabic signs, or with a combination that supplies phonetic information. The signs can be artistically elaborate and have alternative forms. A human or animal head may function as a spelling sign rather than simply illustrating the text. Recognizing that distinction changes everything.

Maya scribes arranged signs in blocks, often read in paired columns across and then down the page or monument. A block can contain a large central sign and smaller ones attached around it. That is one reason a beginner cannot simply assign one modern letter to each carved square and expect a sentence. The system does not have the structure of an alphabet. It also has rules of its own for spelling vowels and choosing signs. Modern epigraphers learn to identify the constituent signs, propose their sounds or words, and reconstruct the language expressed by a particular text.

There is more than one Maya language. The languages spoken today across southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, and neighboring regions form a family. Classic monumental writing especially preserves forms associated with Ch'olan languages; other sources and periods involve other Mayan languages. The script is not interchangeable with the Latin-letter K'iche' manuscript that preserves the Popol Wuj. Those are different texts in different historical settings. But the language family, including the expertise of present-day speakers, helps researchers test interpretations of ancient words and grammar.

A stone monument also has a findspot. The plaza in which it stood, the building to which it was related, its artistic style, and any excavated associated objects matter. Some monuments have been moved, looted, or broken. The text may survive in a museum but its archaeological setting may not. A transcription can preserve glyphs while losing information about which building or political ceremony they once addressed. Epigraphy and excavation therefore work best when each informs the other.

## Calendar first, history later

Before researchers could read sentences, they made real progress with Maya numeration and calendars. The bars and dots are strikingly legible once their numerical values are known: a dot represents one, and a bar five, while a shell-like sign is used for zero in several contexts. Long Count dates record accumulated time in a structured positional scheme. Other calendars organize recurring cycles. The combination allowed scholars to place many inscriptions in an absolute chronological framework, subject to the correlation connecting Maya and European calendar dates.

John Lloyd Stephens and Frederick Catherwood popularized detailed accounts and drawings of Maya ruins in the nineteenth century, helping establish the scale of the surviving record. Their work depended on local knowledge and labor often poorly named in published accounts. The monuments had been known to the people living around them; Stephens and Catherwood did not make them exist by visiting. Alfred Maudslay's later photographs, casts, and measured drawings became important sources for research because weathering can damage inscriptions after they are first recorded.

Ernst Förstemann, studying surviving Maya manuscripts, made major advances in understanding the numerical and astronomical content of the Dresden Codex. Scholars including Joseph Goodman and Juan Martínez Hernández helped develop the calendar correlation and historical chronology used by later researchers; debates and refinements have continued. These achievements were substantial even when longer texts remained unread. A decipherment that begins with numbers can establish a firm skeleton of dated events before the words between those dates are understood.

But the apparent success of a calendar-based approach created a trap. J. Eric S. Thompson became an influential interpreter of Maya writing. He made important contributions to cataloging glyphs and to understanding calendar and religious material; his sign numbering system gave scholars a shared vocabulary. Yet he strongly favored the idea that most non-calendrical signs were symbolic or ideographic, with limited phonetic content. He was also skeptical that inscriptions described the deeds of ordinary mortal rulers.

A method can work brilliantly in one domain and mislead when extended too far. If you can read the date on a stone, you may start to see every nearby sign as another element of astronomy, ritual, or theology. A theory can absorb ambiguous evidence for years, especially when its most respected proponent is also responsible for useful tools. The answer is not to dismiss Thompson's real achievements. It is to ask which predictions his broader interpretation made, and what happened when someone tested them.

## A colonial note that looked like nonsense

The sixteenth-century Franciscan Diego de Landa wrote an account of Yucatán that included a set of Maya signs paired with Spanish letters or letter names. Landa was involved in the violent colonial repression of Indigenous ritual and books. The record he left is therefore both an indispensable source and a product of a destructive system. Its preservation does not erase the loss it helped accompany.

For generations, attempts to use Landa's list as a straightforward alphabet failed. The sign associated with an apparent letter did not reliably function as that letter in inscriptions. The problem was partly the question being asked. A Spanish speaker could have requested an equivalent for a named letter, and a Maya informant could have supplied a sign for a spoken syllable resembling that letter's name. A letter called something like 'bay' in one language might invite a syllabic representation, even though it is not evidence that the script has an alphabetic letter B.

Yuri Valentinovich Knorozov, working in the Soviet Union, reconsidered the material in 1952. He argued that Maya writing mixed meaningful whole-word signs with phonetic elements and that Landa's apparent alphabet should be approached as evidence for syllabic values. He did not need to excavate a new inscription to see this possibility. He needed an explanation that made the old evidence behave coherently. Later papers and books developed the analysis, and his work reached English-language scholars through translation and advocacy by researchers including Sophie and Michael Coe.

Consider a purely hypothetical example. Suppose a language has a word pronounced 'kala.' One scribe writes a symbol meaning the whole word. Another writes two signs that stand for 'ka' and 'la.' A third writes the whole-word sign with an extra 'la' sign to guide pronunciation. If we assume that every sign is a picture-idea, these three spellings seem unrelated. If we test the phonetic hypothesis, a pattern emerges. We can then check whether the same 'ka' sign occurs in other words with the corresponding sound. One successful word is suggestive; a growing network of correct predictions across inscriptions is a decipherment.

The actual script is more complex than the example. Syllables have variant spellings; word-final consonants and vowel conventions are not always represented in the way a beginner expects. Languages change. Some signs are damaged. But the basic test persists: a proposed value should help read additional texts that were not used to invent it. Knorozov's decisive contribution was to make phonetic reading a productive research program rather than an eccentric option.

The Cold War complicated how the work was received. Some Western researchers resisted it for intellectual reasons, some for political reasons, and some because existing interpretations seemed well established. The story is often compressed into a lone genius defeating an establishment. The actual history includes correspondence, translation, arguments over specific signs, and collaborators who changed their minds over time. We can credit Knorozov's breakthrough without pretending that a single 1952 article instantly deciphered the surviving corpus.

## The queen of the dates

Tatiana Proskouriakoff approached the problem from art and archaeology rather than from a formal linguistic training. Trained in architecture, she developed careful drawings and reconstructions of Maya monuments, including work at Piedras Negras in Guatemala. She became interested in patterns among the dated stelae at that site. Some monuments appeared in series associated with the same architectural groups, and the distribution of dates suggested the period of a ruler's life rather than merely the repetition of cosmic cycles.

In her 1960 paper, 'Historical Implications of a Pattern of Dates at Piedras Negras, Guatemala,' Proskouriakoff made a disciplined inference. She identified date patterns consistent with births, accessions, and deaths and proposed that inscriptions recorded dynastic events. Her interpretation predicted that similarly structured texts elsewhere would contain historical information. Subsequent work at Yaxchilan extended the case. This did not supply all the phonetic values. It established that researchers had been wrong about what many inscriptions were doing.

Notice the logic. She did not start by knowing the name of a ruler and guessing which glyph must say it. She noticed a statistical and spatial pattern in dated monuments. A person is born, takes office, and eventually dies; a monument series can reflect that sequence. If multiple groups exhibit comparable patterns and correspond to changes in imagery or building use, the historical interpretation becomes stronger. The dates put constraints on what the surrounding signs can mean.

Around the same time, Heinrich Berlin identified what became known as emblem glyphs, titles associated with particular dynastic centers or political identities. The word 'emblem' was a provisional label, not a declaration that he had completely translated every title. A sign appearing repeatedly with a dynasty allows scholars to track the political geography of inscriptions, including cases where a ruler of one city is named on a monument in another. Later research refined what these titles encoded, including an important relationship to royal and place names.

Together, the approaches reinforced each other. Berlin's signs helped recognize political affiliations; Proskouriakoff's historical pattern helped recognize the events; Knorozov's phonetics helped identify the sounds and linguistic structure of the names and titles. The result was an empirical cycle. A suggested reading made predictions about additional monuments. New inscriptions might support, refine, or overturn the reading. A ruler's name could reappear in different artistic spellings and still denote the same person.

## The breakthrough keeps happening

Linda Schele brought enormous energy to the study and teaching of glyphs and collaborated with researchers working on grammar, political history, and art. Floyd Lounsbury applied linguistic methods to readings and names. Peter Mathews, David Stuart, Stephen Houston, Nikolai Grube, and many colleagues made important contributions to increasingly detailed reconstructions of Classic-period history. In the early 1980s, Stuart demonstrated the importance of multiple spellings and the substitution of phonetic signs. That gave scholars a practical way to recognize a word even when its written appearance changed radically.

A carved name can be a remarkable compression of information. Suppose a ruler is named in a dedication to a building and again in a battle record. If an inscription uses one logogram for part of the name and a second inscription spells the same element with syllables, the two texts provide a check on each other. If the dates also fit one lifetime and the art shows consistent titles, confidence increases. But a reused throne name or a damaged sign may still invite alternatives. Good epigraphy often consists of revising an already plausible reading after an inconvenient new inscription turns up.

The discovery and recording of new texts continues. Modern documentation uses controlled photography, three-dimensional imaging, drawings, digital catalogs, and linguistic databases. Scholars can compare monuments that reside in different countries or were removed from their original settings. Digital access helps, but it cannot reconstruct the lost portion of a broken inscription on its own. A photograph may show a surface more clearly than an old drawing while still leaving an uncertain sign. Editors should mark that uncertainty rather than writing a smooth sentence that disguises it.

There is also an essential change in who participates. Maya scholars and speakers interpret inscriptions about their ancestors and teach the writing system in communities for whom Maya history is a living subject. The Smithsonian's 'Living Maya Time' project, for example, presents readings with work by the Mexican scholar Erik Velásquez and foregrounds contemporary Maya cultures. Epigraphic knowledge is strengthened by linguistic expertise and by decisions about how findings are shared, taught, and displayed. The script's history cannot be reduced to a story about Europeans rediscovering something nobody else cared about.

## A worked example: a royal accession

Let us construct a small hypothetical case study from the kinds of evidence scholars actually handle. In a plaza, archaeologists document three monuments. The first bears a Long Count date we can convert into a year. The second stands decades later and repeats one intricate title block. The third, erected after another interval, introduces a different personal sign combination but retains an emblem title associated with the place.

At first we can securely say only that the monuments were commissioned at different times and that some signs repeat. Next we notice that a specific event sign comes shortly before the repeated title. Comparative epigraphy suggests that it denotes accession to rulership. We test that proposal against inscriptions at other sites, asking whether dates and associated royal names behave as we would expect. If they do, we have learned something about the event even before every name can be pronounced.

Now the phonetic analysis begins. One royal name uses a logogram whose pronunciation is known from other contexts. Another text writes a matching element with two phonetic signs. The grammar indicates that the same title follows both. We infer the relation between spellings and compare the sign sequences against the language reconstructed for that period. A proposed sound that explains both texts has earned its place through repeatability, not through resemblance to a modern word in English or Spanish.

Excavation contributes a different test. The second monument's base is sealed within a later construction; a building inscription names a ruler consistent with the inferred sequence. Ceramics from the associated layer provide a broad chronological check. That comparison can expose a chronology error or reveal a later monument reusing an earlier king's name. The same piece of evidence can change meaning when new context arrives. Scholars must preserve the exact locations and dating assumptions that make the comparison possible.

One final step is historical interpretation. Suppose a monument describes the defeat of another city. The inscription may be a royal claim designed to glorify the victor. It is evidence that a ruler wanted the event announced in that way. Whether the battle occurred exactly as described may require a corresponding inscription elsewhere, archaeological signs of conflict, or a revised reading. Translation gives access to a voice, but access does not make that voice impartial.

## A name is more than a sound

What happens when a deciphered text tells us a king captured another king? We gain a political history with dates, dynasties, alliances, and warfare, but we also inherit the interests of a small part of society. Monumental inscriptions are expensive. They disproportionately preserve elite acts and claims. They have less to say about how common farmers organized their work, how ordinary families disputed land, or what people thought about an unsuccessful ruler. Archaeological households, botanical remains, burial practices, and settlement surveys can ask those questions more directly.

Epigraphers also study inscriptions on ceramics, which can name owners, producers, or contents. The wording may be formulaic. A vessel excavated in a sealed burial tells us about where it ended up, but not automatically where it was made or who last drank from it. Analysis of clay and decoration can help trace manufacture; a written dedication may identify the intended owner. Those are two different claims, and sometimes they disagree. The discrepancy may be part of an object biography involving gifts, trade, inheritance, or conquest.

Maya history reaches far beyond the surviving inscriptions of the Classic period. Some cities were abandoned or transformed, while Maya communities continued. Spanish colonial policies, conversion campaigns, violence, and the disruption of writing institutions changed transmission, but Maya peoples and languages endured. A decipherment can return the words of a Classic court to wider circulation without providing a complete continuous transcript of every century that followed.

The manuscript of the Popol Wuj illustrates the distinction. It is a colonial-era written K'iche' work preserving older traditions, and it is not written in the Classic lowland hieroglyphic system. Scholars can investigate similarities between its stories and older images or inscriptions, but a parallel with the Hero Twins does not date every sentence of the later text. One archive tells us about an earlier writing system; the other has its own authors and manuscript history. Connecting them requires evidence, rather than collapsing the two into one supposed Maya book.

## Why two scholars may give different readings

Even when the phonetic value of a sign is secure, translating an inscription remains an interpretive task. A royal title may combine a name, an office, and a relationship to a place. A personal name can include the name of a deity. Ancient Maya writers also used metaphor and compressed grammatical constructions. When two translations of the same monument disagree, the issue might be a damaged sign, a difference in parsing the grammar, or a debate about the social institution named in the text. It is useful to ask which specific step the translators disagree about.

Consider a glyph block partly worn away at its left edge. One scholar reconstructs a familiar accession expression; another argues that a different event fits the surviving strokes. A new photograph under raking light may clarify the strokes without settling the grammar. A matching phrase on a second monument may settle the grammar but leave the original stone damaged. These are different kinds of improvement. Recording both possibilities allows later scholars to evaluate them against additional monuments.

Even a secure translation is evidence for the society that produced it, and for an intended audience. A royal dedication might advertise legitimacy; a defeated rival's inscription might tell a different story. The existence of opposing accounts would not invalidate decipherment. It would demonstrate that we had recovered enough language to recognize an ancient political disagreement.

## The recoverable past

So how do we know what a glyph says? We begin with a documented object and careful drawings or scans. We determine its reading order, its calendar expressions, and the signs that recur. We propose phonetic or logographic values and grammar. We then test them on other inscriptions, ideally inscriptions not used in creating the proposal. Archaeological context and independent historical patterns provide additional constraints. The process is cumulative and corrigible: one ambiguous sign may stay open while hundreds of others become reliable.

And how do we know that inscriptions tell the history of Maya kingdoms? Because Proskouriakoff's date patterns, Berlin's political titles, Knorozov's phonetic approach, and later linguistic and archaeological work converged on readings that explain numerous independent texts. We can increasingly identify particular rulers and events while remembering that a royal inscription is a crafted account. Its author knew things we may never recover, and wanted its audience to remember things in a particular way.

The people responsible for this recovery include makers of the ancient monuments, their unnamed assistants, modern excavators and conservators, Maya-language specialists, and generations of epigraphers. We owe specific credit to Förstemann for early calendar work, Berlin for identifying political titles, Proskouriakoff for demonstrating historical patterns, and Knorozov for the phonetic framework that made whole sentences readable. We also owe credit to researchers who questioned each other, compared spellings, revised chronologies, and made tools that other scholars could use.

A carved block that once looked like an ornament can become part of a sentence. A sequence of dates can become the life of a person. The strongest result is not the announcement that a mysterious civilization has been solved. It is that we can now ask better questions of the surviving Maya record, in the languages and with the methods appropriate to that record. Ancient scribes wrote about their world. By recovering how they wrote, we can listen with more care to what they actually chose to say.

### Evidence and credit

- **Ancient authors:** Maya scribes, sculptors, scribal schools, and sponsors made the texts; unknown authorship must not be replaced with the identity of an excavator.
- **Calendar:** Förstemann studied codices; Goodman, Martínez Hernández and others contributed to correlating Maya and Western dates. Thompson's catalogs were useful even as his strict anti-phonetic position was displaced.
- **Phonetic decipherment:** Yuri Knorozov's 1952 argument developed phonetic values from Landa and cross-textual comparison. Earlier phonetic proposals and Maya linguistic work were also relevant; no one scholar deciphered all signs.
- **History:** Heinrich Berlin (1958) identified emblem glyphs; Tatiana Proskouriakoff (1960) demonstrated historical date patterns at Piedras Negras and continued at Yaxchilan (1963–1964).
- **Expansion:** Schele, Mathews, Lounsbury, Stuart, Houston, Grube, Coes, Maya linguists and scholars made different contributions. Name a contribution instead of treating the period as a roll call.
- **Verification:** An apparent reading is stronger when it predicts usages in independent inscriptions, grammatical contexts, spellings, and archaeological associations. Decipherment does not transform propagandistic royal claims into objective reports.

### Scope cautions

- The accession example is explicitly hypothetical; it is a demonstration of inference, not a transcription of a named monument.
- Do not equate all Maya languages with Classic Ch'olan or conflate the Classic script with Latin-alphabet K'iche' manuscripts.
- Maya peoples are living communities. Decipherment is a continuing collaborative practice, not an event in which their history was invented by outsiders.
