# The Mahabharata, Part IV: The Kingdom After the War

A man has won a kingdom and wishes he had not. The rivals who cheated him are dead. The family elders who watched those wrongs unfold are dead or dying. His own allies have been killed, and the field of victory is covered with people who will never collect a share of the prize. When the new king is urged to accept his crown, he asks what kind of success could justify what has happened. He thinks of abandoning everything and going into the forest.

The man is Yudhishthira, eldest of the five Pandava brothers in the Sanskrit epic *Mahabharata*. The poem has already given us a catastrophe large enough to end almost any other heroic narrative: a conflict between the Pandavas and their Kaurava cousins, culminating in an eighteen-day war at Kurukshetra. Yet the *Mahabharata* devotes an enormous portion of its surviving text to what happens afterward. It includes laments, instructions on kingship, debates about right conduct, rituals of sovereignty, the deaths of elders, the destruction of Krishna's own people, and finally a journey in which the last surviving heroes leave their kingdom behind.

The epic's long ending changes what we understand the war to have meant. Its question is no longer merely whether the Pandavas had a right to rule. It is what a king owes the living after gaining power through the destruction of the dead. And its final images carry that question beyond the world of courts, warfare, and political responsibility into the difficult business of judging an entire life.

## The women see the battlefield

The epic's *Stri Parva*, the book of the women, moves attention from the combatants' vows to the people who must live with the losses. Gandhari, mother of Duryodhana and many other Kaurava sons, confronts a landscape where almost all her children have been killed. She had warned her son against his destructive course, but warning did not save him. Her grief is not diminished because Duryodhana's conduct caused a great part of the disaster.

At a battlefield, heroic titles become painfully inadequate. A champion who was praised for military skill is now a body searched for by his family. A widow tries to recognize someone among the fallen. Mothers see sons deprived of the rites they expected to perform at their deaths. The women who speak are not simply a chorus designed to honor the surviving warriors. Their laments reveal the human content of casualty numbers. They restore relationships the battle narratives tended to compress into names of combatants and descriptions of weapons.

Gandhari confronts Krishna, the divine ally and strategist of the Pandavas. If he had the capacity to influence events, why did he permit the catastrophe? She places a curse upon Krishna's clan, foretelling that his own people will destroy one another. The scene offers no easy answer in which divine presence guarantees a happy world. Krishna's side has won, but the grief of those who opposed him has become a moral accusation. The later destruction of the Yadavas will return to this accusation with devastating force.

Dhritarashtra, the blind king who repeatedly failed to restrain Duryodhana, must greet the victors. He has lost his sons and fears the man who killed the eldest, Bhima. A well-known episode has Krishna arrange an iron image in Bhima's place when Dhritarashtra asks to embrace him. The grieving father crushes the figure. The scene is shocking because it shows two truths together: a man destroyed by bereavement and a king who still possesses murderous rage. His loss is genuine, and the danger he poses is genuine. The survivors cannot simply declare a new era of harmony.

## A brother discovered after death

Kunti, mother of the Pandavas, reveals a secret with extraordinary consequences. Before her marriage she had given birth to Karna, who was raised in another household and became the Pandavas' enemy. During the war he served Duryodhana, and Arjuna killed him in battle. Karna was therefore the eldest brother of the very men whose side defeated him.

Yudhishthira is devastated. He fought a war for inheritance and justice without knowing the full shape of his own family. Karna had been insulted for supposedly low birth, honored by Duryodhana, and bound to that friend by gratitude. The revelation changes neither his choices nor the consequences, but it changes what the survivors know they have done. They killed a brother they might have recognized under different circumstances.

It is tempting to turn the discovery into a simple lesson about keeping secrets. The epic gives us a more complicated situation. Kunti's silence was formed by fear of social judgment surrounding an unmarried birth. Karna's separation exposed him to lifelong humiliation. Yet when he eventually learned his origins, he refused to abandon the friend who had given him standing. No single disclosure could straightforwardly have reversed the network of loyalties that had formed over decades. The tragedy arises from birth, status, secrecy, gratitude, and military allegiance acting together.

Yudhishthira's anguish grows. He has won the throne through circumstances he cannot narrate as a pure triumph. The brother he did not know and the cousin he did know are both among the dead. His reputation for truthfulness was damaged when he helped mislead the teacher Drona. The family he promised to protect has lost younger members, including Draupadi's sons, murdered in the night after the principal battle. A coronation ceremony cannot give back any of them.

## The king who wants to leave

Yudhishthira proposes renunciation. He wants to leave kingship and pursue the life of an ascetic. His brothers argue against him. Arjuna and Bhima remind him of the responsibilities of government. What would it mean to abandon a population whose previous order has been shattered by war? If he leaves, who will supervise food, adjudication, protection, relief, and the rebuilding of institutions? Personal remorse can be sincere and still fail the people who need practical leadership.

Draupadi and Krishna also take part in these discussions. Their presence recalls the original injuries that led to conflict. Draupadi has strong grounds to demand that the perpetrators of her humiliation be confronted. Yet she too now lives in the world that confrontation has produced. The poem does not let any character solve the problem by declaring that the war should never have happened. It has happened. The survivors must choose within the consequences.

This is an important feature of the *Mahabharata* as a work about ethics. It frequently presents a moral principle, then finds a situation in which applying it becomes difficult. A ruler should seek peace. But what if peace requires submitting indefinitely to a relative who repeatedly attacks your family? A truthful person should avoid deceit. But what if deception seems the only way to stop a devastating warrior? A person seeking spiritual liberation may renounce possessions. But what if a king's renunciation exposes others to neglect? A poem that raises such questions is not simply a manual of approved behavior.

Yudhishthira is urged to seek instruction from Bhishma, the grand-uncle who lies alive upon a bed of arrows. Bhishma fell in the early days of battle but has the boon of choosing the time of his death. He was one of the dynasty's most respected protectors and also served the side whose leadership brought disaster. Now he possesses knowledge that the new king needs, though his own lifetime provides examples of how difficult that knowledge is to use.

## The teacher on the arrows

The *Shanti Parva*, the book of peace, is one of the longest sections of the epic. Much of it consists of Bhishma instructing Yudhishthira on the obligations of kings and on broader questions of right living. The teaching reaches well beyond the mechanics of ruling. It discusses political responsibility, the choices available in times of emergency, nonviolence, generosity, renunciation, the path toward liberation, and competing accounts of what makes an action right.

A king is urged to protect subjects, maintain order, appoint capable advisers, attend to revenue and justice, and restrain appetite. These are not romantic tasks. Royal splendor depends on administrative work and the willingness to hear unpleasant truths. The war has shown what happens when a king lets affection for his own son override his obligation to protect a larger political community. Dhritarashtra's failure is a warning contained within the story the teachers are now discussing.

Bhishma also addresses *apaddharma*, the problem of conduct in emergencies. Rules designed for ordinary conditions may become impossible to follow in catastrophe. But the mere invocation of necessity is dangerous, because every ambitious ruler can call an inconvenient restraint an emergency. The epic's own war has demonstrated how quickly such reasoning can erode all the conventions meant to protect combatants. Bhishma's instruction belongs to a text that knows both the need for practical judgment and the ease with which practical judgment can become a justification for wrongdoing.

Some portions of the teaching emphasize a life oriented toward liberation rather than worldly power. Others insist on the concrete duties of a ruler. The tension is part of the point. Yudhishthira cannot be only a renunciant while occupying the throne, and he cannot be only an administrator while ignoring the moral condition of the person performing the work. The conversation asks how someone who has suffered and caused harm can govern without pretending the past has been purified.

At the chosen time, Bhishma dies. The immense figure who tied himself to the survival of the dynasty finally leaves the world after most of its principal heirs have been killed. He is honored, but his death offers another moment to consider the difference between wisdom and successful action. He could explain kingship in extraordinary detail, yet his oath to the dynasty did not enable him to prevent its self-destruction.

## Ritual and the kingdom's repair

Yudhishthira eventually accepts the burden of ruling. The epic describes the restoration of order and, later, the horse sacrifice or *ashvamedha*, an ancient royal ritual associated with sovereignty. A horse is released to travel, accompanied by an armed force, and rival rulers may be challenged over the authority it represents. The rite combines political claims, military potential, ritual practice, wealth, and the attempt to establish legitimate control after a war.

It would be easy to see this as a public answer to Yudhishthira's guilt: perform the great ceremony, demonstrate renewed authority, and let the past be settled. But the epic includes an episode that disturbs a simple equation between costly ritual and moral achievement. A mongoose, in a famous story, challenges the splendor of the sacrifice by comparing it to a humble family's act of giving food during desperate scarcity. The family gave what it could least afford to give. The moral force of that gift exceeded the display of a wealthy ruler.

The comparison is not simply an attack on every ritual. It asks what the measurable expense of an act tells us about its ethical value. Kings can distribute large sums because they control large revenues. A hungry person who gives up the last meal may sacrifice more. The story's power comes from placing the scale of a public ceremony beside the intimate arithmetic of survival. Yudhishthira is being educated again in the limits of royal magnificence.

Among the episodes of later reconstruction is the preservation of Parikshit, the descendant of Arjuna through Abhimanyu. The destruction of the family has left the future precarious. A surviving child becomes essential to dynastic continuity, and his survival is connected in the epic to Krishna's divine protection. The future audience of the *Mahabharata* is itself woven into this genealogy: the story is recited at a sacrifice held by Parikshit's descendant Janamejaya. The epic becomes an account transmitted to people whose existence is part of its consequences.

## Elders who cannot stay

Time passes. Dhritarashtra, Gandhari, and Kunti leave court for the forest. Their withdrawal has a different meaning from Yudhishthira's earlier desire to renounce the throne. The political order has been re-established, and elders exhausted by loss seek a final life of restraint and detachment. The epic relates their deaths in a forest fire. These are not people who can be divided neatly into the winners and losers of the war. Kunti is mother of the victorious brothers and of Karna, whom they killed. Gandhari has lost the sons who opposed them. Dhritarashtra both mourns and bears responsibility for his tolerance of Duryodhana's course.

Their departure also asks how much reconciliation can accomplish. Living together after such a conflict may be possible, but it does not erase unequal memories of the dead. The survivors cannot return to the royal household as though it were the one in which the princes grew up. The epic grants the elders a final movement away from court and leaves the younger generation to live without their instruction, permission, or disapproval.

Krishna remains active in the story, but the curse spoken by Gandhari has not disappeared. Years later, his own clan, the Yadavas, falls into internal violence. A quarrel escalates into a struggle in which members of the same people kill one another with improvised weapons. In the epic's divine and mythological frame, Krishna's departure from the world occurs in connection with this destruction. His death is told through the episode of a hunter who mistakes him for a deer and shoots him with an arrow. The circumstances vary in the stories and their later reception, but the irony is unmistakable: the family whose great ally helped guide the Pandavas to victory suffers its own collapse.

## A world after Krishna

The disappearance of Krishna changes the epic's scale. He has served as ally, diplomat, strategist, charioteer, teacher, and divine presence. Without him, the remaining heroes find that the powers and certainties of their earlier lives no longer hold. Arjuna's ability to defend people declines in a notable episode. The warrior once able to face great armies cannot simply command the old world to continue. The narrative marks the passing of an age, not through a dry date but through the loss of familiar capacities.

Yudhishthira eventually decides that his time as king has ended. The Pandavas place the succession in the hands of the next generation and set out on a final journey, accompanied by Draupadi. Their route is toward the Himalayas and beyond the ordinary world. They give up the things that distinguished them as rulers. Their last pilgrimage converts the epic's concern with kingship into a question about what remains of a person when titles, victories, possessions, and even companions fall away.

One by one, Draupadi and the brothers fall. Yudhishthira continues. The explanation attached to each fall in the poem identifies an attachment or failing: favoritism, pride, vanity, or an excessive regard for one's own abilities. Modern readers may find some of these judgments troubling, especially the explanation offered for Draupadi. It is important to hear them as the poem's moral accounting, not as self-evident verdicts that should be accepted without question. The story places a lifetime of complex characters into a sequence of stark judgments. The simplification is itself part of what makes the ending demanding.

## The dog that will not be left

A dog accompanies Yudhishthira on the final ascent. When the gods offer him entry to heaven, he is told the animal cannot accompany him. Yudhishthira refuses to abandon it. The dog has followed him faithfully, and to leave a dependent companion at the threshold would violate what he understands as right conduct. His brothers are gone. The kingdom is behind him. He now faces a moral choice with no military advantage or dynastic payoff.

The dog is revealed as a divine figure associated with Dharma. The test has been about fidelity to an apparently insignificant companion. After all the grand claims of a lifetime, a king's worth is measured by whether he will betray an animal that trusts him. This ending offers a powerful counterweight to the assembly hall, where elites argued about rules while permitting a woman to be abused. Here Yudhishthira chooses protection over prestige even when the reward of heaven is held out before him.

But the final tests are not yet over. Yudhishthira encounters a disturbing vision of the afterlife. His enemies may appear honored while his brothers and loved ones seem to suffer. He refuses to accept a paradise that requires him to abandon them. The episode involves illusions or temporary experiences arranged by divine powers, and its details are challenging across translations. Its effect is to renew the question of justice after death. Do rewards correspond to the lives that people actually led? Can heaven be meaningful if loyalty has to be suspended at its gate?

## What does the ending settle?

The *Mahabharata* does not end like a straightforward account of the rightful prince restoring an orderly kingdom. It ends after the kingdom has been restored, ruled, handed onward, and finally left behind. It has spent vast stretches of text on teaching, but its heroes repeatedly discover that knowledge, power, and virtue are insufficient to spare them conflict or grief. The final tests do not erase what they did on earth. They change the scale on which the poem asks us to evaluate it.

The dog episode invites a generous reading of Yudhishthira. He once gambled away his family, including Draupadi, under the pressure of an aristocratic contest. At the end he will not relinquish a powerless companion even for heaven. The contrast is striking. It gives us a way to see moral growth without claiming that the king has become perfect or that his past failures are canceled. A person can learn what loyalty requires by being confronted with the consequences of failing it.

The epic's concluding visions also challenge the listener's desire for tidy cosmic bookkeeping. If someone died heroically while serving a cruel king, what is owed to courage and what to complicity? If a righteous claimant used deceit to win a necessary war, how should the result be judged? If a woman was mistreated by the people whose cause is called just, whose account of justice has priority? The poem offers teachings, competing explanations, and surprising visions rather than a single formula that removes every difficulty.

## The knowledge that survives a catastrophe

One reason the epic's teaching sections are so long is that the death of so many respected elders creates a problem of knowledge as well as a crisis of succession. A society can lose commanders, parents, counselors, and the accumulated memory of institutions in a single generation. Bhishma's instruction is the attempt to recover what can still be transmitted. His words do not reverse his conduct during the war, but they preserve the possibility that the next rulers can learn from both his knowledge and his failure.

The discussions in the *Shanti* and *Anushasana Parvas* sometimes read like collections of distinct teachings. Their internal variety is part of the tradition's history. Some passages endorse active royal protection, others praise withdrawal from worldly desire, and still others explore charitable conduct, nonviolence, and states of emergency. The variation is not always easily harmonized. It gives the listener a sense of an intellectual archive being placed within the dramatic frame of a dying elder instructing a wounded king.

This also complicates the idea that Yudhishthira simply needs to be convinced to stop feeling guilty. His grief recognizes that a moral injury occurred. The teaching asks what follows from that recognition. One can remain overwhelmed by remorse, or turn remembrance into more careful government and more serious attention to people harmed by decisions made at the top. The epic refuses to imagine that the psychological comfort of the ruler is the same thing as the restoration of the realm.

## The long life of an epic

For centuries the *Mahabharata* has been recited, translated, staged, painted, discussed, and reinterpreted throughout South and Southeast Asia and beyond. Its manuscripts survive in many regional traditions; the modern critical edition was constructed through large-scale comparison of witnesses, but living performance traditions are part of the work's history too. A listener may know the stories through television, oral narration, regional poetry, theater, temple art, or a modern printed translation. These forms disagree in detail and emphasis, and none can be understood simply as an inferior substitute for one fixed original.

The poem is connected with Hindu religious traditions and contains the *Bhagavad Gita*, but it also includes debate and tension across different philosophies and practices. It is not a single philosopher speaking in one voice. The characters, narrators, and teaching episodes can put competing claims next to one another. That quality helps explain why the epic has sustained argument for so long. People return to the same scenes with different political, theological, and personal questions.

A historian must distinguish its literary past from what archaeology can establish about ancient northern India. The epic contains memories and conceptions of society drawn from periods of its formation, but identifying a character with an independently documented ruler or assigning one precise archaeological date to the war would go beyond the evidence. Its value as historical evidence lies partly in the long development of its ethical and religious worlds and in the history of the communities that preserved and reimagined it.

## Why the end takes so long

The drawn-out ending has a literary purpose. In a simple dynastic adventure, the hero's acquisition of the throne would conclude the plot. The *Mahabharata* instead asks the winner to live with the consequences for decades. He must listen to the elders, participate in rites, care for a renewed political order, bury or release people he loves, and accept that even the divine ally who guided his victory will depart. The crown is one phase of life, not the final definition of a person.

The successive endings also change the meaning of justice. In the assembly hall justice meant protection of a person being humiliated. On the battlefield it meant finding a way to respond to an aggressor. After the war it means maintaining institutions for surviving households. On the mountain it becomes the refusal to abandon a dependent creature when no audience is watching. These are not interchangeable tasks. The epic is compelling because it makes its central word, *dharma*, answerable to several kinds of situation.

There is no guarantee that every audience will find the ending satisfying. A story that reveals moral failings at the moment of the heroes' deaths can seem cruel. An afterlife test involving deception by gods can seem arbitrary. Those responses are not proof that the listener has failed to understand the text. They are part of the serious engagement this literature invites, and part of the long history of different communities arguing with the story they inherited.

## A final return to the palace

Imagine the assembly where Yudhishthira once lost his freedom at dice. The finest elders were present. Rules were recited. Powerful warriors watched while Draupadi was humiliated and asked a question no one could answer. In the long story that follows, the family fights, wins, mourns, governs, relinquishes power, and confronts further judgments. The outcome does not restore the palace to a state in which its original failures never occurred.

Now imagine the last king on the mountain, refusing to trade a loyal dog for entrance to heaven. The two images belong to the same literary work. One shows the abuse of formal obligation by people protecting their rank. The other shows a person keeping faith when no political advantage can be gained. Between them lies the destruction of a dynasty and an extraordinary amount of argument about how people ought to act.

The *Mahabharata* becomes enduring literature because it makes the gap between knowing a moral principle and living by it so difficult to ignore. Its warriors wanted honor, its kings wanted legitimate authority, and its families wanted loyalty. Their choices brought them into conflict, and their final peace was hard-won, incomplete, and incapable of bringing back the dead. The story's last journey leaves its audience with the question its characters struggled to answer all along: when duties collide, which living being will you choose to protect?
