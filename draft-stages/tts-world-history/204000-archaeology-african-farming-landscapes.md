# Fields Without Written Records: Reconstructing African Agricultural Landscapes

Imagine a potter preparing clay for a cooking vessel somewhere in what is now Mali. To stop the clay from cracking as it dries and fires, the potter mixes in small pieces of plant material. The vessel breaks. Its sherds are discarded, buried, and forgotten. Thousands of years later, a researcher studies their interior surfaces under magnification and finds impressions of tiny plant structures. The pot had become an accidental archive of crops.

It is a striking route to historical knowledge. A field may leave no neat boundary in the archaeological record. A crop may be eaten, burned, or ground until recognizable grains disappear. But crop parts mixed into pottery can survive as voids and impressions. When those shapes are compared with wild and domesticated plants, they can help trace one of the longest technological transformations in African history.

Today we will ask how archaeologists reconstruct farming landscapes in places where written records are limited or absent. Our journey begins with pearl millet and pottery in the ancient Sahel. It continues to the Inland Niger Delta, where floodplain farming, fishing, and livestock supported large settlements long before the first European travelers described the region. It ends with the living terraced landscapes of Konso in Ethiopia, which show how soil, water, architecture, and collective labor can form one agricultural system. Across these cases the central question is the same: what can surviving materials actually establish about land use and the people who organized it?

## A misleading map of world agriculture

Textbook maps often give the Fertile Crescent a prominent place in the history of domesticated plants. That is appropriate for the cereals and animals domesticated there. But the map becomes misleading when its visual emphasis suggests that agricultural knowledge spread outward from only one center. Farming developed through many independent and connected histories. Africa is particularly important because several staple crops were domesticated from African wild relatives, under ecological conditions different from those of Southwest Asia.

Pearl millet, sorghum, African rice, teff, and other crops belong to histories that cannot be folded into a single moment of agricultural invention. The plants adapted to different soils, rainfall regimes, and growing seasons. Their domestication chronologies differ, as does the archaeological evidence for them. Nor does one crop necessarily imply a farming society in which every person cultivates fields. People could combine harvesting, herding, fishing, hunting, and trade, and their combinations could change seasonally or across generations.

Our evidence is also unequal. The Nile Valley has long attracted monumental archaeology and written historical study. In other regions tropical conditions destroy many plant remains, while shifting river channels bury earlier surfaces. Research intensity has not been even. The apparent chronology of agriculture can partly reflect where archaeologists worked, what they sampled, and which techniques were available when they excavated. An old excavation that saved pottery but discarded sediment may leave fewer botanical answers than a smaller excavation with flotation and microscope analysis.

The historian therefore begins by recognizing two unknowns: the original practices and the survival of evidence for those practices. A missing crop in an excavation does not necessarily establish that nobody grew it. But an attractive story about early agriculture cannot substitute for dated plant remains and well-documented contexts either.

## Reading a crop in a potsherd

Pearl millet is a drought-tolerant cereal with a long West African history. Its wild relatives and cultivated varieties offer a way to study domestication as an evolutionary process. The domestication of a plant involves population changes under harvesting, sowing, and selection, sometimes over many generations. A major trait in cereal domestication concerns how seeds detach. For a wild grass, mature seed heads that break apart readily can be advantageous for natural reproduction. For people harvesting a crop, plants that retain mature grains longer may be easier to gather. Repeated human selection can favor nonshattering forms, though the sequence need not be sudden or intentional in the modern breeding sense.

A 2021 study led by Dorian Q. Fuller with Aleese Barron, Louis Champion, Christian Dupuy, Dominique Commelin, Michel Raimbault, and Tim Denham investigated pearl millet remains in ceramic temper from three Middle Holocene sites in northern Mali. The researchers examined impressions of millet spikelets and other features left in pottery, including changes in grain size and characteristics of the structures that hold grains together. A spikelet is part of a grass's reproductive structure. Its shape and attachments can sometimes preserve details relevant to whether a plant population was closer to wild or domesticated forms.

The team used techniques including micro-computed tomography, which can examine the shapes of internal impressions without requiring every pot fragment to be cut apart. They compared these archaeological shapes with botanical reference specimens and with material from different dates. The study reported a sequence: grains became broader over time, while certain traits associated with nonshattering and paired spikelets appeared in later material. The point is not that one unusual pot contains the instant when farming began. The evidence lies in a pattern across dated samples and multiple morphological characters.

Try to reconstruct the reasoning. First, a researcher documents where the potsherd was found. Second, the age of its archaeological context is established through radiocarbon measurements and stratigraphic comparison. Third, the plant impressions are recorded with enough detail to make their identification inspectable. Fourth, the researcher compares traits among samples through time. If the distribution shifts in the direction expected under selection for cultivation, the archaeologist has evidence of a domestication trajectory. Alternative explanations, such as changes in wild populations or sampling biases, must still be considered.

This is quite different from discovering a written tablet that says, in effect, we grow millet here. The potsherd records a physical interaction between plant and craft production. It shows that particular plant structures were present in the pottery-making environment. With many fragments and suitable dating, the sequence can be interpreted as evidence of how people and millet changed together.

## What an accidental impression cannot prove

An impression is not a complete farm census. Temper may have been selected from specific plant residues, while edible grain was handled elsewhere. A vessel's clay could have been prepared in one settlement and carried to another. Seeds pressed into a pot before firing were no longer growing, and their presence does not necessarily place a field beside the kiln. The chronology of a ceramic layer can be complicated by older sherds redeposited in younger soil. Identifying domestication traits also requires enough complete impressions to make the comparison meaningful.

We should separate three propositions. First, a certain plant was present in the material used by potters. Second, the plant showed changes consistent with domestication. Third, a particular form of farming organization existed at that place. The first may be established by the object. The second depends on comparative morphology and chronology. The third demands additional evidence: tools, settlement layout, food residues, land-use indicators, storage structures, and perhaps patterns in animal remains or regional settlement distribution.

This distinction makes the study more interesting, because it prevents the simple word farming from swallowing the process under examination. People can cultivate a plant before its domesticated traits are fully developed. They may tend wild stands, save seed, or deliberately sow it; some of those practices leave similar archaeological signatures. Domestication is a change in plant populations. Cultivation is a human practice. The two are related but should not be treated as interchangeable.

## A floodplain that supports a city

Next move south and west into the Inland Niger Delta. Here the Niger and its tributaries spread seasonal water across a broad, variable floodplain. Depending on rainfall and inundation, different areas become suitable for rice cultivation, fishing, dry-season pasture, and other activities. The agricultural landscape is not a neatly fixed grid. It is a network of changing ecological niches, requiring knowledge of water levels, soil, timing, and transport.

In 1977, archaeologists Susan Keech McIntosh and Roderick McIntosh excavated at Jenné-Jeno, an early urban settlement in Mali. Their 1979 paper on prehistoric subsistence in the Inland Niger Delta presented evidence of a complex economy involving African rice, fish, and cattle. This mattered because it showed that permanent, substantial settlements could develop in a local system of agriculture and aquatic resources, without needing to imitate a Near Eastern model of cities organized around wheat and barley.

At Jenné-Jeno and nearby sites, pottery, animal bones, plant remains, and settlement chronology were used to reconstruct changing livelihoods. Fish bones indicate aquatic foods, but the species and body parts may help distinguish fishing environments and processing practices. Cattle bones indicate livestock use but do not by themselves show when and where animals grazed. Rice remains indicate the presence of rice, yet the question of whether a grain represents a fully domesticated crop can be difficult when the diagnostic structures are missing. The McIntoshes' work helped make the region an important case study for how urbanism could emerge in a landscape of ecological specialization.

Imagine a settlement whose inhabitants possess rice fields along seasonal flood margins, cattle herds using pasture at other times of year, fish-processing locations, pottery workshops, and exchange relationships with nearby communities. The archaeology might preserve charred grain, fish bones, cattle bones, pottery, house floors, and distribution patterns across a region. No one artifact maps the entire economy. But the combination makes a diversified system plausible. It invites a question about social organization: how were the products of different environments brought together and distributed?

The answer need not be a centralized ruler issuing commands to everyone. Regional networks can integrate communities with different skills and resources. Researchers examine settlement sizes, specialization, and exchange to consider possibilities. The exact political institutions of early Jenné-Jeno are less directly visible than its material economy. Avoiding an invented king is as important as avoiding the assumption that a substantial city must have had one.

## Rice and the problem of a domestication date

African rice, known botanically as Oryza glaberrima, belongs to a separate domestication history from Asian rice, Oryza sativa. That distinction is established through botanical and genetic research. But identifying the first place and date of its cultivation is harder. A living crop's genetic history can suggest relationships and possible regions of domestication, while excavated grains provide evidence from particular places and times. Neither kind of evidence supplies a universal calendar without interpretation.

An important site is Dia in the Inland Niger Delta. Archaeobotanical investigations recovered a large quantity of rice remains from the settlement. Direct radiocarbon measurements of some grains placed rice in the earliest occupational levels, broadly between about the eighth and fifth centuries BCE. Researcher Shawn Sabrina Murray examined the material and argued about its significance for the history of African rice. The grain measurements supported the interpretation of domesticated rice, though archaeologists have also emphasized the difficulty of distinguishing wild and cultivated forms without the spikelet bases that preserve key domestication traits.

Why does this difference matter? A grain may be small or large for several reasons, including species, local growing conditions, and charring. Some traits are more informative about domestication than others. If a study reports domesticated rice, read the methods: which plant parts were recovered? Which comparisons were made? Were the grains dated directly or associated with other material? How much of the sample is securely identified? A date on a grain tells us when that plant grew; it does not alone date the original domestication event, which may be far earlier.

More recent work at sites such as Sadia in Dogon country has examined plant economies across different environments in Mali, using archaeobotanical samples to investigate the combination of crops and changes over time. The research demonstrates why generalizing from one famous river valley is risky. A settlement in an upland landscape and a settlement in a floodplain may grow different crops or organize food production differently. Even within one region, the diversity of subsistence strategies is part of the history to be reconstructed.

## The historian's landscape is seasonal

One reason African agricultural archaeology is demanding is that fields are seasonal. A wall, temple, or mound may remain visible for centuries. A planting calendar does not. Researchers therefore look for indirect traces: storage pits, grinding equipment, irrigation or water-management features, field soils, pollen, phytoliths, charcoal, and crop remains. They examine animal dung and the carbon and nitrogen isotope signatures of food webs. These measurements represent different spatial and temporal scales and need to be interpreted together.

For example, a charred millet grain in a domestic hearth tells us a crop was handled or consumed in that household. A cluster of storage structures might suggest more systematic surplus management, but only if their size, use, and dates are established. A soil profile rich in certain plant microfossils could indicate the repeated presence of grasses. Yet grasses can grow naturally or be introduced with fodder. A charcoal record might reflect clearance, fuel collection, or changes in wildfire. Each observation narrows the possibilities without identifying a whole agricultural system on its own.

Suppose archaeologists find domestic grain, cattle bones, and imported beads at a settlement. It is tempting to announce a prosperous farming and trading city. But begin by examining the chronology. Are the bones and the beads from the same period? Could the grain have been preserved in one unusual burned building? Does the bead's composition match distant sources, or only resemble them? Was the settlement occupied continuously or in several separated episodes? The interpretation becomes stronger as the independent observations align.

## Learning from a living terraced landscape

Our third case is the Konso highlands of southern Ethiopia, where communities have maintained terraced fields, stone-walled settlements, and elaborate systems of land management. The Konso Cultural Landscape is recognized by UNESCO for a living tradition documented over more than four centuries and many generations. Its terracing and collective management are tangible evidence of how people can adapt agriculture to slopes, erosion, and seasonal water constraints. This case is more recent than the prehistoric millet samples, but it helps us see what an agricultural landscape actually consists of.

A terrace is built to change the relation between soil and moving water. A stone retaining wall can slow erosion and provide a more stable cultivable surface. But an agricultural terrace only continues to work if water movement, sediment deposition, and repairs are managed. Fields are part of a continuing system of labor. Studying an abandoned terrace can reveal wall construction and soil accumulation. Studying a living system additionally allows researchers to ask local farmers and maintainers how decisions are made, while recognizing that present practice cannot simply be projected unchanged into remote antiquity.

The Konso example makes visible something that potsherds alone may conceal: an agricultural landscape is maintained over time. The initial construction of a field wall is one event. Removing sediment after a storm, repairing a breach, selecting crops, maintaining access paths, and negotiating collective obligations are repeated events. Archaeologists who only date the first construction phase risk telling a history of engineering without a history of maintenance.

A living tradition also raises questions about who gets credited as an expert. Local farmers understand soils, water routes, seasonal risks, and plant varieties. Archaeologists may document terraces, collect sediment, and build a chronology, but the knowledge that sustains the system belongs in part to the people maintaining it. UNESCO's documentation recognizes social organization and traditional land-use knowledge as elements of the landscape. The formal research record and the practitioners' knowledge should be treated as different, complementary forms of evidence.

We should not assume Konso terraces are a direct model for every ancient African landscape. The soils, rainfall, crops, population histories, and social institutions differ greatly. The comparison has a more modest purpose: to reveal how much history can be hidden when a landscape is reduced to dots marking settlements on a map.

## The problem of the empty field

Now imagine an archaeological survey that finds potsherds and house foundations but very few traces of fields. Does this prove people did little farming? It might indicate preservation problems. Fields can be plowed, eroded, flooded, or buried under later cultivation. Earthen banks may be difficult to distinguish from natural features. Water channels can change their course. Plant remains sometimes survive only through charring or direct impressions in pottery.

Research strategies can improve the picture. Archaeologists may use aerial imagery to identify abandoned terraces and field boundaries, but ground survey is needed to determine what the lines represent. They may collect sediment cores to reconstruct changes in vegetation. They may sample nearby habitation deposits with flotation, examine archaeological pollen, or distinguish crop taxa by their phytoliths. Each technique has a different blind spot. Aerial images emphasize larger visible features, while microscopic samples can reveal activities where no surface structure survives.

Researchers may use radiocarbon dates from buried charcoal or plant remains to place land-use episodes in time. But charcoal can be old when deposited, and it may have washed into a field from elsewhere. A date from a terrace wall's infill does not necessarily establish the day the wall was built. Multiple stratigraphic relationships and samples help. There is no method that transforms a whole landscape directly into one unambiguous map of ancient fields.

A careful history is built from different kinds of resolution. Pottery impressions trace long-term changes in millet traits. Directly dated rice grains demonstrate the presence of rice in a specific period. Excavation and survey show how large settlements were distributed in the Niger Delta. Living terraced fields illustrate how agriculture depends on sustained collective knowledge. Together these observations tell a richer story than any one discovery.

## Who made this history knowable?

We can name some of the people who developed the modern evidence. Dorian Fuller, Aleese Barron, Louis Champion, Christian Dupuy, Dominique Commelin, Michel Raimbault, and Tim Denham investigated domestication traits in Malian pottery. Susan Keech McIntosh and Roderick McIntosh studied Jenné-Jeno and the regional subsistence systems of the Inland Niger Delta. Shawn Sabrina Murray and other Dia Archaeological Project researchers have contributed to the study of archaeological African rice. Teams working in Dogon country have expanded archaeobotanical sampling. Communities in Konso maintain and transmit knowledge of agricultural land management.

Even when formal papers credit named investigators, excavations depend on local field staff, museum specialists, archaeological authorities, and specialists in plants, ceramics, bones, and chronology. It is worth asking who collected the material, who recognized an important feature, and whose interpretation later became influential. A claim can appear in a modern summary without its original research history. We should follow that chain as carefully as we follow a pottery fragment back to its archaeological layer.

As for ancient labor, most people who harvested millet, fished floodwaters, tended cattle, or repaired terraces left no writing with their personal names. Their achievements remain visible in the changes they made to plants and land. A domesticated crop records selection and practical skill accumulated across generations. A system that integrates rice, fishing, and livestock records adaptation to a changing floodplain. A maintained terrace records not just one construction episode but continual observation of water and soil.

## A different picture of agricultural origins

Let's return to the impression in a broken pot. Viewed on its own, it might look like a minor accident of manufacturing. Put into a sequence of dated samples, it becomes evidence of plant change. Compare it with grains recovered from settlements, and we begin to ask how cultivation spread and diversified. Compare those remains with ecological reconstructions, and we can investigate how farmers responded to rainfall and flooding. Add settlement surveys, and new questions emerge about exchange, cooperation, and urban life.

The strength of archaeological history is that each step remains open to inspection. If a millet impression is identified incorrectly, comparison specimens can be revisited. If a radiocarbon sequence changes, the chronology can be recalculated. If an earlier excavator overlooked small seeds, new sampling can alter the picture. If one river valley dominated research, work in other regions can reveal differences that the old synthesis could not capture.

African farming has no single starting date and no single landscape. Its histories include crop domestication, floodplain economies, local ecological knowledge, urban specialization, and many ways of combining crops with animals and aquatic resources. We know these histories through small, dated traces: a spikelet in clay, rice in a settlement deposit, fish bones by a hearth, or a terrace that requires continuing care. To reconstruct the past responsibly, we must learn to ask not just what people grew, but precisely how we came to know that they grew it.
