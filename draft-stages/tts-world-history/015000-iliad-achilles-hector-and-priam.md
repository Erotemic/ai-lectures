# The Iliad, Part III: Achilles, Hector, and Priam

An old man enters the shelter of the warrior who has killed his son. The man is a king, Priam of Troy. The warrior is Achilles, the most feared fighter in the Greek army. Priam has crossed the battlefield at night carrying ransom gifts, hoping to recover the body of Hector, his son and the city's greatest defender. A few days earlier, Achilles had killed Hector and dragged his body behind a chariot. Priam now approaches him without an army, without the protection of his city walls, and without any reason to expect mercy.

He takes Achilles' knees. He kisses the hands that killed his son. And he asks Achilles to remember his own father.

This is where Homer's *Iliad* is headed. It is remarkable because the epic does not end with the Greek conquest of Troy, the wooden horse, or the death of Achilles himself. Those belong to other stories in the Trojan tradition. The *Iliad* ends when a warrior who has been consumed by anger can recognize the grief of a man who has every reason to hate him. Its final great action is the return of a body for burial.

To understand why that meeting is possible, we need to follow the dreadful events that precede it. Achilles has been absent from battle after a quarrel with the Greek commander Agamemnon, who publicly took from him Briseis, a captive woman assigned as a prize. Achilles was genuinely wronged. In anger he asked Zeus to let the Trojans defeat his Greek allies until they recognized his importance. The Trojans advanced to the ships. Achilles remained in his camp. His closest companion Patroclus finally persuaded him to let the Myrmidons fight under Patroclus' command, using Achilles' famous armor. Patroclus drove the Trojans back, but went too far, was disabled by Apollo, wounded by Euphorbus, and killed by Hector.

The Greeks saved their ships. Achilles lost the person through whom he had tried to help them without surrendering his anger. No apology, treasure, or battlefield success can reverse that loss. The final movement of the *Iliad* begins when news of the death reaches him.

## The moment the news arrives

Antilochus brings Achilles the message: Patroclus has died, and the Trojans have taken the armor he was wearing. The poem does not give Achilles a dignified, restrained reaction. He falls to the ground, covers himself with dust, tears at his hair, and makes a cry so terrible that his mother hears it far beneath the sea.

Thetis is a sea goddess, and her relationship with her mortal son is full of knowledge she cannot escape. She knows Achilles is destined to have a short life if he follows his path of heroic glory. She can bring him equipment, protection, and divine assistance, but she cannot abolish the mortality for which all his brilliance is a temporary answer. When she arrives, she finds a son who now wants to kill Hector even though he understands that the act will bring his own death closer.

This is a great change from the quarrel with Agamemnon. Achilles earlier refused to fight because he judged his life and service insufficiently valued. He had even considered going home. Now his desire to live has lost its hold on him. He wishes Patroclus had not died; he blames himself for failing to protect him; he wants vengeance. The conflict has moved beyond calculating rewards or restoring public honor. Achilles is willing to spend his remaining life on the death of another man.

Thetis cannot stop him. She promises to obtain new armor from Hephaestus, the divine smith, because the previous equipment has fallen into Trojan hands. Until that armor is ready, Achilles must remain outside the main battle. But Patroclus' body is still at risk. The Greeks struggle to recover it, and Achilles cannot remain completely apart.

He goes to the defensive trench without his armor. Athena places a radiance around his head. Achilles gives an immense cry across the field. The Trojans recoil. He does not even need to strike a blow. For the men who have learned to fear his return, the sound announces an alteration in the war's possibilities. The warrior whose absence enabled their successes has come back to the edge of battle, and grief has made him more terrifying than before.

## The divine smith and the world on a shield

Hephaestus receives Thetis and agrees to make armor for Achilles. The description of the shield occupies a major passage in Book Eighteen. Its place in the poem is astonishing. At the point when we might expect an uninterrupted rush toward revenge, the narration slows to imagine an entire world worked into metal.

There are heavenly bodies: earth, sea, sun, moon, and stars. There are two cities. In one, a wedding procession moves through the streets, and people gather to hear a legal dispute concerning compensation for a killing. In the other, an armed force threatens the city, and defenders prepare an ambush. There are fields under cultivation, a vineyard, herds, a dance, and the ocean surrounding the design.

This is more than a magnificent object for a famous warrior to carry. The shield contains activities and communities that the war at Troy is putting at risk. Wedding celebrations, agricultural labor, law, dispute, song, and movement all continue in the imagined human world. The scene of litigation in the peaceful city is especially striking: men argue over the proper resolution of a death, and the community tries to make a judgment. The question of how to respond when someone is killed appears not only in battlefield revenge but in an institution built to stop violence from repeating itself.

The shield invites us to see that Achilles belongs to a much larger world than the quarrel that has consumed him. Its images do not settle the meaning of the war, and scholars disagree about how far to read the shield as a miniature theory of civilization. But its narrative effect is clear. The poem pauses among ordinary human activities just before sending a grieving man into an extraordinary sequence of killing.

The equipment is beautiful, but it cannot save Patroclus. Nor can it make its wearer immortal. The shield displays the multiplicity of life; Achilles will carry it into a campaign in which he is determined to narrow his world to vengeance.

## Reconciliation without repair

When Achilles returns to the assembly, he and Agamemnon end their public quarrel. Achilles lays aside the anger that kept him out of battle. Agamemnon offers gifts and an explanation of his earlier conduct, partly blaming the deluding power called Ate. Briseis is returned. The army can again operate with its strongest warrior.

Yet this reconciliation happens too late to be a happy resolution. Patroclus remains dead. Achilles is barely interested in the gifts. He wants to fight before the army has eaten. Odysseus urges the practical necessity of food, rest, and organization. A fighting force cannot move on pure grief, even when its greatest soldier thinks himself beyond ordinary needs.

Briseis mourns Patroclus, remembering his kindness to her after her own suffering. Her presence is important because she was the object over whom male leaders competed in the opening quarrel, but her life extends beyond that contest. She has lost family, been made captive, been transferred between men, and has now lost someone who treated her with care. We should not mistake her return to Achilles as a restoration of freedom. The epic's heroic economy still contains enslaved people whose vulnerability exposes its costs.

Achilles refuses food. His mother and Athena intervene to sustain him. The immortal horses are brought out, and one of them, Xanthus, is briefly given speech. The horse warns Achilles about his approaching death. Achilles answers that he knows he will die. He is no longer asking whether the future will be long or secure. He is deciding what he will do with the life that remains.

In this moment the poem makes his power inseparable from a kind of damage. He has recovered his rank among the Greeks, but he has lost the companion who made belonging to that community bearable. His return solves a military problem while opening a moral one: what limits, if any, will a warrior driven by mourning accept?

## The gods re-enter the field

Zeus now permits the gods to take sides openly, and the fighting takes on an epic scale. Achilles charges across the plain. He meets Aeneas, a Trojan ally whose ancestry links him to the gods and whose future will become important in later traditions. Poseidon rescues Aeneas from a confrontation he is not destined to survive. Achilles kills opponents with speed and force, and the poem names them one after another.

The individuality of these deaths often depends on brief details: a father's name, a home, a relative, an earlier story. The effect can be brutal. A person becomes recognizable at the very moment the weapon removes him. The heroic catalogue of killing therefore produces both glory and a record of destroyed lives.

The boundary between courage and cruelty grows especially thin when Achilles encounters Lycaon, a young Trojan prince he had earlier captured and sold into slavery. Lycaon was ransomed and returned home only recently. Now he encounters Achilles again and begs for life, reminding him of the earlier transaction. Achilles refuses. He tells Lycaon that even Patroclus died, that Achilles himself must die, and that no pleading can reverse what he has decided. He kills the young man and sends his body into the river.

The logic of the speech is terrifying. Mortality, which might invite compassion, has become Achilles' reason for withholding it. Since all must die, he treats a particular death as something he may impose without restraint. The knowledge that made Gilgamesh travel in search of life has driven Achilles toward the destruction of others. His own impending death does not produce acceptance; it sharpens his anger.

The river Scamander, called Xanthus by the gods, rises against Achilles because of the bodies clogging its waters. The river is not simply a picturesque landscape. In this epic world it is a divine being with its own life, limits, and anger. Achilles fights the river and nearly perishes in the flood before Hephaestus' fire forces the waters back. Nature itself seems to resist the scale of killing. A hero can dominate human opponents and still discover that he does not command the world through which he moves.

## Hector at the gates

As the Trojans retreat toward their walls, Hector stays outside. His father Priam and mother Hecuba plead with him to come inside. They have reason to fear Achilles. Hector has killed Patroclus and taken Achilles' armor. If the Greek hero reaches him, Hector may have little chance of survival.

But Hector cannot simply walk away from the consequences of his earlier decisions. He rejected the advice of Polydamas to withdraw before the situation became desperate. He led the Trojans in the attack on the Greek ships. Now he fears the shame of facing his people after the defeat. He considers whether he might negotiate, then recognizes that Achilles is unlikely to meet him as a partner in peace.

Hector has already been presented as a husband and father. His wife Andromache begged him to protect the city from a safer position, and their child was frightened by the crest on his helmet. He had removed it and held the boy, a brief interval of family tenderness amid the war. That memory deepens the scene at the gates. Hector is brave, but his idea of what honor demands has repeatedly exposed him and his family to the danger they feared.

When Achilles arrives, Hector loses his nerve and runs. They circle the walls three times. The image has no comfortable heroic simplicity: the city watches its champion flee from the man who has come to kill him. Achilles pursues with the speed and relentlessness for which he is famous. Hector's flight is not evidence that he has suddenly ceased to be courageous. It shows a person who understands, in his body, the difference between deciding to confront death and seeing death come toward him.

The gods bring the chase to its end. Zeus weighs the fates of the two men. Athena appears to Hector disguised as his brother Deiphobus and persuades him to stand. Hector believes he has help. He throws a spear; Achilles avoids or survives the attempt. Hector calls to his supposed brother for another spear and finds him absent. He realizes that the god has deceived him. His chance to face Achilles with support was an illusion.

The deception is central to the encounter. The poem gives Achilles overwhelming power and Hector real courage, but it does not turn the duel into a modern sporting contest with neutral rules. Divine favor and fate shape the outcome. Hector nonetheless chooses to make his final resistance. He cannot win, and he fights.

## A death and a refusal

Achilles strikes Hector in the vulnerable space near the neck. Hector falls and asks for a final mercy: return his body to his family for burial. Achilles refuses, speaking with savage hatred. He imagines even eating Hector's flesh. The man who accused Agamemnon of injustice now rejects an obligation that the poem's world treats as basic to a fallen enemy's humanity.

Hector dies after predicting Achilles' own death. Achilles strips him of the armor, which had once been his own and then Patroclus'. He fastens Hector's body to his chariot and drags it across the ground. From Troy's walls, Priam, Hecuba, and Andromache see the spectacle or hear the terrible news. The death is public, and the humiliation is intended to be public as well.

This is the climax many retellings remember: Achilles defeats Hector. Yet if the poem ended here, the story of anger would have become a story in which suffering justifies further suffering. Patroclus is not restored. The grief that drove Achilles remains. The corpse becomes an object through which he attempts to continue fighting a dead enemy.

The contrast between Hector and Achilles also remains complicated. Hector helped drive the war to this point and killed Patroclus, though the final blow followed other attacks and Apollo's intervention. Achilles has lost a beloved friend and is approaching his own death. Each has committed acts within a war they did not individually begin. None of that absolves the treatment of Hector's body. The narrative deliberately leaves us with anger that has escaped the boundary of battle.

## Funeral games and lives that continue

The next book turns toward ritual. Achilles organizes the funeral of Patroclus. The dead man's shade appears to him in a dream and asks for burial, explaining that without proper rites it cannot fully enter the world of the dead. The friends have been separated by death, but the obligation between them continues. Achilles orders elaborate ceremonies, including sacrifices and the burning of the pyre.

The funeral also includes violent actions. Achilles sacrifices Trojan captives at Patroclus' pyre. It is important not to allow the beauty of the lament to hide this cruelty. Achilles' grief is genuine, and his response kills still more people. Homer can describe a great hero's mourning without giving every act of that mourning our approval.

After the rites come funeral games: chariot racing, boxing, wrestling, running, contests with weapons, and other competitions. The heroes quarrel about prizes, judge performances, make mistakes, and sometimes yield. The atmosphere is different from battle, even though many of the same qualities are on display. Speed, strength, skill, reputation, and honor are still being negotiated, but the participants can leave the field alive.

The chariot race especially recalls the poem's opening quarrel over prestige and prizes. A gift can still provoke resentment. Judges can still be challenged. But in the games there are rules, social pressure, argument, accommodation, and the possibility of settlement without killing. Achilles himself must distribute awards and manage other men's claims. The same man who could not endure a challenge to his honor now helps conduct a public contest in which rival claims must be judged.

We should not exaggerate this as a complete moral conversion. Achilles still abuses Hector's corpse. But the funeral games allow a different form of social life to appear: excellence measured within a community rather than through the annihilation of an enemy. They suggest practices by which personal distinction can coexist with shared order, however imperfectly.

## The grieving father and the grieving son

The poem has been preparing us to understand Priam's journey long before he leaves Troy. Priam is not only a king who rules a city. He is a father in a huge family, and the war has destroyed many of its children. Hector was the son on whom Troy depended most, but Priam's loss is more than the removal of a military protector. With Hector gone, the household has lost someone whose ordinary presence organized its emotional life.

Achilles' father is alive, far away. Peleus has not accompanied him to Troy. Achilles imagines the old man wondering whether his son will ever come home. Thetis has repeatedly told Achilles that his life is short. He knows, with a clarity that does not comfort him, that he may never return to care for Peleus. Priam's appeal is therefore directed at a wound that has nothing to do with which army deserves victory. It asks Achilles to think about a son who will not reach his father and a father who has been forced to bury his son.

This is why the encounter cannot be reduced to Priam offering enough treasure. The ransom has significance within the customs of war, but the exchange becomes possible through recognition. A warrior who refused Agamemnon's impressive gifts will now return his worst enemy's body after hearing an appeal to an old father's suffering. The two negotiations take place under different emotional conditions. The earlier offer asked Achilles to resume a public role while his grievance remained unresolved. Priam's request asks him to do something for a man who has no power to confer honor on him in the Greek camp.

There is courage in Priam's journey, though it is unlike Hector's courage at the gates. Priam exposes himself to an enemy while carrying no weapon capable of protecting him. He chooses a risk that offers no military glory. He goes because the body of his son must be brought home, and he is prepared to cross the space that war has made most dangerous to complete that obligation.

The journey also makes a moral claim about enemyhood. People can fight on opposing sides and remain accountable for how they treat one another's dead. That accountability will not end the war. A promise of burial is temporary; Achilles and Priam will still belong to opposing communities. But temporary compassion is real. The poem's achievement is to give it value without pretending that the larger conflict has disappeared.

## The king who crosses the plain

Days pass. Achilles continues to drag Hector's body around Patroclus' tomb. Apollo protects the corpse from lasting damage. The gods disagree about the abuse, and Zeus finally decides that the body must be returned. He sends Thetis to tell Achilles that he must accept ransom. He sends Iris to Priam, directing him to undertake the dangerous journey.

Priam's decision is extraordinary. He is an old king surrounded by grieving people, many of whom have lost sons or husbands. His wife Hecuba fears he will be killed. He still insists on going. He assembles precious gifts and leaves Troy, accompanied by a herald. Hermes guides him through the Greek defenses, disguised as a young man until he reveals his divine nature.

A battlefield usually separates these two men. Achilles stands with the army that seeks Troy's destruction. Priam rules the city Achilles has helped devastate. Their meeting is possible only because the old king makes himself vulnerable to the person he most fears. Priam cannot compel Achilles by force, and his gifts alone cannot guarantee the return. He has to ask for recognition of a bond that precedes the war: both men are sons, and Achilles' father Peleus is old.

At Achilles' shelter, Priam clasps his knees and kisses his hands. He asks Achilles to remember Peleus and to imagine the pain of a father who has lost his son. Achilles is moved. He thinks of his own father, whom he may never see again, and of Patroclus, whom he will certainly never see alive. The men weep together, but for different losses. Priam mourns Hector. Achilles mourns his friend and the life he will not return to.

This is not an easy scene of universal forgiveness. Priam has just touched the killer of his son. Achilles has not undone his killing or his desecration of the body. They remain enemies. The meeting works because the poem lets their grief remain particular while making each man's suffering intelligible to the other.

Achilles eventually has the corpse washed and prepared out of Priam's sight, so that seeing its condition will not provoke a new outburst. He orders a meal. Priam, who has barely eaten or slept, accepts food. The shared meal is one of the poem's most important actions. It brings two exhausted people back, however briefly, into the rhythms of living bodies. They eat because they are alive, even while mourning someone who is not.

Achilles tells the story of Niobe, whose children were killed by the gods and who nevertheless took food afterward. It is not a cheerful reassurance. It is an image of sorrow existing alongside the necessities of survival. Humans cannot maintain a world in which grief stops every ordinary action forever. Eating after a death does not mean that the dead are forgotten. It means the living must continue.

## The difficulty of returning to human time

Notice how often the poem marks the body's requirements during the closing encounter. Priam has not slept properly. Achilles has refused food. Grief has disrupted their ability to inhabit ordinary time. Each man has been living as though the moment of death could be repeated without ever moving beyond it. Achilles circles a tomb with a corpse behind his chariot; Priam waits in a city where Hector cannot come through the gates.

The shared supper does not repair what has happened. It requires a smaller act of consent. The men acknowledge that a body needs nourishment and rest even while a person's mind remains occupied by loss. They sit in the same shelter, observe one another, and briefly understand an enemy through his vulnerability. The old king sees the terrible beauty and force of Achilles. Achilles sees Priam's age, bearing, and grief. Their admiration is not the reward for a heroic victory. It comes from the astonishing fact that two opponents have met and neither has killed the other.

Their agreement over the length of the funeral also rearranges the meaning of time. Achilles' earlier anger made him demand that the army suffer until the proper recognition arrived. His revenge made him pursue Hector beyond death. Now he asks how many days will be required for rites he does not control. Priam has a community to organize, wood to gather, a body to tend, mourners to receive, and a funeral to conduct. Achilles agrees to wait.

That willingness is easy to miss because it is expressed as an administrative detail. Yet granting time to an enemy is the precise opposite of using power to make the enemy suffer for a private grievance. Achilles cannot restore Hector's life. He can make space for those who survive him to perform their obligations. The decision creates no permanent peace treaty and no certainty about Troy's future. It creates a temporary interval in which one family and one city can grieve.

The terms of that interval are concrete. Priam names days for mourning and preparations, a day for burial, and a time after which fighting may resume. The living must do tasks; death imposes work. The poem lets that work appear as meaningful rather than anticlimactic. It has asked us to watch a man's body destroyed in violence; it now asks us to watch people act carefully for the sake of a body that can no longer act for itself.

## The last gift Achilles can give

Priam asks how long he needs to prepare Hector's funeral. Achilles promises a suspension of fighting for the required days. That promise is a gift more important than the ransom's objects. An enemy commander grants a grieving city room to bury its defender.

The poem then returns with Hector's body to Troy. The grief is not expressed by one official voice. Andromache laments the man whose death threatens her and their child with captivity. Hecuba mourns her son. Helen recalls Hector's kindness to her in a household where others blamed her for the war. The three women's laments preserve distinct relationships to the same dead man: husband and father, son, and compassionate kinsman by marriage.

Their words also tell us something about the future. Troy is still standing, but the people in the city can imagine its fall. Hector was their defender, and without him they are exposed. Andromache knows that military defeat will not end at the battlefield. It will enter her home and alter the life of her child. Helen knows the danger of surviving inside a community that holds her responsible for a disaster. The funeral makes visible the civilian world that heroic combats threaten.

The Trojans gather wood, prepare the pyre, burn Hector's body, and raise a mound. The last lines of the *Iliad* do not describe a final assault. They describe Hector's funeral.

That ending is one of the strongest choices in ancient narrative. The poem has given us terrible battle scenes, divine disputes, astonishing armor, feats of strength, and the killing of a city-saving warrior. It then gives the last attention to a burial made possible by an act of pity. The point is not that war has been solved. It has not. Nor has Achilles escaped death. He has not. What has changed is the response to an enemy's suffering.

## The anger that changed shape

The *Iliad* begins when Achilles' anger destroys the relationships needed for the Greek army to function. Its closing movement shows him first replacing that anger with grief-driven slaughter and then accepting a limit to what his grief entitles him to do. The release of Hector's body is small when measured against the whole war. It is immense when measured against the rage that made Achilles refuse every human appeal.

Patroclus' death is the turning point because it exposes the cost of treating honor as something that can be secured through the suffering of others. Achilles wanted the Greeks to know his value. What he receives is knowledge that his companion's life was not protected by his own greatness. His revenge on Hector achieves a military victory and fails to cure that knowledge. Priam's arrival offers a different act: Achilles can see a father grieving the person he has killed and choose to end the humiliation.

There are no final answers here about whether honor, duty, or love should govern a human life. The epic makes each of those commitments vivid enough to conflict with the others. Hector stays to defend Troy, though he fears for his family. Patroclus goes out to rescue the Greeks, though the fight may kill him. Achilles claims justice for himself, then brings devastation, and eventually learns that acknowledging another person's grief need not diminish his own.

The poem ends before the city falls. It leaves us beside a funeral mound, thinking about the people who still live behind the walls. The greatest event in the last book is a human exchange across the battlefield: an old man asks for his son's body, and the warrior who killed him gives it back. That is the final shape of the anger with which the poem began.

## Research and editorial notes (not spoken)

Primary narrative boundary: Homeric *Iliad* Books 18–24. The story intentionally ends at Hector's funeral: the Trojan Horse, sack of Troy, and deaths of Achilles and Priam are known from other ancient traditions, not narrated as completed events in the *Iliad*. The killing of Lycaon, Achilles' conflict with the river Scamander, the shield, the funeral games, and Priam's supplication are treated as scenes in the epic's fictionalized Bronze Age world. Achilles' physical violence against Hector's corpse and Trojan captives is acknowledged rather than removed for modern heroic expectations. Interpretations of the shield and Priam's appeal are literary readings, not historical observations.
