# Two Soils Can Contain the Same Sand and Behave Differently

Take soil from a healthy garden bed and from a compacted footpath nearby. They may have much the same mix of mineral particles. Yet one accepts rain, supports roots and crumbles into aggregates; the other sheds water and resists a shovel. The difference is not necessarily in what the particles are made of. It may be in how they are arranged, what lies between them, and how much pressure the soil has experienced.

Soil scientists distinguish texture from structure. Texture describes the relative proportion of sand, silt and clay-sized mineral particles. Structure describes how those particles and associated organic matter are organized into aggregates and pore networks. Compaction alters those arrangements. Texture is difficult to change on a meaningful scale; structure can change substantially with management, biological activity and traffic.

## Particle size is not personality

Sand particles are relatively large, silt intermediate, and clay particles very small by conventional size classifications. Smaller particles create high surface area per unit mass and often interact strongly with water and ions. A soil rich in sand often drains quickly when well connected by large pores, but “sand equals dry” is not a universal prediction. A compacted or layered soil can behave very differently. Clay-rich soil can hold considerable water while allowing slower movement through small pores; cracks and biological channels may create rapid preferential pathways.

A soil texture triangle places a mineral soil into categories such as sandy loam, silty clay loam or clay according to the percentage of sand, silt and clay. The word “loam” is not a magical guarantee of fertility. It describes a range of particle-size composition. Chemistry, organic matter, depth, structure, drainage and climate still determine what grows successfully.

## Roots live among pores

Imagine soil as solids plus empty spaces that may contain air or water. Large pores are particularly important for drainage, gas exchange and rapid infiltration. Smaller pores retain water against gravity and can supply plants between rain events. Roots need water, oxygen and physical room to penetrate. A medium can contain plenty of water and still be hostile to roots if its pores remain water-filled and oxygen becomes scarce.

Soil organic matter can support aggregate formation, water retention and biological activity. Fungal hyphae, roots and soil organisms help build and maintain pore networks. Yet adding organic matter is not a cure for every drainage problem. A dense underlying layer may still restrict infiltration. Poor site grading may still send water into a bed faster than it can escape.

## What compaction changes

Traffic compresses the soil, especially when it is susceptible due to moisture and other conditions. Compaction increases bulk density and often reduces large pore spaces. Roots may deflect or spread sideways rather than penetrate a dense layer. Water may pond at the surface or move laterally along an interface. The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service describes compaction as a restriction of water, air, fauna and roots, with consequences for runoff and erosion.

A compacted footpath may be useful for walking, just as compacted fill can be essential in construction. But soil optimized to support a road is not necessarily optimized to support roots. The objective changes with the job. The practical gardener wants a stable, porous rooting environment, not the hardest possible soil.

## Testing with your hands, and its limits

A moist handful of soil gives clues. Gritty texture suggests sand. Smooth flour-like particles suggest silt. A sticky mass that can be rolled into a ribbon suggests substantial clay content. A simple jar-settling experiment can provide a rough impression of particle fractions, but organic matter, flocculation, dispersion and irregular settling can mislead the result. A proper laboratory particle-size analysis separates these effects with specified methods.

Texture by feel remains useful because the purpose is often comparative. Compare the garden bed and the path at similar moisture conditions. Check whether roots extend downward, whether water infiltrates after rain, and whether hard layers appear at depth. Pushing a metal rod into dry soil is not comparable with pushing it into moist soil; penetration resistance changes with water content as well as compaction.

## Why digging does not always solve a dense layer

Mechanical loosening can temporarily increase pore space, but repeated foot traffic or cultivation under wet conditions may collapse it again. Working soil when too wet can smear and compress pore walls, worsening structure after the apparent loosening. Raised beds, designated paths, mulch, plant roots and appropriate organic additions can reduce traffic and encourage biological recovery. Which methods work depend on soil type, climate and use.

Consider a newly planted shrub in a hole filled with very different soil from its surroundings. Water may move differently through the backfill and native soil. An apparently generous pocket can form a hydraulic discontinuity or leave roots reluctant to extend into surrounding material. It is often more useful to investigate the entire root zone, infiltration pattern and site drainage than to treat the planting hole as an isolated flowerpot.

## Diagnose the water story

Suppose a bed is wet two days after rain. Is it clay texture, compacted structure, a perched water layer, a high water table, excessive irrigation, or a blocked outlet? Each mechanism predicts different observations. A pit revealing a dense horizon, a comparison with nearby undisturbed soil, root distribution and changes after rain can narrow the options. Do not infer texture from one puddle.

The foundational distinction is simple: texture describes the particles; structure describes their arrangement; compaction changes the pore system. From there, much of gardening becomes intelligible. Soil is not merely an inert container for roots. It is a changing medium in which water, air, living things and solid material must find room to coexist.

## Technical companion: physical definitions

**Texture:** proportions of sand, silt and clay in the mineral fine-earth fraction under the stated classification system. **Structure:** aggregation and arrangement creating pore geometry. **Bulk density:** oven-dry soil mass divided by bulk volume including pores. **Porosity:** fraction of total volume occupied by pores, not a direct measurement of how connected they are.

**Evidence:** USDA NRCS *Understanding Soil Risks and Hazards* explicitly identifies compaction, restricted infiltration, root barriers and context-dependent field indicators. Additional NRCS soil-health and organic matter references establish structure and pore function. Penetration tests must control for moisture and probe type; jar tests are instructional, not laboratory classification.

**Exercise:** Compare a trafficked path with a mulched bed. Record whether water stands after rain, how soil breaks apart, and root growth where visible. Propose at least two mechanisms for each observation rather than treating “clay” as the default diagnosis.
