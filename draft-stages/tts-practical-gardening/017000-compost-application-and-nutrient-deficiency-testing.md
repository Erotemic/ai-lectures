# 017000 — Compost Application and Nutrient Deficiency Testing

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A row of vegetables has pale leaves and weak growth. A gardener adds more compost every year, so it seems natural to assume the plants need still more fertilizer. But poor growth can have several causes: insufficient light, dry roots, compacted soil, excess water, a nutrient shortage, a nutrient excess, or a pH that makes some nutrients difficult to acquire. Compost is valuable, but it is not a universal cure. Good gardening begins by asking what the soil and plants actually need.

## Compost changes more than nutrient supply

Finished compost adds organic matter, influences how soil aggregates, and can improve water management and biological activity in appropriate contexts. It also contains nutrients, though their amount and release rate depend on the inputs and composting process. A bag or pile of compost is not a standard concentration fertilizer. Two products called compost may differ substantially in nitrogen availability, phosphorus, salts, pH, and maturity.

Adding organic matter can help a sandy soil retain water, while dense clay may benefit from better structure over time. But dumping compost into a hole surrounded by poorly drained clay can create an abrupt boundary that complicates water movement. Mixing and application methods should match the intended planting and actual soil. More organic matter is not always better, especially in containers or beds already rich in nutrients.

Phosphorus is a useful example. Many composts contain appreciable phosphorus even when nitrogen is the nutrient the gardener hopes to add. Repeated heavy applications can accumulate phosphorus beyond crop need. Excess nutrients may move off-site with eroded soil or runoff, depending on soil and landscape conditions. A garden can become over-enriched while plants still show yellow leaves caused by wet roots or iron availability.

## A symptom is not a nutrient test

Plant leaves can reveal clues, but their colors are not chemical assays. Nitrogen deficiency may produce older-leaf yellowing because nitrogen is relatively mobile within the plant. Some iron deficiencies produce yellowing between veins on newer growth. Yet root damage, water stress, pests, pH, temperature, and disease can produce overlapping patterns. The location of the symptom on the plant and the soil history help establish hypotheses, not a definitive diagnosis.

Look first at distribution. Are all plants affected or only those in a low wet patch? Are newer leaves pale while older leaves stay green? Did yellowing follow heavy rainfall, a change in irrigation, or fertilizer application? Are stems stunted or roots rotted? A single plant beside a downspout suggests a different problem from uniform pale growth across a well-drained bed.

Soil testing is a way to turn guesses into data. An appropriate laboratory test can measure pH and extractable nutrients with methods and interpretations calibrated to a regional soil and intended crop. The numerical values depend on the extraction method; a result cannot be interpreted reliably without the laboratory's crop-specific recommendations. Home color-strip kits can be useful for some purposes but are not substitutes for a well-designed laboratory fertility assessment.

## Sample soil so the laboratory learns about the bed

A meaningful sample represents a defined growing area with similar history and soil. Do not mix soil from a fertilized raised bed with soil from an unfertilized lawn or a persistently wet patch. Collect multiple small cores or scoops from the appropriate root-zone depth for the crop and laboratory instructions, remove surface litter, mix those portions thoroughly, and submit the specified amount in a clean container. Label the sample with its location and planting intent. Unusual problem spots may deserve separate samples.

The results should be interpreted with the history of what was applied. Record compost source, approximate volume or depth, fertilizer amounts, dates, and planting. If the lab reports adequate phosphorus, adding phosphorus-rich compost simply because the leaves are yellow is not evidence-based. A recommendation might favor a targeted nitrogen source, pH management, better drainage, or no fertilizer at all.

A critical distinction is between soil fertility and plant uptake. A soil may contain a nutrient, yet waterlogged roots cannot obtain enough oxygen to take it up effectively. A high or low pH can reduce availability of specific nutrients. Compaction can limit rooting and gas exchange. Managing the physical environment may fix the apparent 'deficiency' without adding any nutrient.

## Apply compost by a defined purpose

If the goal is a surface mulch, consider particle size, maturity, weed seeds, runoff, and whether the material should stay away from plant stems. If the goal is a soil amendment, use a rate based on measured soil need and the compost's composition where possible. If it serves as a nutrient source, estimate nutrient release and avoid assuming all total nitrogen becomes plant-available in the first season. Fresh or unfinished compost can immobilize nitrogen or introduce other problems and should not automatically be treated as finished growing medium.

Suppose a small garden receives a five-centimeter layer of compost each spring. That may sound modest, but over years it represents a large mass of material and potentially substantial nutrients. Instead of repeating the habit indefinitely, use a soil test and plant response to guide the next application. A thinner targeted application or a different fertility plan may serve the garden better.

Monitoring after a correction matters. Photograph plants from the same position, record new growth, soil moisture, weather, and application dates. Old yellow leaves may not turn green even after the underlying problem is corrected; newer growth can provide better evidence. Avoid stacking several amendments at once, because then it becomes impossible to tell which change helped or harmed.

Compost is a tool for soil stewardship, not an answer to every plant symptom. The deeper skill is to connect plant observations with the physical root environment and laboratory measurements. Ask what is missing, what is excessive, what the roots can access, and what evidence a new application will provide.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Soil-test request:** define crop and area; note texture, amendments, application history, irrigation and drainage. Use a regional laboratory's sample depth, sampling method and crop-specific interpretation, not generic numeric 'ideal nutrient' thresholds.

**Rate illustration:** A 1 cm layer over 10 m² is 0.10 m³ of compost; dry mass and nutrient content depend on bulk density and composition. Repeat applications accumulate material even if each individual layer looks thin.

**Diagnostic alternatives:** older vs newer leaf symptoms; localized wet patch vs full bed; pH interference; root oxygen; compaction; pests or disease; light and temperature. Foliar appearance alone does not establish which nutrient is limited.

**Environmental:** Avoid excessive phosphorus application and runoff pathways. Compost maturity, salinity, disease risk and contaminant quality vary. Follow relevant extension and local laboratory recommendations.
