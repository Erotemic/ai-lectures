# The Mask Beyond Peter Parker: Symbiotes, Miles Morales, and the Spider-Verse

Spider-Man is one of those characters whose costume seems impossible to improve. Red and blue, black web lines, enormous white eyes. You can recognize him when he is three centimeters tall on a comic-book cover. So naturally, one of the great decisions in Spider-Man history was to take away the red and blue. Another was to give the name to a different teenager. And another was to make entire crowds of Spider-people who do not even agree about what being Spider-Man means.

These aren't all the same kind of change. One is a costume experiment that became a horror story. One creates a new hero who inherits a name. One turns decades of alternate continuities into both spectacle and an argument about identity. Together they offer a better question than whether a new interpretation is canon: what, exactly, do we want to preserve when we say a version of Spider-Man feels right?

A quick orientation, because comic-book universes can become ridiculous before you've even opened the issue. The original Peter Parker began in 1962: a teenage science prodigy whose powers, moral mistake, and Uncle Ben's murder shape his choice to help others. Marvel later published alternate versions of that life. One line might have an older Peter with decades of history; another might start him over as a teenager. The characters can sometimes cross between universes, but they didn't all experience one identical sequence of events. The films are adaptations with their own continuities as well. Knowing which Peter we're talking about solves an astonishing number of apparent contradictions.

## First, the suit gets ideas of its own

In the mid-1980s, Peter acquired a black costume during the *Secret Wars* crossover. It was the sort of visual change that could have remained a stylish temporary novelty. Against the familiar red-and-blue design, the huge white spider on a nearly black body looked stark and dramatic. The suit was sleek enough to look like a new logo without losing the essential Spider-Man silhouette.

But the publication story has a twist. Readers of *The Amazing Spider-Man* number two hundred fifty-two saw Peter return in the black suit before the crossover had published the issue showing him acquire it. *Secret Wars* number eight later filled in that backstory. Fictional chronology and publication chronology were doing separate jobs: one explained where Peter got the outfit; the other kept several monthly titles and a major event moving together. Reading collected editions in a neat sequence can conceal the surprise original readers encountered.

The design's own history has an even better fan-story wrinkle. Randy Schueller, a Marvel reader, had submitted an idea for a black suit in the early 1980s. Marvel purchased the proposal, and the eventual published design and alien explanation developed through further editorial and artistic work. Schueller's original idea wasn't simply the final symbiote story in miniature. The later mythology took a different route. That's a useful reminder that even something as iconic as this costume can have a long, collaborative development history involving a reader outside the regular bullpen.

Within the fiction, the new outfit eventually turns out not to be an outfit at all. It's a living alien symbiote, and its relationship with Peter becomes threatening. A costume usually lets a hero declare who he is. This one can act as if it has opinions about who he should be. Peter rejects it. The rejected entity later bonds with Eddie Brock, becoming the villain Venom. Venom made his full debut in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number three hundred, published in 1988.

That's a clever mutation of the secret-identity premise. Spider-Man uses a mask to separate his public mission from his private person. Venom is an intimate enemy who has worn that separation, knows more than he should, and can turn the hero's visual identity into a threat. A red-and-blue suit announces the protector. A black suit with a white spider can announce the creature that thinks the protector has wronged it. The name Spider-Man is suddenly not the only way to carry his visual language.

## What happens when Peter gets older?

The costume was not the only part of Spider-Man that creators wanted to change. Peter grew up. He built relationships. He and Mary Jane Watson married in the main comics in 1987. Other stories, including 1987's *Kraven's Last Hunt*, pushed into dark psychological territory. Kraven attempts to take Spider-Man's place, and the resulting story asks whether copying the costume and the outward acts amounts to becoming the man.

A comic can do extraordinary things when a character accumulates history. It can also make its publisher nervous. A married, more experienced Peter isn't the same as the insecure student who began the series. For some readers, that growth is the reward for decades of attention. For others, the young man discovering responsibility is the most approachable version. An editor looking for new readers faces a genuine problem: how much of this accumulated life must somebody learn before the latest issue makes sense?

One response is to start over somewhere else instead of destroying the old version. *Ultimate Spider-Man* began in 2000, written by Brian Michael Bendis and drawn by Mark Bagley. It offered a contemporary teenage Peter in a separate universe. It could explore the original situation with different dialogue rhythms, more room for relationships, and updated surroundings, while readers of the main continuity could continue following the older Peter.

The important point is not that the year 2000 made the 1962 origin obsolete. It's that the origin was strong enough to survive a new telling. The old premise could be studied, compressed in some places, expanded in others, and still make somebody care about a boy for whom immense power doesn't produce an easy life.

## Miles arrives in a world that already knows Spider-Man

The Ultimate experiment eventually created something more consequential than another Peter Parker. Miles Morales first appeared in *Ultimate Fallout* number four in 2011, after the death of the Ultimate universe's Peter Parker. His subsequent solo series, *Ultimate Comics Spider-Man*, was written by Brian Michael Bendis with art by Sara Pichelli.

For anyone meeting him through the animated films, here is the important comics context. Miles is a Brooklyn teenager with his own parents, friendships, worries, and extraordinary abilities. He isn't a younger Peter wearing the costume because a marketing department needed a new color scheme. In his original universe, Peter has died. Miles lives with the public meaning of Spider-Man before he knows how to inhabit that identity himself.

Compare the moral starting points. Peter's origin begins with a wrong decision that becomes a personal catastrophe. Miles inherits an example. He has seen a hero who stood for something, and he has to decide whether the mask demands a particular kind of person or leaves room for someone like him. He carries the fear of failing a standard he didn't set. That's a different dramatic problem, even before you look at the costume or the powers.

Miles's distinctive abilities, including camouflage and a bioelectric attack in various versions, make him fun to write in action. But the more important differences belong to his life. His parents, Rio Morales and Jefferson Davis, are not background pieces that can simply be exchanged for Aunt May. His uncle Aaron complicates questions of loyalty and admiration. His home and community shape the way he understands danger. Spider-Man works because the person under the mask has particular relationships, and Miles has relationships that aren't Peter's.

The early work by Bendis and Pichelli also had to make Miles readable before a reader knew anything about him. A black-and-red costume, a different body and personality, and a supporting cast with their own tensions let the new Spider-Man be recognizable without making him a duplicate. Marvel later brought Miles into the main comic-book continuity through the aftermath of the 2015 *Secret Wars* event. It allowed Peter and Miles to coexist as Spider-Man characters in a shared setting rather than requiring one to erase the other.

That is a striking solution to the problem of inheritance. When two heroes can use the name, the name has to stand for something larger than a single biography. But each life still has to matter. If Miles succeeds only by behaving exactly like Peter, the story has given us a duplicate. If he carries none of the original hero's obligations, the borrowed name loses its meaning. The interesting work happens between those extremes.

## Gwen Stacy gets a story she couldn't have had before

Another alternate world turns a familiar tragedy inside out. In the original Peter Parker comics, Gwen Stacy is the girlfriend who dies during a confrontation with the Green Goblin. For many readers, that history makes her inseparable from a single terrible event. Then Marvel imagines a Gwen Stacy who is bitten by the spider instead.

Her first appearance was in *Edge of Spider-Verse* number two in 2014, created by writer Jason Latour and artist Robbi Rodriguez, with Rico Renzi's coloring a major part of the comic's visual identity. This Spider-Woman, often called Spider-Gwen and later Ghost-Spider, comes from Earth-65. In that world the roles and consequences are rearranged. Gwen has a band, a family, and her own problems with responsibility. Her Peter Parker isn't the hero of the same story with the names swapped.

The brilliant move is that Gwen becomes a subject rather than a memory. An old character's possibilities had been constrained by the famous ending of her original continuity. Now we get to ask what sort of comic she supports when her choices drive the plot. The hood, the white-and-black costume, the bright color design, and the sense of a different cultural scene help declare that this is a new creative proposition, not a resurrection paperwork exercise.

This is what alternate continuity can be good for. It doesn't have to erase the older version. It can reveal how one fictional choice—the death of Gwen, the survival of Peter, the person the spider bites—shapes everything that comes afterward.

## A thousand Spider-people and one dangerous word: canon

The major 2014 *Spider-Verse* event, including *The Amazing Spider-Man* number nine, brought different Spider-people together against a multiversal threat. On one level, it is glorious comic-book excess. If you have ever wondered how many ways somebody could wear the spider symbol, or how many genres could accommodate a spider-powered hero, a multiverse event is an excellent excuse to find out.

But quantity alone isn't a story. Bringing several versions together creates a question about what they share. Some have lost loved ones. Some inherited a heroic example. Some had very different accidents, bodies, voices, or surroundings. If every Spider-person must follow one fixed path, then the multiverse starts to feel less like possibility and more like a factory. If nothing connects them, the name turns into decorative branding.

*Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse*, the animated film released in 2018, turned that problem into a spectacular piece of visual storytelling. It centers Miles, pairs him with other Spider-people, and lets their different looks and attitudes matter. The artists use printed-comic textures, deliberate variations in movement, color, and composition to produce something that doesn't simply aim to look like live action. The film won the Academy Award for animated feature at the 2019 ceremony.

Awards aren't the point of the film's achievement, though. Think about what it means to see different drawing traditions apparently sharing the same space. A character from one visual universe can be funny, startling, or moving precisely because the frame refuses to pretend every world has identical rules. The film turns the idea of comic-book variation into an experience you can see and hear.

The 2023 sequel, *Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse*, pushes the problem of inheritance in another direction. Miles encounters Spider-people who believe that certain tragic events are necessary to preserve their universes. Suddenly the franchise's long history of repeated losses becomes a debate inside the fiction itself. Is being Spider-Man a choice to help, or a sentence to endure a predetermined list of tragedies? Does respecting a tradition mean obeying its supposed rules?

That is a very Spider-Man argument. The earliest Peter learned to take responsibility for his own choices. Miles's resistance to an inherited script asks whether responsibility might sometimes require disobeying a person who claims to know how the story must go. A multiverse is most interesting when it makes room for different answers, not just different costumes.

## Which Spider-Man did you meet first?

There's one more reason not to treat a single version as the only possible measure of success. Many fans don't encounter Spider-Man in comics first. They meet him in a cartoon, a film, or a videogame. The director Sam Raimi made a Peter whose personal sacrifices are expressed through unabashed melodrama. The Andrew Garfield films built another version of his awkwardness, charm, and grief. The Tom Holland interpretation placed his teenaged hero in a world of larger superhero institutions and complicated mentors. None of these films has the same job as a monthly comic.

A game has a still different responsibility. It must make the city a place you can move through. Web swinging is no longer just an image of liberty; the player has to feel it in the timing of a button press and the curve of a movement. In a comic, the artist can draw the one pose that conveys flight. In a film, the animator or camera can carry us through an impossible route. In a game, the motion has to remain satisfying even when the player chooses the route badly. Those are different artistic problems, and they produce different kinds of attachment to the character.

Ask fans which Spider-Man is theirs and you often learn which version first made the tension feel personal. Maybe it was Peter hiding from his aunt, Miles leaping into an impossible future, Gwen playing drums, a live-action hero losing someone, or simply the feeling of swinging between buildings with a city stretching out below. An adaptation doesn't need to reproduce every event from a comic to capture that experience. It does need a convincing person beneath the mask and a reason that person's choices matter.

The black suit, Miles Morales, Gwen Stacy of Earth-65, and the Spider-Verse all test the limits of recognition. The best changes are not changes for their own sake, and they're not attempts to preserve a sacred checklist either. They take the Spider-Man ingredients—ability, desire, loss, humor, community, responsibility—and arrange them into another life worth following. Peter Parker started it. He doesn't have to be the only person who can make it work.

Treat apparent contradictions as questions about specific versions, not errors to repair by merging their plots.

- *Secret Wars* #8 (1984), *The Amazing Spider-Man* #252 (1984), and #300 (1988): early black-suit history and Venom.

### Questions

1. Is it more difficult to reinvent a hero's origin or to reinvent the obligations that arise from it?
2. What does Venom show about the difference between displaying a symbol and living by its values?
3. What does Miles inherit from Peter, and what must he refuse to inherit to become a full character?
4. How does a film make different comic-book styles legible in motion? What does the film gain that a comic page cannot, and lose that a page can do?
