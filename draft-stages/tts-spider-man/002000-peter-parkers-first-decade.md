# Peter Parker's First Decade: School, Work, Love, and the Green Goblin

A superhero normally promises the reader some relief from ordinary limits. Spider-Man offered the reverse bargain: Peter Parker can escape the limits of gravity, but not the demands of a calendar. He can outfight a criminal and still fail to appear somewhere he promised to be. He can understand a scientific problem that would baffle most adults and still struggle to make enough money. When we follow the Spider-Man comics from their first issue in 1962 through the early 1970s, these conflicts do not sit in the background. They become the structure of Peter's life.

This is not a retelling of every adventure. It is the story of how a series learned to let its protagonist grow, how a supporting cast became indispensable, and how a comic once associated with exuberant youthful troubles made room for a devastating loss. The route passes through high school, the newspaper office, college, romance, the rise of the Green Goblin, and the moment that changes Peter's relationship to the idea that he can save everyone.

## A boy trying to earn the right to be a hero

The first Spider-Man story, in *Amazing Fantasy* number fifteen, tells of Peter gaining powers, using them for personal advantage, refusing to stop a fleeing criminal, and later discovering that the same criminal murdered his Uncle Ben. He understands that his abilities impose an obligation beyond his immediate self-interest. But the next problem is practical. How will a teenager with this obligation live?

In *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one, the character finally has a magazine built around him. The issue already treats superhero life as a confusing employment and publicity problem. Peter tries to find ways to support his household. He approaches the Fantastic Four, but the meeting does not deliver the obvious solution. These established heroes are not a payroll office waiting to hire him. The joke has consequences: Spider-Man's world belongs to the same universe as grander, richer-seeming heroes, but he does not automatically receive their status, resources, or adult confidence.

A second figure gives this problem a public voice. J. Jonah Jameson, editor and publisher associated with the *Daily Bugle*, treats Spider-Man as a menace or a publicity threat. There is something absurd about a man whose paper profits from stories about a masked figure while attacking that figure in print. Peter eventually earns money by supplying pictures of Spider-Man to the newspaper. To do it, he uses his knowledge of where Spider-Man will be because he is Spider-Man. The secret identity, instead of solving his financial trouble, creates an almost perfect machine for generating it: he is paid for documenting his own dangerous work, often by someone who despises the costumed person he depicts.

Jameson is more than an angry man shouting headlines. He is the embodiment of a central Spider-Man difficulty. Courage and public recognition are different things. Public narratives have owners; the people who do admirable things do not necessarily control their portrayal. For the reader, the joke works because we know two things the editor does not. For Peter, it creates yet another person whose approval he cannot easily earn without revealing what he must keep secret.

## The gallery of impossible opponents

The early series rapidly builds a set of adversaries, each with a distinct logic. Doctor Octopus uses mechanical arms to extend his will and physical reach. The Vulture turns the air into territory he can exploit. Electro weaponizes electrical power. The Sandman makes a body itself unstable. The Lizard is tied to a scientific experiment and a human scientist, Curt Connors, whose personal life complicates the notion of a monstrous enemy. Mysterio manipulates perception and showmanship. The Green Goblin hides his identity behind a monstrous persona and possesses money, technology, and ambition.

These villains are not interchangeable obstacles. They give Spider-Man different kinds of problems to solve. Against some, raw strength is insufficient and Peter's intelligence matters. Against others, the dilemma is moral: what if the villain is someone who can be helped? Against a figure who constructs illusions, Spider-Man's ability to sense danger may not resolve what he should believe. Against a costumed criminal with power and public connections, Peter's own low social position becomes part of the conflict.

The tone is often boisterous. Villains announce themselves extravagantly. Spider-Man jokes while he fights. There are elaborate devices, absurd misunderstandings, and sudden turns of plot. But an early Spider-Man reader also knows that the hero must eventually return to the problem he tried to escape when the fight began. The larger the spectacle, the more sharply the series can bring him back to a damaged personal relationship or a bill that still needs paying.

## The person inside the costume gets older

The comic does not leave Peter in exactly the same high-school situation forever. This is a major feature of the first decade. His social world changes, and it changes at a pace that once made the series feel like an account of a life rather than a permanent episode of a television sitcom.

High school introduces the comic irony of Flash Thompson. Flash bullies or belittles Peter but admires Spider-Man. Peter is forced to watch someone treat his unmasked self with contempt while praising the very person standing in front of him. As a gimmick, this is funny. As characterization, it reveals how unevenly we distribute respect. Flash does not know enough to connect intellect and courage in the same boy. Peter, meanwhile, cannot correct him without surrendering privacy.

Aunt May is not merely a person to be rescued on occasion. She provides the household in which Peter's obligations become intimate and routine. He worries about her health and about making her anxious. Sometimes the secrecy that he justifies as protection also prevents her from seeing his actual reasons for being absent. His care is sincere, but sincerity does not give May perfect knowledge of the person she has raised.

The series introduces other colleagues, classmates, and romantic possibilities. Betty Brant, who works at the *Daily Bugle*, is part of Peter's early emotional life. Liz Allan and others populate the high-school world. They do not all remain in fixed roles. As Peter encounters new settings, the social texture shifts. People misunderstand him, later see him differently, or carry their own troubles into a relationship that at first seemed to exist only for Peter's convenience.

One essential shift arrives when Peter enters college. In *The Amazing Spider-Man* number thirty-one, he begins studying at Empire State University. The issue introduces Gwen Stacy and Harry Osborn. The book's center of gravity moves. A character who began as an isolated schoolboy is now making adult friendships, exploring romance, and entering an institution where his intellectual abilities ought to matter. Instead, college is also a place where his responsibilities make him seem distracted and discourteous. Being a good person and appearing to be a good companion are not the same undertaking.

## Under the machinery

The college transition belongs to a famous sequence, often called the Master Planner story, culminating in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number thirty-three. Peter needs a vital serum for his seriously ill Aunt May. He encounters Doctor Octopus and is trapped beneath enormous machinery in a flooded chamber. The apparent obstacle is simply too heavy. The room is filling. The medicine is out of reach. Even Spider-Man's strength may not be enough.

What follows is one of the clearest examples of why comics are a visual art rather than illustrated prose. Steve Ditko stretches the effort across panels. A movement that could be summarized as a hero lifting a heavy object becomes a study of accumulating resistance. The body compresses. The space feels smaller. The attempted lift becomes an ordeal of time, and then the relationship between frames and movement changes as Peter begins to make progress.

This is not the same as a fight against a villain. At the climactic moment the enemy is the limit of Peter's own body, and the motivation is not abstract victory but someone at home who needs him. The scene connects physical power to the most domestic of concerns. He is not lifting the machinery because he wants to demonstrate that he can. He is doing it because refusing to continue would mean abandoning May.

That sequence is a useful hinge between the origin and the developing series. In the origin Peter learns that failing to help can have disastrous consequences. In the Master Planner climax, he acts before success is guaranteed. The promise of responsibility is no longer a caption at the end of a short story. It is a body straining in a room from which there seems to be no escape.

## The change in the artwork

Steve Ditko left the series after issue thirty-eight. Starting with issue thirty-nine, John Romita Senior became the major visual architect of Spider-Man's next phase. It is easy to treat an artist change as a production fact, important only to collectors. But it affects the storytelling. Ditko's Peter often looks narrow-shouldered, angular, and physically awkward outside the suit. Romita's work brings a different social polish and romantic energy. The young characters increasingly have the visual presence of a lively melodrama, not only a story about an isolated schoolboy.

The change does not erase the character's financial pressures or guilt. It expands what kinds of social stories can occupy the page. Gwen Stacy becomes a central romantic figure. Mary Jane Watson, already anticipated in earlier material, enters the story with a very different social confidence. Peter's life grows crowded. His secret no longer separates merely a lonely child from a world of adults. It complicates the bonds among people who are old enough to make serious commitments, misunderstand them, and suffer for them.

The shift in creative leadership is also a reminder that the long-running comic is not a single artwork written by one unchanging mind. Lee and Ditko established much of its grammar. Romita transformed its look. Later writers and artists would change its moral emphasis and the consequences of its stories. A history of Spider-Man is necessarily a history of successive creative teams negotiating with an inherited character.

## Norman Osborn and the danger of knowing

The Green Goblin brings the public and private worlds into especially dangerous contact. Early stories preserve the mystery of the Goblin's identity. Eventually the antagonist is revealed to be Norman Osborn, an influential businessman and father of Peter's friend Harry. This is more disturbing than merely naming the criminal behind a mask. Someone connected to Peter's life has become his opponent, and the link makes the cost of exposing identities immediate.

Norman possesses forms of power Peter does not: money, corporate status, a position in the adult world, the ability to exert pressure through institutions and family relationships. Yet he also possesses a grotesque instability beneath that respectable exterior. Where Peter's secret identity hides his efforts to protect others, Norman's masks and duplicity conceal harmful ambition. Both inhabit more than one public role, but those roles have opposite moral meanings.

Harry's presence prevents the story from dividing neatly into hero and villain households. A son may live in the shadow of a powerful father and be harmed by that father's failures. The damage of Norman's choices spreads through people who did not choose the conflict. Peter must decide how to be Harry's friend when his heroism puts him into opposition with Harry's father. The secret makes even the effort to help a form of withholding.

## A comic that collided with its industry's rules

In 1971, the series published a story about drug use in *The Amazing Spider-Man* issues ninety-six through ninety-eight. A federal health-education official had asked Stan Lee to help communicate the dangers of drug abuse. Marvel incorporated the subject into the ongoing comic. The Comics Code Authority, an industry approval system governing many mainstream American comic books, declined to approve the issues. Marvel published them without the code's seal.

The details of the code's revision process are more complicated than the simplified legend that one heroic publisher instantly defeated censorship. Historical records held and summarized by researchers show that rule changes were already under consideration, even as the refusal to approve the Spider-Man story exposed a serious problem with the approval system. The incident is still revealing. A superhero comic aimed at young readers could encounter an institutional obstacle precisely because it attempted a public-health story rather than avoiding difficult contemporary subject matter.

The 1971 storyline also matters inside Peter's fictional world, where Harry Osborn's difficulties with drugs become part of the network of family, friendship, and pressure surrounding the Goblin. Spider-Man cannot resolve the problem by punching a stranger. Some threats require care, honesty, and help; superpowers do not supply a simple treatment for all forms of harm.

## Gwen Stacy and the limit of rescue

By 1973 Peter's relationship with Gwen Stacy has become central to the comic. Their difficulties are recognizably human: jealousy, misunderstandings, responsibilities not explained, and the complicated place of friends and family. Gwen is not simply a symbol from the start; in the larger run she is a person Peter loves, a figure embedded in the social world the series has gradually assembled.

Then *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one hundred twenty-one changes the terms. Norman Osborn, as the Green Goblin, threatens Gwen. She falls from a bridge during the confrontation. Spider-Man attempts to save her, but she dies. The exact mechanics of the fatal injury have generated discussion in and around the comics; what is essential to this account is the terrible outcome: Peter acts, and the rescue fails.

The issue was written by Gerry Conway, with art by Gil Kane and inking contributions associated with John Romita Senior and others. This was not a quiet continuation of the Lee-Ditko period but a decisive act by a later creative team. The subsequent issue brings a confrontation with Norman, himself apparently killed. Yet Peter's grief has no tidy ending. Gwen is still dead. He cannot repair what has happened simply by defeating the person responsible.

The origin story had made failure the source of his responsibility. The death of Gwen makes something else undeniable: responsibility cannot guarantee success. For a hero whose defining skill seems to be arriving at the last possible instant, the loss is a particular kind of catastrophe. It attacks the faith that trying hard enough will always be enough.

Mary Jane's role in the aftermath is worth observing. She had often appeared as a vivacious, socially assured presence. In the shadow of Gwen's death, that familiar outward personality does not settle everything we should believe about her. The comic begins to treat the people around Peter as capable of surprising emotional depth. It also opens a different future for his relationships, although that future will be shaped by many later stories and editorial decisions.

## What changed in a decade?

Look back at where we began. In 1962 Peter Parker was a young boy, intensely self-conscious, suddenly powerful, and morally awakened by an irreversible mistake. By 1973 the series had followed him out of high school and into college; given him colleagues, friends, and romantic commitments; made his enemies part of his private life; and demonstrated that his heroism could not protect every person he loved.

These are not merely plot additions. They change what being Spider-Man means. At first the question is whether Peter will use his powers responsibly. Then it is how to balance competing obligations. Eventually it becomes how to continue living responsibly when doing everything one can has still ended in loss.

It is tempting to organize a superhero history as a list of costume changes and villains' first appearances. Those details are fascinating, but they obscure the series' most important achievement during its first decade: Peter's world acquired memory. Decisions left traces. People returned in new roles. A college classmate could become a friend, a romance could become a commitment, and a threat against the mask could pierce the life beneath it. That continuity was the mechanism by which readers could experience Peter not simply as a recurring brand but as a person moving through time.

And yet the humor remained. So did the inconvenience of rent, the humiliations of misunderstandings, and the exhilaration of a person who could take the most crowded city in America and find a new way through its sky. Spider-Man's early decade did not replace youthful comedy with tragedy. It taught both forms to inhabit the same life.
