# J. Jonah Jameson: A Man Who Keeps Hiring His Own Worst Enemy

Imagine owning a newspaper, spending years trying to destroy the reputation of a masked vigilante, and then paying a young freelance photographer for pictures of that very vigilante. Now imagine the photographer is the vigilante. He keeps arriving with miraculous images, you keep buying them, and you keep using them to denounce the man who took them.

At some point you would expect the universe to have the decency to explain the joke. But the universe in question is Spider-Man's, and the man in question is J. Jonah Jameson. He is often played as a machine that turns the words Spider-Man into outrage. He has a spectacular moustache, a voice made for shouting, and a remarkable ability to convert other people's heroism into his own editorial certainty. The routine is funny. It is also one of the most ingenious relationships in the entire comic.

Without Jameson, Peter has physical enemies, money problems, secrets, and deadlines. With Jameson, all those pressures come together inside an office where Peter is expected to cash a check. Spider-Man is a public hero, Peter is a precarious employee, and Jonah is both the loudest critic and one of the people paying for the work that lets Peter survive.

This is not a straightforward defense of Jonah. A man who commissions experiments to manufacture enemies for a superhero has earned some serious criticism. But dismissing him as an ignorant fool misses why the character lasts. He is funny because he is so certain, dangerous because he has power, and occasionally compelling because not every question he asks is unreasonable.

## Who made this man the editor of reality?

Jameson appears very early in the Spider-Man story, in the first issue of *The Amazing Spider-Man* in 1963. The premise has a marvelously cruel symmetry. Peter's greatest ability is to help people while concealing himself. Jameson's professional power is to tell other people what those concealed actions mean. Peter can climb a wall, but he cannot control tomorrow's headline.

The original setup is not simply that the press hates superheroes. Jameson has a much more specific grievance: Spider-Man is masked, mysterious, theatrically successful, and able to escape normal scrutiny. An ordinary citizen of the fictional city cannot listen to Peter's private thoughts. From that citizen's perspective, a stranger with tremendous strength is swinging over the street, acting without a visible license, and offering no name at which to direct a complaint.

There is a real civic question somewhere inside the bluster. If a powerful person acts anonymously, how do we judge their conduct? What if their actions cause property damage? What if the audience sees a rescue but not the event that led to it? In a world without access to the reader's omniscient viewpoint, some suspicion would be sensible.

Jonah's failure is not asking the question. It is deciding the answer in advance. He takes the mask as evidence of bad motives and keeps finding ways to make every new event confirm what he already believes. This is more than a bad attitude. As a newspaper editor or publisher, he has the means to make his preferred interpretation circulate. He is a character about who gets to narrate public life.

And there is a nasty punchline. The most immediate source of Spider-Man images is frequently Peter Parker himself. Jonah's paper benefits from photographs Peter could take because he knows exactly where Spider-Man will be. Readers enjoy an information advantage that no one in the Bugle newsroom has. Jonah thinks he is buying evidence from an enterprising youngster. Peter knows the seller and the subject are the same person. It is an arrangement built for endless comedy, but also for moral discomfort. Peter takes money from a man whose hostile framing hurts Spider-Man. He needs the money. The arrangement keeps working because both men have reasons to tolerate something they dislike.

This gives the employment relationship more bite than a generic secret-identity gag. Peter's problem is not only that he must avoid revealing himself. He is also tied to an institution that misrepresents him, yet pays him for a service he can provide. It is possible to laugh at the setup and still recognize a less glamorous real-world pattern: people sometimes have to work for organizations whose public choices they dislike.

## When suspicion becomes a supervillain factory

Here is the boundary Jameson crosses. It is one thing to write a harsh editorial about someone who refuses to show his face. It is another to pay for someone to be transformed into a weapon for hunting him.

In *Amazing Spider-Man* number twenty, Jameson's campaign helps create the Scorpion. Mac Gargan, a private investigator, receives dangerous enhancements through scientist Farley Stillwell, with Jameson financing the scheme. The resulting Scorpion does not dutifully prove Jameson's theory. He becomes a threat himself. Spider-Man ends up protecting the very man who helped create the problem.

Notice how neatly the plot turns a public argument into a physical one. Jameson is convinced that Spider-Man's uncontrolled power threatens the city, so he tries to arrange a counter-power of his own. But the man he equips is less accountable, less trustworthy, and far more directly interested in violence. Jonah has attempted to solve the danger of a masked individual through a privately funded superhuman experiment. It is hard to design a better demonstration of his blind spot.

The pattern repeats in a more mechanical form in *Amazing Spider-Man* number twenty-five. Professor Spencer Smythe builds a Spider-Slayer robot, and Jameson pays to use it against Spider-Man. A machine literally devoted to hunting one individual gives the editor's obsession a body. It is not enough for Jonah to fill pages with accusations; the accusation has become an enormous device that can chase its target down the street.

The humor is almost too perfect. A newspaper whose business is making claims about reality is funding a machine designed to make one of those claims true. Jonah wants to catch the menace so that he can say he caught the menace. He has mistaken his editorial conclusion for a public service.

These stories prevent the character from becoming only a lovable crank. His actions cause danger. People other than Jonah can be injured because he has treated his certainty as permission. The comics' willingness to let Spider-Man rescue him does not erase the original recklessness. If anything, it makes the moral contest clearer. Peter is willing to save a man who keeps making his life worse; Jonah is reluctant to revise his opinion of a man who keeps saving him.

## The man in the chair, and the people around him

A caricature can shout indefinitely, but a recurring character needs relationships. The Daily Bugle is not Jonah alone. Peter's work brings him into contact with colleagues who have different judgments and different responsibilities. Robbie Robertson, in particular, provides an important counterweight to the editor's anger. The presence of a calmer, more thoughtful journalist changes how we perceive the whole institution. It means the newspaper has internal disagreements. The comics do not require every person at the Bugle to share Jonah's obsession.

This matters to the humor. A rant is funniest when someone else is allowed to react to it. If all the characters shout at the same volume, Jonah becomes background noise. In a room with Peter trying to earn money, Robbie trying to do his job, and Jonah searching for a new reason to dislike Spider-Man, the rant has consequences and resistance. It can annoy, amuse, embarrass, or distract different people in different ways.

There is also a story hiding in Jonah's family. His son John Jameson is celebrated as an astronaut in the earliest comics, and Peter's heroics complicate the public attention surrounding him. An editor proud of one heroic son is confronted by a masked hero who seems to attract admiration without offering the same public credentials. The contrast does not make every later act of jealousy inevitable, but it helps explain why the character is not operating from a blank emotional slate. To Jonah, fame is not an abstract quality. It is something he thinks should belong to the right people.

A particularly useful way to read him is to ask when he is behaving like a journalist, when he is behaving like a parent, and when he is behaving like a man who wants to win an argument. Sometimes two motives appear in the same speech. The best Jonah scenes let the audience recognize the conflict, rather than decide that a moustache and a raised voice are an entire personality.

## When the movies turned the volume up

Many fans first encountered Jonah through J. K. Simmons in Sam Raimi's films. Simmons's speed, indignation, and ability to turn a sentence into a small explosion made the character instantly legible to an audience who had never seen a comic-book newsroom. That performance is a great adaptation of one layer of Jonah: the unforgettable comic rhythm of an editor who treats every interruption as a personal insult.

But the films also show the value of giving him a boundary. In Raimi's first *Spider-Man*, Jonah refuses to reveal Peter Parker as the source of photographs when threatened by the Green Goblin. This detail matters far more than another funny insult would. He is abrasive, unfair to Spider-Man, and still capable of protecting a person who depends on him. Whether we read that as journalistic ethics, loyalty, or defiance, it complicates the picture.

It is a reminder that a character can be an antagonist without being a villain. Jonah's behavior can genuinely hurt Peter. Jonah does not need a master plan for world domination to make a scene difficult. His power is different: a job, a public platform, a stubborn idea about virtue, and the capacity to make other people's lives miserable in relatively ordinary ways.

Later screen incarnations play with the media environment in which Jonah operates. A loud newspaper editor and a loud conspiracy-minded broadcaster do not occupy identical positions. The former owns the established paper where Peter earns money; the latter speaks through a more fragmented attention economy. Both can attack a masked hero, but their social mechanics differ. It is more illuminating to ask what each version says about the flow of information than to ask whether the desk should be bigger or the moustache thicker.

## The conversation you would never expect him to have

One of the most interesting late interventions comes in Chip Zdarsky and Michael Walsh's *Peter Parker: The Spectacular Spider-Man* number six, published in 2017. It is structured around an extended conversation between Spider-Man and Jameson. The story does not need to solve their relationship with a punch-up. The men actually talk, and the history between them becomes harder to dismiss as a running joke. By the end, Peter reveals his identity to Jonah.

That development works because the revelation is not simply a piece of trivia: the editor finally has to connect two people he has kept mentally separate for years. The annoying freelancer and the alleged menace become one human being. Everything Jonah thought he understood about Peter's job, his photographs, and their long argument is suddenly reclassified.

There is a difficult but rewarding irony here. Peter has withheld information to protect himself and others; Jonah has condemned that withholding for years. Yet knowing the truth can become the beginning of a relationship, not the end of one. The comic asks whether a man who has built his identity around being right can accept evidence that changes the story of his life. That is a stronger dramatic question than whether he can shout louder than a superhero.

The issue is especially good evidence against the idea that Jonah must stay frozen in a single comedic function. You can let him suffer, understand, or change without turning him into a soft-spoken saint. The voice may remain familiar; the person behind it can become more intelligible.

## Is Jonah ever right?

There is a cheap answer to the question. Yes, masked strangers with enormous power should be questioned. There is also a cheap opposite answer: no, because the reader knows Spider-Man is a good person. Both dodge the character's actual function.

A better answer requires two separate judgments. Jonah can identify a real problem and behave atrociously in response to it. He can be correct that an anonymous hero presents questions about accountability and wrong to assume every rescue is a hoax. He can possess a legitimate interest in public safety and still spend money creating the Scorpion. He can protect a source in one story and exploit public fear in another. The contradictions are not all neatly reconciled. Long-running serial fiction rarely offers that luxury.

Spider-Man and Jonah need each other as characters because they are opposite kinds of certainty. Peter knows that he must act but rarely knows how the consequences will fall. Jonah knows exactly what he thinks but is remarkably bad at allowing consequences to revise the thought. Peter has physical power without public legitimacy. Jonah has public power without the ability to swing across town and save somebody. Peter often loses his reputation by doing the right thing. Jonah frequently protects his reputation by saying the wrong thing with conviction.

That is why the photographs are such a good joke. Jonah keeps paying Spider-Man for the raw material of attacks on Spider-Man. Peter keeps accepting the checks. Their relationship is an absurd little economy of mutual dependence, and in that economy the loudest person in the room is not necessarily the one with the most information.

You do not have to forgive Jonah to enjoy him. You do not have to declare him right to take his questions seriously. You only have to see how much work the character does. He turns heroism into an argument about evidence, identity, and who gets to write the headline. And he does it loudly enough that nobody can pretend they failed to hear him.
