# When Spider-Man Met the Goblin and the Comics Got Dangerous

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture  
*Lecture ID:* 015000  
*Section:* characters-and-conflicts  
*Editorial form:* Historical and character-focused essay on the Green Goblin's changing narrative role.  
*Spoilers:* The Green Goblin's identity, Gwen Stacy's death in 1973, Norman's apparent death, and Harry's inheritance of the Goblin identity.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The Green Goblin is a man dressed like a Halloween accident, riding a machine that should never have passed a safety inspection, carrying explosives in the shape of pumpkins. By all reasonable design standards, he ought to be an absurdity.

In his first Spider-Man appearance, the Goblin is involved in a scheme to humiliate the hero as part of a phony movie production. There is room for the Incredible Hulk to turn up. The whole affair belongs to an early Marvel universe that was still discovering how much nonsense it could fit into a monthly comic.

Yet this villain eventually becomes the person who can make Spider-Man's most private world feel unsafe. The Goblin learns Peter's identity, destroys relationships, and leaves consequences that outlast any one battle. His most consequential weapon is not the pumpkin bomb. It is his proximity to the people Peter loves.

How did a prankish, cackling figure from a colorful adventure become the center of one of superhero comics' defining tragedies? The answer involves a mystery, a father and son, an artistic handoff, and a decision made by people producing a monthly comic who did not expect us to discuss it half a century later.

## A mask that promises another mask

The Goblin debuted in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number fourteen, in 1964, during Stan Lee and Steve Ditko's partnership. He appeared as a flamboyant technological criminal with a concealed human identity. The disguise mattered. Many early Spider-Man villains could be recognized immediately by their extraordinary powers or occupational equipment: Doctor Octopus has mechanical arms, the Vulture is an older man with an aviation apparatus, the Lizard is a scientist turned into a creature. The Goblin presents an additional question. Who is the human being under that face?

A comic-book mystery creates a different kind of reader. You look at the villain in one sequence and at Peter's acquaintances in another. The story encourages you to wonder whether the two worlds overlap. If a familiar person is secretly dangerous, the villain can enter the hero's domestic life without the costume. That is more frightening than the prospect of another rematch on a rooftop.

Norman Osborn emerges gradually in the early comics as a wealthy, forceful man connected to the young Harry Osborn, Peter's college acquaintance and eventual friend. Harry debuts in issue thirty-one. Norman is identified by name before the final Goblin reveal. The story builds a social structure in which the man behind the mask can matter: a father with power, a son trying to earn affection, and an ordinary college student whose life is already entangled with theirs.

The Goblin's identity was revealed as Norman Osborn in issue thirty-nine in 1966, the first issue of the series drawn by John Romita Senior after Ditko's departure. Stan Lee wrote the story. The reveal is not just a name being supplied to satisfy a guessing game. The Goblin has also discovered that Spider-Man is Peter Parker. Both masks become vulnerable at once.

You will encounter a frequently repeated story about how the reveal caused a quarrel between Lee and Ditko. It says Lee wanted a familiar face and Ditko wanted the Goblin to be someone the readers had never met. The problem is that later recollections conflict. Ditko's published statements indicate that he had planned the character's identity and planted visual clues. Lee and Romita offered accounts that do not line up neatly with his. A careful history should not choose the most dramatic anecdote and declare the disagreement solved. What the printed issues establish is that Ditko helped construct the mystery, and that the Norman reveal was published in the first Romita issue.

This is also a useful example of how quickly the meaning of a character can change when a new creative team takes over. The Goblin in issue fourteen is a dangerous showman whose plots can still feel like extravagant comedy. The Goblin of issue thirty-nine is a man who knows where Peter lives. The difference is not simply an increase in strength. The story has moved the danger indoors.

## A villain who can come to dinner

Norman has a tremendous narrative advantage over many villains: he is someone Peter cannot simply avoid by refusing to meet him in costume. He is Harry's father, a figure from the supporting cast, part of the social world Peter is supposed to preserve. And because Norman knows Peter's identity, the usual division between superhero danger and ordinary life starts to collapse.

It would be easy to describe this as "Norman is evil and Harry suffers." That misses why the relationship is so effective. Harry wants his father's approval. Norman's wealth and position give him a respectable public identity even while the Goblin commits crimes. Harry's distress can appear in stories that do not involve a Goblin attack at all. The resulting damage is domestic, emotional, and repetitive. A glider is not required.

This is one reason Willem Dafoe's Norman, in Sam Raimi's 2002 *Spider-Man*, remains so memorable. The film stages Norman as a charming and terrifying presence around Peter and Harry; the conflict erupts in laboratories, dinners and private rooms as well as rooftop battles. The movie's version of Norman has its own continuity and particulars, but it understands the underlying structural trick. A Green Goblin story is strongest when Peter cannot tell whether a relationship is separate from the battle.

There is an uncomfortable complication. If we make Norman the sole source of all the harm in Harry's life, Harry becomes a passive symptom. The best Goblin stories resist that simplification. Harry makes mistakes. He can act cruelly. He also struggles with pressures that the superhero conflict did not create. That combination is what makes a father-and-son story more than a useful way to produce a second villain.

The character's design participates in the problem. The Goblin face has an enormous fixed grin, a grotesque expression that can be read as mockery even when the man inside it is furious. Spider-Man's mask conceals his emotions; his dialogue and body movement have to communicate them. The Goblin's face seems always to be laughing at him. His bombs are shaped like children's holiday decorations, but the stakes can be lethal. The visual register promises a joke while the narrative prepares a wound.

## The story that changed the meaning of winning

The turning point is *The Amazing Spider-Man* numbers one hundred twenty-one and one hundred twenty-two, published in 1973. Gerry Conway wrote the stories; Gil Kane penciled issue one hundred twenty-one, John Romita Senior inked it, and the issue had other credited production artists. The division of labor matters because a comic's emotion is not produced by its script alone.

For a reader who does not know the sequence, here is the essential context. Gwen Stacy is Peter Parker's girlfriend. Norman Osborn, as the Green Goblin, takes her captive and throws her from a bridge. Spider-Man reaches her with a web line, but the rescue does not save her. Gwen dies. Peter is confronted with a catastrophe at the precise moment when he believes he has acted quickly enough to prevent one.

The story has generated decades of discussion, including arguments about the physics of the fall and the fatal injury. Those arguments are legitimate readings of a famous comic image, but they should not obscure the fundamental turn. At the origin, Peter fails to act when he has the ability to intervene; he then loses Uncle Ben. Here he does act, and Gwen still dies. A moral rule that seemed to promise an answer no longer feels sufficient.

This is what the Green Goblin brings into the series. Not merely a more murderous opponent, but a limit to Spider-Man's control over outcomes. Peter can make the right choice and still experience an irreversible loss. The premise does not imply that trying was pointless. It means responsibility cannot be understood as a guarantee that no one will die when you try hard enough.

We should be careful not to discuss Gwen only as a device designed to injure Peter. She had been part of the comic's college ensemble for years, with a personality, relationships and a future that readers had invested in. There is a serious criticism to make of storytelling that kills a woman largely to reshape the male protagonist. That criticism does not make the event's dramatic effect imaginary. It asks us to notice that the emotional force comes from an established person being lost, and to consider whether the story gives enough of her own life the weight it deserves.

In a 2006 *Back Issue* interview conducted by Dan Johnson, Conway and Romita looked back on the decision. Their memories are vivid but not identical. Romita recalled resisting an initial idea of killing Aunt May, because he thought she remained essential to Peter's domestic premise. Conway described the creators as trying to produce a story that mattered without foreseeing the long historical significance later readers would give it. The interview also makes clear that deciding Gwen's fate and deciding Norman's fate were related but distinct creative questions.

The retrospective is valuable partly because it punctures a comfortable myth: that famous stories always arrive with their place in history already understood. These creators were working under deadlines, arguing about character, trying to make a monthly comic emotionally consequential. The fame came later. We should take their memories seriously without treating a recollection made decades afterward as a transcript of an earlier meeting.

Issue one hundred twenty-two follows the loss with a confrontation. Peter is furious and tempted by revenge. Norman apparently dies when his own glider strikes him. It would be easy to frame this as a satisfying exchange: Gwen dies; the villain dies; balance returns. But balance does not return. Gwen is still gone. Peter does not receive his old life in exchange for the defeat of his enemy.

Conway's later explanation is important here. He explicitly discussed revenge as insufficient, unable to make the loss better. The comic's endpoint is therefore not just the defeat of a villain. It is the recognition that even justified anger cannot undo the consequences.

## The Goblin keeps a family name

The story's second aftermath begins with Harry. He is Norman's son and Peter's friend; the death of his father becomes part of his own crisis. Harry eventually puts on the Green Goblin costume. *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one hundred thirty-six in 1974 is one of the early stories in that inheritance. The Goblin has become more than a single individual's disguise. It is a role that a wounded family member can pick up, carrying grievances that belonged to someone else.

This might be the franchise's most unsettling Goblin idea. A dangerous person can leave a pattern behind. Harry does not have to become a perfect copy of Norman to be damaged by the attempt. In some stories he seems desperate to prove that he is like his father; in others he seems to be fighting the knowledge that he might be. Those are different psychological conditions, and the distinction gives later writers useful territory.

The comics eventually bring Norman back, too. Long-running superhero continuity has a way of returning supposedly final enemies to the board. That can blunt the original story's finality. But the Green Goblin is well suited to revival because the threat is not exhausted by a single physical battle. Norman can be a businessman, manipulator and father, a public figure and a private tormentor. A writer can use him to corrupt institutions as well as to fire a pumpkin bomb at Spider-Man.

The risk, of course, is overuse. If Norman is responsible for every misfortune in Peter's life, Peter's world becomes a single villain's elaborate conspiracy. It loses the texture of bad luck, ordinary choices and unrelated human conflict that makes Spider-Man stories feel alive. The Goblin is most effective when he exploits a functioning social world, not when he replaces that world entirely.

The central question is not whether he is Spider-Man's strongest enemy. Doctor Octopus can match Peter's science; Venom can evade his warning sense; other villains can endanger whole neighborhoods. The Goblin attacks something more specific. He makes Peter wonder whether he can have a private life that remains private at all.

At the beginning, a colorful crook wants to humiliate a costumed hero. At the middle, a known family man unmasks that hero. By 1973, his presence has revealed that a secret identity cannot promise safety and that an honorable rescue attempt cannot guarantee success. Then Harry inherits the damage, and a nominally defeated enemy continues shaping the living.

You can laugh at the pumpkin bombs. You are supposed to, at least a little. The joke makes it easier to forget that the person carrying them knows the way home.
