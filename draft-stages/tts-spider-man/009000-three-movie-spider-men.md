# Three Movie Spider-Men, Three Different Problems

*Series:* Spider-Man: The Comics, the Character, and the Culture  
*Lecture ID:* 009000  
*Section:* reinventions-and-adaptations  
*Editorial form:* Comparative film criticism, with necessary story context.  
*Spoilers:* The three Raimi films, both Marc Webb films, and the Watts trilogy, including the conclusions of *The Amazing Spider-Man 2* and *No Way Home*.

Imagine interviewing three young men for the same job. Each has the same astonishing qualifications: superhuman strength, a talent for swinging over traffic, and a history of making his personal life considerably worse by trying to help strangers. You ask a simple question. What is your biggest problem?

The first looks embarrassed and says that he cannot have the life he wants. The second says that he cannot understand what happened to his family, or keep the people he loves safe from the answers. The third says that the world's greatest superheroes have already saved the planet, and he wants to know why they will not let him help.

They are all Peter Parker. They are also three different movie franchises, three different kinds of leading-man performance, and three different ideas of what Spider-Man is supposed to overcome. Sam Raimi's trilogy began in 2002 with Tobey Maguire. Marc Webb's two *Amazing Spider-Man* films began in 2012 with Andrew Garfield. Jon Watts's three solo films for the Marvel Cinematic Universe began with *Homecoming* in 2017, starring Tom Holland, after that Peter's introduction in *Captain America: Civil War*.

Fans often argue about which actor is the best Peter and which is the best Spider-Man. That can be enjoyable, especially when someone insists it is an objective scientific finding. But it mixes together several separate questions. How does an actor portray embarrassment? What kind of jokes does this Spider-Man tell? What does a director do with the movement? What do the writers think heroism costs? Which world does the film force Peter to inhabit?

Let us compare the answers rather than giving three actors medals.

## Raimi: a superhero story in the shape of a sacrifice

In the first Raimi film, Peter is a painfully shy teenager who admires Mary Jane Watson, is bullied at school, and lives with Aunt May and Uncle Ben. After gaining his powers, he sees a way to win money and admiration. Then he fails to stop a fleeing criminal, and Uncle Ben is killed. This is the basic origin, but Raimi makes the aftermath the grammar of almost everything to follow. Peter does not simply learn to fight. He learns that every attractive possibility must be weighed against his obligation to someone else.

Look at Maguire's Peter in ordinary rooms. The hero who can stop a moving vehicle struggles to explain himself to his aunt. He hesitates before speaking to Mary Jane. He makes excuses that are transparently bad, and then has to live inside the silence they create. In the Spider-Man suit he can be bold. At a dinner table he becomes a man whose mouth is an unreliable piece of equipment.

The comedy of Raimi's films often comes from that gap, and so does the pain. An action sequence may be loud, brightly colored, and physically exuberant. The cut back to Peter's life is not triumphant. It is the rent, a job, a missed date, an elderly relative waiting for an answer. *Spider-Man 2* pushes this contrast nearly to collapse. Peter is exhausted. He cannot sustain his responsibilities, his studies, his work, or his relationships. His powers begin to fail him. The movie treats the loss of power as both a fantastic problem and a psychological one: what if your body simply stopped cooperating with a role you could no longer endure?

The great train sequence in *Spider-Man 2* is often remembered for its spectacle, and rightly so. Spider-Man must stop a runaway elevated train; his mask comes off during the effort; passengers see the young man beneath it. But the surprising part comes afterward, when ordinary New Yorkers quietly protect his secret and look after his exhausted body. The crowd behaves like the kind of neighbor he has tried to be for them. For a moment, the film returns something to Peter instead of taking from him.

Alfred Molina's Doctor Octopus belongs in that same conversation. Otto Octavius begins as an inspiring scientist, someone Peter could imagine becoming, before an experiment leaves him physically and morally entangled with his mechanical arms. Otto's obsession with finishing his project becomes a distorted answer to Peter's temptation to abandon responsibility. Both men want their work to mean something. The difference is whether the desire becomes more important than other people's lives.

Raimi's style helps the drama. The camera is fond of emphatic close-ups and startling transitions; the action can turn suddenly into horror, especially around Norman Osborn and Otto's mechanical limbs. The villains are large, theatrical personalities. Willem Dafoe's Green Goblin, with the monstrous expressions of an actor fighting his own face, attacks Peter's attachments as much as his body. J. K. Simmons's Jameson supplies a different theatrical key: the feverish newspaper boss who can make a professional insult sound like a civic emergency.

There is a limitation to this approach. If every time Peter becomes happy the film immediately presents another reason he should not be, the moral can start to feel like a punishment machine. Mary Jane, particularly, can become someone whose choices are subordinated to Peter's dilemma. And when *Spider-Man 3* adds the black costume, Sandman, Harry's revenge and Venom, the conflicts compete for dramatic oxygen. Even so, one of that film's more interesting ideas is that public admiration does not necessarily make Peter wiser. Wearing the suit does not prevent him from becoming vain, cruel, or self-satisfied.

The Raimi Peter keeps asking whether he may have the ordinary life he wants. The answer is not simply no. It is that love and obligation require decisions rather than an automatic reward for being good. That is a melodramatic conception of Spider-Man, in the best sense of melodrama: feeling becomes visible in gesture, music, sacrifice and the awful effort of telling someone the truth.

## Webb: a mystery about parents that becomes a story about trust

A decade after Raimi's first film, Marc Webb was assigned a difficult proposition: introduce a new Spider-Man to people who could still remember the previous origin almost frame by frame. The 2012 *Amazing Spider-Man* makes a telling change. Its Peter is preoccupied with the disappearance of his parents, and a briefcase belonging to his father leads him toward Oscorp and scientist Curt Connors. He is an outsider, but his alienation is pointed outward as a question. What have the adults hidden from him?

Garfield's Peter has a different social energy from Maguire's. He is restless and wiry. He can be awkward, but he can also be funny with someone he trusts, aggressive with someone who threatens him, and plainly pleased by the physical pleasure of his powers. His Spider-Man is not simply a timid boy transformed into a confident performer. The wit and the unease are both present before he puts on the mask.

The love story is central. Emma Stone's Gwen Stacy is not merely an audience member assigned to watch Peter climb a building. She has her own intelligence, works in a laboratory, questions Peter's evasions, and can do important work while a superhuman battle unfolds. Some of the films' strongest moments involve two people trying to have an actual conversation while one of them is keeping a dangerous secret. Their exchanges move quickly enough that the audience can imagine what they enjoy about each other between scenes.

But the parental mystery and the romance do not always strengthen one another. The first film pulls Peter toward an explanation of his biological parents and Oscorp's research; the emotional center of the second film is his promise to Gwen's father. At the end of the first movie, Captain Stacy dies after helping Spider-Man, and Peter promises to stay away from Gwen. The sequel makes the inability to keep that promise a continuing source of tension. The relationship is appealing because these two characters understand each other. It is dangerous because understanding does not dissolve the risks.

*The Amazing Spider-Man 2* expands Oscorp into a near-universal explanation for the dangers in Peter's city. Max Dillon becomes Electro; Harry Osborn returns in search of a cure; Peter continues investigating his family. These strands create an enormous apparatus around a fairly intimate tragedy. Gwen dies in the struggle with Harry's transformed Goblin, and the film asks its hero to go on after failing to save the person with whom he had been making a future.

This is where comparison with the comics becomes useful. Gwen's death in *The Amazing Spider-Man* number one hundred twenty-one in 1973 was a landmark story in a different continuity, with a different sequence of events and different creative team. The film borrows the catastrophe but not simply the panels around it. It has spent two movies establishing Gwen as an active collaborator and emotionally credible partner. Her loss removes not only someone Peter loves, but someone who knew his secret and helped him reason through his life. That changes the shape of the empty space afterward.

The danger in adapting a famous tragedy is that the audience may watch the character as a countdown clock. A fan knows the story's reputation and begins expecting the fall. Gwen deserves more than being remembered as the person who will die; the strongest portions of Webb's films are those in which she has a recognizable life in the present. The weak portions let franchise mysteries and prospective villains crowd her out.

Webb's Spider-Man therefore feels less like Raimi's endlessly self-denying young man than someone searching for a coherent account of himself. He wants to know who left him, who lied to him, what promises he can keep, and why the right choice may still leave a terrible outcome. The answer is not obtained by solving the Oscorp mystery. It is lived, imperfectly, through relationships.

## Watts: a neighborhood hero surrounded by giants

Jon Watts faced an almost opposite problem. Audiences did not need another full origin story. They already knew the spider, the uncle and the responsibility. And this Spider-Man had been introduced inside a universe full of established heroes. The provocative question was no longer how a lone teenager becomes a superhero. It was how a teenager can remain himself when superheroism already has celebrities, corporate resources, teams and job offers.

*Homecoming* understands the comic potential immediately. Peter has helped fight the Avengers in *Civil War* and is now back in Queens, where the enormous personal event that impressed him seems to have made very little difference to his day-to-day status. He records his exploits on his phone. He pesters Happy Hogan for an assignment. He wants Tony Stark to recognize his potential. Meanwhile there are actual people in his neighborhood who need him, and actual school activities he keeps missing.

The film can turn a familiar Spider-Man problem into a status problem. Peter wants to be more than a local hero precisely when he has not mastered being one. The fancy suit from Stark is useful and entertaining, but it complicates the old pleasure of a clever kid who designs his own solutions. If the suit offers guidance, surveillance and elaborate gadgets, how much of the accomplishment belongs to Peter? The film's most pointed answer comes when Stark takes it away. Trapped under wreckage, Peter must convince himself to lift it without the equipment or the adult's approval. The scene deliberately recalls the famous machinery-lifting sequence from the comics, but here it is also about a boy trying to stop treating his own worth as somebody else's assessment.

Adrian Toomes, the Vulture, is an effective antagonist because his anger belongs to the same world as Peter's insecurity. Toomes has been pushed out of a salvage job by the technology and institutions associated with the Avengers. He converts recovered alien equipment into weapons and argues, in effect, that the powerful have already made their rules. This is not an excuse for his crimes. It is the mechanism that makes his grievance legible. The film's cruelest joke is that the man Peter is investigating turns out to be the father of the girl he is taking to a school dance. Peter can encounter his enemy in a car before he can solve the problem with his fists.

*Far From Home* changes the question. After Tony Stark's death, everyone seems to want Peter to become the next Iron Man. Quentin Beck, Mysterio, offers both false friendship and fabricated spectacle, aided by drones and convincing illusions. Peter's problem is not simply believing the wrong person; he is vulnerable to someone who claims to relieve him of a role he does not want. The movie's trickster villain is appropriate for an era in which convincing images can be manufactured at industrial scale. Peter needs to learn how to judge what he sees and whom he trusts.

Then *No Way Home* takes his craving for recognition and inverts it. His identity becomes public; relationships, education and ordinary mobility suffer. Doctor Strange's attempted solution brings characters from the other live-action movie continuities into this one. That makes room for scenes fans had wanted for years, but the emotional argument is quieter: three Peters with different histories can offer one another understanding without erasing what each lost. May's death places Holland's Peter in a direct confrontation with the moral costs that the earlier films had carried in their origins. At the end, people forget Peter Parker, and the hero must choose whether to reintroduce himself to those he loves. The result is a Spider-Man stripped of several of the advantages that once made this version distinctive.

One can read this ending as a correction toward the lonely, low-resource comics hero. One can also ask why he had to lose so much to be considered the authentic version. That tension is interesting. The MCU films made a genuine experiment out of Spider-Man living among gods and billionaires. The ending does not prove the experiment was a mistake; it shows how far the writers were willing to go to recover secrecy and anonymity.

## The person who survives the comparison

So which interpretation is the most faithful? Faithful to what? The early Ditko comics emphasize a prickly, anxious, occasionally selfish boy. The Romita era brings social confidence, romance and a more glamorous public world. Later comics make Peter a working adult, a husband, a teacher, a professional scientist, a man whose age sometimes advances and sometimes seems to retreat. Any film selecting one of these possibilities leaves others behind.

Raimi excels at the burden of an unwanted calling. Webb gives us a young man whose curiosity and romance are in constant conflict with secrets. Watts makes the superhero's place in a preexisting world the central obstacle, then turns that obstacle into a question of identity. Each trilogy or pair also has failures. A good comparison permits those failures to belong to the actual films, not to some scorecard that awards points for mentioning a favorite comic.

There is a useful test for each movie. When the mask comes off, does Peter become less interesting, or more? If the answer is less, the film has probably spent too much energy on the spectacular Spider-Man and too little on the human being who has to keep coming home afterward. In the best scenes of all three versions, taking the mask off reveals a problem that acrobatics alone cannot solve.

And that, more than the exact color of a suit or the mechanical specifications of a web-shooter, may be why all three can feel like Spider-Man.
