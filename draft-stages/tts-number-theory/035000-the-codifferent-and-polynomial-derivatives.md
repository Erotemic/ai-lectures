# The Codifferent and Why a Polynomial Derivative Appears

Suppose someone tells you that the discriminant of the Gaussian integers is minus four. The minus sign records the complex embedding. The factor four says that something happens at the prime two. But the discriminant is a number attached to the ground field. To measure what happens in the extension itself, we want an ideal of the Gaussian integers. That ideal will be generated by two i.

Why two i? It is the derivative of the polynomial X squared plus one, evaluated at its root i. This is not a coincidence reserved for quadratic examples. When an extension ring really is generated by one integral element, the derivative of its minimal polynomial generates the different. We will prove that statement, and in doing so see why derivatives enter ramification theory.

## Inverting the trace dual

Let L over K be a finite separable extension of number fields. Write A and B for their respective rings of integers. Recall the trace dual of B relative to A: all x in L whose trace of xb lies in A for every b in B. Because B consists of integral elements, products of elements of B have integral trace, so B lies inside this trace dual.

The trace dual is not merely an A-module. It is a fractional ideal of B. If x pairs integrally with all b in B, then for any c in B, the product cx pairs integrally with b because cb still belongs to B. So multiplication by B preserves the trace dual. It is a full lattice, and it is finitely generated, as follows from the same trace-lattice argument used in the Dedekind-domain lectures.

A nonzero fractional ideal of a Dedekind domain has an inverse. Define the different to be the inverse of this trace-dual fractional ideal. Because the trace dual contains B, its inverse lies inside B. Thus the different is a genuine integral ideal.

There are two directions to keep straight. A larger trace dual corresponds to a smaller different. If the trace dual equals B, the different is the unit ideal, which records perfect integral trace duality. If extra denominators enter the trace dual, the different acquires prime factors.

It is tempting to call the different simply a discriminant written in a different language. That misses its main advantage. The different lives in B. It can have different powers at different prime ideals above one rational prime, even when the discriminant records only their combined contribution.

## A power basis is the situation we can calculate

Let A be a discrete valuation ring, or the ordinary integers when appropriate. Suppose B equals A of alpha: every integral element of our extension can be written uniquely as an A-linear combination of one, alpha, alpha squared, up through alpha to the power n minus one. Assume alpha satisfies a monic separable polynomial f of degree n.

I claim that the trace dual is one divided by f prime of alpha, times B. Equivalently, the different is the principal ideal generated by f prime of alpha.

A derivative looks like an unlikely source of integral trace conditions. The bridge is interpolation. Let alpha-one through alpha-n be the distinct conjugates of alpha. For each conjugate alpha-i, the derivative f prime at alpha-i is the product of the differences between alpha-i and all the other conjugates. Those products are denominators in Lagrange interpolation: the formula that recovers a polynomial from its values at n distinct points.

Here is the one coefficient we need. Take a polynomial h of degree below n. Lagrange interpolation expresses h of X as a sum over the conjugates. The term attached to alpha-i is h of alpha-i, multiplied by the product that vanishes at every other conjugate, divided by f prime of alpha-i. Compare the coefficient of X to the power n minus one on both sides. You find that the sum of h of alpha-i divided by f prime of alpha-i equals the leading coefficient of h.

Now choose h equal to X to the power j, where j runs from zero through n minus one. The leading coefficient is zero until j equals n minus one, and then it is one. The sum over conjugates is exactly the field trace of alpha to the power j divided by f prime of alpha. We have proved that those traces are all integers: zero, zero, and finally one.

What about higher powers? Reduce them using the monic equation f of alpha equals zero. Every high power of alpha becomes an A-linear combination of the first n powers. The traces of alpha to any nonnegative power divided by f prime of alpha are therefore also in A. So every element of B divided by f prime of alpha pairs integrally with every element of B. We have proved one inclusion: one over f prime of alpha times B lies inside the trace dual.

To get equality, not merely inclusion, we need n dual coordinates. They can be built from the coefficients of f. If f is X to the n plus a-n-minus-one times X to n-minus-one and so on, form the polynomials obtained by removing successively lower powers from the quotient of f of X minus f of Y, divided by X minus Y. Evaluating at alpha produces n polynomial numerators. Dividing each by f prime of alpha gives the trace-dual basis to one, alpha, and its successive powers.

The key finite-dimensional calculation is triangular: those numerator polynomials have leading powers n minus one, n minus two, down to zero, each with coefficient one. Thus they themselves form an A-basis of B. Dividing all of them by f prime of alpha shows that the full trace dual is precisely B divided by f prime of alpha. The promised formula is proved.

## What the derivative is measuring

There is a direct geometric intuition behind that formula. If two conjugates of alpha become close in the p-adic sense, their difference becomes divisible by a high power of the local uniformizer. Since f prime of alpha is the product of differences from alpha to its other conjugates, its valuation increases. The different measures the accumulated closeness of conjugates, even when the characteristic of the residue field makes their reductions collide.

This is not the ordinary real derivative detecting a tiny physical displacement. It is an algebraic identity that remains valid in p-adic fields. A large p-adic valuation of a derivative means the derivative is divisible by a high power of a prime, and hence small in p-adic absolute value. That is exactly the situation in which Hensel lifting of a root becomes delicate.

There is an important hypothesis: B must be the full integral ring, not just some convenient subring A of alpha. If alpha does not generate the full integral ring, the calculation measures the trace dual of that smaller order. Its polynomial discriminant can then include factors that disappear when we enlarge to the maximal order.

## Three explicit calculations

First take the Gaussian integers. Alpha is i, f is X squared plus one, and f prime at i is two i. The full ring of integers really is Z of i. Therefore the different is the ideal generated by two i. Its norm as an ideal is four. Notice that the generator two i is not a rational integer, but it encodes the same ramified rational prime that appears in the discriminant minus four.

Second take the square root of two. Its minimal polynomial is X squared minus two, whose derivative at the root is twice the square root of two. The full integer ring is Z of the square root of two. So its different is generated by twice the square root of two. The ideal norm is eight, matching the absolute field discriminant.

Third revisit the square root of five. If we choose alpha equal to the square root of five, differentiation gives twice the square root of five and a norm of twenty. That is the discriminant of the nonmaximal order Z of square root of five, not of the field. Choose instead omega equal to one plus the square root of five, divided by two. Its minimal polynomial is X squared minus X minus one, and its derivative at omega is two omega minus one, which is the square root of five. This time the full integer ring is Z of omega, and the different is generated by the square root of five. Its ideal norm is five.

The derivative has not changed its mind. The input ring changed. Different ideals must always be attached to a specified extension of integer rings.

## The discriminant is the norm of the different

We can now make the bridge between the ideal upstairs and the determinant downstairs. For a number-field extension L over K, the discriminant ideal in A is the ideal norm of the different in B. More precisely, when we are over the ordinary integers, the absolute value of the integer field discriminant equals the norm of the different ideal. Over a more general base ring, both sides are ideals of A.

Why is that true? Localize at a prime of A, so that A becomes a discrete valuation ring and B is free as an A-module. Choose an integral basis and its trace-dual basis. The trace Gram matrix gives the change of coordinates from the trace-dual basis to the original basis. Its determinant generates the discriminant ideal. But the index between these lattices is also the ideal norm of the inverse of the trace dual, namely the different. They are two calculations of the same lattice index. Because this equality holds at every prime, it holds for the original ideals.

That theorem tells us more than that two numbers happen to agree in our examples. It tells us exactly how a local power of the different contributes to the discriminant: the power gets multiplied by the residue degree when we take its norm down to the base field. We will use that fact repeatedly.

The different thus turns one integer into a finer local measurement. It comes from a trace pairing. It can be calculated by a derivative when the correct integral generator exists. And its norm recovers the discriminant. The next question is not what to call it, but what exponent it acquires when a prime ramifies, and why the prime two behaves differently from the primes three, five, and seven.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let `A` be Dedekind with fraction field `K`, let `L/K` be finite separable, and let `B` be its integral closure in `L`.

\[
B^*=\{x\in L:\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}(xB)\subseteq A\},
\qquad \mathfrak D_{B/A}=(B^*)^{-1}\subseteq B.
\]

When `B=A[alpha]`, with monic separable `f` of degree `n`,
\[
\boxed{\;B^*=f'(\alpha)^{-1}B,\quad \mathfrak D_{B/A}=(f'(\alpha))\;}.
\]

Lagrange's top-coefficient identity for `0 <= j <= n-1`:
\[
\operatorname{Tr}_{L/K}\!\left(\frac{\alpha^j}{f'(\alpha)}\right)=
\begin{cases}0&j<n-1,\\1&j=n-1.\end{cases}
\]

An explicit dual basis for `(1,alpha,...,alpha^(n-1))` is `g_j(alpha)/f'(alpha)` for `0<=j<n`, where
\[
f(X)=X^n+a_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0,\quad
 g_j(X)=X^{n-1-j}+a_{n-1}X^{n-2-j}+\cdots+a_{j+1}.
\]

With `d_{L/K}` the relative discriminant ideal,
\[
\boxed{\;d_{L/K}=N_{L/K}(\mathfrak D_{L/K})\;}.
\]

Examples: `K=Q(i)` has `D=(2i), |disc|=4`; `Q(sqrt2)` has `D=(2sqrt2), |disc|=8`; `Q(sqrt5)` has `D=(sqrt5), |disc|=5` using `omega=(1+sqrt5)/2`.
