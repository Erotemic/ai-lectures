# From Algebraic Integers to Dedekind Domains

The ring of ordinary integers has two reassuring properties. If you divide an integer by another integer, you can decide whether the result is still an integer. And if you examine an ideal, a collection of integers stable under addition and multiplication by arbitrary integers, it is generated by one element: its greatest common divisor. In quadratic integer rings the second property fails. We have already met ideals that have no single generator, even though the ring remains a perfectly respectable arithmetic system.

Before we can prove that ideals factor uniquely, we must establish what structure these rings actually possess. We have been calling a ring of integers a Dedekind domain, but the name is not a substitute for a proof. In this lecture we will start with an arbitrary finite extension of the rational numbers, construct the lattice of all its algebraic integers, and verify the three properties that make the theory work.

There is one useful change in perspective. Instead of picturing an algebraic integer as a particularly exotic number, we will think of it as an element in a finite-dimensional vector space over the rationals. The integrality condition forces those elements to lie in a discrete lattice. That lattice viewpoint simultaneously explains why ideals are finitely generated and why reduction modulo a nonzero prime ideal gives a finite field.

## The ambient number field

A number field K is a field containing the rational numbers and having finite dimension n as a vector space over them. The simplest examples have degree two: the rationals together with a square root of five, or with a square root of negative five. Degrees three, four, and larger are also possible.

An element alpha of K is an algebraic integer if it satisfies a monic polynomial with ordinary integer coefficients. Monic means that the coefficient of the highest power is exactly one. The ring O sub K consists of all such elements of K.

Why is it a ring? The sum and product of algebraic integers are again algebraic integers. Here is a useful way to understand the proof rather than memorize it. If alpha satisfies a monic equation of degree r, then all higher powers of alpha can be rewritten as integer combinations of the first r powers. The ring generated by alpha is therefore a finitely generated additive group over the integers. If beta is also integral, adjoining beta preserves this finite-generation property: powers of beta above its polynomial degree can be reduced, with coefficients in the earlier ring. Multiplication by alpha plus beta, or by alpha times beta, acts on a finite generating set of this ring. The determinant trick then supplies a monic integer equation for that multiplier. Thus sums and products remain integral.

This argument also gives transitivity: if an element is integral over a ring whose elements are integral over the integers, then that element is integral over the integers. We will need this when proving that our ring has no missing integral fractions.

## First put a rational basis inside the integers

Choose any vector-space basis of K over the rationals. Its elements need not be algebraic integers. But a rational multiple of each basis element can always be made integral. To see this, start with a monic polynomial for the basis element with rational coefficients. Multiply the element by a common denominator of those coefficients. The resulting element satisfies a monic polynomial with integer coefficients after clearing the powers of the denominator.

So we may choose n algebraic integers, say beta one through beta n, that still form a rational vector-space basis of K. The ordinary integer combinations of these beta elements lie inside O sub K. They form a free additive group of rank n, a lattice of candidate algebraic integers.

The significant question is whether there can be algebraic integers whose rational coordinates have denominators of unbounded size. If that happened, O sub K would fail to be a discrete lattice. We rule it out using a trace pairing.

## The trace gives a denominator bound

For any element x of K, multiplication by x is a linear map from K to itself. Write its matrix in any rational basis and take its trace, the sum of the diagonal entries. This defines the field trace of x to the rationals. It does not depend on the chosen basis.

The trace of an algebraic integer is an ordinary integer. One explanation is to look at its conjugates: their sum, counting each field embedding appropriately, is rational and is again an algebraic integer. A rational number that satisfies a monic integer polynomial must be an integer, by the rational root theorem. Thus the trace lands in the integers.

Form a square matrix whose entry in row i and column j is the trace of beta i times beta j. Every entry is an integer because the product of two algebraic integers remains integral.

This matrix is invertible over the rationals. The key reason is that the trace pairing has no nonzero vector that pairs to zero with everything. Given a nonzero x in K, choose its reciprocal as the other vector. The trace of x times its reciprocal is the trace of one, which equals the degree n, and is not zero. Therefore the pairing is nondegenerate and its matrix in any basis has nonzero determinant.

Let that nonzero integer determinant be delta. Now take an arbitrary algebraic integer alpha. Express it in our rational basis using coordinates c one through c n. For each j, the trace of alpha times beta j is an ordinary integer. But this trace is also a linear combination of the coordinates c i, with coefficients from our integral trace matrix. We have obtained a system of n linear equations whose right sides are integers.

Cramer's rule solves this system by dividing integer determinants by delta. Every c i therefore has denominator dividing delta. All the algebraic integers lie inside the lattice generated by the beta elements divided by delta. They cannot wander off into a cloud of arbitrarily fine fractions.

In symbols the containment is between two rank-n lattices: the integral span of the beta elements sits inside O sub K, which sits inside one over delta times that integral span. The middle additive group is therefore finitely generated and torsion free, and hence free over the integers of rank n. This proves that O sub K has an integral basis, even if our first chosen beta basis was not itself such a basis.

The determinant delta is a discriminant associated with the chosen basis. We used it here as a concrete bound on denominators, not merely as a ceremonial invariant.

## Why every ideal has finitely many generators

Take an ideal I in O sub K. As an additive group it is a subgroup of a free group of rank n. Subgroups of free abelian groups of finite rank are themselves finitely generated; this can be proved by induction using the ordinary Euclidean algorithm on successive coordinates.

Choose a finite additive generating set for I. Those same elements generate I as an ideal. Their integer combinations already give all of I, while their combinations with arbitrary elements of O sub K cannot leave I, since I is an ideal. The ideal they generate is therefore exactly I.

This says the ring is Noetherian. Equivalently, ideals cannot form an endlessly strict increasing chain. We have not used the forthcoming factorization theorem to justify Noetherianity. We derived it from the bounded-denominator lattice construction.

Noetherianity will later stop a repeated attempt to enlarge an ideal. It is the algebraic equivalent of having a finite amount of room for such enlargements.

## Why the ring contains every integral fraction

A domain is integrally closed if any element of its fraction field satisfying a monic polynomial with coefficients in the domain already belongs to the domain.

For O sub K this follows from the way we defined it. Its fraction field is K, because the ring contains an integer multiple of every basis element, and hence every element of K can be expressed as a fraction of its elements. Suppose x in K is integral over O sub K. The coefficients of the monic equation for x are themselves algebraic integers. By transitivity of integrality, x is integral over the ordinary integers. But O sub K consists of all such elements by definition. Thus x lies in O sub K.

This point is logically different from a ring being a unique-factorization domain. The ring of quadratic integers we used earlier is integrally closed, although its elements do not factor uniquely. Integral closure means that no algebraic integer fraction was inadvertently omitted from the ring. It is a condition about the ambient field, not about how products split into irreducible elements.

## Why every nonzero prime ideal is maximal

Recall that a prime ideal P is one whose quotient ring has no zero divisors. A maximal ideal is one whose quotient is a field. In general a domain need not be a field, so the words prime and maximal cannot be interchanged. Our ring of integers has an extra finiteness property that makes them coincide for nonzero ideals.

Take a nonzero prime ideal P and choose a nonzero algebraic integer alpha in it. Alpha has a monic minimal polynomial with integer coefficients. Because alpha is nonzero, that polynomial has a nonzero integer constant term, call it c. Evaluate the polynomial at alpha and rearrange. Every term except c contains a factor of alpha. Consequently c lies in the ideal generated by alpha, and hence in P.

Thus P contains a nonzero ordinary integer c. Our lattice of algebraic integers has finite rank, so reducing modulo c leaves only finitely many residue classes. The quotient O sub K modulo P is an even smaller quotient, hence finite.

But P was prime, so this finite quotient has no zero divisors. A finite integral domain is automatically a field: multiplication by any nonzero element is injective, and an injective map of a finite set is surjective, so some element is sent to one. Every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse.

We have proved that P is maximal. We can also see that all nonzero ideals have finite index by repeating the argument with any nonzero alpha contained in the ideal. This finite index will later become the norm of an ideal.

## The three properties together

A Dedekind domain, for our purposes, is a Noetherian integrally closed domain in which every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. We have verified each clause for O sub K: finite rank supplies Noetherianity; transitivity of integrality supplies integral closure; and finiteness of quotients supplies maximality of nonzero prime ideals.

Notice what we have not proved yet. Nothing in this lecture alone asserts that ideals factor uniquely. That will be the next theorem, and it is substantial. We now know exactly why the ring is eligible for that theorem, rather than quoting the word Dedekind and hoping it does the explanatory work.

We also have a clear distinction between rational and integral bases. A rational basis is a coordinate system for K; an integral basis is an additive coordinate system for all its algebraic integers. The trace pairing proves that such an integral basis exists. In quadratic fields we can write it down explicitly; in higher degrees finding one may take work.

Our original example, the integers a plus b times the square root of negative five, fits the general argument. It has rank two and discriminant negative twenty. The prime ideals that repaired the two factorizations of six live inside a discrete rank-two lattice, have finite quotients, and satisfy the three Dedekind properties. We are now ready to show that these properties force every nonzero ideal to have its own unique prime factorization.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Algebraic integrality and finite modules

If \(x\) obeys \(x^r+a_1x^{r-1}+\cdots+a_r=0\) over a commutative ring \(A\), then \(A[x]\) is generated as an \(A\)-module by \(1,x,\ldots,x^{r-1}\). Conversely, if multiplication by \(x\) preserves a finitely generated faithful \(A\)-submodule of a field, the adjugate/determinant trick yields a monic polynomial for \(x\) over \(A\). Iterating finite modules proves the transitivity of integrality and closure under sum/product.

An element \(b\in K\) satisfying a monic polynomial \(f(T)\in\mathbb Q[T]\) can be scaled by a positive integer \(D\) clearing all coefficient denominators so that \(Db\) satisfies a monic polynomial with integer coefficients: substitute \(T=Y/D\) and multiply by \(D^{\deg f}\).

### An integral lattice of rank \(n\)

Take an integral \(\mathbb Q\)-basis \(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n\) of the number field \(K\). Let
\[
B=(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta_i\beta_j))_{i,j},\qquad \Delta=\det B\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}.
\]
The trace pairing is nondegenerate because for every \(x\ne0\), \(\operatorname{Tr}(xx^{-1})=n\ne0\). Its matrix in a basis has nonzero determinant. If \(\alpha=\sum c_i\beta_i\in\mathcal O_K\), then
\[
B(c_i)=(\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha\beta_j))_{j}\in\mathbb Z^n,
\quad c_i\in\Delta^{-1}\mathbb Z.
\]
Consequently
\[
\sum_i\mathbb Z\beta_i\subseteq\mathcal O_K
\subseteq\Delta^{-1}\sum_i\mathbb Z\beta_i.
\]
A subgroup of the latter finitely generated free \(\mathbb Z\)-module is free of rank at most \(n\), and the left inclusion gives rank at least \(n\). Therefore \(\mathcal O_K\simeq\mathbb Z^n\) as an additive group.

For \(0\ne\alpha\in\mathcal O_K\), its monic minimal polynomial has nonzero integer constant \(c\), yielding \(c\in(\alpha)\). Thus \(\mathcal O_K/(\alpha)\) is finite, and so is \(\mathcal O_K/I\) for every nonzero ideal \(I\).

### The Dedekind axioms, proved

- **Noetherian:** any ideal is an additive subgroup of a finite-rank free abelian group, hence generated by finitely many elements; these generate it as an ideal as well.
- **Integrally closed:** integrality over \(\mathcal O_K\) implies integrality over \(\mathbb Z\) by transitivity, so an element of \(K\) integral over \(\mathcal O_K\) belongs to \(\mathcal O_K\).
- **Dimension one:** for a nonzero prime \(\mathfrak p\) choose \(0\ne\alpha\in\mathfrak p\), so \(\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak p\) is a finite domain, hence a field; \(\mathfrak p\) is maximal.

**No circularity:** the unique factorization of ideals is **not** used in these three proofs; it will be obtained in 027750.

### Example and exercises

1. For \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})\), choose \(\beta=(1,\sqrt{-5})\). Compute \(B=\operatorname{diag}(2,-10)\) and \(\Delta=-20\). The lattice bounds need not themselves be the optimal integral basis.
2. Show that if \(\alpha\in\mathcal O_K\) is nonzero, multiplication by \(\alpha\) has integer matrix in an integral basis and \([\mathcal O_K:\alpha\mathcal O_K]=|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)|\).
3. Prove a finite integral domain is a field by analyzing the multiplication map \(x\mapsto ax\) for \(a\ne0\).
4. Give a concrete subring of a number field that is not integrally closed. Hint: \(\mathbb Z[2i]\subset\mathbb Z[i]\); the missing element \(i\) satisfies \(T^2+1=0\).
5. Why does \(\operatorname{Tr}(1)=n\) make the trace pairing nondegenerate over characteristic zero, but not necessarily over a base field of characteristic dividing \(n\)?
