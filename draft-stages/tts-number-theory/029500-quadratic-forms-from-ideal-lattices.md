# Quadratic Forms Hidden in Ideal Lattices

Here are two equations that may look almost identical. One asks whether a prime is a square plus five times a square. The other asks whether the prime is twice a square plus twice the product of two integers plus three times a square. A change of coefficients appears small, but the two equations can recognize different primes. Why should a quadratic expression know so much about prime factorization in a number field?

We have already developed two pieces of mathematics that can answer this. First, the norm of an algebraic integer in an imaginary quadratic field is a positive definite quadratic expression in its two integer coordinates. Second, not every ideal of the ring of integers is generated by an element; the failure of principality is recorded by the ideal class group.

Today's connection is that every ideal class can be seen as a quadratic form. The coefficients of that form record the shape of the ideal as a lattice in the complex plane. And when we multiply ideal classes, we obtain a natural way to compose the corresponding forms.

We need to be precise about what we call the same form. Write a binary quadratic form as a times x squared plus b times x y plus c times y squared, where a, b, and c are integers. Its discriminant is b squared minus four a c. If a is positive and the discriminant is negative, the expression is positive for every nonzero integer pair. Such a form is positive definite.

For this sequence we concentrate on *primitive* forms: the greatest common divisor of a, b, and c is one. We also fix a *fundamental* negative discriminant D, meaning the discriminant of the full integer ring of an imaginary quadratic field. Nonfundamental discriminants belong to quadratic orders, and the ideal theory there needs additional care.

Changing the integer coordinates by an invertible two-by-two matrix with integer entries does not change which integers the form represents. But we make a finer distinction. A transformation with determinant plus one is called a *proper* equivalence. A transformation with determinant minus one is an improper equivalence. Both preserve values, but the second reverses an orientation. If we want forms to correspond exactly to ordinary ideal classes, rather than identifying a class with its inverse, we must keep the determinant-plus-one convention.

Let's see where orientation enters. An imaginary quadratic field sits in the complex plane. Its ring of integers is a lattice, formed by all integer combinations of one and an integral generator. An ideal is also a lattice, with two independent integer basis elements alpha and beta. We choose their order so that the oriented area, as viewed in the complex plane, is positive. Replacing that basis by another oriented integer basis uses a matrix of determinant plus one.

Now take an integral ideal I with basis alpha, beta. Its ideal norm is its index in the full ring of integers. Evaluate the field norm of alpha x plus beta y, then divide by the ideal norm. Because this element belongs to I, the principal ideal it generates is contained in I. After multiplying by the inverse ideal we obtain an integral ideal, so the quotient of norms is an integer. Therefore we have an integer-valued quadratic form.

It is positive definite because the field norm in an imaginary quadratic field is the squared complex absolute value. It has the same discriminant D as the entire field, rather than D multiplied by the square of the ideal norm. Why? The lattice basis of I has area equal to the full-ring lattice area times the index of I. The determinant appearing in the discriminant of a norm form acquires the square of that index. Dividing the norm form by the index removes precisely this square. What remains is the field discriminant.

The form is primitive. One way to understand the condition is to ask what happens if a common integer greater than one divides all three coefficients. A prime dividing that common divisor would have its square divide the discriminant. For an odd prime this is impossible for a fundamental discriminant. For the prime two, dividing all coefficients by two would leave a quadratic form with discriminant D divided by four; when D is divisible by four, D divided by four is two or three modulo four, which is not a possible integral-form discriminant. So primitivity follows from the fundamental nature of D.

We have assigned to the ideal I a primitive positive definite form of discriminant D. The construction does not depend on choosing a particular oriented basis, except up to proper equivalence. If we replace I by a principal scalar multiple lambda I, both the numerator norm and the ideal norm acquire the same factor, the absolute field norm of lambda. Consequently the form class does not change. So an ideal *class* has produced a proper equivalence *class* of forms.

We should also understand the reverse construction. Suppose someone hands us a primitive positive definite form with coefficients a, b, and c and discriminant D. Because b squared minus four a c equals D, b and D have compatible parity, and the algebraic number beta equal to b plus square root of D, divided by two, belongs to the full integer ring.

Take the ideal generated, as an additive lattice, by a and beta. It really is an ideal: multiplying beta by itself yields b beta minus a c, an integer combination of a and beta, and multiplying a by beta causes no problem. Since the integer ring is generated by beta together with the ordinary integers, the lattice is stable under the entire ring.

This ideal has norm a. Indeed, one and beta form an integral basis of the full ring; replacing one by a gives an index-a sublattice. Now take the norm of a x plus beta y and divide by a. The result is exactly a x squared plus b x y plus c y squared. We have recovered the original form, coefficient for coefficient.

This is the key construction. It tells us that quadratic forms of the chosen discriminant are not an unrelated list of equations. They are the norm functions of ideal lattices. It is also why the coefficient a can serve as the norm of a representative ideal, and why the discriminant must be fixed in order to compare the resulting classes.

To finish the correspondence, we should see why two oriented ideals giving the same proper form class differ by multiplication by a nonzero field element. Suppose their normalized norm forms agree after an oriented integer change of basis. Regard each pair of basis vectors as two vectors in the Euclidean plane. Equal normalized norm forms mean their Gram matrices are identical. The orientation condition rules out reflection, leaving a rotation followed by a positive scaling. In the complex plane that transformation is multiplication by one complex number. Since it sends a nonzero element of the first ideal to an element of the second and both lie in the same quadratic field, that multiplier belongs to the field. Thus the ideals are in the same ideal class.

We have now established a bijection between ideal classes of an imaginary quadratic field and proper equivalence classes of primitive positive definite forms with its fundamental discriminant. The inverse of an ideal class corresponds to reversing the middle coefficient b. Multiplication of ideal classes defines a group law on the corresponding forms, usually called Gauss composition. We have constructed the group conceptually from ideals, without needing to memorize a coefficient-only composition formula.

Let's make this tangible in the field generated by the square root of negative five. Its discriminant is negative twenty. The entire ring of integers is Z of square root of negative five. The unit ideal has the basis one and square root of negative five. Its norm form is x squared plus five y squared. So the principal ideal class corresponds to the coefficients one, zero, five.

For the nonprincipal class take the ideal P generated by two and one plus square root of negative five. It has norm two. A general element in the displayed basis is two x plus one plus square root of negative five, times y. Its field norm is the square of two x plus y, plus five y squared. Divide by two. We obtain two x squared plus two x y plus three y squared. So the class that obstructed element factorization is represented by a perfectly concrete quadratic form with coefficients two, two, three.

Test the two forms on the prime three. Three is represented by the second form: take x equal to zero and y equal to one. But it cannot equal x squared plus five y squared, because y equal to zero would require three to be a square, and any nonzero y already contributes at least five. Thus three is detected by the nonprincipal ideal class, not by the principal one.

This explains why a prime can split without being the norm of any element. Splitting produces a prime *ideal* of norm p. An element of norm p would produce a *principal* prime ideal of that norm. If the prime ideal lies in a nontrivial class, the first event happens but the second does not.

The distinction between principal and nonprincipal ideals is now visible in familiar school-algebra expressions. There is still an algorithmic question. How many different forms of a fixed discriminant are there? Could we list all of them without searching infinitely many possible coefficients? The answer will be a reduction procedure that turns every form into one with small coefficients, and this will let us compute class groups directly.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(D<0\) a fundamental discriminant and \(K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)\), let \(I\) be a nonzero integral ideal with **positively oriented** \(\mathbb Z\)-basis \((\alpha,\beta)\) (i.e. \(\operatorname{Im}(\beta/\alpha)>0\)). Define
\[
Q_I(x,y)=\frac{N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha x+\beta y)}{N(I)}.
\]
The coefficients are integers: \((\alpha x+\beta y)I^{-1}\) is integral and has norm \(N(\alpha x+\beta y)/N(I)\). The lattice covolume relation implies \(\operatorname{disc}(Q_I)=D\), and fundamental \(D\) forces \(Q_I\) primitive. Proper \(SL_2(\mathbb Z)\) basis changes preserve the oriented ideal class and form class.

Conversely, for primitive positive definite \([a,b,c]\) with \(b^2-4ac=D\), set \(\beta=(b+\sqrt D)/2\) (choose the square root with positive imaginary part) and
\[
I_{[a,b,c]}=a\mathbb Z+\beta\mathbb Z,\qquad \beta^2=b\beta-ac.
\]
This is an \(\mathcal O_K\)-ideal of norm \(a\), and
\[
Q_{I_{[a,b,c]}}(x,y)=\frac{|ax+\beta y|^2}{a}=ax^2+bxy+cy^2.
\]
The correspondence \(\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)\leftrightarrow\{\text{proper primitive positive definite form classes of discriminant }D\}\) follows. Conjugation/inversion sends \([a,b,c]\) to \([a,-b,c]\). For nonfundamental \(D\), one must use invertible proper ideals of the appropriate order instead.

For \(D=-20\), the unit class gives \([1,0,5]\), and \(I=(2,1+\sqrt{-5})\) gives
\[
Q_I(x,y)=\frac{N(2x+(1+\sqrt{-5})y)}2
=2x^2+2xy+3y^2.
\]
Note \(3=Q_I(0,1)\) but \(3\ne x^2+5y^2\) for integers \(x,y\).

### Exercises

1. Verify \(\beta^2=b\beta-ac\) in the reverse construction and explain why it makes \(a\mathbb Z+\beta\mathbb Z\) an ideal.
2. Show directly that the determinant of an oriented basis change is \(+1\), and explain what determinant \(-1\) would identify.
3. For \(D=-23\), construct the ideal and norm form associated with \([2,1,3]\). Compute the form from the field norm rather than guessing it.
4. In the quadratic field of discriminant \(-4\), apply the construction to \([1,0,1]\), and explain why the principal class is the only one.
5. What changes when \(D=-16\)? Why is this discriminant not fundamental, even though \(x^2+4y^2\) is a genuine integer form?
