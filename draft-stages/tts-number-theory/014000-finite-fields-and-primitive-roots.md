# Finite Fields, Primitive Roots, and Unit Groups

List the nonzero remainders modulo seven: one, two, three, four, five and six. All six can be obtained by multiplying three by itself repeatedly and reducing modulo seven. The powers are three, two, six, four, five, and finally one. Then the cycle repeats. So one number, three, generates every nonzero residue.

This may look like a coincidence. Modulo eight, no single odd number does the same job. There are four invertible remainders, one, three, five and seven, but squaring any of them gives one. No element has order four.

Why is a generator guaranteed for every *prime* modulus, while some composite moduli have none? The answer is a theorem about finite fields. It uses nothing more advanced than multiplication, divisibility of element orders, and a familiar fact about roots of polynomials. Once that theorem is established, we can revisit prime powers and explain exactly where generators survive.

## What a field supplies

A field is a number system in which we can add, subtract and multiply, and every nonzero element can be divided by. Rational numbers form a field. So do real numbers. More surprisingly, the remainders modulo a prime p form a field: every nonzero residue has a multiplicative inverse, by Bézout's identity.

The prime requirement is essential. Modulo six, two times three is zero even though neither factor is zero. Neither two nor three can have a multiplicative inverse. We call them zero divisors. A field cannot have zero divisors, because if a nonzero number a multiplied by b were zero, we could divide by a and conclude b is zero.

In any field, a nonzero polynomial of degree d has at most d distinct roots. You probably first met this principle for ordinary polynomials, but it is still true when coefficients lie in a finite field. Let a be a root of a polynomial f. Polynomial division by x minus a gives f equals x minus a times another polynomial of degree one less. In a field, if b is a different root, then b minus a is nonzero and invertible, so b must be a root of that second polynomial. Repeat. Each different root consumes at least one degree. This argument is an induction proof; it does not need graphs or continuity.

The fact fails over some composite moduli. Modulo eight, the polynomial x squared minus one has four distinct roots, one, three, five and seven, even though its degree is two. The step that would cancel b minus a is invalid because a nonzero residue modulo eight need not have an inverse. This root-count distinction is the exact hinge of the argument ahead.

## Orders and the size of a multiplicative group

The nonzero elements of a finite field form a group under multiplication. That means we can multiply two nonzero elements, there is an identity one, every element has an inverse, and multiplication is associative. Since ordinary field multiplication also commutes, this is an abelian group.

For any element g, its order is the smallest positive integer r for which g to the r equals one. That order exists in a finite group because the successive powers eventually repeat; multiplying by an inverse shows that any repetition yields a power equal to one. The r powers from one through g to the r minus one are distinct.

The order divides the number of elements in the group. We can see this without a general course in group theory. The powers of g form a subgroup with r elements. Multiplying that subgroup by any group element produces a coset with exactly r elements. Different cosets either coincide or are disjoint, and together they partition the group. Therefore r divides the total group size.

For the nonzero residues modulo p there are p minus one elements. Fermat's little theorem already told us that every element raised to p minus one gives one; the group argument explains why an individual element's order divides p minus one.

We need something stronger: at least one element has order *exactly* p minus one. That is not guaranteed in an arbitrary finite abelian group, as the modulo-eight example shows. But the polynomial root bound will force it in a field.

## Combine the largest prime-power orders

Let G be any finite multiplicative subgroup of a field, not necessarily all the field's nonzero elements. Consider the orders of all its elements. Form their least common multiple, call it m. By construction, every element g satisfies g to the m equals one.

A subtle fact about commuting elements lets us construct a single element with order m. Factor m into prime powers. For each prime power q to the e in m, some group element has an order containing at least that power of q; otherwise the least common multiple would have a smaller exponent of q. Take an appropriate power of that element to remove all the other factors from its order. What remains is an element of order exactly q to the e.

Do this for each distinct prime dividing m. Multiply the resulting elements together. They commute because they belong to the multiplicative group of a field. Their individual orders are powers of different primes and therefore are pairwise coprime. The order of their product is the product of those orders, namely m. The companion proves this coprime-orders fact directly.

So some element of G really has order m. It produces m distinct elements, giving m at most the size of G.

But remember how m was defined. Every element of G satisfies x to the m equals one. Thus every element of G is a distinct root of the polynomial x to the m minus one. This polynomial has degree m. The root bound in a field says G can contain at most m elements.

Combining the two inequalities, the size of G is exactly m. And our constructed element has order exactly that size. Its powers visit every element of G. We have proved:

**Every finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic.** In particular, the p minus one nonzero residues modulo any prime p are all powers of some single residue. That generator is called a primitive root modulo p.

Nothing about this proof requires guessing a generator by trial and error. It proves at least one must exist. Finding one efficiently is a separate computational problem.

## How to recognize a primitive root

Suppose we know the complete prime factorization of p minus one and want to test a candidate g modulo p. By Fermat's theorem, g to the p minus one equals one for any nonzero g. The candidate has full order p minus one if and only if, for every distinct prime q dividing p minus one, g raised to the exponent (p minus one) divided by q is *not* one modulo p.

Why are those tests sufficient? If the actual order were a proper divisor of p minus one, the quotient of p minus one by that order would be divisible by some prime q. The order would then divide (p minus one) divided by q, and that power would be one. Conversely, full order makes every smaller divisor fail. We have reduced a potentially long list of powers to one test per distinct prime factor.

For p equal to seven, p minus one is six, with prime divisors two and three. Try g equal to three. Three squared is two modulo seven, not one. Three cubed is six modulo seven, also not one. So three has order six. The two modular exponentiations certify that it is a primitive root, without having to list all six powers.

This is the same order reasoning that makes some primality certificates possible. Once the factorization of a group size is known, powers corresponding to its distinct prime factors tell us whether an element has the whole order.

## Lifting generators to odd prime powers

The modulus p squared is not a field: p is a nonzero residue modulo p squared whose product with p is zero. Nonetheless, every power of an odd prime has a primitive root among its *invertible* remainders. This is a more specific theorem, and the proof uses the binomial theorem.

Start with a primitive root g modulo an odd prime p. Among g and g plus p, at least one has the following useful property: when raised to p minus one, its remainder modulo p squared is **not** one. To see why, expand g plus p to the power p minus one modulo p squared. All terms except the first two contain p squared. The difference between the two candidates' powers is p times (p minus one) times g to the p minus two. Because p is odd and g is not divisible by p, this difference is not zero modulo p squared. The two candidates cannot both have remainder one.

Choose a candidate h with the desired property. It still has order p minus one modulo p, and h to the p minus one has the form one plus c p, where c is not divisible by p.

Now raise that expression to the power p. The binomial theorem shows that it is congruent to one plus c p squared modulo p cubed. Raise it to p again, and the first nonzero correction moves from p squared to p cubed. More generally, after each multiplication of the exponent by p, the first nonzero correction moves up exactly one power of p. The coefficient remains nonzero modulo p.

As a consequence, to make h to a power equal one modulo p to the k, we need its exponent to include the factor p minus one, because h is primitive modulo p, and also the full factor p to the k minus one, because no smaller power of p cancels the correction. Thus the order of h modulo p to the k is exactly (p minus one) times p to the k minus one. This is the number of invertible residues modulo p to the k. So h generates them all.

We have proved that **the group of units modulo every odd prime power is cyclic**. The argument complements Hensel lifting: the earlier theorem lifted simple polynomial roots one digit at a time, whereas this argument lifts the maximal multiplicative order of an element. The binomial expansion controls both, but the targets are different.

For a concrete check, three is primitive modulo seven. Three to the sixth power is seven hundred twenty-nine, which is forty-three modulo forty-nine, not one. Therefore three is also primitive modulo every higher power of seven. In particular its order modulo forty-nine is forty-two.

## Why powers of two are different

Modulo eight, every odd square is one. So the four units modulo eight cannot be generated by one element. This is not an isolated failure. For every k at least three, any odd number has square congruent to one modulo eight. Squaring repeatedly increases the power of two dividing the difference from one. By the time we raise the number to two to the k minus two, the result is one modulo two to the k. But there are two to the k minus one units modulo two to the k. No element can have order equal to the size of that group.

The exception explains why it would be wrong to say that all groups of modular units have primitive roots. The prime-field theorem proved cyclicity because a polynomial could not have too many roots. Modulo a power of two, the same polynomial can have extra roots, and the proof no longer applies.

The Chinese remainder theorem adds one more warning. For coprime moduli r and s, the units modulo their product correspond to pairs of units, one modulo r and one modulo s. Multiplication works coordinate by coordinate. Even if each component group is cyclic, their product need not be cyclic. Modulo fifteen, for example, the groups modulo three and five have orders two and four. Their product has eight elements but no element of order eight; the order of a pair is the least common multiple of its two component orders, at most four.

## A field with four elements

Not every finite field is obtained simply by reducing ordinary integers modulo a prime. There is also a field with four elements, even though integers modulo four do not form a field. Here is how it can happen.

Start with arithmetic modulo two, where one plus one equals zero. Introduce a symbol alpha satisfying alpha squared plus alpha plus one equals zero. The polynomial x squared plus x plus one has no root in the two-element field: at zero it gives one, and at one it gives one again. That makes the polynomial irreducible. In the resulting quotient construction, every element can be reduced to one of four forms: zero, one, alpha, or alpha plus one. Every nonzero element is invertible. Indeed, alpha squared equals alpha plus one, and alpha cubed equals one. Its powers run through the three nonzero elements.

We have exhibited a four-element field and a generator for its nonzero multiplicative group. This is a useful preview of a broader fact: finite fields have prime-power sizes and their nonzero multiplicative groups are cyclic. We proved the cyclicity theorem and built one extension field explicitly. The general existence and uniqueness theorem for a field of every prime-power size belongs to a fuller treatment of polynomial quotients and field extensions, and is not being smuggled in as part of this proof.

## Two roles played by the same algebra

A finite field supplies two kinds of control. Its division law prevents zero divisors, and that makes the polynomial root bound possible. Meanwhile its finite multiplicative group lets us ask about element orders. Combining those two facts forces a generator.

For prime moduli this gives primitive roots. For odd prime powers, a separate binomial argument keeps primitive roots alive even though the whole residue ring is no longer a field. For powers of two and many composite moduli, the expected generator does not exist.

These distinctions matter beyond number theory. They underlie discrete logarithms, coding theory, polynomial factorization, and the arithmetic of larger finite fields. The central result, however, is elementary enough to deserve its own proof: **a finite multiplicative subgroup inside a field has exactly enough polynomial roots to force a single generator**.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

### Root bound over a field

If \(f\in F[X]\) is nonzero of degree \(d\), then \(f\) has at most \(d\) roots in the field F. If \(f(a)=0\), then \(f=(X-a)g\), with \(\deg g=d-1\). Every other root b satisfies \((b-a)g(b)=0\); since \(b-a\ne0\) and F has no zero divisors, \(g(b)=0\). Induct on d.

**Failure over a nonfield:** modulo 8, the degree-two polynomial \(X^2-1\) has four roots: 1, 3, 5, 7.

### Finite multiplicative subgroups are cyclic: complete proof

Let \(G\subseteq F^\times\) be finite and write \(m=\operatorname{lcm}\{\operatorname{ord}(g):g\in G\}\). For each prime power \(q^e\Vert m\), choose an element of G whose order is divisible by \(q^e\), and raise it to a power to obtain \(h_q\) of order exactly \(q^e\). Because G is abelian and the orders of the \(h_q\) are coprime, their product \(h=\prod h_q\) has order \(\prod q^e=m\). (If commuting \(x,y\) have coprime orders r,s, then \((xy)^k=1\) implies \(x^k=y^{-k}\), an element simultaneously of orders dividing r and s, hence 1; so r and s both divide k.)

Thus \(|G|\ge m\). But every \(g\in G\) is a root of \(X^m-1\), so the field root bound gives \(|G|\le m\). Hence \(|G|=m=\operatorname{ord}(h)\): G is generated by h.

### Primitive-root test modulo a prime

For \(g\not\equiv0\pmod p\), \(\operatorname{ord}_p(g)=p-1\) iff for each distinct prime \(q\mid p-1\),
\[
g^{(p-1)/q}\not\equiv1\pmod p.
\]
For \(p=7\), \(g=3\), \(3^2\equiv2\), \(3^3\equiv6\pmod7\), certifying order six.

### Generator for every odd prime power

Let p be odd. Choose \(g\) primitive modulo p. Binomial expansion gives
\[
(g+p)^{p-1}-g^{p-1}\equiv(p-1)g^{p-2}p\not\equiv0\pmod{p^2}.
\]
At least one of \(g,g+p\) gives a candidate h with \(h^{p-1}=1+cp\), \(p\nmid c\). If \(u=1+c_r p^r\) with \(r\ge1\), \(p\nmid c_r\), then for odd p the binomial theorem gives
\[
u^p\equiv1+c_r p^{r+1}\pmod{p^{r+2}}.
\]
Thus \(h^{(p-1)p^j}\equiv1+c_jp^{j+1}\pmod{p^{j+2}}\), with \(p\nmid c_j\). Since the order modulo \(p^k\) divides \(\varphi(p^k)=(p-1)p^{k-1}\) and is divisible by \(p-1\), the first exponent that gives 1 modulo \(p^k\) is precisely \((p-1)p^{k-1}\). Therefore \((\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z)^\times\) is cyclic.

For p=2 and k≥3, every odd a satisfies \(a^2\equiv1\pmod8\); repeated squaring yields \(a^{2^{k-2}}\equiv1\pmod{2^k}\). Since \(\varphi(2^k)=2^{k-1}\), no element has full order.

### CRT and the four-element field

If \(\gcd(r,s)=1\), CRT gives
\[
(\mathbb Z/rs\mathbb Z)^\times\cong(\mathbb Z/r\mathbb Z)^\times\times(\mathbb Z/s\mathbb Z)^\times.
\]
The order of \((u,v)\) equals \(\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{ord}(u),\operatorname{ord}(v))\). For modulus 15 the orders divide 2 and 4 respectively, so no unit has order 8.

In \(\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^2+X+1)\), let \(\alpha=[X]\). Then \(\alpha^2=\alpha+1\) and \(\alpha^3=1\); the four elements are \(0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1\). The polynomial has no root in \(\mathbb F_2\), so the quotient is a field. This is \(\mathbb F_4\).
