# Wild Ramification in Cyclotomic Towers: Ninth and Sixteenth Roots

There is a difference between knowing a discriminant and understanding why it has its particular exponent. Earlier we computed the discriminant of a prime-power cyclotomic field by differentiating its defining polynomial. For a primitive ninth root of unity, the answer involved three to the ninth power. For a primitive sixteenth root, it involved two to the twenty-fourth power. Those are exact answers, but a derivative hides the different kinds of Galois symmetry inside a single product.

Higher ramification groups separate those symmetries. We will calculate every group for both fields, not by guessing from the discriminant, but by measuring how far each automorphism moves a uniformizer. Then Hilbert's different formula will reconstruct the discriminant exponent from the filtration alone. Finally, we will do the calculation for every prime power in one formula.

## One automorphism for each invertible residue

Fix a prime p and a positive integer n. Let L be the extension of the p-adic rationals generated by a primitive p-to-the-n-th root of unity, zeta. The polynomial of zeta shifted by one is Eisenstein at p. Consequently the extension is totally ramified, its degree is N equal to p to the n minus one times p minus one, and pi equal to zeta minus one is a uniformizer.

The Galois group has one automorphism sigma sub a for every invertible residue class a modulo p to the n. That automorphism sends zeta to zeta to the a. Its action on pi is zeta to the a minus one. Subtract pi itself and the displacement becomes zeta to the a minus zeta. Factoring out zeta, a unit, reduces its valuation to the valuation of zeta to the a minus one minus one.

This is almost a complete answer. Let k be the number of times p divides a minus one, for a that is not the identity modulo p to the n. The root zeta to the a minus one has exact order p to the n minus k. One minus this root is a uniformizer in the corresponding smaller cyclotomic field. The larger field has ramification index p to the k over that smaller field. Therefore the valuation, measured in the larger field, is p to the k.

Our entire calculation rests on this simple rule: for a nonidentity automorphism, its displacement depth is p raised to the p-adic valuation of a minus one. The more strongly a approaches one modulo powers of p, the more digits the automorphism fixes. We need no separate method for every level of the tower.

## The ninth-root field, automorphism by automorphism

Set p equal to three and n equal to two. The units modulo nine are one, two, four, five, seven, and eight. The group has six elements. All of them belong to inertia, because the field is totally ramified.

For a equal to two, five, or eight, a minus one is not divisible by three. The valuation depth is three to the zero, or one. These automorphisms move pi at the very first available order: they lie in G zero but not in G one.

For a equal to four or seven, a minus one is divisible by three but not by nine. Their depth is three. These two automorphisms fix every integer modulo pi, pi squared, and pi cubed. They stop belonging when we demand congruence modulo pi to the fourth.

The identity belongs to every group. Collect the information. G zero has six members. G one and G two each have the three members one, four, and seven. G three is trivial. Notice that the tame quotient has order two, while wild inertia has order three. The filtration sees the two factors of the degree six extension in different ways.

Now apply Hilbert's formula. At level zero we contribute six minus one, or five. At levels one and two we contribute three minus one twice, or four more. The different exponent is nine. The derivative method and the higher-group method have arrived at the same answer, but the latter has explained exactly where the four units of wild excess come from.

## Sixteenth roots: wildness begins immediately

Now let p equal two and n equal four. The extension generated by a primitive sixteenth root has degree eight. Its automorphisms correspond to the odd residues modulo sixteen. Here every nonidentity a has a minus one divisible by two. Thus every nonidentity automorphism has depth at least two. The first two groups G zero and G one are identical: both contain all eight automorphisms. There is no nontrivial tame quotient.

There are four odd residues congruent to one modulo four: one, five, nine, and thirteen. Their automorphisms have depth at least four, so G two and G three each have four elements. Among them, only one and nine are congruent to one modulo eight. G four, G five, G six, and G seven each have two elements. At G eight only the identity remains.

The pattern is easy to read if we calculate the individual depths. The maps a equal to three, seven, eleven, and fifteen have depth two. The maps a equal to five and thirteen have depth four. The map a equal to nine has depth eight. The identity has infinite depth. The group counts are therefore eight, eight, four, four, two, two, two, two, and then one.

Hilbert's sum is seven plus seven, then three plus three, then four copies of one. The total is twenty-four. That recovers the exponent of two in the sixteenth cyclotomic discriminant without differentiating the polynomial. The discriminant is a signed integer; in this example its sign is positive because the field has four pairs of complex embeddings, so it is positive two to the twenty-fourth power.

There is a worthwhile comparison. In the ninth-root field, some automorphisms have depth one and others depth three. In the sixteenth-root field, every nonidentity automorphism has depth at least two. The prime two forces the entire inertia group to be wild, and multiple later groups persist for increasingly long runs of indices. Merely knowing the degree, six versus eight, would not reveal those depths.

## Calculate the pattern for all prime powers

Return to arbitrary p and n. The Galois group has N members, where N is p to the n minus one times p minus one. For each k between one and n minus one, the automorphisms congruent to one modulo p to the k form a subgroup with p to the n minus k elements.

Which lower indices see that subgroup? The displacement rule says that the k-th block begins at p raised to the quantity k minus one, and ends one less than p raised to k. Throughout that block, the surviving automorphisms are precisely those congruent to one modulo p to the k. The arithmetic is easiest to picture in blocks. The first nontrivial higher block has p minus one indices, the next has p squared minus p, the next p cubed minus p squared, and so on. In the k-th block the group has p to the n minus k members.

Therefore Hilbert's formula begins with N minus one, and then adds, for every k from one to n minus one, the block length p to the k minus p to the k minus one, multiplied by the group-size contribution p to the n minus k minus one.

Multiply out one generic block. Its first term, the block length times p to the n minus k, is p to the n minus one times p minus one: exactly N, independent of k. Summing those first terms contributes n minus one copies of N. The negative terms telescope to p to the n minus one minus one. Including the initial N minus one leaves n times N minus p to the n minus one.

We have recovered the familiar prime-power cyclotomic different exponent. Because the extension is totally ramified with residue degree one, it is also the exponent of p in the corresponding global cyclotomic discriminant. The derivative formula gave this expression in a different way; now we see it as a sum over ramification layers.

## Check boundary cases before trusting a formula

If n equals one and p is odd, the first cyclotomic extension has ramification degree p minus one, which is prime to p. There are no higher groups after G zero. Our formula gives p minus two, exactly the tame exponent. If p equals two and n equals one, the root is negative one and the field is just the base field. The formula gives zero.

For the field of fourth roots of unity over the 2-adics, the degree is two. Both G zero and G one have size two, and G two is trivial. The different exponent is one plus one, equal to two. We already encountered the same number as the discriminant exponent of the Gaussian integers at two.

These small cases are useful tests because the cyclotomic formula spans trivial, tame, and wild extensions. A block formula that silently assumes p odd, or assumes n at least two, can give the wrong interpretation of its first layer. Our derivation explicitly includes the boundaries.

## The next problem: comparing fields in a tower

We have now computed lower ramification groups for an entire tower of cyclotomic extensions. There is a subtle inconvenience. When we pass from a large cyclotomic field to a smaller quotient field, the numbering of the groups does not simply pass to the quotient with the same indices. The lower numbers depend on which field supplies the normalized valuation. For example, we encountered jumps at one, three, and seven in the sixteenth-root field. Those particular indices have been stretched by the ramification in the full field.

A second numbering, called upper numbering, corrects that stretching. In the cyclotomic tower it will replace the rapidly growing jumps at powers of p minus one by the consecutive integers one, two, three, and so forth. That new scale is one reason ramification groups are important far beyond calculating a discriminant: they begin to behave naturally when we compare Galois fields and their quotients.

The result of our present calculation is already complete. We know the precise displacement of every cyclotomic automorphism. We know every lower ramification group. And we have turned that information into the exact different exponent and the exact discriminant power. This is the concrete laboratory in which the remaining conductor theory will make sense.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let `L=Q_p(zeta_{p^n})`, `N=phi(p^n)=p^(n-1)(p-1)`, `pi=zeta-1`, `sigma_a(zeta)=zeta^a`.

For `a != 1 mod p^n`, put `k=v_p(a-1)`, with `0<=k<n`. As `zeta^{a-1}` is primitive of order `p^(n-k)`:
\[
\boxed{i_G(\sigma_a)=v_L(\sigma_a\pi-\pi)=p^k.}
\]

For `1<=k<n`, the indices `p^(k-1) <= i <= p^k-1` have
\[
G_i=\{\sigma_a:a\equiv1\pmod{p^k}\},\quad |G_i|=p^{n-k}.
\]
For `p>2`, `G_0` has size `N`, with congruences at later indices as displayed; for `p=2`, `G_1=G_0` when `n>=2`. The trivial terminal group begins at `i=p^(n-1)`.

| Field | `|G_0|,|G_1|,...` until trivial | `d` |
|---|---|---:|
| `Q_3(zeta_9)` | 6, 3, 3, 1 | 9 |
| `Q_2(zeta_8)` | 4, 4, 2, 2, 1 | 8 |
| `Q_2(zeta_16)` | 8, 8, 4, 4, 2, 2, 2, 2, 1 | 24 |

In general
\[
\begin{aligned}
d&=(N-1)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(p^k-p^{k-1})(p^{n-k}-1)\\
 &=\boxed{nN-p^{n-1}=p^{n-1}(np-n-1)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
