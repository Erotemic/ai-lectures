# Hilbert's Different Formula: Counting the Depth of Wild Ramification

We have two ways to measure ramification. One comes from the trace pairing and produces an ideal called the different. Its exponent tells us how far the ring of integers is from being self-dual. The other comes from automorphisms: an automorphism can fix all integers modulo one power of a uniformizer, or two powers, or many more. Those successive layers form the higher ramification groups.

Hilbert's different formula says that these are not two unrelated measurements. They are the same integer, computed in two languages. We can find the exponent of the different by adding up the sizes of the nontrivial ramification groups after removing the identity from each. The formula makes the extra cost of wild ramification visible one layer at a time.

## Recall what the different measures

Take a finite extension L over K of complete discretely valued fields with finite residue fields. Its integral trace pairing sends a pair of algebraic integers x and y to the trace from L to K of x times y. The codifferent is the set of elements y of L for which that trace lies in the integers of K for every integral x. The different is the inverse fractional ideal. Because the ring of integers of L is a discrete valuation ring, every nonzero ideal is a power of its maximal ideal. We therefore write the different as that maximal ideal to the power d, where d is a nonnegative integer.

In the preceding study of polynomial derivatives we proved a powerful formula. If the full integer ring is generated by one integral element alpha over the base integer ring, and F is the monic minimal polynomial of alpha, then the different is generated by F prime of alpha. Its exponent is the valuation of that derivative.

There is a reason to be careful. A primitive element for the fields need not generate the entire ring of integers. We may use the derivative only after proving the integral monogenic hypothesis. Fortunately, in our local Galois setting, the needed version is available after taking out the unramified part.

## Remove the unramified extension first

Let L over K be a finite Galois extension with group G. Let K nought be its maximal unramified intermediate field. The subgroup fixing K nought is the inertia group G zero. The residue extension from K to K nought is separable, so the unramified extension has trivial different: its trace pairing is perfect over the integer rings, or equivalently a lift of a primitive residue element has minimal polynomial with unit derivative.

The different obeys a tower law. Applied to K contained in K nought contained in L, it says that the different of L over K is the product of the different of L over K nought with the extended different of K nought over K. The second factor is a unit. Consequently both different exponents are equal.

Now L over K nought is totally ramified. Choose its uniformizer pi. It generates L over K nought, and it generates the entire integral ring as an algebra. Its minimal polynomial F is Eisenstein. This is exactly the case in which we may use the derivative formula without guessing about an integral basis.

Since the extension is Galois with group G zero, the minimal polynomial of pi factors as the product, over sigma in G zero, of X minus sigma of pi. Differentiate that product and evaluate it at pi. Every term vanishes except the term in which we differentiated the factor X minus pi. We get a product of pi minus sigma of pi over all nonidentity automorphisms in inertia.

Taking normalized valuations turns the product into a sum. The different exponent d is the sum, over nonidentity sigma in G zero, of the valuation of pi minus sigma of pi. That valuation is the intrinsic displacement i of sigma from the preceding lecture. Automorphisms outside inertia have displacement zero. So we may equivalently sum over every nonidentity element of G.

That is the first form of Hilbert's formula: the exponent of the different is the sum of the displacement depths of all nonidentity automorphisms.

## Convert automorphism depths into group sizes

An automorphism whose displacement depth is three belongs to G zero, G one and G two, but not G three. More generally, if its depth is the positive integer m, it belongs to exactly m groups in the list G zero, G one, and so on: the groups with indices zero through m minus one. We can therefore count the same finite set of pairs in two directions.

First, fix an automorphism. It contributes once for every group that contains it, giving its depth. Or fix a group G sub i. Every nonidentity member contributes one, giving the group size minus one. Since only finitely many groups are nontrivial, interchanging the two counts is legitimate. We obtain Hilbert's formula: d is the sum, from i equals zero onward, of the size of G sub i minus one.

The proof has not invoked any approximate reasoning. It used the derivative formula, the maximal unramified field, and an elementary double counting. It works for a finite Galois extension of our local fields, even when part of that extension is unramified.

## Read the tame contribution off the first term

The order of inertia G zero is the ramification index e. Thus the first term is e minus one. Every further term is nonnegative. We immediately recover the familiar inequality that d is at least e minus one.

If the extension is tame, G one is trivial. Then every later group is trivial as well. The entire sum is just e minus one. This recovers the tame different formula, which we previously derived using the derivative of an Eisenstein polynomial. We now understand why it has that form: each nonidentity inertia automorphism changes the very first available pi-adic digit, and none fixes one extra digit.

If the extension is wild, G one is nontrivial. We proved that it is a p-group. Its order is therefore at least p. The very next term adds at least p minus one. So a wildly ramified Galois extension has different exponent at least e plus p minus two. That is strictly larger than the tame baseline. Further groups may increase it again.

The exact excess over e minus one is the sum over all i at least one of the sizes of G sub i minus one. This is a way to quantify wildness, not merely to label it. A wildly ramified extension can have the same ramification index as another yet a larger different exponent because its automorphisms keep fixing more digits.

## A tame calculation and a wild calculation

Start with a primitive fifth root of unity over the 5-adic rationals. The extension has degree and ramification index four. It is Galois and tame, since five does not divide four. Every nonidentity automorphism has displacement depth one. The group G zero has four members, G one has only the identity, and Hilbert's sum is four minus one, equal to three. The derivative calculation for the fifth cyclotomic polynomial gave the same different exponent three.

Now use a primitive ninth root of unity over the 3-adic rationals. Its Galois group has six members. Three automorphisms lie in the group G one: the identity, the map sending zeta to zeta to the fourth, and the map sending it to zeta to the seventh. All three also lie in G two. G three is trivial. Hilbert's sum is therefore five, plus two, plus two. The result is nine. The tame baseline would have been five, but wild ramification contributes an extra four.

This also agrees with the cyclotomic discriminant calculation. The ninth-root field has global discriminant of absolute value three to the ninth. There is a single prime above three, with residue degree one. The exponent of three in that discriminant is thus the same local different exponent, nine.

For a contrast with unramified behavior, adjoining a square root of two to the 3-adic rationals gives an unramified quadratic extension. The nontrivial automorphism moves a residue-field element, so it has displacement zero. Inertia is trivial, every term in Hilbert's sum vanishes, and the different exponent is zero.

## From a local different to a global discriminant

The local formula does not simply tell us that the discriminant is the product of group sizes. There are two distinct operations. First sum the group-size contributions to find d at one place of L. Then take the ideal norm down to K. For a local Galois extension with residue degree f, the valuation in K of the relative discriminant is f times d. If the extension of number fields has several primes over the chosen rational prime, we sum the corresponding residue-degree-weighted local exponents.

Keeping that residue-degree weight matters. In the cyclotomic examples the extension was totally ramified with residue degree one, so it was invisible. In a general extension the group G minus one may be larger than inertia, and that extra unramified part affects the norm back down even though it makes no contribution directly to the different exponent.

## What this formula does not yet tell us

Hilbert's formula gives an exact computation once we know the lower ramification groups. It does not by itself tell us where the groups jump. For an arbitrary wild extension, calculating those jumps is still hard. And lower numbering is not the best scale when we pass from a Galois extension to a Galois quotient. The next layer of theory rescales this filtration so that quotient fields behave naturally.

But we have already made a major conceptual connection. The different is defined by traces, an algebraic pairing of numbers. The same integer emerges from the fixed-digit behavior of automorphisms. Wild ramification is the accumulated cost of automorphisms continuing to act trivially modulo higher powers. Trace duality and Galois symmetry are measuring the same defect.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For a finite Galois local extension `L/K` of residue characteristic `p`, with `G=Gal(L/K)`, inertia `G_0`, and maximal unramified subextension `K_0`:
\[
\mathfrak D_{L/K}=\mathfrak D_{L/K_0},\qquad
\mathcal O_L=\mathcal O_{K_0}[\pi],\qquad
\mathfrak D_{L/K_0}=(F'(\pi)).
\]
Because `F(X)=prod_{sigma in G_0}(X-sigma(pi))`:
\[
\boxed{d_{L/K}=v_L(F'(\pi))=\sum_{\sigma\ne1}i_G(\sigma)=\sum_{i\ge0}(|G_i|-1)}.
\]
The last identity counts pairs `(sigma,i)` with `sigma!=1` and `sigma in G_i`.

Consequences:
\[
d=e-1+\sum_{i\ge1}(|G_i|-1),\quad
\text{tame}\iff G_1=1\iff d=e-1.
\]
For wild Galois extensions `|G_1|>=p` and `d>=e+p-2`. Equality in the sharper bound `d>=e+|G_1|-2` holds iff `G_2=1`.

| Local example | `|G_0|` | nontrivial later groups | `d` |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| `Q_5(zeta_5)/Q_5` | 4 | none | 3 |
| `Q_3(zeta_9)/Q_3` | 6 | `|G_1|=|G_2|=3` | 9 |
| `Q_3(sqrt(2))/Q_3` | 1 | none | 0 |

**Global/local distinction.** For a local Galois extension the discriminant ideal exponent in `K` is `f(L/K)*d(L/K)`; for a global extension sum over primes above a fixed prime before comparing exponents.
