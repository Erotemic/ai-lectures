# Quadratic Discriminants and the Prime Two

There is a remarkably short answer to the question, Which rational primes ramify in a quadratic number field? They are exactly the primes dividing its field discriminant. Yet writing down that discriminant requires knowing something subtle about its integers. In particular, a factor four appears or disappears depending on a congruence modulo four. When the factor four appears, the prime two may ramify wildly, and the exponent of two in the discriminant can be either two or three.

We have developed enough machinery to explain all of this without guessing from a polynomial reduction. This lecture will calculate the different and discriminant for a general quadratic field, then place several examples side by side.

## Start with the right integers

Let d be a squarefree integer other than one, and consider K equal to the rationals with the square root of d. We proved earlier that its ring of integers has one of two possible bases. If d is congruent to one modulo four, use one and omega, where omega is one plus the square root of d, divided by two. Otherwise use one and the square root of d.

This is not a mere aesthetic choice. When d is one modulo four, omega is integral, satisfies a monic quadratic with integer coefficients, and enlarges the ring Z of square root of d by index two. A discriminant calculated from the smaller ring would carry an extra factor four, which could falsely implicate the prime two.

Suppose first that d is one modulo four. The minimal polynomial of omega is X squared minus X plus one minus d over four. Its derivative is two X minus one. At omega, that is exactly the square root of d. By the monogenic different formula, the different ideal is generated by the square root of d. Its ideal norm is the absolute value of d, and the signed field discriminant is d.

Why does the sign agree with d? If d is positive there are two real embeddings, so the discriminant is positive. If d is negative there is one pair of complex embeddings, so it is negative. The absolute norm loses the sign, but the embedding signature restores it.

Now suppose d is not congruent to one modulo four. The full integer ring is Z of the square root of d. Its minimal polynomial is X squared minus d. Differentiating at the root gives twice the square root of d. Thus the different ideal is generated by twice the square root of d. Its ideal norm is four times the absolute value of d, and the signed field discriminant is four d.

We have therefore recovered the general quadratic discriminant formula from the different, not merely from a determinant. If d is one modulo four, the field discriminant is d. Otherwise it is four d. The different is generated by square root of d in the first case, and by two square root of d in the second.

## Where the odd primes go

At an odd rational prime p, a quadratic extension can be split, inert, or ramified. We already derived the splitting criterion using quadratic residues and the squarefree part of the defining polynomial. The new perspective says that ramification occurs exactly when p divides the field discriminant.

Because d is squarefree, an odd prime dividing d occurs to the first power in the discriminant, whether the discriminant is d or four d. The corresponding quadratic extension of the p-adics has ramification index two, residue degree one, and different exponent one. It is tame: p is odd, so p cannot divide the ramification index two.

The odd primes are therefore uniform in this setting. Their ramification tells us which odd primes divide d, and the discriminant exponent is always exactly one. The interesting complications are concentrated at two.

## Why two is different

If d is congruent to one modulo four, the field discriminant is odd. So two does not ramify. It may split or remain inert, and that finer distinction depends on d modulo eight. If d is one modulo eight, two splits. If d is five modulo eight, two is inert. The discriminant tells us there is no ramification but does not by itself choose between those two possibilities.

If d is congruent to three modulo four, then d is odd and the field discriminant is four d. The exponent of two in the absolute discriminant is exactly two. Since d is odd, four d has two factors of two and no more. The prime two ramifies with index two, and the local different exponent is two. Because the residue characteristic divides that index, the ramification is wild.

If d is even and squarefree, it has exactly one factor of two. The field discriminant is four d, which now has three factors of two. Again two ramifies with index two, but the local different exponent is three, not two. It is a more heavily ramified quadratic extension according to the different.

Thus the possible exponents of two in a quadratic field discriminant are zero, two, and three. There is no exponent one. This is a useful diagnostic when someone hands you an alleged fundamental quadratic discriminant. If an integer with exactly one factor of two is proposed as the discriminant of a quadratic field, something has gone wrong.

## Three fields, three answers

Take the field with square root of five. Five is congruent to one modulo four, so the field discriminant is five. The only ramified prime is five. At two, the field is unramified; in fact the defining polynomial for omega reduces modulo two to X squared plus X plus one, which is irreducible, so two is inert. This is the example that exposed the index trap in our earlier lecture.

Take the field with i, which is the square root of minus one. Here d is minus one, congruent to three modulo four, so the discriminant is minus four. Only the prime two ramifies. Its discriminant exponent is two. The local different is generated by two i, and the ideal above two has residue degree one, so that local exponent transfers unchanged to the ordinary discriminant.

Take the field with square root of two. Here d is even, so the discriminant is eight. Again only two ramifies, but now its exponent is three. The local different is generated by two times square root of two. Both the field with i and the field with square root of two are ramified quadratic extensions of the two-adics. Their differences are genuinely arithmetic, not merely matters of notation.

There is a fourth example worth keeping close: the field with square root of minus five. Its field discriminant is minus twenty, or minus one times four times five. Two ramifies wildly with exponent two, and five ramifies tamely with exponent one. The norm of its different, generated by two square root of minus five, is twenty. The factorization into primes reveals two different kinds of local behavior inside a single global field.

## Computing the local picture from an integral basis

We should test that the polynomial derivative agrees with our local claims rather than relying only on the global formula. For the field with square root of two, use the polynomial X squared minus two. At the unique prime over two, the square root of two is a uniformizer. The two-adic extension has index two, so two has normalized valuation two. The derivative two square root of two has valuation three. There is our three.

For the field with i, use X squared plus one. The derivative is two i. The element i is a unit, and two has normalized valuation two in the ramified extension. The derivative has valuation two. There is our two.

For the field with square root of five, use omega. Its derivative is square root of five, which is a unit at the prime two. There is no contribution to the different from two. The same expression is not a unit at the prime five, where it has valuation one, so five contributes exponent one.

That is the full mechanism at work: the chosen integral generator identifies the correct derivative, the local valuation reads its different exponent, and the norm turns that local exponent into a power of the rational prime in the discriminant.

## Fundamental discriminants and what they encode

The discriminant of a quadratic field is often called a fundamental discriminant. The classification we just derived can be reversed. A nonzero integer D is a fundamental discriminant when it is either congruent to one modulo four and squarefree, or it is four times a squarefree integer congruent to two or three modulo four. The quadratic field is then the rationals with the square root of D, interpreted up to square factors.

For example, minus twenty is fundamental, since it is four times minus five and minus five is congruent to three modulo four. Minus sixteen is not a quadratic field discriminant, because its putative squarefree part minus four is not squarefree. Eight is fundamental; twelve is fundamental; twenty is not, because five is one modulo four and the true discriminant of the field with square root of five is only five.

A field discriminant knows the ramified primes and their exponents. It also knows the sign determined by the real and complex embeddings. In quadratic fields that much information is strong enough to identify the field itself. In higher degrees distinct fields can share the same discriminant, so this identification is special to the quadratic case.

There is one more boundary worth marking. We have worked with full rings of integers. For nonmaximal quadratic orders, their discriminants acquire square factors from their conductors. Those order discriminants have an important theory of their own, connected to ring class groups and binary quadratic forms, but they must not be confused with fundamental field discriminants. We will return to conductors when we study orders systematically.

The main lesson is that ramification is controlled by the integer ring, not by an arbitrary equation for the field. Once the correct ring is in hand, the different, derivative, and discriminant make every quadratic calculation explicit. The prime two remains special not because we refuse to simplify its formulas, but because degree two is divisible by its residue characteristic. That is exactly the wild case, and the exponent remembers it.
