# The World We Construct: Perception, Memory, and the Evidence of Our Senses

Consider a familiar experience. You are walking home near dusk and see a person standing beneath a tree. For an instant you recognize a neighbor. You have already begun to lift your hand when the figure resolves into a signpost with a jacket hanging from it. The light reaching your eyes did not abruptly change. What changed was your interpretation of it.

The interesting question is not whether your eyes played a trick. They supplied a pattern of light that could have several explanations. Your brain had to settle on one. It used the shape, the setting, and perhaps the expectation that your neighbor sometimes walks that route. When a closer look supplied better evidence, the interpretation changed. Most of the time the process works so quickly that we experience its conclusion as the thing itself.

Now imagine trying to describe the incident a week later. Did the object look like your neighbor, or did you only think of your neighbor after you recognized the mistake? What color was the jacket? Was anyone actually passing nearby? A vivid memory of the occasion may contain parts of the original perception, later interpretations, and details supplied by the telling of the story. A memory can be useful without being an untouched recording.

Our subject is a difficult relationship: between the world, the information we receive from it, and the experience we construct. We will follow several researchers who approached that relationship from different directions. A nineteenth-century physician learned to look into a living eye. A Cambridge psychologist asked students to retell an unfamiliar story. A psychologist studying witnesses changed one word in a question. And researchers watching people count passes in a basketball video discovered that attention can exclude an event occurring in plain sight. The evidence points to a mind that performs astonishing feats of interpretation. It also tells us when those feats fail.

## An eye that could be examined

Hermann von Helmholtz was born in Potsdam in eighteen twenty-one. His father taught classical literature and philosophy. Young Hermann was interested in physics, but the family's finances made that route difficult. A state-supported medical education came with an obligation to serve as an army doctor. He entered the Friedrich Wilhelm medical institute in Berlin, attended university lectures when he could, and studied mathematics independently. After qualifying in eighteen forty-three he worked as a military physician in Potsdam, devoting spare hours to research.

At the time, the eye was both an organ familiar to physicians and a major scientific mystery. It was clear that light was focused onto the retina, the sensitive layer at the back. But observing the retina in a living person was a technical challenge: illumination and the position of the observer had to be arranged so that light returning from the eye could be seen. Around eighteen fifty, Helmholtz devised an instrument using simple optical materials to accomplish this. The ophthalmoscope became a practical way for doctors to inspect the back of a living eye, transforming clinical examination.

The instrument's significance extended beyond medicine. Here was someone who could look into an eye and still ask why looking out of that eye was so complicated. The retina receives patterns of light. The world contains objects, surfaces, distances, shadows, movement, and causes. There is a substantial inferential journey between the two.

Helmholtz's career also shows how scientific understanding develops through correction. In his early investigations of color he reached a conclusion later criticized by Hermann Grassmann and James Clerk Maxwell. Subsequent experiments led him to revise it. A researcher who helped revolutionize optics could be mistaken about optics. That combination of achievement and revision is worth carrying into the theory of perception itself: even a highly successful interpretation may need replacing when better evidence arrives.

## Why a patch of light is not an object

Begin with a very small visual problem. A gray patch appears on a wall. It could be gray paint. It could be white paint in shadow. It could be a hole through which we see a darker surface beyond. A camera records light intensity at a sensor, but the same intensity can arise from different objects under different illumination. Without some account of the light source and surroundings, the physical cause of the patch is ambiguous.

For many ordinary surfaces, the light reaching the eye depends on at least two things: how much light falls on the surface and what fraction the surface reflects. A dark surface in strong sunlight may send more light toward us than a pale surface in a dim room. Yet we generally recognize the same white piece of paper across changes in illumination. The visual system uses the relationships among regions, shadows, edges, and expected lighting to estimate stable properties of things. This ability is called lightness constancy, though it has limits.

A crucial distinction follows. The physical quantity reaching a receptor is a measurement. The perceived brightness or identity of an object is an interpretation of that measurement in context. We need both. Without measurements, the mind would have little constraint from the environment. Without interpretation, the measurements would be an unruly stream of signals with no clear account of what is out there.

Try another example. A word written in unclear handwriting contains an ambiguous letter. In one sentence the mark is read as an A; in another it is read as an H. The ink stays the same, but neighboring letters alter what we expect. Context can help us identify the mark correctly. It can also lead us to overlook a spelling error because the intended word is so familiar. The same resource, expectation, can improve or distort interpretation.

Helmholtz called attention to something like unconscious inference: the perceiver reaches conclusions from sensory clues without deliberating through an explicit argument. We should not imagine that the mind has a tiny person inside solving equations. This is a way of describing the computational problem: the nervous system must use incomplete evidence to select among possible causes. Modern theories differ about exactly how those computations are implemented, but the ambiguity of the evidence is a physical fact.

## A small calculation about an ambiguous signal

We can express the problem with a simple set of imagined numbers. Suppose a maintenance worker sees a dark spot on a pavement. Some such spots indicate an actual hole; others are harmless discoloration. In this invented setting, only one spot in ten is a hole. The worker's quick visual impression detects nine out of ten holes, but it also falsely flags three out of ten harmless spots.

Picture one hundred dark-looking locations. Ten contain holes. Of those ten, nine trigger the warning impression. Ninety are harmless, and twenty-seven of those also trigger it. That leaves thirty-six warning impressions, only nine of which correspond to holes. So after the warning impression, the chance of a hole in this constructed population is one in four.

The calculation can feel surprising. An impression that catches ninety percent of real holes can still be wrong three-quarters of the time when it says 'hole.' Why? Because harmless spots are far more common. To understand an observation we need both the reliability of the signal and how common the competing explanations were beforehand.

Now change the setting. A worker inspecting a badly damaged street might know that six in ten suspicious spots are real holes. With the same impression, the chance that a warning means a real hole rises dramatically. The sensory process did not improve. The prior situation changed.

That illustrates a form of Bayesian reasoning: observations update what was plausible before they arrived. It does not mean that every visual judgment literally computes exact probabilities. It gives us a clear mathematical model of why context can influence perception without making it arbitrary. A sensible observer combines the likelihood of a signal under different explanations with how plausible those explanations are in the present setting.

The lesson also has a moral limitation. If a prior belief is based on an unfair stereotype, using it can systematically distort judgments about people. The fact that a system can use expectations does not tell us that every expectation is justified. A psychological explanation of a judgment is different from an ethical defense of it.

## The person walking through the basketball game

A century after Helmholtz, psychologists Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris investigated a different limitation. Participants watched a brief video showing people passing basketballs and were assigned a task: count the passes made by one team. In one version, an unexpected person in a gorilla costume walked into the scene. Some viewers, attending carefully to the passes, failed to report noticing the unexpected figure.

The study was published in nineteen ninety-nine. Its enduring importance is not a claim that everybody misses a gorilla, or that exactly half of all people are inattentive. Detection rates depend on which version of the video is shown, what task the observer performs, the similarity of the unexpected object to attended objects, and how attention is directed. The controlled observation is that an event can occupy visible space and still fail to enter a person's report because attention was committed elsewhere.

Notice how odd that sounds from the inside. Someone might say, 'I looked directly at the screen.' That can be true. Light from the unexpected event may have reached their eyes. They were simultaneously doing something else, searching for one kind of action. Their failure to report the event does not require a damaged eye or an inability to understand what a gorilla is. Vision and attention are related processes, but attending to a task involves selection.

Selection is often the price of expertise. A radiologist studies an image for signs of disease. A driver searches traffic for vehicles, pedestrians, and signals. A pianist concentrates on a difficult passage. We should expect the attentional strategies of those people to be shaped by their tasks. Specialized attention can make critical features easier to detect, while leaving some unexpected details less likely to be noticed. Whether that trade-off is wise depends on the environment and the consequences of missing something.

The next time you hear that an observer 'could not possibly have missed' an event, ask which task occupied their attention, how much advance warning they had, and what the scene required them to distinguish. The correct response is not automatically to distrust the observer. It is to examine the conditions of observation rather than treating a confident report as a complete inventory of what was present.

## A Cambridge laboratory and an unfamiliar tale

Frederic Bartlett approached the problem from memory rather than immediate seeing. In the early twentieth century, psychology was still establishing its experimental methods, and Bartlett worked at Cambridge across psychology and anthropology. In nineteen thirty-one he became the university's first professor of experimental psychology. The next year he published *Remembering*, the work for which he is best known.

Bartlett was interested in more than how many nonsense syllables a student could repeat. People remember stories, gestures, and situations through a background of expectations about how the world works. He had students read or hear material and later reproduce it. Some material was deliberately unfamiliar to the participants' cultural expectations. A story that became famous in subsequent discussions, 'The War of the Ghosts,' had reached Bartlett through anthropological collection; it belonged to a different storytelling tradition from that of his Cambridge students.

In retellings of the story, participants sometimes altered features that were difficult to interpret within their familiar world. Names and details disappeared, strange actions became more conventional, and the sequence was reorganized into something that seemed to make sense to the teller. The changes were not all identical, and they did not occur according to one inevitable script. Bartlett's point was that recall depends partly on an organized framework of prior experience, which he called a schema.

The cultural encounter demands care. The original tale was not a failed version of an English story awaiting correction. The students' substitutions revealed something about the students' assumptions, not a deficiency in the tradition from which the tale came. And Bartlett's demonstrations were historically important without satisfying every standard of later tightly controlled memory experiments. His materials, procedures, and interpretive analysis need to be read as part of their period.

Still, the essential phenomenon is familiar. Tell a friend about an event from last year. You usually do not deliver a literal replay of every movement and spoken word. You produce a coherent account, selecting details that help make the event intelligible. The structure that allows us to remember meaningfully can also pull a recollection toward what we expect should have happened.

## Remembering an event versus remembering its meaning

Suppose you meet a friend who says, 'I arrived at the station, bought a ticket, and missed the train.' A few days later you remember that they ran toward the platform. Perhaps they did, but the original sentence did not say so. Your knowledge of how people behave when they miss trains provides a plausible action that can slip into the retelling.

A schema is useful here because stories are not random lists. We know that railway stations contain tickets, platforms, departure times, and hurried people. This structure helps us infer what mattered and recall it later. Without knowledge of typical events, our memories would be harder to organize and retrieve. But the ease of completing a story can conceal the difference between remembered observation and plausible inference.

We should distinguish several questions that are often combined under the word memory. Can someone recognize a face they have seen? Can they recall an exact sentence? Can they explain what happened in broad outline? Can they remember where a detail came from? These abilities need not have the same accuracy. Someone may remember the central event while misplacing its date, or remember a fact while forgetting whether they learned it by witnessing it, reading it, or hearing a friend discuss it.

This last problem, remembering a source, will become central when we turn to a psychologist who studied witnesses. The mere presence of a detail in a person's present recollection cannot tell us where that detail entered. It may have come from original perception, from an inference made shortly afterward, from a later conversation, or from repeated exposure to a public account.

A useful way to think about the issue is that memory must be retrieved through a present state of mind. We come to a recollection with a current question, current knowledge, and a purpose for remembering. None of this makes the past imaginary. It means our access to the past is mediated by a process with its own strengths and vulnerabilities.

## The one word in the question

In nineteen seventy-four Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer published two experiments involving films of automobile collisions. Participants watched filmed events and answered questions. In the first experiment, the researchers varied the verb in a question about how fast the cars were going when they made contact. The estimated speeds differed depending on whether the cars were described as having 'contacted,' 'hit,' 'bumped,' 'collided,' or 'smashed' into each other.

The mean estimate with 'smashed' was forty point five miles per hour; with 'hit' it was thirty-four. That difference shows that phrasing can change a report. It does not by itself prove that the underlying memory changed. A participant might retain the same imperfect recollection and merely adjust their numerical answer because a more dramatic verb suggests a more violent collision. Loftus and Palmer recognized that possibility and designed a second experiment.

This time, one hundred and fifty participants saw a film containing a multiple-car accident. Afterward, fifty were asked a speed question using 'smashed,' fifty using 'hit,' and fifty were not asked about speed. One week later the researchers asked whether participants had seen broken glass. The film contained none. Yet sixteen of the fifty who had heard 'smashed' said yes. In the 'hit' group, seven said yes. Among the fifty who received no speed question, six said yes.

That is thirty-two percent in the first group, fourteen percent in the second, and twelve percent in the control group. Those numbers are specific to this experimental setup. They show that an earlier question affected a later report of a nonexistent detail. They do not establish that every affirmative respondent had a vivid false visual image, nor do they tell us which exact mental mechanism operated in each person.

The researchers offered a plausible interpretation: information acquired after an event can become integrated with information from the original event. Later questioning may influence what people report remembering. Their work helped establish an experimental program around the misinformation effect and the ways human memory is susceptible to suggestion.

The personal and professional consequences of this line of research extended beyond the laboratory. Loftus became deeply involved in debates about eyewitness testimony and contested recollections. Those debates concern people with real injuries, real legal jeopardy, and sometimes irreconcilable accounts. The experiments establish susceptibility under particular conditions. They do not provide a general license to declare another person's memories false, or to assume that confident witnesses are always unreliable.

## How certain can a memory feel?

A recollection may arrive with great clarity. You may remember exactly where you stood when you heard alarming news, who was beside you, and how the room looked. Researchers have studied such 'flashbulb memories,' including memories of the Challenger shuttle disaster and the attacks of September eleventh. The resulting evidence distinguishes subjective confidence from the stability of particular reported details. A person can remain confident even when later accounts differ from what they wrote down soon after the event.

That finding can be misunderstood. An inconsistency between two accounts does not automatically reveal which account is correct. If both are reports from memory, neither has privileged access to the original event merely by being later or earlier. The best studies use early records, controlled conditions, and independent corroboration where possible. Even then, what is being measured—consistency, accuracy, confidence, or narrative coherence—must be specified.

A dramatic event may also be remembered better in some respects than an ordinary one. Central details, emotional significance, and later rehearsal can improve retention. Some peripheral information may remain inaccurate. There is no single law that emotional memories are either indelible recordings or hopeless fabrications.

Consider two witnesses to a crowded intersection. One remembers that the pedestrian signal was red and the other remembers an approaching bus. They might have attended to different parts of the scene. If a police officer later asks whether the bus was moving quickly, the question may influence the way they describe its movement. Good interviewing separates an open invitation to recount events from increasingly specific questions, records what was asked, and avoids feeding a witness details that originated with the interviewer.

The evidence supports a modest but powerful principle: confidence is a feature of a person's current belief, while accuracy concerns the relationship between that belief and what occurred. They often correlate, but one cannot simply substitute for the other without considering the task, the conditions, and the history of the report.

Consider a study that tried to separate the feeling of a memory from its stability. On September twelfth, two thousand one, the day after the attacks in the United States, Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin asked fifty-four Duke University students to record how they first heard the news. The students also described an ordinary event from the preceding days. Some returned a week later, others after six weeks, and others after thirty-two weeks. At the follow-up they were asked about both events again.

The results resist an easy story about exceptionally vivid memories. Recollections of the attacks and recollections of everyday events became less consistent with the original written accounts over time. The researchers did not find that the emotionally charged memories preserved details more accurately. Yet the students' ratings of vividness, the feeling of remembering, and belief in their own accuracy remained comparatively high for the September eleventh memories. Their everyday memories lost that subjective assurance as the weeks passed.

Notice how careful the comparison was. An experiment cannot bring back every sensory detail of an event to establish what a student truly experienced. Here the first written account served as a reference against which later accounts could be compared. That makes consistency measurable, even though consistency and truth are themselves different concepts. It is possible to repeat an initial error consistently, and possible to report a true detail that was omitted from the first account.

The distinction is valuable when a witness says, sincerely, 'I can see it as clearly as the day it happened.' The statement reports a quality of present experience. It does not, on its own, settle whether the description matches the original event. A listener can respect the person's experience while still asking whether independent records, photographs, timestamps, or other accounts support the particular claim.

We should also resist the opposite overreaction. Studies of mistaken or changing memories do not prove that eyewitnesses are always unreliable or that everything recalled is invented. Memory can preserve information for decades. It can also change under suggestion, repeated retelling, and shifts in understanding. What matters is identifying the conditions under which a specific memory should carry more or less evidentiary weight.

## Prediction is useful because the world has structure

We have now seen two related forms of inference. Perception uses context to estimate the present causes of sensory data. Remembering uses stored information and current cues to reconstruct aspects of the past. These operations are related, but they are not identical, and a theory of visual interpretation is not automatically a theory of autobiographical memory.

Some contemporary accounts describe the brain as a prediction-making system. They emphasize that perception involves expectations about incoming signals and corrections when actual signals differ. This family of ideas has produced fruitful models and experiments, but researchers debate which versions make distinctive predictions and which neural mechanisms carry out the proposed computations. We should use the language of prediction as a useful model, not as permission to say that science has already solved consciousness or perception.

Why predict anything? Because the environment is structured. A thrown ball follows a trajectory. A moving person tends to continue moving for a short interval. Words in a sentence constrain which words are likely to follow. If a nervous system waited for every signal to arrive before making any estimate, it would often respond too late. Expectations help it interpret noisy evidence and prepare actions.

But an effective prediction must also be revisable. The apparent neighbor beneath the tree becomes a signpost when you approach. If you remain so committed to the first interpretation that no new evidence can change it, prediction has ceased to serve perception. That is the difference between using a prior belief as a guide and protecting it against correction.

One can see the same problem in science. An investigator begins with a theory, designs an experiment, and interprets the results. Background knowledge is essential to deciding what to measure. Yet the investigator needs methods that make it possible for the evidence to contradict expectations. The power of a model lies partly in what it would allow us to discover if it were wrong.

## Two kinds of failure, two kinds of protection

Return to the original pavement calculation. The worker needs to detect holes while avoiding false alarms. If missing a hole could seriously injure someone, it may be reasonable to investigate many harmless spots. If every investigation shuts a road for a day, unnecessary alarms also have a cost. The optimal threshold depends on the consequences of different errors. We cannot choose it by accuracy alone.

The same distinction matters when recalling a conversation. If your purpose is to remember the gist of a friend's advice, a coherent paraphrase may be adequate. If a legal case turns on whether a particular sentence was spoken, the exact wording matters. In that setting, a recording or contemporaneous note can preserve information that unaided memory may not reliably reproduce. The appropriate standard depends on the question.

Several practices follow from the experiments, though none guarantees perfect recall. Record observations promptly before discussion reshapes them. Separate what you directly noticed from what you inferred. Ask open questions before leading ones. Preserve independent reports rather than having a group agree on a single version first. When a fact has serious consequences, seek corroborating records or physical evidence. These are ways to improve the evidence available, rather than simply urging people to try harder to remember accurately.

There is also a social lesson. We can disagree with someone's perception without assuming bad faith. Two people can have sincerely different recollections, especially after attending to different events or receiving different subsequent information. Sincerity does not establish correctness, and error does not establish dishonesty. An investigation should allow both possibilities.

That does not imply that testimony lacks value. Human beings routinely identify familiar people, navigate roads, interpret speech, and recall important events successfully. The same mechanisms that create errors make these accomplishments possible. A theory that explains only mistakes while overlooking successful perception would be missing most of its subject.

## What we see, and what we can justify saying we saw

Helmholtz's improvised instrument made it possible to observe the eye in new detail, but it did not turn seeing into a direct photograph of reality. Bartlett's students could recall an unfamiliar tale in a form influenced by their own experience. Loftus and Palmer showed that the words of a question could alter later reports. Simons and Chabris showed that focused attention could exclude an event from an observer's awareness. Different experiments, different mechanisms, but a shared warning against treating experience as a complete and neutral record.

There is an equally important positive conclusion. Perception is an achievement. A small, moving biological system transforms limited physical signals into useful knowledge of stable objects and events. Memory allows us to draw on experience without having to start from nothing each morning. Those capacities work because our interpretations are usually constrained by a world with regularities. Their failures teach us how to make the constraints more explicit.

The next time you confidently recognize a shape in poor light, consider both the signal and the expectation. The next time you tell a familiar story, consider which details you witnessed and which make the story fit together. And when the stakes are high, give the evidence a chance to correct the interpretation before it becomes a settled memory. We see a world worth trusting, but learning to trust it well means understanding the work our minds perform to make it visible.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Inferring a physical cause from an ambiguous sensory report

Let `H` mean a real hole and `W` mean a warning impression. The synthetic
pavement example specifies

\[
P(H)=0.10,\quad P(W\mid H)=0.90,\quad P(W\mid \neg H)=0.30.
\]

Bayes' rule gives

\[
P(H\mid W)=\frac{0.9(0.1)}{0.9(0.1)+0.3(0.9)}
=\frac{0.09}{0.36}=0.25.
\]

For a high-damage setting with prior `P(H)=0.6`, the same likelihoods yield
`0.54/(0.54+0.12) = 0.81818...`. Both are **invented** numerical models,
not estimates from a perception experiment. A discriminating sensory channel
alone cannot determine posterior probability without a prior or base rate.

### 2. The visual inverse problem

A simplified diffuse-reflection model is `I(x) ≈ L(x) R(x)` where `I` is
observed intensity, `L` illumination, and `R` reflectance. Multiple
illumination/reflectance pairs produce the same intensity; contextual
assumptions are needed for inversion. The model suppresses three-dimensional
geometry, spectrum, specular reflection, and receptor transformations.

### 3. Loftus and Palmer's published values

1974, experiment I (five verb conditions, mean estimated speed in mph):

| Verb | Mean estimate |
| --- | ---: |
| Smashed | 40.5 |
| Collided | 39.3 |
| Bumped | 38.1 |
| Hit | 34.0 |
| Contacted | 31.8 |

1974, experiment II, **50 subjects per group**, response to a question about
broken glass, which was absent from the film:

| Initial question | Reported glass | Did not report glass | Percent reporting glass |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Smashed | 16 | 34 | 32% |
| Hit | 7 | 43 | 14% |
| No speed question | 6 | 44 | 12% |

The later response is an observed report, not a direct measurement of memory
representation. The study discusses response effects and reconstruction as
alternative and partly distinguishable mechanisms.

### 4. Distinguishing failures

- **Signal ambiguity:** multiple physical states can give compatible sensory data.
- **Attentional selection:** an event is present, but the observer's task makes
  report of that event less likely.
- **Reconstruction:** retention and retrieval involve organized knowledge and
  may differ from the original event record.
- **Suggestibility/source monitoring:** externally supplied details may affect
  later reports, with uncertain provenance.
- **Confidence calibration:** subjective certainty is a different measured
  variable from objective accuracy.

### 5. Historical and evidence notes

- Helmholtz's financial constraints, medical training, military service,
  independent mathematics study and color-vision revision are described by
  MacTutor; the Stanford Encyclopedia details his theory of perception.
- The ophthalmoscope date and priority require nuance: Helmholtz introduced a
  usable instrument around 1850; Purkinje and Babbage had earlier relevant
  approaches, which the American Academy of Ophthalmology discusses.
- Cambridge University documents Bartlett's 1932 *Remembering* and the
  unfamiliar-story experiments. Different retellings should not be presented
  as a deterministic sequence or a test of another culture's story quality.
- Simons and Chabris (1999) vary the attention task and unexpected objects;
  no single detection fraction should be generalized to all viewers.
- Loftus and Palmer (1974) is the authority for the speed means, group sizes,
  absence of broken glass, and follow-up reports. Its results do not establish
  a universal degree of eyewitness error.
- Talarico and Rubin (2003) measure agreement with an early written report, not independent historical truth. Their September 11 and everyday-event conditions allow comparison of consistency, vividness and confidence.
- The Bayesian and optical equations here are **modern teaching models**,
  not formal mathematics Helmholtz or Bartlett claimed to use in those forms.
