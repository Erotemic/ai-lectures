# Why the Whole Behaves Differently from Its Parts: Feedback, Cities, and Emergence

A traffic jam appears on a highway without an accident. No driver wants to create the jam. Each may be trying to preserve a safe distance and get home as quickly as possible. Yet an almost invisible disturbance, a single brief slowdown, can travel backward through the cars until hundreds of people are braking in a queue. If you interviewed every driver separately, each account might sound reasonable. The resulting traffic pattern would still be miserable.

We have a habit of explaining a collective event by finding a person responsible for it. Sometimes that is exactly right. A reckless decision may cause a catastrophe. But many important patterns arise through interactions among people or components that individually behave in understandable ways. A city can become segregated without a central organizer. A fish population can oscillate even if fish do not agree on a schedule. A machine may swing wildly because a controller corrects mistakes too aggressively. A market can spread panic as each participant tries to reduce personal exposure.

The question is how a system made of individually comprehensible parts acquires behavior we cannot read off from any single part. The answer involves feedback, networks, time, and the arrangement of interactions. We can learn it through small models and two people who thought seriously about cities: Jane Jacobs, the journalist and activist who refused to see urban streets as mere lines on a planning map, and Thomas Schelling, a scholar who explored how modest individual preferences could produce severe collective separation.

## The street that was supposed to be disorderly

Jane Jacobs moved to New York in the 1930s and found that the streets around Greenwich Village were full of uses that large-scale planners sometimes regarded as messy. Shops opened at different hours. People walked to work, spoke at doorways, watched children from windows, crossed paths with strangers, and became familiar enough to notice trouble. The neighborhood's vitality was not the product of one central organizer. It came from many overlapping routines.

Jacobs became a writer and, eventually, a critic of urban renewal schemes that would have cleared established neighborhoods in the name of modernization. She joined campaigns defending Washington Square Park and the West Village and opposing the proposed Lower Manhattan Expressway. The battles were political. They involved residents organizing meetings, gathering evidence, dealing with city officials, and arguing over whose knowledge of a place should count. Jacobs's observations could be challenged, but they could not simply be dismissed because she did not describe a city using an official engineering diagram.

Her 1961 book *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* developed the idea that lively sidewalks and mixed uses support social functions through countless local contacts. Her famous emphasis on eyes on the street should not be turned into a guarantee that every busy street is safe. It was an argument against treating the layout and uses of a city as independent mechanical pieces. Remove a shop, move residences far from employment, widen a road, and you may alter an entire web of interactions.

Consider a cafe that survives on morning commuters, afternoon students, and evening residents. If zoning removes the residences, the cafe loses evening customers. The owner shortens operating hours, and people who once walked past an illuminated shop now encounter a dark storefront. That change might reduce pedestrian activity, which in turn hurts nearby businesses. A planner may have changed one land-use category on paper; the social consequences propagate through relationships.

Jacobs's contribution was partly methodological. She asked observers to follow the interactions before deciding which components were dispensable. The lesson applies beyond cities: knowing the pieces of a system is necessary, but we must also know how they influence each other.

## A feedback loop in four sentences

Start with a thermostat. A sensor measures temperature. A controller compares it with a chosen target. A heater changes the temperature, and the sensor measures again. This is a feedback loop because the output of the action returns as new information for the next action.

When the room cools below the target, the heater turns on; when it warms enough, the heater turns off. In a well-designed system, this is negative feedback. A deviation from the target triggers an action that opposes the deviation. Negative means counteracting, not bad.

Positive feedback works differently. Suppose a microphone picks up sound from a speaker that is amplifying the microphone signal. The picked-up sound is amplified again, then again. Under the wrong conditions, a screech develops. An initial sound makes a larger sound, which produces still more input. Positive means reinforcing, not good.

The signs do not determine all behavior. Delays and sensitivities matter. Imagine a heater that responds only ten minutes after a temperature change. If it turns on too strongly and stays on while the room is already warming, the temperature may overshoot. The controller then cuts power, but the room keeps heating briefly and later cools too far. What was supposed to stabilize temperature can create oscillation.

The nineteenth-century physicist James Clerk Maxwell analyzed governors, devices used to regulate the speed of engines. His 1868 paper investigated conditions under which their corrective behavior would remain stable. The mathematical challenge was the same in outline: a mechanism sensing a departure from a target and acting back on the system can either damp disturbances or make them grow.

We can sketch a simple model aloud. Suppose each minute a controller corrects half of the current temperature error, and there are no delays or outside disturbances. Begin four degrees below target. After a minute the error is two degrees. Then one degree. Then half a degree. The error shrinks geometrically. If instead each correction repeatedly overshoots by a factor greater than one, the deviations can alternate and grow. Feedback explains why the details of timing and adjustment matter more than the good intentions of the designer.

## From individual preference to collective segregation

Thomas Schelling approached a different problem. People choose where to live, and their choices depend partly on who already lives nearby. Schelling wanted to know what the resulting pattern might look like. In a 1971 article, *Dynamic Models of Segregation*, he built abstract models showing how local preferences and movement could produce much sharper collective segregation than a simple reading of individual preferences might suggest.

We can imagine a very small neighborhood with houses in a row. Half the residents are represented by blue pieces, half by yellow pieces. Each resident checks the nearest neighbors and considers moving if the local mix falls below a preferred threshold. No resident needs to demand an entirely same-color neighborhood. Some might be content with an approximately mixed block. Yet if a few people move away from locally imbalanced spots, those departures can change the neighborhood composition for the remaining residents, leading to more moves. The eventual distribution can be far more segregated than anyone explicitly requested.

It is important to describe the model's limits. Actual residential segregation has also been created and maintained through law, violence, discriminatory lending, exclusionary zoning, disparities in wealth, and institutional power. Schelling never showed that these forces were irrelevant. His abstract demonstration identifies one possible additional mechanism: the dynamics of interdependent choices can amplify distinctions. It cannot tell us which mechanism dominates any particular city's history.

Schelling's insight is easier to understand if we distinguish two questions. Why did a particular household move? Why did the city acquire its eventual pattern? The first answer may cite income, preference, or housing availability. The second requires following how one move changes the options and incentives of others. A good collective explanation traces the interactions, including the institutional constraints shaping them.

This distinction also warns against inferring motives directly from outcomes. Seeing a segregated map does not establish that every resident strongly desired segregation. Seeing cooperation does not establish that everybody is generous. Seeing a queue does not establish that the first driver wanted to inconvenience the others. The whole does not give us a transparent window into any one person's intentions.

## A small model we can work through

Imagine a group choosing between two roads, A and B, each connecting home to work. Road A takes ten minutes plus one minute for every vehicle using it. Road B always takes thirty minutes. Suppose twenty vehicles need to travel.

If all twenty choose A, each spends thirty minutes. If ten choose A and ten choose B, the A travelers take twenty minutes while the B travelers take thirty. At first this arrangement appears stable, but a driver on B notices the faster A route and switches. As more drivers move onto A, the journey there lengthens. Eventually a selfishly chosen equilibrium may leave almost everyone using A, with no traveler able to improve the journey by moving to B alone.

Now consider what would happen if a coordinator could direct some vehicles to B. Some travelers would experience longer trips than those on A, but the total time spent traveling could fall. The system's objective and the individual's immediate incentive do not necessarily agree.

Let us calculate a simpler numerical version. If twenty drivers use A, each takes thirty minutes, for a total of six hundred driver-minutes. If ten take A at twenty minutes and ten take B at thirty, total time is two hundred plus three hundred, or five hundred driver-minutes. One hundred minutes have been saved across the group. But a driver personally assigned thirty minutes on B may want to take the twenty-minute route. Keeping the socially beneficial arrangement may require cooperation, pricing, scheduling, or redesigned infrastructure.

This is a toy model with invented travel-time functions, not a universal rule about highway policy. Its value is revealing the gap between a stable individual-choice pattern and a desirable collective outcome. Sometimes those two align; sometimes they do not.

## Networks create possibilities and vulnerabilities

Picture two hospitals receiving medication from suppliers. In the first arrangement each hospital has an independent supply chain. In the second they share a central distributor that saves money and reduces duplication. Under ordinary conditions the shared system may be more efficient. If the distributor fails, both hospitals may lose supplies simultaneously.

The parts have not changed their medical purpose. The pattern of connections changed the consequences of failure. Network structure is as important as the reliability of individual components.

The same principle appears in epidemics. A pathogen does not spread merely because one person is contagious. It spreads through contacts between people. A gathering, a workplace, or a household creates routes along which infection might pass. Breaking some connections can reduce transmission even if the organism's biological properties stay constant. Conversely, a highly connected individual or institution can have effects disproportionate to its size.

In networks, averages can mislead. Suppose most people have three contacts, but a few have hundreds. The average contact count might look moderate while transmission potential is shaped by highly connected locations. The pattern of contacts, not simply the total number, helps determine spread. But it would be mistaken to label any highly connected person inherently dangerous; risk also depends on timing, behavior, susceptibility, environment, and protective measures.

Networks also carry benefits. Diverse social contacts spread information, employment opportunities, and assistance during emergencies. Redundancy may be expensive but protective. A second bridge may appear underused on ordinary days and become vital after a flood. A systems explanation must account for gains and losses rather than assuming that connectedness or decentralization is always preferable.

## Emergence without mystery

The word emergence can sound mystical, as though the whole possesses a hidden force absent from the parts. In many cases the phenomenon can be explained through straightforward interactions. A traffic wave travels backward though the cars move forward. Each driver's acceleration and braking can be described locally. The wave emerges from the sequence of responses.

Consider the audience at a stadium starting a wave. No participant moves around the stadium. People stand and sit in sequence, responding to nearby people. The visible pattern travels even though the individuals mostly stay in place. There is no extra wave substance circling the seats. The wave is a property of coordinated behavior over time.

Likewise, a school of fish may turn as individuals respond to local positions and motion. A market price emerges from many offers and bids subject to rules, resources, and expectations. Language changes through use by speakers who do not centrally coordinate grammar. These analogies differ in important ways; they help us notice a recurring kind of explanation, not a single law covering all complex phenomena.

One useful test is whether the pattern has properties that are difficult to attribute to an isolated component. A traffic jam has a speed and direction. A car has speed and direction too, but a jam can move opposite to the cars. To understand it, we must describe a pattern of interactions.

Another test is whether changing connections changes the outcome. Removing a link in a supply chain may protect against a cascade but also reduce efficiency. Changing the timing of traffic signals may smooth a flow without modifying any vehicle. Such interventions help turn a conceptual description into a causal theory.

## Predictable rules, difficult predictions

A system governed by clear rules can still be hard to forecast. Weather provides a classic example. Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist working with numerical models, became associated with the study of how sensitive dynamical systems can be to their starting conditions. Two nearby initial states may diverge over time even though the mathematical rules are deterministic.

This is not the same as randomness in the everyday sense. If the evolution is deterministic, the same exact starting state produces the same future under the model. But our measurements are never infinitely precise, and the model itself is an approximation. Small errors in initial measurements can become large discrepancies later. Forecast skill therefore has a horizon.

Imagine two nearly identical sequences of decisions in an organization. On one day a warning email arrives before a deadline; on another it arrives after. That timing difference may affect whether a committee acts, which alters later staffing and budgets. It would be careless to declare that the organization is mathematically chaotic from this story. But it illustrates why chains of interactions can magnify small differences.

Complexity does not imply complete unpredictability either. We often predict aggregate patterns better than individual details. Weather forecasts may be probabilistic and still highly useful. Traffic planners may estimate congestion under different road designs without predicting every driver's exact movements. The appropriate goal is not perfect prediction but a model good enough for the decision at hand, accompanied by an honest account of uncertainty.

## When policies push back

Institutions change the behavior of the people they measure and regulate. Suppose a school rewards teachers solely for average test scores. Teachers may devote more time to tested skills. Some may avoid difficult students or narrow the curriculum. The metric becomes an incentive, not just a description. When that happens, data collected after the policy may no longer have the same meaning as data collected before it.

This is a feedback problem. The institution sees a number, reacts to it, and changes the process producing the number. If the response targets the proxy rather than the intended goal, the system may improve the displayed metric while harming education. Sometimes the effects are benign: clearer targets can organize attention. Sometimes they produce gaming and exclusion. We must examine the actual causal loop.

An effective design can include several measurements, qualitative review, protections against manipulation, and opportunities for appeal. It can also avoid assuming that people will behave exactly as they did before the rules changed. Any policy that alters incentives is also an intervention in the system's dynamics.

Jane Jacobs's critique of large urban schemes is helpful here. A map may show blocks and traffic routes clearly while hiding informal support networks or small businesses sustained by mixed use. The information needed for a plan is distributed among people living with the consequences. That does not guarantee that residents will agree or that every existing practice should remain. It means design should make room for local knowledge and for learning from real outcomes.

## A practical method for seeing systems

When faced with a frustrating collective outcome, begin by identifying the components and their goals. Who acts? What can each actor observe? What rules constrain them? What information arrives late? Then trace the connections. How does one actor's response alter what the next actor sees?

Draw a loop in words. More traffic means slower commutes. Slower commutes may motivate departure from the busiest hour. That departure may reduce congestion. This is a potentially balancing loop. Or more popularity brings more visitors, whose recommendations attract still more visitors. That may be a reinforcing loop until crowding or cost changes the relationship.

Ask about delay. Does an action improve the immediate number while creating trouble months later? Ask about thresholds. Does a small change have little effect until capacity is exceeded? Ask about distribution. Who benefits and who carries the burden? Ask about scale. Does an individual-level improvement remain beneficial when everybody adopts it?

Most of all, test the proposed mechanism. A persuasive story about interconnectedness is not yet evidence. Compare places where the relevant connection differs. Examine time series before and after a change. Investigate rival explanations. A complex diagram can be wrong just as a simple one can.

## Thresholds: when gradual changes become sudden

Some systems change smoothly. Increasing the number of people in a room by one raises the number by one. But other properties depend on thresholds. A room with enough fresh air may feel comfortable until ventilation no longer keeps up with occupancy. A bridge may withstand increasing loads with little visible movement until stresses exceed a critical limit. The external change can be gradual while the observed response is abrupt.

Imagine an emergency service capable of handling twenty calls an hour. On average it receives ten. A modest increase to twelve may cause little trouble. If calls rise to nineteen, the service is near capacity. A few minutes with more incoming calls than average may create a queue that takes hours to clear, even if the long-run average remains below twenty. Why? Arrivals are uneven, and work that cannot be completed immediately waits. If the queue grows, the next caller must wait for the callers already in line.

We can make the simplest version audible. If a service receives twenty-five requests in one hour and completes twenty, five remain. If the next hour brings another twenty-five, the backlog rises to ten. The service has not become less competent. Demand has exceeded capacity. Hiring one additional staff member might help, but whether the queue disappears depends on the resulting service rate, the variation of arrivals, and how long calls take.

These mechanisms explain why a system can appear healthy for years and then become overwhelmed. It was operating with little margin. Resilience sometimes requires capacity that seems idle under normal conditions. A spare bed, extra transformer, or alternate supply route may look inefficient until demand spikes or a component fails.

But unused capacity also costs money. A systems analysis should make the tradeoff explicit. An organization might tolerate delay for low-stakes tasks and reserve extra capacity for emergencies. There is no universal optimal margin. The right amount depends on the consequences of failure.

## Look for the mechanism, not only the metaphor

Complexity has become a favorite word in discussions of institutions and society. It is easy to gesture toward interconnectedness when no one knows what went wrong. The cure is to identify a testable mechanism.

Suppose a city adds a new transit line and rents rise in neighborhoods near its stations. One story says improved accessibility raised demand for housing there. Another says the transit project was placed in neighborhoods already undergoing redevelopment. A third points to citywide construction restrictions. The mechanisms imply different patterns in the data and different responses to policy.

We could compare rents before and after construction, look at similar areas that did not receive the line, inspect development permits, and interview residents and businesses. None of those measurements alone resolves every causal issue. Together they can help distinguish explanations. A diagram showing many arrows becomes valuable only when we know what the arrows claim and how they might be checked.

Schelling's work provides a warning about reverse inference: a collective pattern does not uniquely determine individual preference. Jacobs's work provides a complementary warning about incomplete abstraction: a plan that excludes everyday relationships may miss consequential connections. Neither warning says that mathematical models or city planning are useless. They say that models should be used to ask sharper questions and revised when the world resists them.

There is also a difference between a system being complicated and being adaptive. A complicated clock may have many interlocking gears but no capacity to learn from the world. A small group of people may have relatively few members yet alter their rules in response to feedback. In adaptive systems, the parts change their behavior as the system evolves. A traffic model that assumes fixed routes may fail after a new road changes commuting habits.

We should therefore specify the time scale. A model may accurately predict traffic during the first week after a new signal is installed, then become less accurate as drivers adapt their routes. It may describe how quickly a disease spreads during one stage of an epidemic but need revision as people change behavior. An accurate local model is not automatically a permanent law.

## An experiment in collective behavior

Consider a classroom of twenty students. Each is privately asked to choose between two meeting times. Twelve favor the earlier one and eight prefer the later one. The teacher announces that students should choose whichever time most of their friends are likely to attend. Now the preferences may change. A student initially favoring the later time might choose the earlier one to remain with friends, making it more attractive to others.

The final schedule can depend on the order in which people hear about one another's choices. If the later-time students announce first, they might gather a viable group. If the earlier-time group coordinates first, it might become dominant. The collective outcome emerges from preferences plus information, timing, and interaction.

This thought experiment shows why designing institutions cannot be reduced to collecting one survey. A survey tells us what people answer under one information structure. The final decision environment may change what they want to do. Sometimes that change is beneficial: coordination makes a shared meeting possible. Sometimes it shuts out minority preferences. A good organizer can explain the tradeoff and consider whether multiple times or remote access would preserve more options.

The practical point is that collective patterns are partly designed. By changing information, incentives, capacity, and routes for participation, we can sometimes improve outcomes without demanding that every individual change their personality.

One last distinction concerns the level at which a policy acts. If a busy intersection has a collision problem, we can educate individual drivers, change the signal timing, redesign the sight lines, or reduce conflict between movements. These interventions target different mechanisms. A driver education campaign might help when rules are misunderstood. A redesign may help when the physical layout induces predictable mistakes. The right intervention follows from evidence about the cause.

A systems explanation can therefore make accountability more precise. Instead of saying that everybody contributed and nobody is responsible, it can identify which rules or designs amplify risk and which people have the authority to change them. Sometimes correcting one decision-maker's behavior is enough. Sometimes durable improvement requires changing the environment within which many reasonable people make choices.

The distinction is also useful in personal life. A household chronically misses appointments. One response is to demand that everyone become more conscientious. Another is to investigate how reminders, transportation, shared calendars, and conflicting obligations actually interact. If a single forgotten message regularly causes the failure, a better communication procedure may help more than another argument about character. That is the practical promise of thinking in systems: use the structure of the problem to find the level at which change is possible.

## The whole, the parts, and responsibility

We began with a traffic jam nobody intended. It would be foolish to conclude that no one can ever be responsible for a collective outcome. Drivers can violate safety rules; officials choose road designs; employers choose commuting schedules. The system perspective adds layers of responsibility rather than dissolving it.

Schelling's neighborhood models remind us that social patterns can arise through repeated local decisions, while also demanding that we examine the powerful institutions that limit people's options. Jacobs's street-level observations remind us that a place contains relationships that disappear when we represent it only as parcels and roads. Feedback theory shows how small corrections can stabilize or destabilize a machine. Network theory shows how patterns of connection distribute both opportunity and danger.

The central habit is to stop asking only what each part does and start asking what happens when the parts respond to each other over time. When we do that, problems that once seemed like failures of individual character may reveal failures of design. And designs that seemed efficient in isolation may expose hidden costs through their connections. Understanding the whole means making the interactions visible enough that we can change them wisely.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Feedback maps

Synthetic negative feedback \(e_{t+1}=\tfrac12 e_t\) gives \(e_t=2^{-t}e_0\), which converges to zero. A synthetic overshooting correction \(e_{t+1}=-1.2e_t\) alternates sign with increasing magnitude. Delays and coupling alter real stability conditions; see Maxwell's 1868 analysis of governors.

### Routing model

Road A travel time \(10+n_A\) minutes, B fixed at 30, 20 travelers. With all on A, total is \(20(30)=600\) driver-minutes. With 10 on each: \(10(20)+10(30)=500\). This is a synthetic illustration, not an estimated urban traffic model. Note that a selfish equilibrium is reached when no single traveler can strictly improve by changing routes; the role of indifferent switches at the all-A configuration should be distinguished from a unique equilibrium.

### Schelling model boundaries

Schelling's 1971 models show that local preferences can generate large-scale sorting. They do not estimate the proportion of historical segregation attributable to preferences instead of discriminatory institutions. Genuine cities also have unequal budgets, mobility, legal constraints, information, and housing supply.
