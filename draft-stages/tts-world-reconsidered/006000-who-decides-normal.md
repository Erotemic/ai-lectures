# Who Decides What Is Normal? Numbers, Medicine, and the Boundaries of Belonging

A child writes with the left hand. A doctor looks at a blood test. An employer asks whether a candidate's career followed a standard path. A psychologist compares a patient's behavior with a diagnostic manual. In each case somebody may say the same word: normal. Yet the word is doing remarkably different jobs. It may mean common, expected, healthy, desirable, socially approved, or permitted. Treating these meanings as interchangeable can change lives.

Suppose eighty people out of a hundred sleep between six and nine hours. That is a statement about a distribution, assuming we measured carefully. It does not tell us that someone sleeping five and a half hours is unhealthy, or that someone sleeping eight hours is healthy. Health depends on much more than frequency. The same distinction becomes harder when the measurements involve emotions, relationships, intelligence, disability, or identity. A classification can make an observation easier to communicate, while also carrying the power to define what receives treatment, what counts as an accommodation, and whom society recognizes as belonging.

How did a word describing ordinary frequency acquire so much authority? We can follow the history of statistics, medicine, and psychiatry to find out. We will also encounter a question that no graph alone can settle: what should we do when people's lives do not fit a preferred category?

## An astronomer counting people

In the early nineteenth century, the Belgian mathematician and astronomer Adolphe Quetelet carried mathematical habits from the observatory into the study of society. An astronomer measuring the position of a star several times does not obtain precisely the same result on every attempt. Instruments, weather, and observation introduce small disturbances. Averaging many measurements can help estimate the position of a stable object. Quetelet wondered whether the distributions emerging from social and biological measurements might reveal order beneath individual variety.

His investigations ranged from stature to crime statistics. He developed the image of an average man, an imagined representative whose properties emerged from collections of measurements. The project was intellectually ambitious. The same quantitative language used to study a physical world might describe regularities in population-level human life. That step helped make social statistics possible. It also created a temptation: to treat the center of a statistical distribution as the proper model for a person.

Consider an invented city where the mean adult height is one hundred seventy centimeters. If a particular resident is one hundred fifty-eight centimeters tall, the number records a difference of twelve centimeters from the mean. Nothing in that subtraction says the resident is ill, defective, less admirable, or in need of correction. The mean is an answer to a question about a population. It does not secretly contain a moral ideal.

There are further difficulties. A population may contain two groups with different patterns; their combined average might describe neither group well. It may contain a long tail of very high values, so the mean is pulled away from the typical observation. The definition of the population also changes the result. Measuring only people who can enter a particular building, or only patients attending a clinic, can exclude the very individuals a policy needs to understand. The apparent precision of an average does not rescue an unrepresentative sample.

Quetelet's larger project joined genuine insight to questionable generalizations about human qualities. His historical importance lies partly in that combination. He demonstrated the regularity of aggregate patterns while encouraging later readers to ask whether statistical regularity should be taken as a model of perfection. Once measurements could be made at scale, authorities gained a powerful new kind of evidence, and a powerful new language for making comparisons.

## A distribution is not a command

Imagine one thousand workers whose commute times are measured accurately. Six hundred travel between twenty and forty minutes, two hundred take less, and two hundred take more. We can say that the central range is common in this particular group. Now the employer announces that everyone outside that range has an abnormal commute and must change it.

That decision cannot be deduced from the measurements. The commute may depend on housing costs, disability access, caregiving, transportation infrastructure, and the employer's own location. A descriptive statistic tells the employer about workers' circumstances. It does not tell the employer which circumstances are legitimate.

The mathematical gap is important. Suppose we draw a bell-shaped curve, highest in the middle and tapering at both ends. This is the familiar normal distribution of probability theory. The word normal in its name is historical terminology. A random variable having such a distribution does not mean that its most likely values are morally preferable. Nor do all real human traits follow a bell curve. Many have skewed shapes, distinct clusters, limits, or complicated mixtures.

Under a bell curve, about sixty-eight percent of observations fall within one standard deviation of the mean. The standard deviation measures the spread around the center. A common convention labels observations more than two standard deviations away as statistically extreme. But even if the curve is an excellent model, these thresholds say how infrequent observations are, not how harmful they are. Rare talent, rare illness, rare birth dates, and rare preferences do not share one ethical status.

We could choose a different threshold. Why two standard deviations rather than one and a half? The mathematics predicts what portion of the population is affected by each cutoff, provided the model is appropriate. It cannot choose the human purpose of the cutoff. To make a good decision we need an objective, evidence about consequences, and a way to consider the people affected.

This is the first major separation in our story: normal as a statistical description, and normal as a standard people are expected to meet. When those are confused, we may mistake the convenience of a measurement for the justice of a rule.

## A physician challenges the average

A century after Quetelet, the French physician and philosopher Georges Canguilhem asked a subtler question. What does it mean for a living organism to be healthy? His 1943 work on the normal and the pathological argued that the difference cannot be read directly from an average biological measurement. Illness involves the ways a life can or cannot respond to its surroundings, and the losses of possibility experienced by the organism.

Canguilhem's dual training mattered. He was a philosopher who earned a medical degree, and the years in which he worked were marked by war and political danger in France. His inquiry was grounded in medicine's encounters with individuals who might function very differently under different conditions. An isolated number rarely captures the entire meaning of those encounters.

Think about a person who needs glasses. Without corrective lenses, a given visual acuity may prevent reading a road sign. With glasses, the same person reads comfortably. Is the person's situation exhausted by how frequently that uncorrected acuity occurs? Obviously not. We need to know the person's aims, available tools, and surrounding environment. A measurement that predicts difficulty under one condition may predict much less under another.

A person living at high altitude and a person living at sea level may show different physiological values. Some differences reflect adaptation, some illness, and some a mixture of both. The interesting question is how a body functions in its circumstances. Canguilhem's argument is not that physiology is irrelevant or that every condition should be declared healthy. It is that the living meaning of an impairment or illness exceeds its distance from a population mean.

This changes how we interpret a clinical reference interval. If a laboratory report lists a normal range, that range may have been estimated from a selected reference population. A test result just outside the interval deserves interpretation, but it is not automatically a disease diagnosis. A result inside the interval does not guarantee health. Clinicians have to consider measurement error, the patient's history, other evidence, and whether an intervention is likely to help.

Canguilhem therefore brought a practical question into a philosophical one. Instead of asking only how far somebody deviates from a reference value, ask what possibilities of life are available to that person, what has changed, and what care might restore or enlarge those possibilities.

## When a label changes the life it names

Some categories describe phenomena that stay much the same when we name them. A mineral does not read its chemical classification and decide to become something else. Human classifications work differently. People learn the labels applied to them. They may resist them, adopt them, organize around them, or be treated differently because of them.

Consider a school introducing a category for pupils who need additional time on an examination. At first the label may be a practical way of arranging support. But teachers might start expecting less from any child carrying it. Some students might discover an explanation for a difficulty they had felt alone in facing. Others might avoid asking for help because the label carries stigma. The classification can affect the very experiences it is meant to describe.

The historian and philosopher Ian Hacking examined this interplay between classifications and people, sometimes described as a looping effect. A category influences behavior and institutions; those changes alter what the category seems to capture; the criteria may then be revised. This does not imply that the underlying difficulties are invented. It means that social knowledge and social practice can become entangled.

There is an elementary causal trap here. Suppose students given a particular label have lower test scores five years later. We cannot conclude that the label itself produced the entire difference. Students were classified because they already differed in some respect; they may also receive different instruction, encouragement, accommodations, or social treatment. To determine whether the label caused harm or help, we would need a comparison that separates these pathways.

Yet it would be equally mistaken to think labels are powerless. Naming a condition can unlock treatment and community. Naming a person as deviant can justify exclusion. The question is neither whether to classify nor whether to abolish all categories. It is how to make classifications accurate enough for their purposes, revisable when evidence changes, and responsive to the people who must live with them.

## A diagnostic manual changes

Psychiatry provides an important historical case. The American Psychiatric Association's early Diagnostic and Statistical Manuals categorized homosexuality as a disorder. During the twentieth century, evidence, professional debate, changing public action, and testimony from gay and lesbian people challenged that classification. In December 1973, the association's board voted to remove homosexuality as a general diagnosis from the second edition. A membership referendum in 1974 upheld the decision. The revision initially replaced the old category with a narrower one concerned with distress about sexual orientation; later editions removed those remnants in stages.

It is tempting to tell this as a simple story in which science finally noticed a fact it had previously missed. But the history involves more than a single experiment. It includes disputes about what constitutes mental illness, how clinicians interpreted suffering, how social stigma itself produces distress, and whose experience had been treated as authoritative. Activists and professionals challenged institutions that had long treated a moral and social judgment as a medical category.

The correction also did not eliminate discrimination. A manual's revision could remove a diagnostic label while laws, family practices, employment policies, and personal prejudices remained. A category has effects partly because it is embedded in institutions. Changing the label can be necessary and important without being sufficient.

There is a lesson about evidence and value here. Suppose a clinician observes that a group experiences high rates of anxiety. The observation does not by itself tell us whether the group's identity causes illness, whether discrimination contributes to anxiety, or whether selection into the clinic creates a misleading picture. Nor does it settle whether an identity ought to be treated as a defect. Causal explanation and ethical judgment are separate tasks, though each can inform the other.

The history is a warning against assuming that the medical vocabulary of a particular era is simply a neutral transcription of nature. Medical categories can be rigorously useful, and they can be revised through better evidence and ethical criticism. Both facts are true.

## The difference between suffering and difference

Consider two people with identical measured hearing loss. One works in a setting where all meetings are held over a poor-quality telephone line. The other works in a setting with captions, written agendas, and colleagues fluent in sign language. Their audiograms may be alike, but opportunities and barriers differ. The environment helps determine the practical consequences of the impairment.

Disability scholars and advocates have long emphasized this distinction. In the medical model, an impairment is often approached as a problem located within an individual body. A social model draws attention to barriers in architecture, communication, employment, and collective expectations. These models illuminate different aspects of the situation and can be combined with care. A person may want treatment for pain and also demand accessible transportation. One goal need not cancel the other.

A staircase is a simple example. If a person uses a wheelchair, a staircase without an alternative excludes them from a building. We could describe their mobility limitation. We could also describe a design decision that made the building inaccessible. Neither fact disappears when we name the other. The question of accommodation becomes concrete: what changes would allow the person to participate?

Now think about an institution that awards every employee the same fixed start time, regardless of the work itself. Equal treatment under one rule may be unequal in effect for people with caregiving obligations, chronic symptoms, or different transportation options. The institution may have good reasons for coordination. But it should be prepared to explain those reasons, study their consequences, and consider alternatives.

Calling one way of functioning normal can make alternatives appear unnecessary even when they are affordable and effective. The word then conceals a policy choice. The remedy is to make that choice visible: What goal are we trying to achieve? Which differences are relevant to the goal? Who pays the costs of the rule? What would happen if the environment changed?

## Two meanings of a standard

Some standards are indispensable. Electrical plugs need compatible dimensions. Medication must be manufactured within tolerances. Aviation depends on shared procedures. We would be in trouble if every safety-critical system treated all variation as equally acceptable.

But a standard for interoperable devices is not the same kind of thing as a standard for a good human life. One can be tested against a well-defined engineering objective; the other inevitably involves contested values. Even in engineering, the standard is chosen for a purpose. A temperature tolerance may be narrow because materials fail outside it. A color choice may be arbitrary, chosen for clarity rather than necessity.

Suppose a manufacturer tests bolts and finds that virtually all are within half a millimeter of the target diameter. Should the product specification allow a half-millimeter deviation? We cannot answer merely from the production histogram. We need to know what the bolts hold together. If failure could bring down a bridge, even a rare outlier may be dangerous. If the bolt secures a decorative panel, a wider tolerance might be fine.

This is an example of decision theory. The frequency of an event informs us, but the cost of different outcomes guides the action. A rule can reasonably exclude a statistically common value if it is hazardous, and allow a rare one if it is harmless. The governing criterion is not commonness itself.

In social settings, measuring consequences is harder. We should therefore resist two easy slogans: that all norms are oppressive, and that any established norm deserves obedience. Some shared expectations support trust, predictability, and cooperation. Others protect convenience for a dominant group at the expense of excluded people. Evaluating them demands attention to their function and to whose experiences they reflect.

## A thought experiment about thresholds

Imagine a city screening residents for a disease. A diagnostic marker can be measured on a continuous scale. Residents with high values are more likely to develop serious complications, but risk does not jump from zero to one at a single number. The city must decide when to recommend an expensive intervention with side effects.

If the threshold is low, more people receive treatment. The city catches more eventual cases, but also treats more people who would not have become ill. If the threshold is high, fewer people undergo unnecessary treatment, but some future cases are missed. A cutoff is a choice among competing errors, guided by evidence about benefits and harms.

Now suppose the intervention becomes safer and cheaper. The same biological measurements might justify a lower threshold. Nothing in the population's anatomy changed. The rational policy changed because treatment consequences changed.

Or imagine a new study showing that risk differs greatly depending on age and another medical condition. A single threshold may no longer be appropriate. Again, the word normal has not solved the problem. We need a predictive model, evidence about causal treatment effects, an account of uncertainty, and a value judgment about tradeoffs.

This example reveals why even responsible medical boundaries are not eternal natural borders. They are operational decisions made within a changing body of knowledge. Good practice makes the reasoning open to challenge and revision instead of pretending that the line is self-explanatory.

## The danger of counting without asking who is counted

Return to Quetelet's great hope: that large numbers would reveal durable social patterns. They often do. But the movement from individual observation to collective conclusion passes through a process of selecting who appears in the data.

Suppose we measure workplace satisfaction using an optional survey. People who have time to answer may differ from those struggling with impossible schedules. People who have left the organization are not present to complain. If the satisfaction score rises after a wave of departures, leadership may congratulate itself on improved culture. Perhaps the environment improved. Perhaps dissatisfied workers simply disappeared from the denominator.

A statistic's denominator is a political and practical decision as well as a mathematical one. Who counts as a resident, a student, an employee, a patient, or a person needing support? A classification can include and exclude long before anyone calculates a percentage.

Consider an average constructed from a hundred people. If ten with the greatest difficulties are left out, the group mean may improve, even though nobody's life has improved. This is not an exotic statistical paradox. It is a familiar way metrics fail when they are treated as goals without examining the data-generating process.

To audit a norm, then, ask four questions. What is being measured? Who is represented? What purpose does the boundary serve? What happens to the people on either side? Each question invites a different kind of evidence. A historian can show where the category came from. A statistician can test how it behaves. A clinician can ask what it predicts. A person affected can explain what the measure misses.

## Expertise, disagreement, and the person living the rule

Does this mean expert authority is worthless? It means that authority should make its grounds understandable. Expertise can reveal hazards an untrained observer cannot see, and careful measurement can protect people against prejudice. When a community lacks reliable medical information, arbitrary beliefs may govern treatment. The answer to misuse of evidence is more careful evidence and more accountable decisions.

At the same time, expert methods do not automatically confer the right to set every value. A physician may know the probability that a treatment reduces pain. The patient must still weigh the burden of side effects against what they value in their life. A school may have evidence that a particular schedule improves average attendance. Families may know serious costs the average conceals.

Dialogue is useful precisely because perspectives contribute different information. Lived experience is not a substitute for carefully conducted research about population-level effects. Statistical research is not a substitute for understanding what outcomes matter to individuals. Decisions improve when both can be heard and when neither is treated as infallible.

Language can change the consequences, too. Calling a person abnormal can make the individual seem to be the problem. More precise descriptions can identify the actual issue: a hearing barrier, an elevated risk, a mismatch between task and equipment, a legal exclusion, or a symptom causing distress. Precision may sound less dramatic than a sweeping category, but it can make action possible.

A respectful description also leaves room for disagreement. Someone can reject a social expectation without denying that the expectation is widespread. A clinician can recognize suffering without reducing a patient's identity to a disease. A community can celebrate difference without pretending that all differences have identical consequences.

## Who gets to change the rule?

Think about the standards used to design a public library. Door widths, desk heights, lighting, signage, and computer interfaces each reflect assumptions about the people who will use the building. Some assumptions are informed by engineering and safety. Others may simply reproduce the requirements of the people who planned the previous building.

A library that has no accessible entrance effectively classifies some residents as outside its ordinary use case. That classification may never appear in a written policy. It is embedded in the building. When residents request a ramp, elevators, or accessible digital interfaces, the argument is partly about design and partly about who counts as a full participant in public life.

Suppose the city asks the architect for the average body dimensions of local adults. That is useful information, but it will not identify an optimal entrance width for every person. Doorways need to accommodate equipment, mobility aids, groups carrying objects, and emergency movement. Designing for a range of users may produce a better building than designing exactly for the mean.

This illustrates another mathematical distinction: fitting the average case and satisfying a constraint for a specified proportion of cases are different optimization problems. A shirt that fits an average body may fit almost nobody if real dimensions vary in different ways. A transportation system based only on average travel times may strand the people whose connections fail at the extremes. Considering the distribution changes the design question.

The same lesson applies when an institution chooses what counts as a successful career. An average time to promotion may reflect history, training, and performance. It may also reflect unequal opportunities or a selection process that rewards certain biographies. If the average becomes the mandatory schedule for everyone, it can penalize people whose paths include caregiving, disability, immigration, or research that takes longer to develop. The organization may have legitimate performance needs, but it should evaluate work against those needs rather than the convenient shape of a historical average.

Standards improve when their designers consult those who encounter their limitations. That consultation does not imply that every requested change is feasible. It improves the evidence about what the standard actually does. Measurements describe bodies and behavior; people explain which barriers and opportunities the measurements fail to represent.

There is also an accountability question. Who may appeal a classification? Can a medical diagnosis be reconsidered when better evidence appears? Can a school explain why its cutoff exists? Can a public agency publish how a seemingly neutral rule affects different populations? A category that cannot be examined or revised tends to acquire more authority than its evidence warrants.

A good rule should be sufficiently stable for people to rely on it and sufficiently revisable for errors to be corrected. That balance cannot be discovered by asking what is most common. It comes from combining measurement, explanation, values, and institutions capable of listening. This is what it means to treat normality as a question rather than a verdict.

## What normal can and cannot tell us

We began with a child writing left-handed and a doctor reading a laboratory report. The child's preferred hand may be rare in a classroom without being a defect. The laboratory result may be common without ruling out disease. The same adjective seemed to apply, but the questions were different.

Quetelet showed how population regularities can be investigated mathematically. Canguilhem challenged the assumption that a living person's health is exhausted by resemblance to an average. Changing psychiatric classifications showed that diagnostic boundaries emerge in institutions where evidence, values, and power interact. Disability debates revealed how environments can turn bodily differences into barriers or remove them. None of these insights requires us to abandon standards. They require us to say what the standard is for.

The practical habit is to translate the word normal into a more precise question. Do you mean frequent? Expected under a model? Functionally adequate? Free of illness? Socially accepted? Required by a rule? Good? These claims may sometimes agree, but one does not prove the others.

When a category affects a human life, ask who chose it, what evidence supports it, what purpose it serves, and how it could be changed. The most important fact about a person may be precisely what the category leaves out. A useful standard makes that limitation visible. A harmful one hides it behind the authority of a familiar word.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Distribution versus decision

For a population with observations \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), mean \(\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i\). The mean reports a population summary; it does not select an ethical or clinical optimum. If \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(P(|X-\mu|\le \sigma)\approx0.6827\). Applicability of this model must be checked, and its name carries no moral endorsement.

### Operational thresholds

A threshold \(t\) separates people selected for intervention from those not selected. An appropriate \(t\) depends on sensitivity, specificity, baseline risk, treatment benefit, harms, costs, and preference. Reference intervals, risk thresholds, and diagnoses are different objects.

### Interpretation boundaries

The 1973 APA decision removed homosexuality as a general DSM diagnosis; subsequent categories remained until later revisions. This is not the same as a claim that all psychiatric diagnoses are arbitrary. Quetelet's statistical regularities do not imply biological determinism or ethical perfection of the average. Canguilhem's account focuses on the organism's norms and its possibilities of life; it is not a numerical diagnostic method.
