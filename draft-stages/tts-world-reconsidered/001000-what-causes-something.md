# What Would Have Happened Otherwise? How We Know What Causes Something

On the evening of September seventh, eighteen fifty-four, a London physician named John Snow addressed a local board responsible for the welfare of a neighborhood in Soho. Cholera was killing people at a terrifying rate. Snow believed that a public water pump on Broad Street was involved. He asked the board to disable it. The next day, the pump handle was removed.

That is the version of the story that often appears in textbooks: a doctor draws a map, identifies a deadly pump, removes its handle, and ends an epidemic. It makes a satisfying picture. But by September eighth the outbreak was already declining. Residents had fled the area. Snow himself acknowledged that he could not determine whether the pump water remained infectious when its use was stopped. The famous intervention was a prudent public-health decision; its timing does not establish that it caused the epidemic to end.

Snow's real achievement is more interesting. He assembled several imperfect kinds of evidence and asked whether one explanation could account for all of them. Why did people who lived near a particular pump become ill? Why did some people living nearby escape? Why did several people far away become ill after drinking that water? And why did households supplied by two different water companies experience very different death rates, even where those companies served the same streets?

Every one of those questions has a counterpart in ordinary life. Did a new medicine make a patient better, or would the patient have recovered anyway? Did a new school policy improve learning, or were students already improving? Did a company's reorganization increase productivity, or did a change in demand do the work? Did a new law reduce a harm, or did something else change at the same time?

To answer, we need a surprisingly difficult idea: the outcome we observe must be compared with an outcome that did not happen. We need to ask what would have happened otherwise.

## A physician trained to measure carefully

John Snow was born in York in eighteen thirteen, the eldest of nine children. His father worked in a coalyard before moving into farming. Snow began his medical apprenticeship at fourteen, working with a surgeon-apothecary near Newcastle. During the cholera epidemic of eighteen thirty-one and eighteen thirty-two, he attended people in the mining district around Killingworth. The disease was no abstract question to him. He had watched it kill people at the beginning of his professional life.

Snow later established a medical practice in London. His other great specialty was anesthesia, a field in which small errors of measurement could have immediate consequences. An anesthetic given in the wrong concentration might fail to relieve pain or might become lethal. Snow studied how gases and vapors behaved and developed equipment for delivering carefully measured doses. His work eventually brought him to Queen Victoria's bedside when she received chloroform during childbirth. The cholera investigations were undertaken alongside a demanding clinical career, not in a research institute devoted exclusively to infectious disease.

His personal habits could be exacting too. He joined the temperance movement and for years followed a vegetarian diet, later relaxing those restrictions. These details should not become a retrospective diagnosis of character, but they do help us picture a doctor who paid close attention to what entered the body, whether it was a drug, food, or drinking water.

In Snow's day, disease was frequently explained by miasma: unhealthy air associated with filth, decay, and crowded conditions. That idea was plausible within a world of overflowing cesspools, dreadful smells, and neighborhoods where poverty, pollution, and sickness occurred together. People who improved sanitation could sometimes improve health without having a correct account of how a particular infection spread. There were good observational reasons to associate bad surroundings with disease.

But association leaves the central question unanswered. If a neighborhood contains both foul air and contaminated water, which exposure causes cholera? Perhaps both are related to something else: poverty, overcrowding, access to infrastructure. Seeing disease where the air smells terrible cannot isolate the responsible pathway.

Snow's hypothesis, published in eighteen forty-nine and developed much further in eighteen fifty-five, was that cholera passed between people through material from the intestines, often reaching others through contaminated drinking water. He did not have our modern microbiological account of the cholera bacterium. His argument had to work from patterns of illness, observations of transmission, and consequences one would expect if the hypothesis were true.

## A map needs a question

By the beginning of September eighteen fifty-four, deaths were concentrated around Broad Street. Snow obtained death records and visited affected households. He asked where people had obtained their drinking water. The answers could be awkward. A household might live nearer to another pump but prefer the taste of the Broad Street water. A child might attend school near the pump while living elsewhere. A person might drink at work and sleep in a different part of town.

Snow's map, showing deaths near the pump, made the geographic concentration visible. Yet a map of deaths alone could mislead. A crowded street has more people at risk than an empty street. People who live near a pump are more likely to use it, but proximity is still an imperfect measure of what they actually drank. The decisive variable in Snow's explanation was exposure to particular water, and he tried to investigate that variable directly.

One revealing exception was the brewery near Broad Street. According to Snow's account, its proprietor said that more than seventy workers were employed there and none had suffered severe cholera during the outbreak. They had access to their own well and were supplied with malt liquor; the proprietor was certain they did not obtain water from the street pump. This was an observation that the water hypothesis could explain. It was not a controlled experiment. Brewery workers might differ from their neighbors in other ways, and the proprietor's report was not a complete medical survey. But a theory must account for both cases and apparent exceptions.

Snow also investigated a woman living well outside the neighborhood who received bottled water from the Broad Street pump because she liked its taste. She and another member of the household became ill. These cases weakened an explanation based simply on breathing the air in Soho. They were also small in number, relying on reports of exposure and illness. Their value came from how they fit with the larger pattern, not from their power to settle the question alone.

The pump handle became famous because it was a tangible action. The interviewing was less photogenic, though logically indispensable. A pin on a map says where someone lived. A household interview asks what that person did. A possible explanation becomes more convincing when it predicts observations that a competing explanation has trouble accounting for.

But even the strongest-looking observation presents a trap. Suppose that after removing the handle, the number of deaths falls. One can say that the handle was removed and deaths subsequently fell. To say that removing the handle caused the decline, we must consider what the epidemic would have done had the handle remained. Snow knew enough about the epidemic curve to recognize that the available data did not answer this narrower question.

## The missing comparison

Imagine a person with a severe headache who takes a pill. An hour later, the headache has diminished. Was the pill effective? We cannot infer that from the before-and-after observation alone. Headaches often improve with time. The person may also have rested, drunk water, or stopped working. The pill may have helped, made no difference, or even slowed recovery despite the eventual improvement.

Now imagine that, for the same person at the same moment, we could observe two futures. In one, the pill is taken. In the other, it is not. Everything else that could influence the outcome is held to the same conditions. The difference between those two outcomes is the individual causal effect of taking the pill at that time.

There is a physical obstacle: we experience only one of those futures. The other is called a counterfactual, a description of what would have occurred under an alternative action. It is not an observed event that someone forgot to measure. It is absent from the world we actually lived through.

For a population, however, we can try to construct a defensible comparison. We could give a medicine to some patients and not to others, making the groups similar in relevant respects. We could follow them over time. A difference between their average outcomes would then be evidence about the medicine's average causal effect, subject to how the groups were formed and how outcomes were measured.

This is the key distinction. A correlation describes how two measured quantities vary together. A causal effect asks what would change if we intervened on one of them while keeping the relevant alternative conditions comparable. Those questions can give different answers using the same data.

Consider umbrellas and wet streets. They occur together. Does opening an umbrella cause rain? Of course not. Rain produces both umbrella use and wet streets. The common cause is obvious here, so we rarely make the error. More difficult cases have hidden common causes: illness severity, wealth, age, prior education, workplace conditions, or a thousand other features that influence both what people do and what happens afterward.

We call a common cause of treatment and outcome a confounder. The name sounds technical, but it refers to a concrete problem: our comparison groups may differ for a reason that also affects their outcomes.

## When the apparent winner loses in every group

Let's make that problem audible with numbers. Imagine a study of a treatment for a serious illness. The following data are invented for teaching; they are not results from an actual medical trial.

There are two kinds of patients, those with mild disease and those with severe disease. Recovery is naturally more common in mild cases. Doctors tend to give the treatment to the more seriously ill patients, precisely because those patients need help.

Start with the mild patients. Ten receive the treatment, and nine recover. That is a ninety percent recovery rate. Ninety do not receive the treatment, and seventy-two recover. That is an eighty percent recovery rate. Among mild patients, those treated do ten percentage points better.

Now look at the severe patients. Ninety receive the treatment, and twenty-seven recover: thirty percent. Ten do not receive it, and two recover: twenty percent. Again the treated group does ten percentage points better.

Within both severity groups, the treatment is associated with better recovery. What happens when we combine the groups?

There were one hundred treated patients: ten mild and ninety severe. Of those, thirty-six recovered. The overall recovery rate for treated patients is thirty-six percent.

There were also one hundred untreated patients: ninety mild and ten severe. Seventy-four recovered. The overall recovery rate without treatment is seventy-four percent.

The combined figures seem to show that treatment is disastrous. Yet the figures within each severity group seem to show that treatment is helpful. Nothing has been miscounted. The treated group contains mostly severe patients; the untreated group contains mostly mild patients. The two percentages answer different questions about differently composed groups.

This is a version of Simpson's paradox, the reversal of an association when data are grouped or divided. The arithmetic is elementary. The difficult part is deciding which comparison is relevant to a causal question.

Suppose we want to know the treatment's effect in a population with equal numbers of mild and severe patients, and suppose that within each severity group the treated and untreated people are otherwise comparable. We can give the two severity groups equal weight. With treatment, the expected recovery rate is the average of ninety percent and thirty percent: sixty percent. Without treatment, it is the average of eighty percent and twenty percent: fifty percent. The adjusted difference is ten percentage points in favor of treatment.

Notice what just happened. We did not discover a new patient. We changed the comparison to reflect a common mix of disease severity. We made the question more precise: what recovery rates would we expect if the two treatment options were applied to populations with the same composition?

The assumption about comparability within each severity group is essential. A doctor might still reserve treatment for the very sickest among the people labeled severe. We may have measured severity too crudely. We might have omitted age, another drug, or the time when treatment began. Adjusting for one confounder does not guarantee that all confounding is gone.

There is another complication worth retaining. Dividing the population is not always beneficial. If we condition on a variable caused by both the treatment and the outcome, or by other relevant causes of each, we can create an association that was absent before. Statisticians sometimes call such a variable a collider. The practical lesson is that selecting variables for adjustment requires a causal account of how they relate to one another. A spreadsheet cannot decide that account from correlations alone.

A treatment comparison should therefore begin before the arithmetic, with a description of who received each option, why they received it, when the relevant factors were measured, and which outcomes we seek to change.

## The comparison in the water pipes

Snow's strongest numerical evidence brings us back to London, but away from the one famous pump. South of the Thames, two companies supplied water to overlapping neighborhoods. One, the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, drew from a more polluted part of the river. The Lambeth Company had shifted its intake upstream, beyond much of London's sewage contamination.

The important circumstance was that the companies' customers were intermingled. In some streets, houses served by different companies stood close together. Compared with an ordinary comparison of rich and poor neighborhoods, this offered a better opportunity to separate the condition of the water from the condition of the surroundings.

Snow still needed to find out which company served which houses. That was much harder than it sounds. Residents often did not know the name of their supplier, especially when landlords paid the water rates. Snow examined receipts and, when necessary, collected water samples. He found that a chemical test based on dissolved salt could help distinguish the two supplies. He also credited a colleague, John Joseph Whiting, with helping investigate deaths in part of the study area. This was collaborative, time-consuming fieldwork that required practical judgment about the reliability of each observation.

In a table in his eighteen fifty-five book, Snow reported deaths during the first seven weeks of the epidemic relative to the numbers of houses supplied. For the Southwark and Vauxhall Company, the figure was one thousand two hundred sixty-three cholera deaths among approximately forty thousand supplied houses. For Lambeth, it was ninety-eight deaths among approximately twenty-six thousand supplied houses.

To put them on a comparable scale, he reported about three hundred fifteen deaths per ten thousand houses for the first supplier, and thirty-seven per ten thousand for the second. That's roughly eight and a half times as many deaths per supplied house in the first comparison as in the second.

Be careful about the denominator. These are deaths relative to the number of supplied houses; they are not percentages of residents who died. Houses can contain different numbers of people, and more than one death can occur within a house. The numbers provide a striking comparative mortality measure, though not a perfect individual risk estimate.

These figures did not emerge from a randomized trial. The suppliers were not assigned to homes by drawing lots. Customers might differ in ways that affected their likelihood of contracting or dying from cholera. Water quality could vary over time, and people sometimes drank water away from home. Nonetheless, a large mortality contrast between supplies distributed through overlapping neighborhoods strengthened the waterborne explanation. It was more difficult to explain entirely by differences in neighborhood air or elevation.

Here the causal argument is a chain. First, specify how the proposed cause could act: contaminated water enters the body. Next, identify a setting in which exposure varies independently of at least some rival causes. Then measure outcomes on a comparable denominator. Finally, ask which remaining differences could explain the observed contrast without invoking the proposed cause.

Snow did not need to prove that all cholera transmission occurred in precisely one way to support a powerful public-health intervention. Nor did he need to claim that the famous pump handle ended an epidemic already in decline. His contribution lay in building an account strong enough to guide action despite incomplete observation.

## Why a better story can still be wrong

There is a temptation to treat a coherent explanation as evidence for itself. A company launches a new product and revenue rises. A politician gives a speech and public opinion changes. A school introduces an app and test scores improve. We can readily invent a sequence of intentions and mechanisms connecting each action to the outcome. Humans are adept at making stories fit the observations we already have.

A causal hypothesis becomes more credible when it predicts evidence that was not used to construct it, especially evidence that would differ under competing explanations. Snow's distant water users, brewery workers, and intermingled water-company customers were useful precisely because they tested different consequences of his proposed mechanism.

But the distinction between a plausible mechanism and a proven causal effect is still important. We may understand how a drug could work in the body and discover that, in practice, it does not improve outcomes. People can compensate for an intervention in ways laboratory measurements miss. Conversely, an intervention can reliably help before its complete mechanism is understood.

A clinical trial can also succeed in one population while offering weak guidance for another. If a treatment is tested among young adults in a hospital, the measured average effect may differ among older patients living independently. The causal question must specify whose outcome, under which version of the treatment, over what period, and compared with what alternative.

This precision can initially feel fussy. But suppose a study reports that exercise improves sleep. Does that mean vigorous exercise immediately before bedtime, walking outdoors at lunch, joining a sports team, or being randomly encouraged to exercise more? Each version changes other things too: sunlight, social contact, stress, time available for work. Different interventions can have different effects even if we place them under the same broad label.

The question 'does exercise cause better sleep?' has several possible answers because it contains several possible interventions. Good causal reasoning begins by deciding exactly which intervention and outcome we are talking about.

## Two maternity wards and an unwelcome hypothesis

A few years before Snow's Soho investigation, another physician confronted a related problem in Vienna. Ignaz Semmelweis worked in the city's large general hospital, where two maternity clinics had strikingly different rates of death from childbed fever. One clinic trained physicians and medical students. The other trained midwives. The difference alarmed women seeking care, and it troubled Semmelweis.

He was born in eighteen eighteen in Buda, in what is now Hungary. His father hoped he would pursue law. Semmelweis began legal studies but shifted to medicine and found himself working in obstetrics rather than the medical specialty he had originally hoped for. His professional position gave him access to records and patients, but also placed him inside an institution with established teaching practices and a hierarchy of authority.

The two wards were a comparison waiting to be investigated, but the mere difference in mortality did not reveal its cause. Perhaps the patients differed. Perhaps the wards differed in ventilation or procedures. Semmelweis examined possible explanations, including changes in practice that seemed too small to account for the enormous gap.

In eighteen forty-seven his colleague Jakob Kolletschka died after a wound sustained during an autopsy. Semmelweis saw similarities between the pathological findings and those in women who died of childbed fever. Medical students and doctors moved between autopsies and examinations of women in labor. Midwives did not follow the same pattern of dissection work. Semmelweis proposed that material carried on the hands of examiners was causing some of the deaths.

He introduced hand cleansing with chlorinated lime before examining patients. Mortality in the physicians' clinic fell dramatically. A historical analysis of the hospital records reports that in eighteen forty-eight the rate in that clinic was about thirteen deaths per thousand births, close to the rate in the midwives' clinic. This was a change worth taking seriously, with lives attached to every entry in the table.

But historical interpretation needs the whole situation. Handwashing was not assigned to randomly selected women while an otherwise identical group remained untreated. Other practices might change over time. The decline was nevertheless large, repeated in relevant records, and consistent with a specific transmission mechanism. It provided powerful practical evidence for hygiene even without a complete theory of microbes.

Semmelweis met resistance, and his own responses to criticism grew increasingly combative. Historical accounts have sometimes turned that history into a fable of one pure genius confronting a uniformly foolish medical establishment. The record is more complicated. Some clinicians supported his practice; others disputed his explanation or its implications. His delayed and polemical publications, the state of pathology, institutional politics, and his position as a Hungarian physician in Vienna all shaped how the idea was received. The women whose deaths were recorded had little say in the dispute.

The human stakes of causal inference are plain here. An incorrect theory can preserve a lethal practice. A correct intervention can save lives before an adequate theory is accepted. But conviction, even conviction joined to a morally urgent cause, cannot substitute for checking competing explanations.

## Deliberately creating a comparison

If ordinary treatment decisions tend to give the sickest patients the most intensive care, observational data will compare groups that are different from the start. One way to improve the comparison is to assign an intervention by a random process rather than by the patient's prognosis, the doctor's preference, or the patient's means.

Imagine two hundred eligible patients with a condition for which researchers do not yet know whether a new treatment works. They agree to participate. A random procedure assigns one hundred to receive the new treatment and one hundred to receive an appropriate comparison, such as existing standard care. Before treatment, the groups may differ by chance, but the allocation does not systematically favor one group on the basis of severity, income, or the clinician's expectations.

Suppose sixty-five patients recover in the treatment group and fifty-five in the comparison group. The observed difference is ten percentage points. Under appropriate study conditions, this is an estimate of the average effect of assignment to that intervention in the eligible study population. With only two hundred patients, chance variation can be substantial. We would need an uncertainty interval and a prespecified outcome to judge how precisely the effect was estimated. Ten percentage points is the estimate, not a guarantee about every individual.

Randomization has a practical precondition: the person enrolling patients should not be able to predict and manipulate the next assignment. If researchers choose which patients to include after seeing the assignment, they can reintroduce imbalance. Concealing the allocation until eligibility is established protects the comparison. Blinding patients and outcome assessors, when possible and ethical, can reduce additional biases, especially for subjective outcomes.

In Britain in the nineteen forties, the Medical Research Council faced these questions while studying streptomycin for pulmonary tuberculosis. The antibiotic was new and scarce. Demand exceeded supply. Investigators designed a comparison of streptomycin plus bed rest against bed rest alone, with treatment assignments determined using random-number schedules and sealed, numbered envelopes held centrally. The first patients entered the study in nineteen forty-seven; results were published in nineteen forty-eight.

The scarcity of the drug was part of the ethical and administrative situation. Random allocation helped prevent doctors from deciding which among eligible patients would receive a limited medicine. The trial is rightly remembered as a landmark in clinical experimentation, though it was part of a longer history of controlled trials, and it was neither the first human experiment nor a model for every later ethical requirement. Its patients lived with a dangerous disease while researchers confronted uncertainty and limited resources.

A randomized trial estimates a causal effect under several conditions. Treatment should be implemented as specified. Participants' outcomes should be measured comparably. Loss to follow-up should not selectively remove people in a way that destroys the comparison. The interpretation also changes if people assigned to one option switch to another. The effect of being assigned treatment can differ from the effect of actually taking it.

Sometimes randomization is impossible or unethical. We cannot randomly assign people to live in polluted water for a decade merely to estimate its effects. We cannot reverse a childhood or rerun a war. In those settings, we seek natural experiments, policy changes, discontinuities in eligibility, or longitudinal comparisons. Each design has assumptions that need to be argued from how the world works, not just from a statistical formula.

## The language of two possible outcomes

We can now state the counterfactual idea more formally without letting symbols obscure it. For each person, imagine two possible outcome values. The first is the outcome under a specified treatment. The second is the outcome under a specified alternative. The individual's causal effect is the first value minus the second.

In symbols on a page, researchers often call these Y of one and Y of zero. In ordinary language, they are 'the result with treatment' and 'the result without treatment.' We observe one under the actual treatment received. The missing value is the other possible outcome.

Average causal effects are averages of those individual differences across a defined population. Random assignment makes the average observed outcome in each treatment group an informative estimate of the corresponding average potential outcome. With enough data, the difference between group means can approximate the average causal effect. The approximation has sampling uncertainty, and its interpretation depends on the intervention and population.

This language clarifies why a treatment can help on average without helping everybody. Some people may benefit greatly, some a little, and some may be harmed. If an average is positive, it does not tell us which individual will benefit. It also clarifies why a result can change when the population changes: an average is partly a description of the people over whom we average.

The framework assumes that the treatment has a sufficiently clear meaning and that one person's assigned treatment does not change another person's outcome in ways our design ignores. In infectious disease, that second condition can fail. Vaccinating one person may reduce transmission to others. A water-supply intervention can change risk across a neighborhood. The effects spill over the boundaries of individual assignments. Researchers must then define causal effects at the level of groups, networks, or communities, or explicitly model the spillovers.

A formal definition does not make these complications disappear. It tells us exactly where the conceptual work is needed.

## What to adjust for, and what to leave alone

To understand why statistical adjustment sometimes helps and sometimes harms, picture three simple diagrams expressed in words.

In the first, illness severity influences both whether a patient receives treatment and whether the patient recovers. Severity is a common cause, so comparing treated and untreated patients of similar severity is a sensible step. This was the problem behind our invented treatment table.

In the second, treatment influences blood pressure, and blood pressure influences stroke risk. If we want the total effect of treatment on stroke, adjusting away the change in blood pressure would remove part of the very effect we are trying to measure. Blood pressure is on the causal pathway. We may choose to study direct and mediated effects separately, but they are different questions.

In the third, suppose two independent causes each make hospital admission more likely. One is having a particular disease; the other is having a serious injury. Among all people, the disease and the injury might be independent. But among people admitted to the hospital, learning that someone was admitted because of an injury can make the disease less likely as the explanation for admission. Restricting analysis to admitted patients can create a misleading relationship between the two causes. Admission is a common effect, and selecting on it changes the comparison.

These diagrams are compact models of assumptions about the world. Sometimes our assumptions are uncertain, and different plausible diagrams imply different analyses. We can investigate them with subject-matter knowledge, temporal ordering, experiments, sensitivity analyses, and new measurements. We should not adjust automatically for every variable in a dataset merely because software makes it easy.

The hardest causal questions often turn on details that do not look mathematical: how a hospital assigns patients, how a landlord pays a water bill, why someone chooses a school, when a policy was announced, whether the measured outcome is the one people actually care about. Those details determine whether the numbers answer the intended question.

## How confident should we be?

In nineteen sixty-five, the British statistician and epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill reflected on how researchers move from observed associations to decisions about causes. He discussed considerations such as the strength and consistency of an association, whether exposure preceded the outcome, the plausibility of a mechanism, and experimental evidence. These were aids to judgment, not a mechanical checklist that could turn weak data into proof.

Time order is necessary for the usual claim that an intervention caused a later outcome. A policy implemented in June cannot explain an improvement that began in March, although it may influence what happened after June. A strong association can be compelling, but a strong association is still vulnerable to confounding. Replication across different settings is valuable, especially when the studies have different weaknesses. A plausible mechanism guides investigation, while an unknown mechanism does not automatically invalidate a repeated intervention effect.

Hill's discussion emerged from decades of work on disease, smoking, and public health. The practical problem was that waiting for certainty could impose real costs while acting on a false causal claim could also do harm. Researchers and decision makers had to judge how strong the evidence was, what the consequences of error might be, and what further tests were possible.

That is an important separation. Whether the evidence supports a causal relationship is one question. Whether to act is another. A water company may be obliged to improve a suspected contaminated supply before scientists identify every organism and pathway. A new drug with serious side effects may require stronger evidence of benefit before widespread use. The threshold for action depends partly on risks, reversibility, available alternatives, and who bears the cost of mistakes.

## A small experiment from ordinary life

Consider a student who believes that listening to music while studying improves memory. She notices that her best exam grades occurred during weeks when she studied with music. But those may also have been weeks when she slept better, faced easier material, or studied longer. The observation is a starting point for an experiment, not a conclusion.

She could define a precise question: for her, does listening to a particular kind of instrumental music during a forty-minute study session improve recall on a comparable test the next day? She could prepare a series of similar learning tasks and randomly assign each session to music or no music. She could record sleep, task difficulty, and time spent. She might use several weeks of sessions so that one good or bad day does not dominate the result.

Even then, the design has limitations. She cannot easily blind herself to whether music is playing. Tasks may differ in difficulty. Learning from one session can carry into another. Her expectation that music helps may change how much effort she puts into each task. These are specific threats, not reasons to abandon investigation.

After collecting results, she should look for the size and consistency of the difference, not just whether one group had a higher average. Perhaps music helps with routine practice but impairs reading difficult prose. That is a more useful answer than the universal claim that music is good or bad for studying. The effect has conditions.

This small experiment also illustrates why scientific humility is compatible with meaningful action. She does not need a universal theory of attention to change her own study habits. She needs a comparison sufficiently credible for the decision she is making, while remaining willing to revise it when new observations contradict it.

## Back at Broad Street

John Snow died in eighteen fifty-eight, only four years after the Soho outbreak. The fuller bacteriological story of cholera would develop after his death. Today the Broad Street pump is a public symbol of epidemiology. If we remember only the handle, however, we remember the least demanding part of the reasoning.

The demanding part happened in homes, streets, registries, water-company records, and chemical measurements. Snow had to discover what exposure people actually experienced. He had to find comparison groups and examine exceptions. He had to resist the temptation to treat the sequence of a famous intervention and a declining epidemic as sufficient evidence of cause. The London investigation remains instructive because its strengths and limitations are both visible in the surviving work.

Semmelweis likewise used patterns across maternity wards and a change in medical practice to infer that something physicians carried on their hands was killing patients. His story shows how professional authority and institutional habits can complicate evidence, even when the consequences are urgent. Later randomized trials developed new ways to create comparisons, but they did not eliminate the need for careful judgment about what is being compared and why.

When someone tells you that an action caused an outcome, you can ask a series of questions. What exactly changed, and for whom? What would have happened without that change? Why is the proposed comparison credible? Could a common cause explain the association? Did the exposure come before the outcome? Are we comparing outcomes over the same time period? Does the claim survive a different source of evidence? Would it still be true in another population or under another version of the intervention?

Those questions do not guarantee that we will discover the truth. They make our uncertainty more informative. They also prevent an appealing sequence of events from becoming a causal story before the evidence has earned it.

The pump handle is easy to picture. The missing counterfactual is harder. Learning to ask about it changes how we read a scientific result, a historical argument, a headline, or an account of our own successes and failures. We observe what happened. Understanding why it happened begins by taking the unseen alternative seriously.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. The causal estimand

Let `T` be a well-defined binary intervention for a unit, and let `Y(1)` and
`Y(0)` denote its potential outcomes under treatment and control. The
individual contrast is `Y(1)-Y(0)`, and the population average treatment effect
(ATE) is

\[
  \operatorname{ATE}=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)].
\]

Consistency links observed outcomes to the potential outcome corresponding to
the intervention received; lack of interference is a further substantive
assumption in the simplest unit-level model. If treatment is randomized with
positive probability in both arms, the difference in observed arm means
estimates the ATE for the eligible study population (with sampling uncertainty).
Observational identification by standardization instead requires conditional
exchangeability and positivity for measured pre-treatment covariates `X`:

\[
  \mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
  = \sum_x \mathbb{E}[Y\mid T=t, X=x]\Pr(X=x),\quad t\in\{0,1\}.
\]

### 2. Synthetic Simpson reversal (not historical data)

| Severity | Treatment: recovered / total | Comparison: recovered / total |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Mild | 9/10 = 90% | 72/90 = 80% |
| Severe | 27/90 = 30% | 2/10 = 20% |
| **Overall** | **36/100 = 36%** | **74/100 = 74%** |

Equal-severity standardization yields `0.5*(0.9+0.3)=0.60` treated and
`0.5*(0.8+0.2)=0.50` untreated: `+0.10` recovery probability difference.
This is a pedagogical construction; even its conditional associations support
a causal effect only under adequate assumptions about comparability.

### 4. Causal graph sketches

- Confounding: `S -> T`, `S -> Y`, and possibly `T -> Y`, with severity `S` measured before treatment.
- Mediation: `T -> M -> Y`, where adjusting for mediator `M` changes the estimand away from total effect.
- Collider / selection: `D -> H <- I`, where disease `D` and injury `I` both affect hospital admission `H`; conditioning on `H` can associate `D` and `I`.

These are causal hypotheses to be evaluated from temporal and substantive
knowledge; statistical association does not determine the arrows.

### 5. Historical and source notes

- Snow's account distinguishes the removable handle from the already-declining
  outbreak. The September 8 intervention cannot by itself be credited with
  ending it. Paneth (2004) provides an explicit corrective reading; Snow's
  own account, pp. 51–52, records the decline and flight of residents.
- The brewer's report, outlying water consumers, the water-supplier inquiries,
  Whiting's assistance, and silver-nitrate test are in Snow (1855), pp. 40–53
  and 76–87. Snow's claims about what the water contained were formed before
  modern bacteriological understanding.
- Snow's biography, apprenticeship, temperance and work in anesthesia are
  covered by the York Civic Trust and the historical medical review listed
  below. Do not infer personality or motivation from dietary practices alone.
- The Semmelweis clinic histories and the effect of chlorinated lime are
  discussed in the Science History Institute biography and a retrospective
  analysis of Vienna mortality records. Historical figures vary by period and
  denominator; the spoken ~13 per 1,000 figure refers to 1848 in the first
  clinic, not a universal before/after reduction.
- The 1947–48 streptomycin trial and its enrollment and allocation mechanisms
  are described by Yoshioka's archival study. It was neither placebo-controlled
  nor double-blinded; its historical significance is not a claim to have
  invented every form of randomized experiment.
- The Simpson reversal uses invented counts. The Stanford Encyclopedia article
  discusses why a reversal alone does not tell us whether to condition on a
  variable; the causal interpretation requires a model of the data-generation
  process.
- The potential-outcomes formalization is a modern expository framework;
  it should not be attributed retroactively to Snow or Semmelweis.
