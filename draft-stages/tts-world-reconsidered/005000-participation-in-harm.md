# How Ordinary People Participate in Harm: Authority, Institutions, and Refusal

On March sixteenth, nineteen sixty-eight, an American helicopter crew flying reconnaissance over the village of My Lai in South Vietnam encountered a scene that made no sense as an ordinary battle. The crew saw civilians, including women and children, being killed by American soldiers. The pilot, Warrant Officer Hugh Thompson Junior, brought his aircraft down and confronted troops on the ground. With his crew members Glenn Andreotta and Lawrence Colburn, he helped protect and evacuate civilians. He reported what he had witnessed.

Thompson's actions were neither easy nor welcomed by everyone. He faced criticism and hostility for challenging his own side. The United States Army awarded him and his crew the Soldier's Medal for their actions in nineteen ninety-eight, thirty years later. The official accounts document an intervention amid a massacre in which hundreds of Vietnamese civilians were killed. They do not turn that event into a simple story in which one person rescued everyone or had the power to reverse the war.

The questions raised by My Lai are frightening. How can a group of people take part in acts they might once have condemned? What changes when the relevant decision comes from an authority, is divided among many participants, or appears to serve a collective mission? And how can someone refuse when people around them continue?

There are two easy answers, and neither is sufficient. One says that perpetrators must be a completely different kind of person from those who would never harm anyone. The other says that circumstances made their choices inevitable and therefore removed their responsibility. Human history and psychological research support a harder inquiry. Situations and institutions exert influence, but people interpret those circumstances, make decisions, and sometimes resist. Understanding the pressures can help prevent harm without excusing the people who cause it.

## When the group agrees on something false

Consider an experiment with an apparently trivial question: which of three straight lines is the same length as a reference line? If you are alone in a well-lit room, the answer is generally obvious. There is no difficult calculation, moral dilemma, or uncertain evidence. You simply look and choose.

In the nineteen fifties the social psychologist Solomon Asch arranged situations in which a participant answered such questions while seated with several other people who were actually cooperating with the experimenter. On selected trials, the others deliberately gave the same wrong answer before the participant spoke. The participant then had to decide whether to report what seemed plainly visible or join the unanimous group.

Some participants went along with the group on some trials. Others resisted. Across the classic study's critical trials, roughly a third of responses conformed to the erroneous majority, but that summary must not be mistaken for the behavior of every person on every question. The point was variability under social pressure in a task whose perceptual answer was clear.

Asch's research was situated in a world still reckoning with dictatorship, propaganda, and the social consequences of war. He was born in Warsaw in nineteen hundred seven and emigrated to the United States as a young person. His experiments were concerned with how social settings affect judgment, not with proving that people literally stop seeing the lengths of lines when a group speaks.

The participant may wonder whether the others know something they do not. They may fear appearing foolish. They may suspect a misunderstanding of instructions. They may decide that disagreeing is not worth the embarrassment. These are different mechanisms, and an observed wrong answer alone cannot identify the one responsible. A person can publicly conform while privately doubting the group.

One finding from Asch's variations was especially important: introducing another person who departed from the unanimous majority could sharply reduce conformity. The dissenting person did not always need to provide the correct answer. Breaking unanimity changed the social setting. It became possible to disagree without standing completely alone.

That observation matters far beyond simple line judgments. It suggests that a group's apparent certainty can be a product of its public response structure. If everyone waits for someone else to dissent first, private doubts can coexist with outward unanimity. A disagreement becomes more likely to surface when the environment makes it possible to express one without immediate isolation.

## A laboratory that tested the meaning of obedience

Stanley Milgram, a psychologist at Yale, began experiments on obedience in the early nineteen sixties. His work emerged in the aftermath of the Second World War and the Holocaust, when scholars and citizens were confronting the relation between authority, institutions, and mass violence. Milgram wanted to study a component of obedience experimentally: would a person continue a harmful-seeming task when an authority figure instructed them to do so?

Volunteers were recruited for what they believed was a study of learning and memory. A participant assigned the role of teacher was instructed to administer electrical shocks to another person, the learner, when the learner answered incorrectly. The learner was actually part of the experimental arrangement, and the shocks were simulated. The teacher did not receive an honest explanation of that fact at the beginning.

The apparatus made the task concrete. A control panel displayed increasingly high voltages, progressing toward a maximum of four hundred fifty. Labels suggested increasing danger. The experimenter, wearing the authority of the research setting, instructed the teacher to continue. The learner protested and eventually stopped providing answers. The participant faced a series of decisions about whether to keep pressing the switches.

In the condition reported in Milgram's nineteen sixty-three paper, forty men took part. Twenty-six continued to the highest voltage setting; fourteen stopped after the learner had protested. Some participants displayed visible distress. Milgram described trembling, sweating, nervous laughter, and signs of tension. The conduct mattered precisely because it was difficult for many of the people involved.

It is tempting to turn those numbers into a universal declaration: sixty-five percent of people will obey orders to inflict extreme pain. That is not what the experiment measured. The participants were recruited from particular communities, the laboratory used deception, the stated purpose was scientific research, the victim was not actually shocked, and the researcher controlled an elaborate social situation. Milgram varied the conditions in further studies, and behavior varied with them.

There is still a real finding. Under some circumstances, an instruction from an apparently legitimate authority could lead participants to continue a task that they found disturbing. The experiment made that phenomenon visible and measurable. But the interpretation requires attention to exactly what participants believed they were doing, how they understood the experimenter's role, and why some refused.

## What did the participants believe?

Researchers have returned to Milgram's archived materials and questioned the simple explanation that people obeyed mechanically against their judgment. Participants did not all interpret the procedure in the same way. Some apparently doubted the reality of the shocks. Others understood the experiment as a serious scientific project and may have believed the researchers would not permit lasting injury. Many protested, bargained, or sought reassurance even when they ultimately complied.

An archival analysis published in twenty nineteen by Gina Perry and colleagues examined an earlier unpublished test related to participants' beliefs. Across many of Milgram's experimental variations, people who fully believed that the learner was receiving painful shocks tended to stop at lower levels than those who were less convinced. The data have methodological limitations, but they undermine an account in which the participant's understanding of the situation is irrelevant.

Stephen Reicher and Alex Haslam, among other scholars, have argued for an interpretation involving identification with an authority's apparent cause rather than blind obedience alone. Participants may become willing to act when they believe they are contributing to a valued project. That possibility is important because institutions rarely present harmful actions as purposeless cruelty. They often describe them as necessary for education, security, efficiency, or a larger mission.

These interpretations are debated. They are also compatible with the observation that authority influences behavior: the dispute concerns why and under which conditions. It would be misleading to present a single explanatory theory as established by the famous percentage alone.

There is an ethical issue in the research itself. Participants experienced a deception that sometimes caused distress, and later scholars questioned whether the procedures and debriefing met appropriate standards. Psychological experiments involving simulated harm are different from real violence, yet the dignity and welfare of research participants remain serious considerations. The experiment's ethical controversy is part of its history, not a footnote that can be ignored because the results were influential.

Most importantly, a laboratory measure of compliance does not furnish a complete explanation of a massacre, genocide, or sustained program of state persecution. Those events involve ideology, institutions, racism, military organization, threats, opportunities, historical choices, and victims with lives outside the perpetrators' decisions. A psychological mechanism may help explain one part of the process. Reducing historical violence to a single experimental percentage would flatten its causes and responsibilities.

## How responsibility becomes difficult to see

Albert Bandura developed an account of moral disengagement: ways people can temporarily avoid applying their own moral standards to actions that injure others. In a nineteen ninety-nine review, he described mechanisms such as making harmful conduct appear morally justified, using euphemistic language, comparing it with something worse, displacing responsibility onto authority, diffusing responsibility across a group, minimizing consequences, and blaming or dehumanizing victims.

These mechanisms are understandable as distinct arguments, and that is what makes them worth examining. 'I was carrying out the policy' appeals to authority. 'My role was only to process the paperwork' may limit the speaker's attention to one segment of a larger operation. 'It could have been worse' supplies a comparison that does not establish whether the action was acceptable. 'They brought it on themselves' relocates responsibility from the actor to the person harmed.

Not every use of these phrases is dishonest. A subordinate may genuinely lack control over a decision. A small task may have only a slight causal role in a large outcome. The question is whether the explanation accurately represents the speaker's knowledge and available choices, and whether it hides harms they have reason to consider.

An organization can make wrongdoing easier when its structure separates decision, execution, accounting, and response to complaints. No single employee needs to see the entire result. One person approves a rule, another enters data, another sends a notice, and another handles the consequences. Division of labor can be necessary and beneficial in complicated societies. It also creates opportunities for each participant to regard the harm as someone else's problem.

The institutional remedy is not simply asking every employee to feel more empathy. Organizations can make consequences visible, establish obligations to report hazards, protect dissenters, and assign real authority to investigate complaints. They can also impose effective external oversight where internal incentives prevent honest self-scrutiny.

To understand why such safeguards are needed, consider an actual institutional case whose history cannot be reduced to one intimidating supervisor or one moment of conformity.

## A rule can make responsibility harder to recognize

There is a difference between having authority to decide and having responsibility to notice. A nurse may lack power to rewrite a hospital's policy but can recognize that a patient is being harmed by it. A technician may not control a launch schedule but can document a measurement that contradicts an assumption about safety. A civil servant may be bound by procedures while also knowing that those procedures impose injury on people the institution is supposed to serve.

The moral problem does not disappear because authority is distributed. Yet it would be unfair to pretend that a junior employee can always correct a powerful institution simply by choosing to be brave. Career risks, access to evidence, professional obligations, and legal protections change what actions are feasible. An adequate account must consider both the person's choices and the constraints on those choices.

We can ask several concrete questions. What did the person actually know at the time? What further evidence could they reasonably obtain? Who could hear an objection, and how likely was that channel to work? What risks followed from acting, and who else could help share those risks? Did the institution give participants a way to question a decision without losing their livelihood? These questions help locate responsibility more carefully than a story about villains versus heroes.

They also suggest what a better institution needs. A complaint should not have to pass only through the supervisor whose decision it challenges. A safety warning should preserve the measurements and the argument that produced it, so that a later reviewer can examine the original reasoning. A professional should be able to raise a concern in an environment where retaliation is constrained. These are mechanisms for exposing errors and resisting harm before a single person is forced to make an extraordinary sacrifice.

## The study that continued after treatment existed

In nineteen thirty-two the United States Public Health Service, working with Tuskegee Institute, began a study of syphilis among Black men in Alabama. Researchers enrolled hundreds of men, some with syphilis and others without it, in a project intended to follow the disease over time. The participants did not provide informed consent of the kind the study required. They were told they were receiving care for 'bad blood,' a broad local expression covering several ailments, and offered examinations and other benefits.

The men were not deliberately infected as part of the study. That distinction is historically important. The wrongdoing lay in deception, the failure to obtain informed consent, and the withholding of effective treatment. Even after penicillin became an accepted and available treatment for syphilis, the men in the diseased group were not offered it. The project continued for decades, ending in nineteen seventy-two after public reporting and review.

The story is sometimes told as though one corrupt doctor conducted a secret experiment while the rest of the medical system remained unaware. The actual record describes an institutional program that continued through changes in personnel, public-health administration, and medical knowledge. People performed specific tasks and made decisions within a larger structure. Systemic racism and unequal power shaped whose rights were disregarded and whose health was treated as an acceptable cost.

There were people who challenged the study. A Public Health Service employee, Peter Buxtun, raised objections in the late nineteen sixties. After his concerns did not end the program, journalists reported the case in nineteen seventy-two, and an advisory panel recommended that it stop. The eventual public exposure does not erase the years of harm, but it shows that professional dissent and external scrutiny were available paths through which a harmful institution could be challenged.

It also shows why a research project can acquire a momentum that is morally dangerous. Participants' welfare may become subordinated to preserving a dataset, completing an institutional plan, or maintaining the authority of an existing program. A procedure can continue because it is established, even when the reasons that once supported it have ceased to be credible. Ethical review must be capable of interrupting ongoing work, not merely approving its initial design.

The consequences of the Tuskegee study extended to participants, families, public trust, and research ethics. Those involved were people whose lives were shaped by illness, families, work, and community, not merely entries in a mortality table. An intellectually serious account must retain that human scale while also analyzing the institutional mechanisms that allowed the abuse to persist.

## When engineers warn that an organization is taking a risk

A different kind of institutional failure was examined by the presidential commission investigating the loss of the space shuttle Challenger on January twenty-eighth, nineteen eighty-six. Seven crew members died shortly after launch when the shuttle broke apart. The technical failure began in a joint of the right solid rocket booster, where sealing performance was compromised in the severe cold. The commission also investigated why the launch went ahead under those conditions.

The night before, engineers at the contractor Morton Thiokol expressed concern about the behavior of rubber O-ring seals at low temperatures and initially recommended against launching below a temperature for which they lacked adequate supporting evidence. The contractor's management reversed that recommendation after discussion with NASA personnel. The commission found that senior decision-makers had not been fully informed of the earlier written no-launch recommendation or the extent of engineers' continued opposition. Critical technical knowledge failed to travel through the decision structure in a form that could guide the people who approved the launch.

The commission described a conflict between engineering data and management judgments. It also documented a longer history of damage to seals and partial leakage during earlier flights. Some had begun to treat signs of degradation as an acceptable operational risk because previous launches had survived them. But a successful previous flight does not prove that the same defect is safe in more severe conditions. If a component has survived repeated tests under uncertain stress, the absence of catastrophe is evidence to examine, not a guarantee.

One failure of reasoning deserves attention. The burden of proof can be inverted. Instead of requiring evidence that a system is safe enough under the conditions planned, managers may demand that engineers prove it will fail. But an engineer can have compelling evidence that a hazard is insufficiently understood without possessing a calculation that predicts the exact moment of failure. A decision rule that requires certainty of disaster before acting on a warning makes precaution almost impossible.

We need to distinguish this case from deliberate persecution or a program that knowingly withheld medical treatment. The historical harms, intentions, and responsibilities are different. What the comparison offers is an institutional mechanism: information becomes fragmented, warnings are diluted as they pass upward, and the system rewards keeping an operation moving more than revisiting a contested safety assumption. The problem is not explained by calling everybody in the organization malicious or everybody innocent.

An effective safety culture has to make it possible for technical objections to stop a process long enough for independent review. It must preserve the original warnings, identify the decision-makers who accepted a risk, and avoid rewarding people for turning uncertain danger into confident reassurance. The Challenger inquiry gives us a documented instance of how much those communication and authority structures can affect consequences.

## The people whose lives were treated as research material

The passage of time makes the Tuskegee study especially difficult to comprehend. Children grew up while men enrolled in the project aged; families dealt with illness and uncertainty; ordinary opportunities to seek treatment were shaped by deception. Researchers could describe decades of observation as the accumulation of scientific knowledge, but the men being observed did not thereby lose their standing as people entitled to truthful information and effective care.

Public acknowledgment arrived late. In May nineteen ninety-seven President Bill Clinton formally apologized on behalf of the United States government to survivors and families. Herman Shaw, one of the survivors, spoke at the White House ceremony. The apology took place twenty-five years after the study ended, and decades after effective antibiotic treatment had become available. It could not restore lives or opportunities lost. It did publicly recognize the government's responsibility and the people against whom the institution had acted.

An apology can be important while remaining incomplete. Future participants in medical research need informed consent, transparent oversight, trustworthy avenues for complaint, and a genuine ability to withdraw. Communities that have been mistreated may reasonably require evidence of changed practices before extending trust again. This is an institutional problem as well as a moral one: people must be able to verify that safeguards exist and are used.

A lecture about obedience can make the mistake of concentrating so much on perpetrators' psychology that victims become illustrations. In this case, the men and their families were the ones living with the consequences. Their experiences are necessary to understanding what the institutional choices meant. Analyses of authority and responsibility should remain accountable to the people whom those choices affected.

## A simple model of the temptation not to speak

Suppose a worker notices a safety problem in a factory. They estimate that reporting it has a decent chance of preventing a large loss, but speaking up might also threaten their job or relationships. We can represent the choice with a simple imagined calculation, though real decisions are far more complicated.

Imagine that the worker believes there is a two-percent chance of a severe failure that would cause a loss worth one hundred units. The expected harm is two units. Suppose reporting the concern carries a personal cost the worker values at three units. If the worker considers only the expected loss they themselves would suffer, or expects that someone else will report it, they may decide not to act. Yet the potential loss may fall mostly on many other people. The private calculation and collective consequences diverge.

The numbers are fictional. They are useful because they expose a structural problem. A participant may face concentrated personal costs for speaking and receive only a fraction of the benefit when harm is prevented. Even someone who believes the organization should change can have a reason to remain quiet if others bear most of the risk. This is a kind of collective-action problem, shaped by power and information.

The solution cannot be only a sermon about personal courage. Reduce the retaliation risk. Give people confidential, accessible reporting channels. Require supervisors to document and respond to credible warnings. Ensure that evidence can reach independent reviewers. Make the cost of ignoring a serious report visible to the organization, rather than leaving all the costs with the person who raises it.

That model has a limit too. People often act from professional duty, solidarity, compassion, or moral conviction even when a calculation of personal advantage favors silence. Institutions should not presume that everyone is selfish. They should make ethically responsible action feasible for people who are trying to do the right thing.

## A lesson from a story told incorrectly

Social psychology itself offers an example of how an appealing moral explanation can outrun historical evidence. After Kitty Genovese was murdered in New York in nineteen sixty-four, a newspaper account popularized the story of thirty-eight witnesses who supposedly watched the attack and did nothing. For decades that version appeared in discussions of bystander behavior.

Historical reassessments found that the central claims about thirty-eight fully informed, passive eyewitnesses were not supported by the available record. Some people heard or saw fragments; there were interventions and attempts to summon help; the geometry, timing, and reporting were more complicated than the famous account suggested. Kitty Genovese's life and the circumstances of her murder deserved better than a simplified parable about the indifference of strangers.

Psychologists have studied bystander behavior in independent experiments, and the presence of others can influence whether and how people offer assistance. The correction of the Genovese story does not erase that research. It does remind us to distinguish an actual event from a dramatic account later used to motivate a theory.

This is particularly important in a lecture about how ordinary people participate in harm. A story can become compelling because it seems to illustrate the theory we already favor. If we continue telling it after its major details have been challenged, we are demonstrating another version of the very process we are trying to understand: a group accepting a familiar narrative more readily than it checks the evidence behind it.

## The person who refused

Return now to Hugh Thompson, Glenn Andreotta, and Lawrence Colburn above My Lai. Their intervention did not arise because the events around them were morally ambiguous. They saw unarmed civilians being killed and acted against members of their own military. Thompson landed his helicopter between troops and civilians, confronted soldiers, and helped arrange evacuations. His crew also rescued a wounded child from a ditch.

Thompson reported the violence, but his actions did not guarantee immediate vindication. Later official recognition came after decades. In the intervening years he faced hostility for challenging the conduct of his fellow soldiers. Standing up can be costly, and the existence of a possible act of resistance does not make all resistance equally safe or accessible.

His case prevents the social-harm lecture from ending at a fatalistic conclusion. Authority does not always command the same allegiance. Group pressures do not remove every alternative. A person may recognize a competing duty: to protect civilians, obey the law of war, preserve professional integrity, or tell the truth about what occurred. Those commitments can provide a basis for refusing an instruction or intervening against a harmful practice.

We should avoid making individual heroism the only safeguard. A system that protects vulnerable people only when an exceptional individual risks a career or life is a poorly designed system. Courage matters. So do accountability, independent records, channels for protest, competent oversight, and protections against retaliation. Institutions that make ethical action easier reduce the number of occasions when someone must choose between doing their duty and keeping themselves safe.

## The difference a witness can make

Hugh Thompson's actions at My Lai are remembered because he did something in direct conflict with what armed members of his own military unit were doing. But consider the narrower actions that made his intervention possible. He and his crew observed conditions on the ground, communicated what they saw, put themselves between threatened civilians and soldiers, and made an immediate decision to protect people. Later reporting and investigation required other witnesses, journalists, and institutions to act.

There is an important distinction between being the first to disagree and being the only person who can make a difference. An employee who documents a danger may not control the final decision, but a dated record can become evidence for an independent investigation. A colleague who supports someone making a complaint may not settle the case, but can change the cost of speaking. A supervisor who requests that technical concerns be attached to a decision document may be doing something less dramatic than stopping an operation, yet that action can prevent warnings from disappearing.

None of these steps guarantees success. Some people who resist are punished; some reports are ignored; some institutions fail even after repeated alarms. An account that assures every conscientious dissenter that courage will be rewarded would be false to history. At the same time, people and organizations can change the conditions of action through reporting protections, independent review, collective representation, and clear duties to consider evidence.

We can now return to Asch's group of people judging lines. The addition of a dissenter changed how participants behaved, even when that dissenter did not solve the perceptual task for them. It did not abolish social influence, but it altered the environment in which a person had to speak. That is a modest experimental insight with a serious institutional implication: make room for disagreement before everyone appears to agree.

## Responsibility without a comforting myth

Asch's line judgments show that public agreement can be shaped by the presence of a unanimous group. Milgram's experiments show that an authority's instructions can lead some people to continue a troubling task, while later archival scholarship complicates any simple account of blind obedience. Bandura's analysis helps name the arguments by which responsibility and harm can be obscured. The Tuskegee study shows how these problems can be embedded in an institution over decades. My Lai shows both the destructive consequences of organized violence and the possibility of refusal from within a group.

The lesson is not that everyone would commit an atrocity under the right circumstances. Experiments do not establish such a universal claim, and history contains both perpetrators and resisters acting under grave constraints. Nor is the lesson that structural pressure absolves individuals. A causal explanation for how a harmful action becomes possible can inform judgments about responsibility; it does not replace them.

Ask instead: what do participants believe they are helping accomplish? Who can see the consequences? What happens when someone objects? Are instructions recorded and reviewable? Can an institution revise a dangerous practice when new evidence appears? Who bears the cost of refusing, and who bears the cost of continuing?

Those questions can be applied before a catastrophe, while procedures are still being designed and warnings are still being offered. They are more demanding than declaring other people monstrous or ourselves immune. They suggest that responsible behavior depends both on personal moral judgment and on social arrangements that allow people to exercise it. The possibility of harm belongs to our institutions; so does the possibility of building them to make refusal, correction, and care more effective.

## Mathematical, methodological, and historical companion (display only)

### 1. The boundaries of the Milgram statistic

Milgram (1963) reported a **specific laboratory condition** with 40 recruited
male volunteers and a simulated shock apparatus. In that condition 26/40
continued to the highest switch, while 14/40 discontinued after the learner
had protested. `26/40 = 0.65` is a within-condition fraction, **not** a
population probability that 'ordinary people' commit real-world violence.
Other experimental variations changed observed behavior. Deception and
participant beliefs complicate interpretation.

### 2. Four analytically distinct mechanisms

- **Public conformity:** reporting the group's answer despite personal doubt.
- **Authority influence:** behavior affected by the apparent status and
  instructions of an experimenter or institution.
- **Moral disengagement:** reframing responsibility, consequences, or victims
  to reduce self-sanction against harmful acts.
- **Institutional persistence:** procedures and divisions of labor preserve
  actions even when individual personnel change.

These are mechanisms to investigate, not diagnoses that can be assigned from
one observed action or one numerical percentage.

### 3. A fictional safety-reporting calculation

For illustrative units of loss, `P(incident)=0.02`, `severity=100`, and
personal reporting cost `3`, the expected collective harm is `0.02*100=2`.
No normative decision follows from that arithmetic alone. The payoffs to
other people, the decision-maker's duty, uncertainty, severity distribution,
report effectiveness, and institutional retaliation policies must be
specified. This example is **not historical Tuskegee, Asch, or Milgram data**.

### 4. Specific historical corrections

- **Tuskegee:** participants were **not intentionally infected** as part of
  the study. The central violations were deception, absent informed consent,
  and withholding treatment even after penicillin became available.
- **Kitty Genovese:** the traditional '38 people watched the entire murder and
  did nothing' account is contradicted by historical reassessments; it should
  not be used as an uncontested empirical instance of nonintervention.
- **My Lai:** the Army's account names Thompson and crew's rescue actions,
  their intervention against soldiers, subsequent hostility, and their 1998
  award. Laboratory obedience findings cannot be equated with a massacre.

### 5. Challenger launch decision: two distinct findings

The 1986 presidential commission distinguished the **physical accident**
from the **contributing management failure**. The seal joint of the right
solid rocket motor failed in conditions of extreme cold. The Commission's
chapter V found that decision-makers lacked full knowledge of the earlier
Thiokol recommendation against launch below 53°F and the continuing concern
of engineers following management's reversal. Chapter VI describes how
prior O-ring erosion and related anomalies were treated as acceptable risk.

This historical case illustrates decision-making with incomplete technical
communication and contested safety assumptions. It is **not** treated as
morally or causally equivalent to the Tuskegee study or the My Lai massacre.
The invented reporting-cost arithmetic is also a separate teaching example.

### 6. Interventions implied by the mechanism, not guaranteed remedies

Recording decisions, requiring independently reviewable evidence,
protecting truthful reporting, establishing proportionate response to
complaints, and assigning responsibility for outcomes are possible design
measures. Their benefits and costs must be examined empirically; none
prevents wrongdoing by itself.
