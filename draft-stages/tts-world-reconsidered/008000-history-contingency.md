# Why History Could Have Been Different: Chance, Choice, and the Roads Not Taken

Knowing how a story ends changes how we read its beginning. Once a war has occurred, its causes can seem to have been gathering toward it for decades. Once a technology becomes familiar, rival designs appear like quaint mistakes. Once a movement has won, its victory is often retold as the inevitable result of larger forces. But the people who lived through those earlier moments did not possess our knowledge of the ending. They argued, hesitated, miscalculated, revised plans, and sometimes made decisions with consequences they could barely imagine.

Consider Europe in the summer of nineteen fourteen. At the end of June, the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne and his wife were assassinated in Sarajevo. By early August, several major powers were at war. We can reconstruct the sequence in a schoolbook paragraph, and that compression makes the escalation appear automatic. But a paragraph erases meetings, telegrams, threats, alternatives, and delays. Each date held decisions that might have been handled differently.

Or consider a volcano in Indonesia. Its eruption in eighteen fifteen affected skies and harvests far away in eighteen sixteen. Farmers who had no knowledge of Tambora nevertheless faced shortages, migration, and changing prices. Nature entered their historical circumstances without asking whether the event fit the prevailing political narrative.

History combines structures that constrain action, choices made by identifiable people, and events they could not foresee. To understand why it could have been different, we need a disciplined kind of counterfactual thinking. That means asking what might have happened under a specified change, without simply inventing a story we prefer.

## From assassination to war

On June twenty-eighth, nineteen fourteen, Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie were killed in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip. Austria-Hungary's leaders faced a difficult and dangerous political situation. Some wanted to use the assassination to confront Serbia. Germany's leadership encouraged Austrian action, giving assurances of support in early July. The government in Vienna developed an ultimatum that Serbian authorities would find difficult to accept fully.

On July twenty-third Austria-Hungary presented its demands, allowing forty-eight hours for a response. Serbia accepted much of the ultimatum but did not fully yield on demands threatening its sovereignty. Diplomatic relations were broken. Britain proposed a conference of mediators. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July twenty-eighth. Russian mobilization, German decisions, French commitments, and the invasion of Belgium accelerated the transition to a wider war.

The chronology preserves choices because it preserves choices that disappear in the word inevitability. There was no physical law compelling an ultimatum to take precisely that form, an alliance promise to be interpreted in precisely that way, or mediation to be accepted or rejected at that moment. The leaders operated under fears and beliefs, and under military and political constraints. Some of those constraints were severe. Yet describing them does not turn a series of decisions into a chemical reaction.

Historians disagree about the relative responsibility of governments and leaders. Annika Mombauer's work on the July Crisis reconstructs evidence and continuing debate about how European war began. A serious counterfactual should respect that contested record. We cannot simply imagine a single wise minister saying a magic sentence and the entire continent becoming peaceful.

A more constrained question might be: What if Austria-Hungary had accepted mediation in the last week of July? We would need to consider the government's aims, Berlin's assurances, Russian responses, domestic pressures, and how likely other leaders were to accept the arrangement. We could not know the answer with certainty. But examining the question forces us to understand what was and was not yet settled.

## A chain is more than its first link

The assassination was a triggering event. The European crisis had deeper conditions: rival empires, nationalist movements, military planning, mutual suspicion, and alliances. A trigger has different consequences in a system already prepared to escalate than it would in a system of trusted mediation.

Imagine a forest after months without rain. A spark starts a fire. The spark is genuinely causal. So are the dry conditions, the wind, the available fuel, and failures of fire prevention. Had the spark landed on wet ground, the outcome might have been different. Had a different spark appeared the next day, the forest might still have burned. A single cause can be necessary in the actual sequence without being the only possible path to the outcome.

This distinction helps historians resist a false choice between individual responsibility and structural explanation. It is possible that particular leaders made consequential decisions and that the international system made those decisions more dangerous. Saying the war was not inevitable does not imply that avoiding it would have been easy. Saying the international environment made war likely does not make each act of aggression morally irrelevant.

When analyzing a causal chain, ask where the story branches. At which points did participants consider alternatives? What capacities did they possess? Which constraints were imposed by institutions, and which were assumed? Did some actors deliberately narrow the options available to others? These questions turn a vague claim about destiny into an investigation with documentary evidence.

The archival record will never contain every unspoken possibility. That is a limitation worth accepting. We can identify credible alternatives when contemporaries discussed them or when institutions possessed recognized procedures for acting differently. We should be more cautious about alternatives requiring a dozen people to change their personalities simultaneously.

## When the mountain intervened

In April eighteen fifteen, Mount Tambora on the island of Sumbawa erupted catastrophically. Its effects were first and most devastating for people nearby. But material injected high into the atmosphere also changed how sunlight reached the Earth's surface. In the following year, many regions experienced climatic disturbances associated with the eruption, including cold weather and harvest failure.

In parts of northeastern North America, eighteen sixteen became known as the Year Without a Summer. Late frosts and unexpected cold damaged crops. European communities also suffered weather and agricultural disruption. Conditions varied by region; it would be wrong to describe a uniformly cold planet or attribute every famine and disease outbreak in the period to a single volcano.

The global connection is what makes the event intellectually important. A farmer in New England did not need to know the geological cause to suffer the agricultural consequences. A family's decision to relocate might depend on a damaged harvest, prices, debt, and the existence of relatives elsewhere. The eruption was a distant physical event entering a social network of markets and migration.

Imagine two otherwise similar households in neighboring towns. One has savings and access to a reliable grain supplier. The other depends on one crop and must borrow for seed. The same late frost can create very different outcomes. It may cause temporary discomfort for the first and irreversible displacement for the second. A historian studying the year after Tambora must trace both the shock and the institutions that determine who can absorb it.

Now ask a counterfactual: What if Tambora had not erupted in eighteen fifteen? Some crop losses and price changes would likely have differed. But we cannot assume all political conflicts, migration decisions, and personal relationships would simply reverse. Many other causes continued operating. Counterfactual restraint helps us distinguish plausible consequences from an alternative novel.

## Four ways to mean cause

When we say something caused an event, we may mean different things. A trigger started a particular sequence. A background condition made that trigger effective. A mechanism explains the steps by which the cause produced its effect. And a difference-making cause is something whose absence would have changed the outcome under specified conditions.

Take a bridge collapse as an imaginary example. A heavy vehicle enters the bridge, which fails. The vehicle's arrival is a trigger. Corroded supports are a background condition. The mechanism involves forces exceeding the remaining structural capacity. Would removing the vehicle have prevented collapse that day? Perhaps. Would repairing the supports have prevented it? Perhaps as well. Several causes can jointly explain the same event.

In historical scholarship the difficulty is that we cannot rerun the past. There is no laboratory where the July Crisis can be repeated one thousand times under slightly varied settings. We can compare other diplomatic crises, investigate documents, test the plausibility of mechanisms, and make explicit which conclusions rest on uncertain assumptions. Counterfactual reasoning is necessary for causal explanation, but it must remain disciplined.

Notice that questions about responsibility add another layer. If an official knowingly ignores a bridge inspection, their responsibility does not disappear because the heavy vehicle was also causal. The causal map identifies how events happened. The moral and legal judgment evaluates decisions, knowledge, duties, and options available at the time.

## Path dependence: yesterday changes today's options

A second idea explains why history may be contingent yet difficult to reverse. Path dependence occurs when earlier events change the costs or benefits of later choices. A technology can become dominant because people adopt compatible equipment, train workers, and build institutions around it. Once enough users commit, switching to a technically superior alternative may require expensive coordination.

Imagine two mutually incompatible communication standards, called A and B. Both work adequately. At the beginning, fifty users can choose either. Ten early users happen to select A. Newcomers may then choose A because they can communicate with more people. That attracts still more users. Eventually A becomes dominant, even if B would have offered a slightly better technical feature. The early advantage can become self-reinforcing.

This is a teaching model, not proof that a particular historical keyboard layout, railway gauge, or software system was adopted by pure accident. Famous stories about technological lock-in are sometimes simplified beyond what the documentary record supports. A real investigation must compare performance, cost, regulation, competition, and user choice rather than assuming that the most popular design is obviously inferior.

The essential mechanism is clear, though. The value of an option may depend on how many others have already chosen it. A choice made yesterday changes what is reasonable today. Historical causes can therefore persist through infrastructures that outlast the original decision-makers.

Path dependence also helps explain institutional habits. An agency created to solve one problem acquires staff, budgets, expertise, and legal authority. When the original problem changes, the institution may continue performing familiar tasks because those are what its people and procedures were built to do. Reform can require coordination among actors who do not share an incentive to move first.

## A numerical picture of lock-in

We can make the mechanism concrete with invented numbers. Suppose each user receives five points of intrinsic benefit from adopting A and six from adopting B. B is intrinsically better by one point. But each user also gains one point for every one hundred nearby users already using the same standard.

In a network with three hundred existing A users and one hundred B users, a newcomer evaluates A at eight points and B at seven. The newcomer reasonably chooses A, despite B's higher standalone quality. The choice increases the installed base of A. Another newcomer may face an even stronger incentive to do the same.

These numbers illustrate network effects; they do not demonstrate that history always locks in inefficient technologies. If B's advantage rose sufficiently or if a coordinating institution supported switching, the pattern could change. A common standard also creates genuine benefits. Historical path dependence means decisions depend on prior states, not that every inherited institution is bad.

The thought experiment helps us ask a better question about progress. Why did this option win in the environment where people actually chose? It may have possessed a valuable feature. It may have been compatible with an existing network. It may have been imposed by law. Different mechanisms predict different consequences if we attempt a change.

## Why hindsight is such a persuasive storyteller

We know that the Titanic sank, that the First World War occurred, and that certain companies became dominant. When reading warnings that preceded those events, we may interpret them as obvious signals that everyone should have understood. But contemporary decision-makers faced many warnings that never became disasters. A historian has to reconstruct the information available at the time, including false alarms and possibilities that later vanished from view.

Consider an invented ship operator receiving ten warnings during a season. Nine turn out harmless; one precedes a failure. After the disaster, it is easy to point to the one warning and claim that its meaning was unmistakable. But a fair evaluation asks whether the warning differed reliably from the other nine, whether the operator could have acted, what the action would have cost, and what safety duty applied despite uncertainty.

Hindsight can distort our understanding of agency. If we describe a choice as obviously doomed, we may miss why reasonable participants thought it could succeed. If we describe the victorious side as destined to win, we may overlook its mistakes and narrow escapes. Conversely, an appeal to uncertainty can become an excuse for decisions that ignored clear contemporary evidence.

The remedy is not to suppress hindsight. We need to know what happened. The task is to use that knowledge while reconstructing the earlier decision problem faithfully. What did participants believe? What did they actually know? What alternatives were debated? Which uncertainties were acknowledged, and which were concealed?

## Why historical prediction is limited

Patterns in history are real. Epidemics often spread through networks. States sometimes face recurring problems of taxation, legitimacy, and succession. Technological systems frequently develop network effects. But a pattern is not an iron law. Historical cases differ in institutions, technologies, climate, cultural meanings, and relationships among actors.

When someone declares that history repeats itself, ask what exactly repeats. Is it a sequence of incentives? A form of propaganda? A credit cycle? A state responding to a border dispute? Or is the speaker merely noticing a superficial resemblance after the fact?

A useful historical analogy should identify a mechanism and its conditions. Suppose a present-day institution is described as another failing empire. Which relevant features are shared? Does it depend on extracting resources from distant territories? Does it suffer from a particular succession problem? Are its economic and technological constraints comparable? The analogy can illuminate only what the comparison actually supports.

Historical reasoning resembles causal science in its discipline, even when experiments are impossible. We need rival explanations, attention to selection, consistent standards of evidence, and humility about predictions. The historical record is both more constrained and more complicated than a parable with an obvious moral.

## People who never appear in the great decisions

A narrative centered on kings, diplomats, and inventors can make history seem like a chess game played by powerful individuals. But every decision changes lives beyond the room where it was made. Farmers after Tambora had to adapt without knowing the geological cause. Soldiers in nineteen fourteen received orders that embodied diplomatic choices they never made. Workers living under a technological standard bear costs and benefits inherited from earlier investments.

Their stories are essential to causal explanation. Policy consequences do not stop with the intention of the policymaker. To understand a war, we need the social arrangements that made mobilization possible and the human consequences that shaped later political life. To understand a famine, we need distribution systems, property rights, and the households most exposed to risk.

History becomes more accurate when it brings these people into the account. It also becomes harder to reduce to one person's genius or failure. A leader may make a decisive choice while being constrained by predecessors and dependent on thousands of others to carry it out.

We should also distinguish whose records survive. Governments leave documents. Merchants leave accounts. People who were poor, enslaved, displaced, or deliberately excluded may leave fewer written records. The archive itself is a product of historical institutions. Silence in the record does not mean an absence of experience, though responsible historians cannot fill every silence with imagined testimony.

## What a good counterfactual looks like

To evaluate a historical alternative, begin with one clear change. Specify when it occurs and which facts remain fixed. In July nineteen fourteen, imagine a particular government accepting a mediation proposal. Do not simultaneously change every empire's ambitions, alliances, and public opinion. Otherwise the question becomes too unconstrained to teach us anything.

Next, identify a mechanism. How would accepting mediation alter mobilization, diplomatic signals, and the perceived costs of escalation? Look for contemporary statements and established procedures. Ask whether others could have responded in more than one way.

Then compare the proposed history with the actual sequence. Are some downstream effects robust across plausible responses? Are others highly uncertain? One counterfactual may suggest that a specific week of fighting would have been avoided without showing that Europe would have remained peaceful for decades.

Finally, resist selecting alternatives solely because they produce a satisfying moral ending. A history of a narrow escape can show that success was contingent. It does not prove that a different decision would have yielded utopia. The purpose of the exercise is to understand causal leverage and limits of knowledge, not to write a consoling sequel.

## The difference between possibility and probability

Historians sometimes establish that an alternative was possible. That is a modest but meaningful claim. It does not establish that the alternative was likely. A government may have possessed legal authority to delay mobilization while its leaders regarded delay as politically unacceptable. The fact that a decision could have been different does not tell us how often it would have been different in similar conditions.

Suppose a diplomat had three realistic options: demand immediate concessions, seek mediation, or accept a limited compromise. We might reconstruct the arguments offered for each option. But assigning a precise probability to them would require a model of the actors' incentives, beliefs, and constraints. It is not enough to say that there were three choices and therefore each had a one-in-three chance. Options are not equally likely merely because we can list them.

This is a common error in counterfactual history. We imagine several outcomes, then treat our imagination as a probability distribution. A disciplined account separates what documents show, what a causal model suggests, and what remains speculative. Sometimes the most accurate conclusion is that a particular outcome was avoidable, while the likelihood and long-run consequences of avoiding it cannot be determined.

That distinction also protects against fatalism. If a catastrophic choice was possible but not inevitable, we can investigate what institutions narrowed the options. We need not claim that a peaceful outcome was probable to learn from missed opportunities for mediation and restraint.

## Who owns a historical accident?

The word accident can obscure responsibility. A storm is outside a mayor's control, but whether a city's emergency shelters have working generators is a policy choice. A volcanic eruption cannot be negotiated with, but trade systems and public relief can reduce the suffering associated with harvest failures. The unexpected event and the social response belong to different parts of the causal account.

Suppose two regions experience identical crop losses. One has public granaries, functioning credit, and transport routes that allow food imports. The other has restricted trade, debt burdens, and little public relief. Calling both outcomes natural disasters misses the institutions that explain why suffering differs. The physics of the shock is necessary to the story; it is not sufficient.

An analogous problem arises with technological accidents. After a system fails, investigators may identify a defective component. But why was the defect not detected? Did an inspection rule create incentives to ignore it? Was maintenance deferred because budgets rewarded short-term savings? Did someone warn of the hazard? The history of the accident includes organization and power, not only mechanics.

This is why narratives focused on a single remarkable event can be misleading. Tambora's eruption was a major physical shock. Its human history contains variations in exposure and social protection. The assassination in Sarajevo was a political shock. Its transformation into war depended on decisions and institutions. In both cases, contingency meets structure rather than replacing it.

## Archives, silences, and the danger of a neat story

We should ask where the evidence comes from. Diplomatic historians can read telegrams, official minutes, personal letters, and later memoirs. Each source has strengths and problems. A telegram sent during a crisis may reveal what an official wanted another government to believe. It may conceal the sender's private doubts. A memoir written decades later may contain vivid recollection but also self-justification and errors of memory.

A historian examining a decision should distinguish a document produced before the outcome from a later explanation written with hindsight. This is especially important when leaders seek to excuse themselves. The existence of an official plan does not guarantee it was the only plan considered; the absence of a written proposal does not prove nobody imagined it.

The people who preserved records also shaped the archive. A state's administrative apparatus may provide elaborate evidence about military orders and little about displaced families. Archaeology, oral history, economic data, and local records can sometimes recover perspectives that the central archive barely represents. Each method has its own sources of error.

A vivid historical narrative often depends on a select few scenes. Good storytelling makes those scenes comprehensible. Good historical practice also asks what alternative scenes have been omitted and whether they would change the explanation. If we tell a story entirely through the decisions of a ruler, we may make popular organization invisible. If we tell it entirely through impersonal structures, we may remove the very choices we set out to understand.

## Learn from the path, not a fable about destiny

Imagine an investigator claiming that a new institution is destined to collapse because an older institution once collapsed under vaguely similar pressures. A better investigation would name specific mechanisms: unequal taxation, breakdown of legitimacy, inability to maintain infrastructure, conflict over succession, or loss of trade routes. It would then ask whether those mechanisms are operating in the present case and what countervailing forces exist.

The logic is comparative, not prophetic. If a mechanism can be interrupted, history may offer insight into prevention. If important conditions differ, the old case may be a poor guide. A sober historical analogy can be genuinely useful without claiming that the future must reenact the past.

This makes contingency both intellectually demanding and practically valuable. The goal is to identify causes whose effects can be understood and, where possible, altered. It is harder than saying history repeats itself and more honest than saying every outcome was a matter of luck.

The same discipline applies to success stories. Imagine a scientific discovery made after one researcher happened to notice an odd measurement. The chance observation may have been important, but the discovery also required trained attention, instruments, colleagues, funding, and a community able to test the result. If that researcher had missed it, someone else might eventually have found the same phenomenon. We cannot infer either inevitability or uniqueness without examining the state of the field.

This separates the timing of an event from its broad possibility. A new technology may have been likely to emerge eventually, yet its precise form, the people receiving credit, and its social consequences may have depended on local choices. One invention can spread through multiple institutions; another may fail because it lacks a compatible market or political support. The historical question is richer than whether progress was inevitable.

Another useful practice is to list the evidence that would change your interpretation. If you believe a leader had no serious alternative, what documents or comparisons would establish otherwise? If you believe a policy could easily have been adopted, what contemporary constraints might undermine that confidence? A causal account becomes stronger when it states how it could be corrected. That is a standard historians share with other investigators, even when their sources and methods differ.

## The past was once an open future

The most important change in perspective may be this: the people we call historical figures lived forward. They did not know the titles future historians would give their lives. Their choices were constrained by geography, institutions, power, and the information available, but their outcomes were not always settled in advance.

The July Crisis shows how decisions, commitments, and misinterpretations can create a destructive cascade. Tambora shows how a distant physical event can reshape opportunities through agriculture and markets. Network standards show how small early advantages can accumulate into durable institutions. These are different mechanisms, and their differences matter.

The proper response to contingency is neither despair nor the fantasy that anything is possible. It is careful attention to where the real alternatives lie. We can ask which structures limit choice, which institutions magnify mistakes, and which decisions remain reversible. We can recognize responsibility without claiming omniscience.

Every present decision will one day be part of somebody's history. The fact that the past could have been different is a reason to examine our choices with greater care. The future has not yet become a finished story, and the causes that shape it are already at work.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Counterfactual structure

Given an outcome \(Y\) and intervention \(do(X=x)\), compare actual \(Y\) with hypothetical \(Y_x\) under an explicitly specified change. Historic cases generally cannot identify a unique counterfactual outcome; evidence constrains plausibility rather than providing experimental repetition.

### Illustrative network effects

Synthetic payoff \(U_A=5+n_A/100\), \(U_B=6+n_B/100\); for \(n_A=300\), \(n_B=100\), \(U_A=8\), \(U_B=7\). This demonstrates a possible switching incentive under given assumptions, not a historically estimated benefit.

### July Crisis chronology boundaries

28 June: assassinations in Sarajevo. 5–6 July: German assurances. 23 July: ultimatum. 25 July: Serbian reply. 28 July: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia. 30 July: Russian general mobilization ordered for next day. Early August: wider declarations and invasion. The chronology is grounded in Annika Mombauer's source work, not presented as a complete account of responsibility.
