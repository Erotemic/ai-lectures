# Why Cooperation Is Possible: Games, Trust, and the Institutions We Build

Two farmers depend on the same irrigation channel. If either draws too much water near the top, the people farther downstream receive too little. If nobody clears silt from the channel, eventually everyone loses. Each farmer understands the benefits of a well-maintained canal. Each also faces a temptation: take water while it is available, leave the cleanup to someone else, and hope that enough neighbors contribute to keep the system working.

Why doesn't cooperation collapse? Sometimes it does. Irrigation networks can be captured by powerful users, exhausted by overuse, or abandoned because nobody trusts anyone else to contribute. Yet other communities develop dependable schedules, labor obligations, monitoring, and ways of settling disputes. Their rules can persist for generations without a distant authority making every decision. How can those outcomes arise among people who have their own needs and interests?

The question is broader than farming. A group maintaining open-source software depends on people reviewing difficult changes and reporting problems. A neighborhood shares streets, drainage, and public space. Workers in an organization depend on colleagues not to conceal hazards. Countries negotiate over emissions and arms. Families divide chores. In each case, the result depends on the choices of more than one person, and the best choice for an individual can depend on what everyone else does.

We will begin with a simple mathematical game that seems to make cooperation impossible, then alter one of its assumptions. From there we will follow a political scientist who invited computers to play against each other, and an economist and political scientist who went to actual communities to study how people organized shared resources. The most important discovery is that cooperation is neither a guaranteed triumph of kindness nor an inevitable casualty of selfishness. Its prospects depend on the rules, information, relationships, and opportunities for correction within which people act.

## A game in which both lose by protecting themselves

Imagine two neighbors, Alice and Ben, who can each choose to cooperate on a shared task or refuse. The benefit to each depends on both decisions. We will use fictional scores rather than money. If both cooperate, each gets three points. If Alice cooperates while Ben refuses, Alice gets zero and Ben gets five. If Ben cooperates while Alice refuses, Ben gets zero and Alice gets five. If both refuse, each gets one.

Stop at Alice's decision. If Ben cooperates, Alice gains five by refusing, compared with three by cooperating. If Ben refuses, Alice gains one by refusing, compared with zero by cooperating. On these numbers, refusing gives Alice a higher score whichever choice Ben makes. Exactly the same reasoning holds for Ben.

So if both follow this individually advantageous rule, both refuse, and each receives one. Yet if both had cooperated, each would have received three. There is a gap between the individually tempting action and the outcome both would prefer. This is a prisoner's dilemma, named for a familiar story about two suspects who cannot communicate when offered incentives to confess.

Nothing in the arithmetic proves that people are fundamentally selfish. We chose the payoffs, and the payoffs encode a particular kind of situation. A person who values the neighbor's well-being can have a different utility from the fictional scores. Social consequences, shame, friendship, promises, and future dealings can also change which outcome is best for them. The game's force is conditional: **if** these are the payoffs and the choices are made only once, defection is the individually better option in every case.

This distinction is important because people often use a model to announce a conclusion that was built into its assumptions. 'The game proves humans cannot cooperate' would be such a mistake. What the game establishes is that good collective outcomes need not arise from individuals pursuing their isolated immediate interests under every arrangement of incentives.

## Changing one assumption changes the game

Real neighbors often meet again. If Alice refuses today, Ben may refuse tomorrow. If they cooperate today, they may maintain a working arrangement for years. The future gives present actions consequences that the one-round game omits.

Imagine that both neighbors can choose again and again. Suppose each future round matters almost as much as the current one. If they maintain cooperation, each can receive three points every round. If Alice defects once, she can get five immediately. But suppose Ben then refuses to cooperate in later rounds and Alice receives only one point from then on. Whether the initial five is worth the later losses depends on how much Alice values future payoffs.

We can make this precise with a simple idea called a discount factor. Imagine that a point earned one round from now counts as ninety percent of a point earned today. A point two rounds from now counts as eighty-one percent, because ninety percent of ninety percent is eighty-one. If the cooperative relationship continues indefinitely and tomorrow matters at ninety percent, the total value of three points every round is thirty points in present-value terms.

A one-time defection gives five points immediately. If it destroys the relationship and leaves one point in every later round, the total is fourteen points: five today plus nine from the discounted future stream. Under those assumptions, maintaining cooperation is much more valuable than taking the tempting immediate gain.

The numbers are not a prediction about every human relationship. They show why repeated contact can change incentives. If the next interaction is very unlikely, or if one party has little reason to care about it, the future may not discipline current behavior. If a betrayal cannot be detected, the threatened response has no practical force. If the punishment is too severe, an accidental mistake may wreck a relationship that both sides would have preferred to preserve.

A workable arrangement therefore requires more than repetition. People need some way to recognize one another, observe relevant actions, interpret mistakes, and decide how to respond. In the world outside our little game, these are often problems of information and governance as much as problems of goodwill.

## Cooperation and coordination are different problems

Not every social dilemma has the logic of the prisoner's dilemma. Imagine two neighboring communities that need to schedule a shared ferry crossing. Either an early crossing or a late one could work, but everyone gains most if they agree on the same timetable. If one village expects the early departure while the other expects the late one, both may lose access to markets and appointments. Here the central problem is coordination, not the temptation to exploit a helpful neighbor.

That distinction changes the remedies. In the irrigation game, each household may benefit from defecting even if it expects the other to cooperate. In the ferry example, a shared public schedule can solve much of the problem. Clear signals, standards, and reliable expectations allow people to choose the same successful arrangement. There may be several cooperative outcomes, and what is hard is choosing one that everyone can anticipate.

Real institutions often face both problems at once. Fishers may agree that certain boats should use certain harbor channels, a coordination issue, while still having incentives to take more fish than an agreed quota, a collective-action issue. A city may need a common system of road signs, yet also a credible system for enforcing speed limits. When we describe every dispute as a competition between selfish individuals, we risk recommending punishment for a problem that really needs shared information. When we describe every dispute as an innocent misunderstanding, we miss situations in which rule-breaking carries a genuine private benefit.

An institution therefore has to diagnose the game before choosing the rule. If people cannot coordinate, publish standards and make them predictable. If people cannot trust one another's commitments, establish monitoring and proportionate responses. If the costs and benefits are distributed unfairly, negotiate the allocation rather than assuming the conflict is merely an error of communication. Mathematics helps us state what incentives are present; it does not determine the legitimate distribution of a resource by itself.

The ferry and fishing examples are invented, but the distinction between coordination games, cooperation dilemmas, and distributional conflicts is general. It helps explain why successful communities build more than goodwill. They build ways to communicate, make promises credible, settle disputes, and change the arrangement when circumstances shift.

## A computer tournament born from concern about war

Robert Axelrod studied mathematics and political science. In a retrospective account of his work, he recalled becoming interested as a student in computer programs that learned to play games. He followed early computer checkers and chess and, at the same time, became concerned with international politics and the risk of nuclear war. He encountered the prisoner's dilemma as a model for conflicts in which countries might be safer if both restrained themselves, yet each feared being exploited by the other.

The problem stayed with him. Years later, he began wondering whether a computer tournament could reveal effective ways of playing a repeated prisoner's dilemma. Human experiments were informative, but people changed strategies, grew bored, or interpreted the same situation differently over time. A computer program would follow an explicit rule. Put many rules against one another, and the results could be examined and reproduced.

He invited researchers to submit programs that would play repeated rounds. An especially simple program, submitted by mathematician and game theorist Anatol Rapoport, performed remarkably well. It became known as tit for tat. It cooperated on the first move; after that, it made the same choice the other player had made on the preceding move.

That strategy has three qualities worth distinguishing. It begins cooperatively, so it does not create a conflict before one exists. It responds to defection rather than accepting exploitation indefinitely. And if the other player returns to cooperation, it does so as well. A short rule makes retaliation and reconciliation both possible.

Axelrod was struck by a result that seems paradoxical at first. Tit for tat did well across the collection of opponents even though, in a head-to-head repeated game with the usual payoffs, it cannot systematically outscore the opponent it faces. Its success came from producing many mutually beneficial rounds with a range of strategies. The tournament measured performance across an environment of different opponents, not victory in a single contest by dominating the other player.

Axelrod later collaborated with the evolutionary biologist William Hamilton to explore how reciprocity might matter in biological evolution. The link between a political scientist thinking about nuclear conflict, a mathematical model of two prisoners, and the behavior of living organisms is an example of interdisciplinary work that is attractive precisely because the underlying structure may recur in different settings. It is also a reason to watch the assumptions: nations, organisms, and software agents differ in what they can remember, choose, and communicate.

## A victorious rule with a serious flaw

One of the best features of Axelrod's later account is that he identified a problem with his own early result. The original tournament did not adequately model errors in how actions were carried out or observed. Two players might both intend to cooperate, but one action is reported incorrectly as a defection. Under strict tit for tat, the other player retaliates. Then the first retaliates in response. What began as one mistake can produce alternating acts of punishment.

Anyone who has argued with a friend about something that was misunderstood can recognize the shape of the problem. 'You ignored me.' 'I was responding to the way you treated me.' 'But I only did that because you ignored me.' Each side sees a response to the previous offense; neither sees itself as the initiator. A model of reciprocity that excludes misunderstandings can recommend a brittle strategy for human relations.

Later variants added forgiveness or contrition. A generous form of tit for tat sometimes declines to punish a perceived defection. A contrite strategy can recognize that its own action went wrong and avoid escalating the resulting dispute. These changes reveal something subtle: the capacity to retaliate can discourage exploitation, but the capacity to forgive can protect cooperation from accidental breakdown.

We should not turn that into a universal instruction to be forgiving. When the other party persistently exploits you, forgiveness without boundaries may prolong the exploitation. And some conflicts are about power, rights, or violence rather than two equal players choosing between symmetric rewards. The mathematical game provides a lens; it does not replace moral judgment about the actual situation.

There is also no universally best strategy in every repeated game. If interactions are brief, monitoring is unreliable, or the opponent is unresponsive, a cooperative strategy can perform badly. The tournaments were important because they explored a specified environment and prompted further theory, not because they discovered a timeless code of human conduct.

## When the resource itself can run out

Now return to the irrigation channel. The prisoner's dilemma describes a conflict over choices, but a shared water source has additional properties. Removing water may reduce what remains for others. The flow changes with rainfall and seasons. Maintenance affects future capacity. Farmers differ in their land, equipment, and position along the channel. A rule that works upstream may be costly downstream.

Economists call many such systems common-pool resources. They are difficult to exclude people from and can be depleted or congested by use. A forest, fishery, groundwater basin, or irrigation system often has these properties. It is important to distinguish the resource itself from the rules that govern access. A resource can be owned or governed in several ways; the mere presence of multiple users does not tell us whether it has rules or how well those rules work.

In nineteen sixty-eight the ecologist Garrett Hardin published an influential essay titled 'The Tragedy of the Commons.' His pasture example described herders each benefiting from adding another animal while the cost of overgrazing is spread across everyone. Under unrestricted access, individual incentives can drive depletion. As a model of an unmanaged and overcrowded resource, it identifies a real danger.

But 'commons' came to be used too casually as a synonym for a resource with no effective governance. That blurs an important distinction. A pasture that anyone may use without rules differs from a pasture managed by a community with boundaries, monitoring, agreed limits, and sanctions. Whether a shared resource is sustainable is an empirical question about institutions, technology, ecology, and power.

This is where the work of Elinor Ostrom becomes essential. She did not deny that people overuse shared resources. She asked why the same general type of resource could deteriorate in one setting and endure in another. To answer, one needs to examine the actual rules people use, including rules that may never have been written in a formal law.

## The person who went to see how people actually worked

Elinor Ostrom was born in Los Angeles in nineteen thirty-three and grew up during the Great Depression. In her Nobel autobiographical account, she remembered a backyard garden filled with vegetables and fruit trees, preserving food in summer, and swimming competitively. She later taught swimming to help save for college. These details offer a picture of someone whose early life included practical labor, shared effort, and learning skills that could be used immediately.

Her education was shaped by gender expectations. She recalled being advised against advanced mathematics in high school, a decision that complicated later efforts to pursue graduate economics. When she considered a doctorate in economics at UCLA, the response was discouraging. Political science also had reservations about admitting women doctoral students. She entered that program and completed her PhD in nineteen sixty-five. This was not a smooth path through institutions designed to welcome her.

Her dissertation examined the governance of groundwater in Southern California. Underground water cannot be managed simply by assigning a visible fence to each withdrawal point; pumping from one location affects a larger basin. Communities had to develop ways to measure extraction, negotiate claims, and coordinate rules. From the beginning, Ostrom studied people who were already trying to solve complicated collective problems, instead of assuming that only a distant authority could devise a solution.

In nineteen sixty-three she married Vincent Ostrom, himself a political scientist. They later worked at Indiana University, where they built a research community around institutions, collective choice, and governance. Their Workshop in Political Theory and Policy Analysis became a place where field studies, laboratory experiments, theory, and discussions across disciplines could inform one another. Elinor Ostrom's achievement was an individual intellectual contribution developed within a web of sustained collaboration.

Her work carried a persistent methodological insistence: visit the communities, learn what their participants actually do, compare cases, and examine which arrangements endure. It is easy to assume, from a distance, that a rule is simple. A farmer may tell you that the schedule is fair, but the schedule may depend on the season, historical rights, water level, and whether a neighbor helped clear the channel. The social institution is a set of practices that cannot always be reduced to one sentence in a statute book.

## How an enduring commons is organized

Ostrom studied many cases of common-pool resource management and identified recurring features of institutions that lasted. In *Governing the Commons*, published in nineteen ninety, she discussed what she called design principles. These are not a guarantee that any group following a checklist will succeed. They are patterns found across a body of evidence that help explain why some organizations can sustain cooperation.

Imagine our canal again. First, users need to know who can withdraw water and which water source the rules apply to. A boundary can be social and practical as well as geographical. If nobody knows who is entitled to take water, an agreement among the known users may be continually undermined by unaccounted-for withdrawals.

Second, the rules must fit local conditions. A rigid equal allocation may be unfair when farms need different amounts for different crops, or when one group must supply more labor for maintenance. The contributions required to sustain the system should have a plausible relationship to the benefits received. People may disagree about what counts as fair, but a rule that obviously ignores local conditions can make compliance difficult to sustain.

Third, people affected by the rules need a meaningful opportunity to participate in changing them. A water schedule that worked during a wet decade may fail in drought. If users can revise it through recognized procedures, the group has a way to adapt without every conflict becoming a fight over whether the institution should exist at all.

Fourth, someone must know what is happening. Monitoring can concern both the resource and the people using it. If a downstream farmer cannot tell whether a shortage results from drought, a broken gate, or unauthorized withdrawal, there is no reliable basis for response. Monitors who answer to the users may have stronger incentives to collect information others trust.

Fifth, enforcement benefits from proportion. A first minor violation can be treated differently from repeated deliberate theft. Graduated sanctions allow an institution to respond without making each mistake a permanent rupture. Sixth, there must be accessible ways to resolve disputes. If two farmers disagree over whether a gate was open for too long, a locally understood procedure may be more useful than an expensive lawsuit several years later.

Seventh, outside authorities need to recognize a community's right to organize. An internally fair schedule cannot function well if a more powerful actor can disregard it without consequence. And eighth, when the resource is part of a larger system, organization may need nested levels: local canals within a basin, local monitoring within regional water management, and coordination beyond any one village.

Taken together these principles suggest why cooperation is a problem of institutional design. People need information, recognized rights, opportunities for voice, credible responses to violation, and ways to adapt. None of those requirements is supplied simply by telling everyone to behave better.

## The cost of making rules together

There is a price to cooperation that simple two-person games usually conceal: someone has to build and maintain the rules. Neighbors attend meetings, inspect equipment, keep records, settle disputes, and argue over exceptions. None of those activities is free. A fishing group may decide to restrict the catch, but then has to determine the season, decide who can fish, and handle years when stocks are unexpectedly low. The rule that worked yesterday may require revision tomorrow.

Ostrom's research is powerful partly because it treated these details as central evidence. Rules were designed in places where participants understood local conditions and could observe how behavior affected the resource. Her design principles did not assert that every community succeeds, or that one set of rules guarantees cooperation. Clear boundaries, ways for affected users to participate in rule changes, monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible dispute resolution, and recognition of the community's right to organize each address a possible failure point. In larger systems, several levels of organization may need to fit together.

Think of a dispute over water from a mountain canal. A farmer upstream can divert more than agreed and enjoy the benefit before anyone downstream can respond. The other farmers need a way to notice the diversion and a credible means of correcting it. A single violation should not necessarily bring permanent expulsion, especially if the cause was a broken gate or misunderstanding. But rules nobody enforces give conscientious households a reason to abandon their sacrifices. Monitoring and proportional sanctions make the promise of sharing believable.

There is also a question of legitimacy. A rule written without the people who bear its costs may be rejected even if an outside analyst judges it technically efficient. A community may exclude people who depend on the resource, or assign authority in a way that reproduces local inequalities. Successful collective action is not automatically fair collective action. We have to ask who can participate, whose losses are counted, and who can appeal the outcome.

This is where the human lives in the institutional story become visible. An irrigation schedule can determine when a household can plant, how much time someone spends maintaining a ditch, and whether a family can remain on its land during a dry season. The mathematics of cooperation becomes important because the stakes are concrete. People do not build institutions solely to solve abstract puzzles. They build them to live and work together under conditions where an individual cannot achieve a secure outcome alone.

## What the elegant model leaves out

Ostrom's findings do not show that all communities are harmonious. Local rules can exclude newcomers, impose unequal burdens, or be captured by powerful families. A resource can collapse because of environmental changes beyond a community's control, because of external markets, or because neighboring groups do not recognize one another's boundaries. Monitoring can become coercive. Sanctions can become unfair. Democratic participation on paper can conceal unequal influence.

These limitations are also reasons to avoid romanticizing government, private ownership, or local management as universal solutions. Each arrangement has mechanisms that can succeed under some conditions and fail under others. Good policy asks what the resource requires, who has power, how rules are enforced, and whether the community can learn from evidence and revise its arrangements.

The research challenge is harder than observing one successful village and declaring its methods universally valid. Cases vary in scale, climate, history, technology, and economic pressure. Some institutions last because their environment is forgiving. Others survive difficult conditions because they adapt. Distinguishing those explanations requires comparison, careful measurement, and attention to alternative causes.

The mathematical game also leaves out identity and moral commitments. People cooperate with friends, strangers, institutions, and future generations for different reasons. A person can contribute even when they expect no direct return. Someone may refuse cooperation that would require participating in injustice. Reciprocity is one source of stable behavior, but a complete account of human cooperation must include norms, care, law, political power, and competing conceptions of fairness.

The value of the game is that it isolates a mechanism. The value of the fieldwork is that it shows how that mechanism is embedded in complicated lived arrangements. Neither should be mistaken for the whole explanation.

## A model of a repaired relationship

Imagine that Alice and Ben have cooperated for months. Alice misses a scheduled maintenance day because she is ill, but Ben does not know that. He believes she has defected, so he withholds help the following week. Alice returns, sees Ben's refusal, and feels betrayed. Without communication, each can interpret the other's response as proof of bad faith.

A brittle rule produces retaliation. A forgiving rule might let one lapse pass. A monitoring procedure could provide the missing information: Alice reports illness to a coordinator, the absence is recorded, and the contribution can be made up later. A dispute-resolution process gives Ben a way to challenge a false excuse if he has evidence. The institution protects against both deliberate free riding and mistakes.

This is a fictional vignette, but it illustrates why rules sometimes outperform spontaneous trust. The participants do not need to know one another's private intentions with perfect accuracy. They need a way to make relevant actions visible and disagreements manageable. Trust can grow when people see that commitments are kept and that errors are repaired without letting exploitation become routine.

Such systems can fail if they become too complicated or expensive to use. A village should not need a tribunal for each misplaced shovel. A large water network may require precise accounting and formal appeals. Design is partly the art of matching the cost of governance to the size and danger of the underlying coordination problem.

A useful question for any organization is therefore: what would a well-intentioned participant need in order to cooperate reliably here? They might need information, time, legitimate expectations, an accessible complaint process, or reassurance that others will contribute. A second question is equally necessary: what prevents a participant who wants to take advantage from doing so indefinitely? The answers must be compatible with each other.

## When trust breaks, the institutions have to change

It is tempting to imagine that cooperation can always be recovered by asking each party to behave more generously. But trust is often damaged for good reasons. Suppose a flood destroys a portion of a shared canal. Upstream farmers can still reach water, while downstream farmers cannot. The old irrigation schedule no longer allocates a usable resource fairly. Demanding that everyone follow the old agreement will not rebuild the canal or resolve who should pay for repairs.

The participants now need a new bargaining process. They have to determine how serious the damage is, who has the means to repair it, and what sacrifices each household can reasonably afford. They may require a neutral record of water flow, a temporary distribution system, and a rule for sharing repair costs. None of this is reducible to a single choice to cooperate or defect. Good collective action depends on the ability to revise agreements under new facts.

At the scale of nations, the difficulties multiply. States have different resources, different exposure to danger, and no single authority able to enforce every promise. The repeated-game model still identifies mechanisms of reciprocity and reputation, but it cannot supply the missing fairness judgments or erase differences in bargaining power. A country facing a large immediate loss might require credible assistance before it can afford an agreement with distant benefits. Another may benefit from a rule while retaining the power to violate it with little immediate cost.

The practical question is therefore broader than 'Are people naturally cooperative?' We should ask what signals reveal genuine cooperation, what institutions make promises credible, whose interests the rules represent, and how participants can adapt when an arrangement stops working. That is a framework for studying societies rather than a prediction that all groups will converge on peace.

## What makes cooperation possible

The prisoner's dilemma showed why independent choices can produce a collectively poor outcome. Repetition gave future consequences to present conduct. Axelrod's tournaments showed that strategies capable of initiating cooperation, responding to exploitation, and restoring cooperation can perform well in some repeated environments, while his later work exposed their fragility under misunderstanding. Ostrom's field research moved us from two abstract players into real communities, where people create rules about boundaries, monitoring, fairness, and conflict resolution.

We should resist two attractive but incomplete stories. One says that people are naturally cooperative and merely need freedom from outside interference. The other says that shared resources always collapse unless authority or ownership is imposed from above. Evidence from both games and communities gives us a more demanding picture: incentives influence behavior; relationships change incentives; institutions shape relationships; and institutions themselves must be judged and revised.

When you next see a community maintaining a resource, a research team sharing credit, or an organization struggling with free riders, ask what its members can observe, what happens after a mistake, who participates in making rules, and how much each person expects from the future. Those questions do not guarantee a solution. They help explain why cooperation sometimes works where simple models predict failure, and why an arrangement that worked yesterday may need deliberate repair tomorrow.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. One-shot prisoner's dilemma payoff table

Payoffs `(Alice, Ben)` are a **synthetic teaching game**:

| | Ben cooperates | Ben defects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alice cooperates | (3, 3) | (0, 5) |
| Alice defects | (5, 0) | (1, 1) |

Defection strictly dominates cooperation for both players, so the unique
one-shot Nash equilibrium is `(D,D)`, with `(1,1)`, although `(C,C)` is
Pareto superior at `(3,3)`. This conclusion depends on the specified payoffs,
not a general theorem about human nature.

### 2. Repetition, discounting, and the threat of future withdrawal

In an indefinitely repeated model with discount factor `0 <= δ < 1`, mutual
cooperation forever is worth `3/(1-δ)` from the current round. A unilateral
defection yielding `5` now followed by mutually defective play worth `1`
per later round has value `5+δ/(1-δ)`. Cooperation is at least as valuable if

\[
\frac{3}{1-\delta}\ge 5+\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta\ge\frac12.
\]

At `δ=0.9`, the two streams are `30` and `5+0.9/0.1 = 14`.
This is the incentive check against a single deviation when both players
follow the stipulated grim-trigger response, not a proof about every repeated
game. Tit for tat has different continuation dynamics, especially under noise.

### 3. A separate coordination game

A synthetic ferry-timetable game, payoffs (Town A, Town B), distinguishes
coordination from the one-shot prisoner's dilemma:

| | B chooses early | B chooses late |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A chooses early | (3, 3) | (0, 0) |
| A chooses late | (0, 0) | (3, 3) |

Both `(early, early)` and `(late, late)` are Nash equilibria. This game
has no dominant strategy to defect; the main challenge is selecting the
same equilibrium with shared information. Real institutions can combine
coordination, public goods, congestion, unequal bargaining power, and
monitoring in a single arrangement.

### 4. The original tournaments and their limitations

Axelrod's retrospective describes why he replaced human opponents with
programmed strategies, Rapoport's simple tit-for-tat submission, its success
across tournament opponents, his collaboration with W. D. Hamilton, and the
problem of observation/implementation noise. No general optimality theorem
for tit for tat across all opponents is claimed.

### 5. Resource versus governance

- A common-pool resource is typically rival/subtractable in use and costly to
  exclude access to, unlike a pure public good (nonrival and nonexcludable).
- **Open access** describes one governance condition; it does not exhaust the
  possibilities for shared or collectively governed resources.
- Ostrom's design principles summarize recurrent properties of more durable
  institutions. They are conditional empirical findings, not an algorithm that
  guarantees equitable or sustainable management everywhere.

### 6. Ostrom's eight design principles (research synthesis)

1. Clearly defined resource/user boundaries.
2. Rules fit local conditions and contribution/benefit distribution.
3. Users have meaningful collective-choice authority.
4. Monitoring of the resource and user behavior is accountable.
5. Sanctions are graduated.
6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms are rapid and low-cost.
7. External authorities recognize rights to organize.
8. Nested governance for resources embedded in larger systems.

### 7. Historical notes

- Ostrom's garden, swimming, saving for college, graduate admissions
  obstacles and interdisciplinary collaborations derive from her Nobel
  autobiography and institutional biographies.
- The canal example and Alice/Ben scenarios are **fictional teaching
  constructions**. Do not present them as cases Ostrom personally observed.
- Hardin's 1968 essay describes a particular unmanaged-resource incentive
  problem. Subsequent criticism distinguishes open access from a commons with
  effective rules; critique does not entail that resource depletion never occurs.
