# What Do We Owe the Future? Risk, Responsibility, and People Not Yet Born

Imagine a town deciding whether to build a reservoir. The project will provide water for decades, but it may also flood a valley and change the river's ecology. The current residents can attend meetings, protest, bargain, and vote. The children who may live there fifty years from now cannot. Some may not yet exist. Nevertheless they will inherit the dam, the water system, the debt, and any environmental damage.

What does the town owe those future people? It cannot ask them what they want. It cannot even know exactly who they will be. Some benefits are immediate; other consequences take generations. The answer cannot rest entirely on present preferences because present people have a disproportionate power to set the conditions in which others will live.

This question becomes urgent when technologies offer enormous benefit and possible irreversible harm. Nuclear weapons, biotechnology, industrial emissions, and powerful information systems have all raised versions of it. We need to make decisions before every consequence can be measured. And we need institutions that can revise those decisions when evidence changes.

We will follow two historical debates in which scientists wrestled with the responsibility accompanying their discoveries. One concerned atomic weapons near the end of the Second World War. Another concerned the ability to combine genetic material in new ways. Then we will confront a philosophical puzzle about future people and a mathematical puzzle about how to compare benefits and harms separated by decades.

## A petition in July nineteen forty-five

In July 1945, physicists and other researchers working on the Manhattan Project knew that atomic weapons were approaching use. They were also aware that a new class of destructive power might alter international relations for generations. Among the scientists was Leo Szilard, a Hungarian-born physicist who had long considered the possibility of nuclear chain reactions and had been involved in efforts to warn American officials of the military implications of nuclear fission.

Szilard helped circulate a petition to President Harry Truman. The final July seventeenth petition requested that the United States make terms of surrender known to Japan and refrain from using atomic bombs without giving Japan an opportunity to accept those terms. It also warned about the future consequences of setting a precedent for nuclear warfare. Scientists differed sharply over policy, and the petition represented one intervention in an already complicated military and political debate.

The document did not reach Truman before the bombs were used. That fact is significant because it shows a failure of communication as well as disagreement about policy. Writing a petition was not sufficient to bring an ethical argument into a decision-making process with secrecy, military command, and tight deadlines.

The war's end, Japan's decisions, Soviet entry into the war, and American policy have all been studied through extensive archives. Historians continue to debate the alternatives to the atomic bombings. Those counterfactual questions remain contested. The central point is that some people involved in creating the weapons were already asking about consequences beyond the immediate battle, and they tried to influence the process before use.

Szilard's concern was not confined to his reputation as a scientist. He anticipated that a weapon whose destructive power could increase would alter the incentives of states. Even leaders who did not wish for future wars might become trapped in competition, secrecy, and fear. The question was how a technical achievement changes the political system into which it enters.

## Why expertise did not settle the moral choice

The physicists possessed specialized knowledge about nuclear reactions, explosive power, and technical feasibility. But choosing whether to use a weapon against a city is a moral and political decision involving civilians, military personnel, governments, and future generations. Scientific expertise can clarify likely effects, but it does not confer sole authority over whose lives may be placed at risk.

At the same time, the scientists' knowledge gave them reasons to raise warnings others might not yet grasp. There is no contradiction in saying that scientists had relevant responsibilities and that their authority was limited. A public decision becomes stronger when technical uncertainty and moral disagreement are articulated rather than hidden behind professional status.

Consider a modern engineering team developing a device with a small chance of catastrophic failure. Engineers may estimate failure probability, investigate likely mechanisms, and propose safeguards. Regulators may require testing and transparency. Affected communities may argue about acceptable exposure and the distribution of risks. Each contributes something the others cannot simply replace.

If only engineers decide, community costs may be underrepresented. If only public opinion decides without reliable technical evidence, a real hazard might be misunderstood. If only a political executive decides without disclosure, errors and conflicts of interest can remain hidden. Institutional design is partly about combining knowledge with accountability.

The July 1945 petition also shows that procedures matter. A warning that does not reach a decision-maker cannot affect the decision through that route. Systems need channels that allow dissenting evidence and ethical concerns to move upward, even when they are inconvenient.

## A conference at Asilomar

Three decades later, researchers confronted a different kind of uncertainty. New recombinant DNA techniques allowed scientists to join genetic material from different sources. The applications held great scientific and medical promise. But researchers also worried about creating organisms with properties whose risks were not yet understood.

In 1974 a group of scientists called for a voluntary pause on some experiments while risks could be assessed. In February 1975, the international Asilomar conference brought researchers and others together to discuss safeguards. Participants debated how to classify experiments by risk and what containment measures should apply. A statement published by Paul Berg and colleagues described proposed guidelines.

The meeting is sometimes celebrated as the moment scientists responsibly regulated themselves. There is truth in the willingness to pause and create safeguards before every danger had been demonstrated. But the history is more complicated. The range of participants and the limits of public representation mattered. Some critics argued that risks affecting society should not be negotiated only by specialists. Over time, broader governmental and institutional oversight developed.

The important feature of the process was its responsiveness to uncertainty. The researchers had substantial evidence about molecular techniques and incomplete evidence about their consequences. They attempted to identify categories of possible harm, restrict the riskiest practices, and develop conditions under which research could continue. The resulting guidelines could then change as knowledge improved.

That is different from demanding perfect safety before any research. If zero risk were required, most scientific progress would halt. It is also different from treating absence of observed harm as proof of safety. A sensible system asks how severe the plausible harms are, how uncertain the mechanisms remain, and which precautions allow learning without unacceptable exposure.

## Risk is probability times consequence, but not only that

A familiar starting point in risk analysis is expected loss. Imagine a project with a one-in-one-thousand chance each year of causing one million dollars of damage. If we treat the loss as a single monetary quantity and assume the estimate is valid, the expected annual loss is one thousand dollars: one million divided by one thousand.

That arithmetic can help compare repeated decisions. But it is not the complete ethical story. Suppose the consequence is permanent loss of a unique ecosystem or exposure of a community to a severe health risk. Some harms are difficult or inappropriate to value in dollars. Some outcomes have probabilities that are poorly known. And a small average expected loss can conceal intolerable risk concentrated on people who receive none of the benefits.

Consider two projects with equal expected monetary harm. Project A causes a small, reversible loss frequently. Project B has a remote chance of a catastrophic, irreversible outcome. A decision-maker may reasonably treat them differently because reversibility, distribution, uncertainty, and institutional capacity to recover also matter.

The distinction between known risk and deep uncertainty is essential. We might know the failure rate of a well-tested mechanical component from millions of observations. We may have no reliable frequencies for a new technology interacting with a poorly understood ecological system. Assigning a precise probability to the second case does not create the evidence the number implies.

How should we act when probabilities are uncertain? One response is robustness: seek plans that perform adequately across a range of plausible models. Another is staged deployment: test on a small scale, monitor outcomes, and retain the ability to stop. Another is redundancy: build independent safeguards so that one failure does not cause the worst consequence. Each response has limits and costs, but each makes uncertainty part of design.

## A decision tree with future people

Imagine a community considering a water treatment upgrade. The upgrade costs ten million dollars now and is expected to prevent contamination episodes over the next forty years. The benefits may include avoided illness, lower emergency expenses, and increased reliability. We want to compare those future benefits with the present cost.

A simple decision tree might branch into two outcomes: the old system fails at some time, or it does not. The upgrade changes the probability of failure. If the expected avoided costs exceed the expense, that is evidence in favor of the upgrade. But a good evaluation should also include who bears the burden of contamination and how vulnerable they are.

Now suppose the failure probability is uncertain because climate conditions are changing. We could calculate outcomes under low, medium, and high risk estimates. We might discover that the upgrade is worth doing across all reasonable assumptions, or only under some. The sensitivity of the decision to uncertain inputs is itself useful information.

A robust policy may have lower expected benefit under the most optimistic model but avoid disastrous performance if that model is wrong. This is the logic behind safety margins and diversification. It is not a proof that one policy always wins; the value of robustness depends on what we are trying to protect.

We also need to account for maintenance. A structure built for future residents is only a gift if the institution can maintain it. Deferred costs, access to expertise, and governance arrangements belong in the calculation from the beginning.

## Why the distant future shrinks in arithmetic

Economists often discount future monetary benefits and costs. At a constant annual rate of five percent, a benefit of one hundred dollars arriving one year from now is valued at about ninety-five dollars today. After twenty years, its present value is about thirty-eight dollars. After fifty years, about nine dollars. At a rate of one percent, the fifty-year value is about sixty-one dollars.

The arithmetic is straightforward: divide the future amount by one plus the rate, multiplied by itself for each year. The ethical interpretation is much harder. A discount rate may reflect investment opportunities, uncertainty, or people's preferences for earlier consumption. It does not automatically establish that a future child's suffering counts less because it occurs later.

Some analyses distinguish pure time preference from forecasts about future consumption. If future generations are likely to be richer, an additional dollar may have lower marginal value to them. But that is an assumption requiring examination, particularly when climate damage or political instability could make some descendants worse off.

Let's calculate a concrete difference. A future environmental harm estimated at one hundred million dollars fifty years from now is worth about eight point seven million at five percent and about sixty point eight million at one percent. That gap can reverse a cost-benefit recommendation. A policymaker who reports only the final present value hides a decision about the rate.

The lesson is not that discounting must be forbidden. Money invested now can sometimes create resources later; opportunity cost is real. The lesson is that technical-looking arithmetic embeds assumptions about wealth, uncertainty, and moral priorities. When consequences stretch across generations, those assumptions should be explained and contested openly.

## The puzzle of people who would otherwise never exist

The philosopher Derek Parfit developed a difficult problem about obligations to future people. Many choices made today change who will eventually be born. A decision about when to establish a community, build housing, or pursue a family can alter which people meet and have children. The future population under one policy may consist of different people from the population under another.

Now imagine a policy that leaves a future society with worse living conditions than a cleaner alternative would have produced. Yet suppose the people living under the worse policy would not have existed at all under the alternative, because the chain of births would have been different. If their lives are still worth living, in what sense did the policy make those particular people worse off?

This is the nonidentity problem. It challenges a simple person-affecting principle according to which an action is wrong only if it makes some identifiable person worse off than they would otherwise have been. We may have powerful reasons to prefer the policy producing better conditions for future people even though it changes who those people are.

Several responses are possible. We might hold that creating a worse future can be wrong independently of harming a particular individual relative to their own alternative life. We might appeal to rights or duties that do not depend on comparing individual identities. We might refine our account of harm, consider impersonal values, or resist the assumptions of the example. Each approach faces difficult questions.

There is no need to settle the entire population ethics literature in order to see the practical point. Future people can have moral importance even when we cannot name them. Their inability to attend today's meeting is a fact about political representation, not evidence that their welfare has no value.

## Reversibility as an ethical resource

Under uncertainty, the ability to change course can be valuable. Imagine choosing between two ways of providing energy. One requires permanent disposal of hazardous material in a location that may be difficult to monitor. The other relies on infrastructure that can be upgraded as technology improves but has different immediate costs. The decision is not determined merely by labeling one reversible and the other irreversible; both have complex impacts. Still, reversibility is a factor worth measuring.

A reversible policy creates an option for future people. If new evidence arrives, they may have more freedom to adapt. An irreversible decision consumes some of that freedom. Preserving options can be a reason to favor modular designs, continuing monitoring, and institutions capable of revision.

Yet there is also a cost to inaction. Refusing to build a needed flood barrier can leave people exposed to harm. Waiting for perfect evidence may allow damage to accumulate. In some cases, acting early is the more reversible choice because it prevents permanent losses.

The right question is therefore not simply whether a decision feels cautious. It is which action preserves valued opportunities under plausible futures, including the consequences of delay. That requires comparing actual options rather than an idealized world with no constraints.

## Institutional memory and the future

Governments and organizations often rotate their leadership much faster than the consequences of their decisions unfold. A company may design a product whose waste persists long after its executives have retired. A city may borrow to build infrastructure that descendants must maintain. A scientific field may establish safety practices that successors inherit without remembering why they were adopted.

Institutions need ways of carrying evidence forward. These include archives, monitoring, long-term maintenance funds, public reporting, independent review, and procedures for revisiting assumptions. A promise to be careful is weaker than a design that remains accountable when the original decision-makers are gone.

The Szilard petition shows the fragility of an ethical warning without an effective channel to authority. The Asilomar conference shows one way a research community attempted collective self-restraint, with significant questions about participation. Neither example supplies a universal template. Each demonstrates that the architecture of decision-making affects what happens to knowledge and dissent.

A community may also consider formal representation of future interests: planning bodies evaluating long-term risks, environmental impact assessments, guardians of public resources, or requirements that major decisions state effects across generations. Such institutions cannot literally know future preferences. They can nevertheless reduce the incentive to count only benefits arriving within the current election or budget cycle.

## A compact test for long-horizon decisions

When considering a choice whose consequences may outlive us, ask five questions. First, what benefits are expected and to whom? Second, what harms are plausible, including those that may be delayed? Third, how strong is the evidence for the estimates? Fourth, how easily can the decision be changed if assumptions fail? Fifth, who will have the authority and resources to monitor and correct it?

These questions make risk analysis more complete. They also expose ethical disagreement. One person may prioritize protecting a vulnerable community from a small possibility of catastrophe. Another may prioritize a large immediate benefit to people currently suffering. Serious deliberation must confront the disagreement rather than hide it in a single risk score.

The test can be used at small scales. A homeowner deciding whether to remove a mature tree may consider shade, roots, storm risk, wildlife, and future residents. A school deciding how to manage student data may consider present convenience and the lifelong consequences of retention. A research laboratory deciding whether to release a powerful tool may examine foreseeable misuse and whether safeguards can adapt.

The details vary, but the underlying discipline is the same: expand the time horizon and make the affected people visible, including those who cannot yet speak.

## Climate decisions and compounded effects

Climate policy is a contemporary example of decisions whose consequences extend beyond the people making them. Carbon dioxide can influence the climate system over long periods. Infrastructure built today may shape emissions for decades. Some damages are difficult to reverse, while some protective investments reduce risk for generations.

The technical assessment depends on physical models, observations, and scenarios of future human behavior. A projection under one emissions scenario is not an unconditional prediction that society will choose that pathway. A policymaker needs to know both what climate science says about physical consequences and what assumptions the scenario makes about choices still available.

Suppose a community is choosing between building houses in a flood-prone area and developing higher ground. The flood risk over fifty years may be uncertain. Avoiding the risky location could impose immediate costs; choosing it could expose residents and future owners to repeated danger. Insurance prices, public flood defenses, housing demand, and regulations can alter what households decide. The resulting future is shaped by physical processes and institutional incentives together.

The analysis should consider the people who face the risk without having chosen it, including renters and future residents. It should also examine what alternatives are genuinely available. Advising people simply to move is inadequate if affordable housing elsewhere does not exist.

Climate decisions thus connect the language of intergenerational responsibility to engineering, economics, and political inclusion. The right policy cannot be chosen by counting only the costs visible this year or by pretending that distant effects are precisely known. It requires modeling, adaptation, protection of vulnerable populations, and the willingness to revise plans as evidence changes.

## Why we need more than one moral framework

Suppose a proposal would bring enormous benefits to a million people while placing a small community at risk of severe, involuntary harm. A straightforward sum of expected benefits might favor the project. A rights-based view might reject it unless protections meet a stringent standard. A prioritarian view might give extra weight to those worst off. A democratic approach might emphasize the legitimacy of deliberation and fair representation.

These approaches need not be enemies. In practice, institutions often combine them. Engineers estimate risk, legal rules prohibit certain harms, public hearings allow objections, and policymakers compare costs among permitted alternatives. Trouble arises when the final decision hides all these steps behind one aggregate number.

The problem grows when future people have no formal vote. Present residents may place too little weight on long-term harms, while a decision-maker claiming to speak for the future might impose their own preferences without accountability. Institutions need to protect future interests without treating speculation about descendants as a license to ignore the rights of people alive today.

One possible approach is to set protective constraints, such as preserving essential ecological functions, and then compare feasible projects within those constraints. Another is to reserve resources for long-term maintenance. A third is to make major decisions subject to review on a specified schedule, with measurements capable of showing whether assumptions hold.

None makes the ethical puzzle disappear. They move it into a process where disagreements can be examined and decisions corrected.

## When waiting is itself a decision

Imagine scientists identify a plausible but uncertain danger in a widely used technology. Regulators could restrict it immediately, commission further research while leaving it in use, or require monitoring and phased substitution. Each option acts on uncertainty differently.

Waiting may preserve current benefits and allow better evidence to emerge. It may also increase exposure and allow damage to accumulate. Immediate restriction may prevent harm but can disrupt necessary services if substitutes are unavailable. Staged action may preserve learning opportunities but fail if the hazard develops too quickly.

The central question is how consequences change with time. If an intervention is reversible and harms are limited, experimentation may be reasonable. If there is a credible pathway to catastrophic irreversible damage, stronger safeguards may be justified even under uncertainty. But credible must be argued through evidence, mechanisms, and the magnitude of potential harm rather than merely asserted.

Asilomar's legacy is valuable here. Its participants did not possess complete knowledge, but they attempted to make research conditions responsive to risk. The precedent does not imply that any group of researchers should govern itself without public oversight. It shows that a deliberate pause, transparent reasoning, and adjustable safeguards can be tools for responsible innovation.

Szilard's petition supplies a different warning. Ethical foresight has little effect if decision systems are designed so that inconvenient questions cannot reach the people with authority. Preserving channels for dissent is therefore part of caring for the future, not an optional courtesy to present critics.

## What future people might reasonably thank us for

It is tempting to imagine descendants evaluating our choices with perfect hindsight. They will not possess perfect knowledge either. They may live with problems we have not imagined and develop values we cannot fully predict. This makes the idea of leaving one comprehensive plan for them suspect.

A more defensible ambition is to preserve important forms of agency: habitable environments, trustworthy knowledge, maintainable institutions, basic rights, and the capacity to adjust. These are not guaranteed to resolve future conflicts. They make it more possible for future people to address their own problems.

If we leave them an infrastructure that cannot be repaired, a record that cannot be trusted, and debts whose terms were hidden, we reduce that capacity. If we leave open scientific methods, resilient public institutions, accurate records, and some freedom to choose among technologies, we improve it.

This perspective helps connect the practical and philosophical questions. We cannot choose the exact people who will exist, and we cannot guarantee their happiness. But we can act with regard for the conditions under which they will exercise judgment. Respect for future people begins with recognizing them as eventual decision-makers, not merely passive beneficiaries of our own plans.

## A future that can answer us back

We began with a town deciding whether to build a reservoir. Its responsibilities include people present at the meeting and people who may live there long after the participants are gone. Some future consequences can be estimated from engineering and environmental evidence. Others depend on changing society and uncertain events. No calculation removes the need for judgment.

The history of nuclear weapons shows researchers struggling to influence decisions about a force they understood technically but could not govern alone. The recombinant DNA debates show a scientific community attempting to create safeguards as new capacities emerged. Discounting shows how choices about time can reshape quantitative results. The nonidentity problem shows why our obligations cannot always be reduced to comparing named individuals under two policies.

The future is not a passive recipient of our plans. Future people will reinterpret, maintain, improve, or reject what we leave. We can respect that agency by avoiding preventable harm, preserving options where practical, and giving them institutions capable of learning from mistakes.

We cannot guarantee a good future. We can make decisions that future people have reason to recognize as serious efforts to consider their interests. That standard is demanding, but it asks nothing supernatural. It asks us to use the knowledge we have, expose uncertainty where it remains, and remember that those who are absent from today's conversation may still live with its consequences.

### Discounting calculations

\(PV=FV/(1+r)^t\). For \(FV=100,t=50\), \(r=.05\) gives \(8.72\), \(r=.01\) gives \(60.80\). With \(FV=\$100\) million, units scale accordingly. The monetary discount framework does not, by itself, justify discounting moral concern for future people.

### Expected-loss illustration

Synthetic loss \(L=\$1{,}000{,}000\) with probability \(p=.001\): \(E[L]=pL=\$1{,}000\). This illustrative expected value does not price non-market goods or capture distribution, ambiguity, or worst-case risk.

### Nonidentity distinction

An intervention \(a\) can change both future quality of life and the identities of people born. Compare \(W(P_a)\) for population \(P_a\) with \(W(P_b)\) for \(P_b\) only after specifying an ethical framework. A person-affecting comparison of the *same* individual under different policies may be undefined where that individual would not exist under the alternative.
