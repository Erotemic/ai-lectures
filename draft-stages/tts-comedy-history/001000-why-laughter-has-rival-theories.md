# Why Things Are Funny: Rival Theories of Comedy

A city employee places a sign on a door: 'Department of Unexpected Events. Appointments required.' Some people smile at the contradiction. Others find it predictable. A third person asks whether the office is actually open. This is a deliberately small joke, but it introduces an enormous intellectual problem. What exactly happened between the sign and the smile? Was the audience surprised? Did it recognize a contradiction? Did it feel superior to an incompetent bureaucracy? Was there relief at finding a harmless target for frustration? Or did the words announce that an impossible institution is, for a moment, safe to imagine?

Philosophers, psychologists, performers, and critics have answered such questions differently for centuries. They have not produced one account that reliably predicts every joke, every audience, and every form of laughter. This should not surprise us. Laughter is a bodily response that can accompany play, embarrassment, nervousness, social affiliation, mockery, and joy. Humor is an experience of amusement. Comedy is also an artistic and social practice: a staged quarrel, a fictional plot, a ritual inversion, a speech designed to provoke laughter, or an entire performance tradition. These are related phenomena, but they are not the same object.

We need a map that respects that complexity. The classic philosophical families are usually called superiority, incongruity, and relief. Newer approaches examine benign violations, play, social bonding, and the conventions of particular genres. Each helps explain some cases. Each needs counterexamples. For a writer, the point is not to find the philosopher who has proven how to be funny. It is to gain instruments for recognizing the mechanism a joke attempts, why the mechanism might fail, and how to revise it.

## Superiority: who is being brought down?

Imagine a man entering a town hall in an expensive suit. He approaches a microphone to announce that he alone understands how the new public transport system works. The microphone is not connected. He continues speaking with magnificent confidence while the crowd gradually leaves. One source of amusement is that he has claimed exceptional competence and exposed his own ignorance. The distance between his self-image and his performance lets the audience feel temporarily above him.

This resembles what is now called superiority theory. Ancient discussions of laughter often linked the laughable to human deficiencies or misplaced pretensions. Thomas Hobbes famously analyzed laughter in terms of a sudden experience of one's own advantage, although his formulation was more specific than the casual phrase 'laughing at people.' Such accounts illuminate ridicule, humiliation, competitive joking, and the pleasure of puncturing arrogance. Political satire can use exactly this move: it shrinks an official who presents himself as enormous.

But the theory is neither necessary nor sufficient for humor. We can feel superior to someone without laughing. We can laugh when a tiny character outsmarts a powerful one, feeling admiration rather than superiority. We can laugh at ourselves while still feeling embarrassed, or enjoy wordplay without imagining a person beneath us. Even in the town-hall scene, different spectators may respond differently. A supporter of the official may feel secondhand humiliation. A passerby may laugh only when the microphone squeals and startles everyone. A victim of the official's policies may find the humiliation insufficient.

The useful writerly question is therefore not whether superiority explains all laughter. It is whose status is changing, who gets to enjoy the change, and what the audience is invited to believe about the target. Comic exposure can attack vanity, or it can reinforce a hierarchy by mocking people with little power. Neither outcome is determined by the mechanical existence of a punch line. A theory of superiority makes power visible in comic form.

## Incongruity: the expected thing does not arrive

Return to the Department of Unexpected Events. The office has a name that suggests unpredictability. The appointment rule suggests predictability. The humorous possibility lies in holding both concepts together. The mind had a model of what such a department might do; the sign forces a conflicting model onto it. Philosophers call such a mismatch an incongruity. This family of accounts became especially prominent in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century European philosophy, including discussions by James Beattie, Immanuel Kant, and Arthur Schopenhauer.

The writer's practical version is setup and reinterpretation. A setup encourages one expectation. The next information forces the audience to recast what it has heard. Consider an original line: 'Our village has a brilliant emergency plan. First, we lose the plan.' The first sentence invites admiration; the second converts 'brilliant' from an evaluation of competence into a claim the speaker cannot defend. More elaborate jokes arrange the same shift across a scene rather than a single sentence. A character confidently applies a rule in the one circumstance where the rule defeats its stated purpose.

Yet surprise by itself is not comedy. A sudden crash may frighten us. A shocking insult may anger us. A revelation in a mystery may be satisfying but not amusing. An impossible mathematical assertion may be confusing rather than funny. Some forms of absurdist comedy intentionally preserve an unresolved contradiction, while many verbal jokes reward the audience for finding the alternative interpretation. So incongruity theory needs an account of the emotional and social conditions under which a mismatch becomes enjoyable.

There is also the problem of repetition. We may laugh at a comic routine for the tenth time though its outcome is known. Some of the pleasure comes from anticipation, timing, recognition, and admiration for the performer's execution. The incongruity can remain in the represented world even when the spectator knows exactly what is coming. Performance reveals why a joke is not simply a packet of information delivered once. Rhythm, pauses, and shared expectation contribute to the experience.

A writer can use incongruity at multiple levels. A dignified person behaves like a child. A small errand acquires the apparatus of an international tribunal. A rule intended to increase safety generates danger. A world obeys an absurd law with rigorous consistency. The last form may be the most effective in long fiction: comedy is sustained not by random strangeness but by letting a coherent, misplaced principle produce increasingly consequential results.

## Relief: what tension is released?

Suppose the town-hall employee spends ten minutes trying to announce a disastrous funding cut. Everyone in the room fears losing a vital service. The speaker's overprepared slide deck stalls. An elderly resident asks whether the presentation is experiencing the same budget reduction as the transport network. The room laughs. The line does more than contradict an expectation. It permits the audience to express anger and fear without directly confronting the threat. It turns a tense meeting into a shared moment of release.

Relief theories try to explain amusement through the discharge or reorganization of tension. Historical versions treated the body as a system of pressures or nervous energies. Some accounts, especially those associated with Sigmund Freud and earlier nineteenth-century psychology, used biological mechanisms that contemporary physiology does not support in their original form. The everyday observation that tension and release can shape laughter remains useful, but that should not be confused with scientific confirmation of an old hydraulic theory of the nervous system.

A comic performer uses tension as a resource. A pause before a confession can increase anticipation. A prohibited subject can make the room alert. A joke may invite the audience close to an uncomfortable idea and then alter the frame so the danger becomes manageable. But release is not always relief from genuine suffering. An audience can be perfectly comfortable and still laugh at a pun. People sometimes laugh when pressure increases, not when it ends. Social anxiety can also produce laughter without enjoyment.

The key distinction is between an observable performance pattern and an explanation of why the brain laughs. A writer can deliberately build tension and release. The writer cannot infer that an eighteenth- or nineteenth-century physiological hypothesis is true because a joke feels relieving. The practice works even if the theory is incomplete.

## Benign violation: wrong and acceptable at once

A more recent proposal asks whether humor arises when something violates a norm but is simultaneously experienced as acceptable or safe. A. Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren tested versions of this idea in work published in 2010, particularly involving moral violations. The idea has intuitive force. A person stepping onto a stage with a bucket of water may make us apprehensive if the bucket is near electrical equipment. The same splash in a clearly staged farce can be comic. The scene contains an apparent violation of ordinary conduct, yet the audience also recognizes a protective frame.

This account helps explain why distance matters. We may laugh at an absurd historical bureaucratic procedure that would enrage us if we had to endure it tomorrow. We may enjoy slapstick when a performer displays the skill needed to avoid actual injury. We may laugh at an exaggerated fictional tyrant while feeling no amusement at a real person harmed by that tyranny. Different audiences draw the line between harmless and threatening in different places. What is benign to a distant observer may be a familiar injury to somebody else.

The theory is not a universal machine for generating jokes. First, defining a 'violation' can become so broad that nearly everything surprising counts. Second, the harmlessness judgment can be socially contested. Third, some gentle comedy seems to rely on recognition and warmth rather than a salient violation. Fourth, someone can judge a violation benign and still find the joke tedious. We have learned why a comic proposal might become acceptable, not necessarily why it will be artful.

Nevertheless, this approach offers a concrete revision diagnostic. If a joke about a difficult subject fails, ask whether the audience understood the intended target and whether the scene offered enough distance or shared trust. If there is no felt violation, the joke may have no energy. If the violation feels too damaging, the audience may refuse the invitation to laugh. The solution is not always to soften the material; sometimes it is to change the point of view so the actual object of ridicule becomes clear.

## Bergson: the mechanical applied to life

Henri Bergson's essay on laughter takes a different route. One of his central images is rigidity imposed on life's flexibility: a person behaves mechanically where adaptation is needed. Imagine a clerk whose office has been flooded. Every visitor must still fill out a form explaining why their shoes are wet. When the clerk's own desk floats away, he asks the water to present identification. This is funny not only because of surprise. The rule-bound pattern persists after reality has made it ridiculous.

The idea is valuable for character comedy. A recurring comic figure often has a fixed strategy: protect status, obey procedure, avoid embarrassment, interpret everything as a business transaction. Ordinary life changes; the strategy does not. The audience begins predicting the character's error, and anticipation becomes pleasure. Such characters need not be stupid. They can be highly competent within a narrow model and catastrophically unable to update it.

There is a moral edge to Bergson's analysis: laughter can function as social correction of rigidity. But who gets corrected, and according to whose social norms? A character who resists an unjust convention may be ridiculed by a conformist crowd. The scene's social function is not automatically admirable. The theory teaches us to locate a misfit between living circumstances and habitual behavior, while leaving open the ethics of the group doing the laughing.

The clerk's case also shows how comedy can become tragic without changing its premise. If the form merely inconveniences citizens, the character may be ridiculous. If the form prevents medicine reaching a sick child, the same behavior can become horrifying. Comedy depends on framing, consequence, audience expectation, and the distribution of vulnerability. Writers cannot isolate mechanism from stakes.

## Play, social connection, and the audience as participant

Philosophical theories frequently treat the joke as an object and the listener as an individual mind. Performance complicates this. A laugh can invite others to join, signal that aggression is playful, or establish solidarity against a shared annoyance. Laughter can also exclude. Some jokes work because the audience recognizes a particular profession's rituals, a regional dialect, a shared political frustration, or a family habit. Someone outside that group may understand the sentence perfectly and miss the social event.

The audience therefore co-produces comedy. A performer discovers what a room expects. A pause allows a spectator's inference to complete. Repetition can build shared complicity. A callback can reward people who have attended closely for several minutes. In improvised theater, the audience may recognize an offer before any performer explicitly explains it. In a novel, readers can experience the same pleasure through recurring motifs and characters whose delusions become legible over time.

This also helps explain why jokes travel unevenly. Translation can preserve plot but lose timing, sound pattern, social status, or background assumptions. A comic performance made under censorship may rely on the audience recognizing something the performer cannot state. Change the regime, medium, or audience, and the coded joke can become opaque. Historic comedy needs reconstruction of conditions of reception, not merely an inventory of surviving punch lines.

## A craft workshop with one comic situation

Let us return to the Department of Unexpected Events. We can write five versions, each emphasizing a different mechanism. For superiority, a pompous director explains that unpredictability is his personal invention, then panics when a visitor arrives early. For incongruity, applicants must schedule a surprise, then are turned away for failing to be surprised on time. For relief, anxious citizens queue outside, and a clerk jokes that the only emergency the department can solve is a forgotten lunch. For benign violation, actors stage an escalating but obviously safe bureaucratic emergency, allowing the audience to laugh at the imagined failure of authority. For Bergsonian rigidity, the whole building loses power and the director continues stamping invisible forms.

These are schematic versions. They are not guaranteed to be funny. The writer must still discover characters, timing, specificity, and escalation. A single premise can support comedy through several mechanisms at once. It might satirize professional vanity, violate logical expectations, and permit relief from a stressful institution. That overlap is evidence against treating philosophical categories as mutually exclusive genres.

Now do a harder revision. Give the clerk a legitimate reason for the rule. Perhaps the office once lost emergency records during a disaster, and its paperwork really did save lives. The premise becomes richer. We can laugh at procedural rigidity while understanding why the person fears improvisation. A character who is merely an idiot offers little resistance and little surprise. A character who is right about one danger and wrong about another can sustain an entire comic narrative.

## Timing is itself a form of meaning

A comic line can survive being read and fail when spoken too quickly. Imagine that the Department of Unexpected Events has announced a three-hour delay. The clerk says, 'We would have told you sooner.' The audience expects an explanation. The clerk pauses. 'But that would have been unexpected.' The timing makes room for the audience to infer the foolish logic before the final sentence arrives. If the clerk speaks without a pause, the same sentence may feel like an ordinary excuse rather than a comic reversal.

This does not mean there is one correct pause length. A live room responds with breath, murmurs, and interruptions. In recorded audio, silence can feel longer because the performer receives no immediate reply. In prose, a paragraph break or sentence boundary acts as a timing device. In comics, the spacing of panels can postpone information. A writer who studies only the semantic content of the joke may miss how the medium allows the audience to complete an expectation before the reversal arrives.

Compare laughter at a single surprise with laughter at a repeated rule. A clerk keeps adding stamps to a form, even after it catches fire. The first stamp establishes rigidity; the second creates a prediction; the third becomes funny partly because spectators see the clerk's next move coming. Repetition gives the audience a little predictive expertise. Escalation then tests how far the character will persist. The pleasure is not pure surprise. It is a collaboration in which the performer and audience understand a pattern together.

## What a theory can and cannot promise

Suppose we wish to develop a universal formula for comedy. We specify an unexpected event, a perceived norm violation, a safe context, and some shift in social status. That sounds impressive. But the result could still be flat, because humor is sensitive to prose rhythm, acting, shared knowledge, and individual taste. Two people can agree completely about why a joke is supposed to work and disagree about whether it works. A theory often describes conditions under which comedy is possible rather than sufficient conditions for artistic success.

Scientific research can test some hypotheses with experiments, ratings, and observed laughter. Historical scholarship can show how philosophers and artists understood their work in their own contexts. Close reading can reveal structures in a play or novel. None replaces the others. A comic writer may also learn more from trying a line in front of a particular audience than from perfecting a definition of incongruity. Theory becomes useful when it sharpens the experiment, not when it forbids feedback.

There is one final distinction worth preserving. We do not have to laugh in order to recognize something as comedy. Satire can be bitter. Tragicomedy can make laughter catch in the throat. An audience can appreciate a comic scene's architecture while finding its values objectionable. Comedy is not defined solely by one physiological sound. It is also a way of staging human limitations, power, language, accident, and the discrepancy between our plans and the world.

Our imaginary department promised unexpected events while demanding appointments. The sign can be read as a logical contradiction, a satire of administration, a moment of harmless absurdity, or a social invitation to share frustration. That plurality is not a failure. It is the lesson. Theories of comedy are lenses for examining what a comic construction does, and their disagreements reveal why comedy is both a craft and a serious subject of inquiry. The writer's task is to find which mechanism the scene offers, what the audience knows, what costs are at stake, and whether the resulting laughter is worth earning.

### Competing explanatory lenses

| Lens | Central question | Useful for | Not sufficient because |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Superiority | Whose status falls? | Satire, embarrassment, ridicule | We can laugh without feeling superior |
| Incongruity | What expectation is frustrated or reinterpreted? | Wordplay, absurdity, reversals | Surprise can produce fear or confusion |
| Relief | What tension changes? | Taboo, tension/release, group discomfort | Tension release need not be humorous |
| Benign violation | What norm is violated and why is it okay here? | Slapstick, dark humor, social distance | Acceptable violations can still be unfunny |
| Bergsonian rigidity | What mechanical habit resists a changing situation? | Character farce, bureaucratic satire | Social correction can reinforce unjust norms |
| Play/social | What relationship does laughing establish? | Callbacks, in-group humor, performance | Affiliation alone does not specify a joke mechanism |

### Exercises

1. Construct one scene for each lens with the same characters and setting; compare what changes besides the punch line.
2. Design a joke that meets an incongruity criterion yet is clearly unfunny. Identify the missing ingredient.
3. Rewrite a cruel joke so that the target of ridicule becomes the powerful rule rather than its vulnerable subject.
4. Analyze a recurring comic character whose fixed behavior makes them funny before they speak.
