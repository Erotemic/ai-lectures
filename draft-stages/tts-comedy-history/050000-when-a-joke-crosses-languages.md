# When a Joke Crosses Languages: Wordplay, Meaning, and the Translator's Choice

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Modern comparative humor theory and translation  
**Status:** Substantive audio-first manuscript

A city museum puts up a sign that reads, "We have a new exhibition on impatience. It opens eventually." A visitor smiles because the announcement is doing the very thing the exhibition supposedly studies. It makes the listener wait for a promised opening, then offers an evasive substitute for a date. There is no elaborate pun, and no specialist cultural knowledge is necessary to see the contradiction. Now imagine that the museum is making a version of the sign for visitors who speak another language. Should the translator keep the exact wording, reproduce the delay, invent a more locally natural sentence, or abandon the joke and simply announce the exhibit?

This is the central puzzle of humor translation. Words carry information, but in comedy they also arrange expectations, imply relationships, establish a voice, conceal a second interpretation, and determine the moment at which a listener discovers that a first interpretation was inadequate. A translation can be faithful to the words while being unfaithful to the event those words were supposed to create. Equally, a translator can invent an amusing new line that changes the original target or ethical position. Neither literal fidelity nor the production of laughter is an adequate universal measure.

Consider a different original notice: "The committee on unnecessary meetings has scheduled an emergency meeting to discuss whether it should meet." The joke here is not that a single English word has two meanings. It is the self-defeating relation between the committee's purpose and its behavior. A translator has considerable freedom to change the sentence structure and still preserve that relation. Now suppose the notice reads, "Our new bank has a lot of interest in the neighborhood." The word interest supports both civic concern and a financial sense. A language without an equivalent overlap cannot reproduce the exact joke by substituting dictionary entries. The second example does not prove that humor is inherently untranslatable. It proves only that one route to the joke is blocked.

## What is being transported?

A useful first distinction is between the surface wording and the comic operation. In the bank example, surface wording includes the sound and written shape of the word interest. The operation asks the listener to move from one sense to another. The target might be the bank's cheerful public relations language, or it might be nobody at all: a gentle wordplay designed to advertise a neighborhood business. The social setting determines whether the line feels like affectionate cleverness or corporate evasiveness. The timing consists of leading the listener toward a charitable interpretation and then letting the financial one arrive.

The translator cannot make all these levels equally important at all times. Suppose we need an archival edition of a famous comedian's routine. Preserving the particular language may matter enormously, even if the audience needs a short note to grasp the double meaning. Suppose instead we need a joke in a live theater performance where the actors cannot stop and explain anything. The priority may be the audience's immediate recognition of a reversal. A translation for subtitles faces still other limits: the audience is watching the actors and may be reading at the same moment the image delivers a separate joke.

Dirk Delabastita's 1996 work on wordplay and translation helped make such decisions analyzable rather than treating the pun as a sacred puzzle that must be solved with a single clever equivalent. Wordplay can be kept as wordplay, changed to a different play on words, replaced by non-punning humor, explained, or omitted. These are possibilities, not a ranking from good to bad. The choice becomes meaningful only when we ask which feature the artistic work depends upon. What is lost by a particular substitution? What new relation does it create? Who notices the difference?

For a workshop, imagine translating the museum's impatience sign into a language whose natural way of expressing "eventually" sounds like a solemn commitment rather than a comic refusal. The translator might make the museum say, "The exhibition about waiting is waiting for its opening date." This is not a literal copy of the English but it preserves the institutional self-contradiction. Another version might create a second, unrelated pun on a word for time. Both could be viable, although they would have different rhythms and emotional tones. We should not call either a verified translation into a language we have not actually consulted. These are English-language alternative constructions illustrating competing aims.

## Six questions from linguistic humor theory

In 2002, Salvatore Attardo proposed using the General Theory of Verbal Humor as a guide to translation. The theory describes jokes using six kinds of information, conventionally called knowledge resources. At one level there is the exact language: the sounds, word choices, and syntax. Another is the narrative strategy: whether the joke is told as a riddle, a conversation, a short anecdote, or some other arrangement. The target identifies who or what is being mocked. The situation supplies the objects and people. A logical mechanism describes how the conflicting interpretations are made to coexist or connect. Finally, a script opposition identifies the larger conceptual contrast, such as efficient versus wasteful, sincere versus insincere, or official authority versus practical incompetence.

Attardo's approach suggests that two jokes can differ in wording yet remain close in other respects. If the museum sign becomes "The museum of punctuality will be late," the language, setting, and perhaps the named institution have shifted, but much of the opposition and mechanism remain recognizable. If the sign becomes "Our director never arrives on time because she is careless," the institution is no longer contradicting its own mission in quite the same way. We have introduced a person as a possible target. If it becomes "The museum is closing because the exhibits have escaped," we might still have a comic sign, but the underlying joke is radically different.

The six resources are not six boxes that have to receive identical entries. They are a method for exposing a decision. They also do not establish that one version will cause the same proportion of an audience to laugh. The theory provides a proposed similarity metric, not a universal prediction of actual response. Two listeners with equal linguistic competence might differ in whether they enjoy a joke about bureaucracy. Translation studies and humor studies meet precisely because textual similarity and audience effect are related but not identical problems.

Return to the bank. If the original joke targets a predatory lender, then preserving the target may matter more than preserving the word interest. A target-language line that implies the bank is an innocent, friendly neighbor could turn a satire into an advertisement. If the original is a harmless welcome sign, an aggressively cynical replacement could reverse its social function. The translator is not only searching for a word with two senses. The translator is deciding which comic relationship is worth transporting.

## The danger of the magical equivalent

There is a familiar story that every pun has one perfect solution in another language, if only the translator is talented enough. That is an unfair demand. Languages distribute ambiguity differently. A word may have two linked senses in one language and only one in another; a grammatical gender may be available in one and absent in another; a homophone may exist only within a particular pronunciation. A pun on a person's name may also be a clue about class, region, or prestige. The search for a one-to-one substitute can conceal what the original was doing.

A better working method begins by writing the joke's mechanism without trying to translate it. For the bank joke, write: "Public claim of benevolent attention can be reinterpreted as profit motive because of a double meaning." Then identify what must remain. Does the bank have to be a bank? Does the accusation need to be pointed or gentle? Is the double meaning part of the plot, or merely an optional flourish? If the sentence is a recurring slogan later repeated by a character, the exact verbal form might be essential. If it is a single advertisement on a background wall, a different joke with the same social direction may serve better.

Peter Alan Low's 2011 article on translating jokes and puns argues for creative latitude and practical strategies, rather than assuming that any lost pun is proof of inevitable failure. Yet there is a disagreement worth preserving: one researcher can emphasize the possibility of producing new humor, while another can emphasize that replacing a pun may change an author's precisely chosen verbal texture. That disagreement is not solved by declaring the final audience laughed. A laugh alone does not show that the translation preserved the same characterization, target, or tone.

We can test this on a fictional courtroom comedy. The judge says, "This court is adjourned, although we are still trying to find the court." In the source setting, court can refer to a legal institution and perhaps a physical space. Suppose that ambiguity vanishes in the destination language. The translator can preserve the absurd spatial search: "The hearing is over. The building, apparently, is still missing." Or preserve the legal register and invent a different bureaucratic contradiction. Or leave a more literal line and rely on the visual image of the judge standing in an empty parking lot. The best answer depends on whether the visual, institution, or sound pattern is doing the work.

## Translation as a series of constrained experiments

A translator can create a small test matrix. First write a plain explanation of what the original joke asks the audience to notice. Second, write one candidate that preserves wording as much as possible, one that preserves comic operation, and one that preserves the speaker's social stance. Third, ask bilingual readers to explain what each version led them to infer. The feedback should not be reduced to a single number. Did the person understand the intended target? Did the timing feel natural? Did any version make a sympathetic character sound cruel? Did a reference seem strangely foreign in a way that was fruitful, or merely confusing?

It is important to distinguish these workshop procedures from established experimental findings. We are designing a practical comparison, not claiming there is a scientifically validated three-column recipe for every genre and language pair. A different piece of evidence might matter more: a stage rehearsal, a subtitle reading test, a focus group, or a close analysis of the text. A comedy translation is successful within a purpose and medium, not because a checklist has declared every feature identical.

The same logic applies to comedy that is written in one language with another audience in mind. A bilingual comedian may deliberately leave one phrase untranslated because the audience's partial exclusion is part of the act. An author might want readers to encounter a foreign cultural term rather than replace it with a domestic analogy. Translation can preserve an obstacle when that obstacle is meaningful. A smooth reading experience is not always the artistic goal.

## Try translating the operation, not merely the sentence

Imagine an original scene. A municipal office advertises a new fast-track process. A customer arrives and asks how long it takes. The official replies, "The fast track has been temporarily moved to the slow track while we consider whether speed is appropriate." There are at least four comic ingredients: an official register, a promised improvement, a reversal in procedure, and a character whose confidence survives the contradiction. None requires a particular English homophone. If one language makes the term fast track sound bureaucratic and another makes it sound like athletic coaching, the translator may change the words drastically while preserving the official's self-importance.

Now remove the last element. Let the official sound embarrassed rather than confident. The scene becomes a story about a person trapped in a broken system. It might still be funny, but the comic target has shifted away from complacent authority. Translation is not a neutral transfer belt. The speaker's rhythm and emotional position carry information about whose failure the audience has been invited to notice.

The deepest lesson is that we should stop asking whether jokes are translatable in the abstract. Some relationships survive a language change with little intervention. Some survive only through invention. Some rely on the audience recognizing the loss. Some are so entangled with a name, an accent, or a historical circumstance that any translation becomes a parallel work with its own aesthetic commitments. Good theory helps describe the tradeoff; good craft makes a choice that can be defended. The joke does not merely travel in a suitcase of words. It is reconstructed for a different act of understanding.

## Companion: source map and original examples

All museum, bank, courtroom, and municipal examples are original and illustrative, not records of historical translations. The discussion of Attardo follows the published abstract and available study description; it does not claim a controlled empirical accuracy rate for the GTVH similarity metric. Delabastita's book is an edited collection; Low's recommendations are a position in translation studies, not a universal benchmark.

### Workshop

Choose a short original joke in a language you know. Specify the speaker's aim, the target, the relevant double interpretation and what audience knowledge it presumes. Draft three alternatives for an imagined second audience without pretending to know a language you do not speak. Ask real speakers to assess any actual translation. Explain one feature that you intentionally did not preserve, and why.
