# Can a Joke Be Sincere? Post-Irony, New Sincerity, and Metamodern Comedy

**Section:** Modern schools of thought - post-ironic aesthetics and comedy  
**Status:** Substantive, audio-first manuscript

A performer walks onto a small stage carrying a handmade cardboard moon. She explains that she built it to cheer up a friend who had a difficult week. A light falls off the cardboard. She catches it and says, 'The celestial maintenance budget has been cut.' The audience laughs. She fixes the light, holds up the moon, and finishes the story about her friend. The feeling she expresses is genuine within the performance. The joke does not cancel it. In fact, the moment of comic failure may make the effort feel more believable.

Now imagine the same performer delivering the story entirely as a mock commercial for emotional support products. Every affectionate detail receives an advertisement jingle. The final slogan is, 'Buy a moon today, because relationships are available while supplies last.' That might be an effective satire of commercialized intimacy. But it asks the audience to occupy a different position. The first version permits visible vulnerability. The second surrounds the vulnerability with protective quotation marks.

In discussions of late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century culture, people often use phrases such as post-irony, new sincerity, and metamodernism to describe work that makes fun of its own feelings while still having them. The phrases can be useful, but they are not interchangeable, and they do not form a single experimentally verified school of humor. Some began in literary criticism or broader cultural theory, not in studies of joke perception. They are descriptions of aesthetic problems and historical tendencies. Their value comes from the distinctions they help us draw when a work is funny and emotionally serious at the same time.

The problem itself is older than any of the labels. Comic theater has long combined affection and ridicule; satire can be wounded; farce can be tender. The more recent debate concerns the cultural environment in which irony has become such a familiar language that an earnest statement may sound embarrassing before anyone has criticized it. What kinds of comedy become possible when a writer wants to escape that reflex without pretending the reflex never existed?

## The protection offered by a knowing smile

Irony allows a speaker to separate the words spoken from the attitude communicated. A character walks through a disastrous office renovation and says, 'Exactly how I imagined paradise.' The audience is expected to recognize that the character means something opposed to the literal praise. The distance can create pleasure and precision. It can also protect the speaker. If someone objects, the speaker can claim that nobody was supposed to take the praise seriously.

A knowing comic style can become a habit of preemptive defense. The performer anticipates the audience's skepticism and mocks the performance before anyone else can. Imagine a fictional musician introducing a love song by saying, 'I wrote something deeply meaningful, which is a terrible idea, so here comes the embarrassing part.' The sentence may get a laugh because it recognizes the social risk of sincere expression. It also tells the audience how to judge the song before hearing it. If every emotional turn is accompanied by such a disclaimer, sincerity becomes difficult to sustain.

David Foster Wallace examined the relationship between irony, television, and contemporary American fiction in his 1993 essay E Unibus Pluram: Television and U.S. Fiction. Among other concerns, he argued that a culture saturated with self-aware irony can absorb critical gestures and turn them into familiar entertainment. His diagnosis is historically situated, focused on the television and literary culture he was discussing. It should not be mistaken for an empirical proof about every television viewer or an argument that all irony is destructive.

The tension he describes is a writer's tension. Irony excels at identifying pretense. It can expose a false claim with one glance. But after the pretense is exposed, what allows a writer to make a positive claim without seeming naive? An audience trained to expect a wink may interpret every sincere declaration as another level of parody. The writer can respond with even more elaborate irony, but the escape route may lead back to the original problem.

Our performer with the cardboard moon faces this dilemma in miniature. The line about the maintenance budget acknowledges the ridiculousness of her prop. It gives the audience permission to laugh. She then keeps working with the moon. The feeling survives the joke. That movement is distinct from a performance in which the joke proves that the feeling was foolish all along.

## What critics mean by new sincerity

The phrase new sincerity is used in several contexts, with different histories and associations. In a 2010 scholarly essay, Adam Kelly discusses David Foster Wallace and the New Sincerity in American Fiction. This literary-critical context concerns writers negotiating the legacies of postmodern self-consciousness and attempting forms of genuine ethical or emotional engagement. It is not a claim that all new writing after a certain year became sincere. The concept names an artistic aspiration and a set of problems about authenticity, performance, and the reader's relation to the text.

There is an easy caricature of sincerity: the artist suddenly stops using humor, speaks in an earnest voice, and announces an uplifting lesson. That is not the only available form. Sincerity can involve openly acknowledging awkward motives, making a promise the speaker is capable of breaking, or letting a character care about something the narrative has shown to be imperfect. It can coexist with irony because the artist has no obligation to conceal contradictions.

Consider a fictional scene between two friends who built a tiny community garden beside a railway. One says, 'I believe this garden will improve the neighborhood.' The other looks at three wilted lettuce plants and replies, 'The neighborhood should lower its expectations.' The first friend answers, 'Maybe. I still want to water them.' The comedy comes from the mismatch between an ambitious ideal and a stubbornly small practical effort. The scene need not resolve the mismatch by declaring the project ridiculous or triumphant. The desire to care remains real while the world resists its scale.

A satirical version might end with the garden becoming a corporate branding exercise, proving that idealism is always exploitable. An uncritical sentimental version might have the garden transform the whole city overnight. A more complicated version lets the garden fail sometimes and continue to matter to the characters. It can be funny precisely because their optimism is measured against stubborn material conditions.

A critic might call some of this new sincerity. The label helps if it draws attention to how the work earns trust after acknowledging self-consciousness. It hinders if it becomes a certificate of moral superiority. A relentlessly sincere character can be manipulative or self-righteous. An ironic character can be humane. The work has to make those qualities visible through events, not through an aesthetic label attached from the outside.

## Metamodernism as oscillation

In 2010, Timotheus Vermeulen and Robin van den Akker published Notes on Metamodernism. Their essay examines cultural tendencies in art, architecture, and film. They propose a movement or oscillation between modern enthusiasm and postmodern irony, sometimes describing a sensibility that acts as if ideals are worth pursuing while remaining aware of their instability. Their argument is a cultural-theoretical proposal, not a measurement model of audience laughter.

Oscillation is a useful word here. A work can move between distance and commitment without resting permanently at either pole. Imagine a fictional documentary about a town that builds a lighthouse far inland so people will never forget the sea. The director films the lighthouse with majestic music, then cuts to a farmer asking whether the light can be turned off because it keeps his chickens awake. The project is grand and absurd. The farmer's complaint is practical and deflating. The film can return to the lighthouse at night, when it lights a path for someone walking home. We may now feel both the foolishness and the beauty of the gesture.

A purely debunking ending would reveal that the lighthouse was financed through a corrupt scheme, rendering the dream a disguise for exploitation. A purely affirmative ending would ask us to forget that an inland lighthouse is a peculiar expense. An oscillating form can preserve the contradiction. The gesture was silly; it also offered something to a person. The comic and emotional registers shape one another.

The concept has been popular well beyond the cases originally examined by Vermeulen and van den Akker. That popularity makes care important. Calling any artwork with one joke and one sincere moment 'metamodern' turns a historically specific critical proposal into an empty synonym for mixed tone. To use the idea seriously, ask how the work sustains its movement between incompatible but compelling attitudes. Does it use ridicule to test an ideal without erasing it? Does the audience experience hope as an active choice made under uncertainty, rather than as an uncomplicated final answer?

Our cardboard moon fits this analysis only if the whole performance develops a meaningful relation between embarrassment and care. A fallen lightbulb followed by a sentimental anecdote is not automatically a metamodern masterpiece. If the failure changes how we perceive the performer's effort, and if the effort changes how we perceive the absurd prop, then the interplay may illuminate why this critical vocabulary exists.

## Post-irony is not the end of irony

The phrase post-irony is used loosely in criticism and popular discussion. It can refer to earnestness after a period of pervasive irony, deliberate ambiguity about whether a statement is sincere, or a mode that layers sincere feeling inside exaggerated or absurd presentation. Its meanings are contested. There is no reliable single test that identifies all post-ironic comedy, and writers need not accept the label to use the techniques associated with it.

Consider a fictional motivational speaker who declares, 'I genuinely believe in each of you, including the person who stole my parking space.' At first the audience may hear a sarcastic insult. The speaker then explains that the parking-space thief helped them learn patience, admits that the lesson was miserable, and still thanks the thief for something entirely unrelated. The emotional stakes grow more complicated. The speaker may be both petty and generous. A listener has to keep revising the expected target of the joke.

The mode can also fail in an instructive way. Suppose a writer gives a character an obviously ridiculous statement but never indicates whether any of it is held sincerely. Some viewers may enjoy the ambiguity. Others may feel that the author is avoiding responsibility for having a viewpoint. Ambiguity can be artistic material; it can also be a method of preserving deniability. The difference depends on whether the uncertainty produces meaningful dramatic tension or merely prevents the speaker from being criticized.

One of the most difficult questions for such comedy is whether the audience can care about a character who is aware of being ridiculous. Awareness can humanize, but constant self-commentary can prevent action. A character who says, 'This is probably an emotionally manipulative gesture,' before every kind act may never perform an act that the audience can evaluate directly. At some point the story must allow a person to take a risk without first securing the status of the smartest observer in the room.

## The craft of letting a joke and a feeling coexist

Let's build a short scene in three versions. Two siblings attempt to repair a cheap toy robot that belonged to their grandfather. In version one, the scene is pure parody. The robot repeatedly announces, 'I have achieved independent consciousness,' and immediately runs into a wall. The siblings trade jokes about grandiose technology. The humor is clear, but the family relationship remains mostly ornamental.

In version two, the siblings argue seriously about the toy's sentimental value. One insists that it should be preserved. The other believes it is beyond repair. The scene is earnest, but the robot's mechanical absurdity no longer contributes much. We have preserved emotion by removing the comic complication rather than integrating it.

In version three, the robot struggles forward and announces, 'I have achieved independent consciousness.' One sibling laughs. The other says, 'He loved that line.' We learn their grandfather repaired the same defective toy every winter, never improving the motor enough to stop the collision. The robot hits the wall again. This time both siblings reach to catch it, using a movement they learned as children. The joke has become evidence of a relationship. The prop's repeated failure does not undermine the memory; it gives the memory a physical rhythm.

Notice how little explanation is needed if the scene is staged carefully. The audience sees the collision, hears the remembered line, and watches the simultaneous gesture. There is no need for a character to announce that humor and grief can coexist. The writing has allowed two interpretations of the same event to occupy the stage. The laugh is still available; the affection has acquired a form.

A useful revision exercise is to identify where a script protects its characters against embarrassment. Underline lines that announce a feeling and immediately joke about how foolish it sounds. Ask whether those protective jokes earn their place. Some may reveal the character's fear of being seen. Others may simply repeat the author's nervousness. Remove one. Let the character say the sincere part plainly. If the scene becomes sentimental, restore a concrete obstacle rather than automatically restoring the verbal wink.

Another exercise starts from a wholly sarcastic scene. A fictional town holds a competition for the most useless public bench. Every bench is installed facing a brick wall. The judges deliver increasingly lavish praise. The satire is straightforward: officials celebrate a service nobody can use. Introduce one resident who sits on a bench every evening because it is the only place they can hear birds in the courtyard. Do not rewrite the officials as noble or pretend the benches were well planned. Allow one person's attachment to complicate the target. The laughter may remain, but the people affected by the situation stop being mere evidence in a joke.

Then reverse the method. Start with an openly tender scene, such as friends building a ridiculous birthday cake. Give the cake a technical problem: every layer slides toward a different point of the compass. Let the characters try to solve the problem with increasingly elaborate procedures. The physical comedy should express their different temperaments. A practical friend brings a ruler. An ambitious friend draws a load-bearing diagram. A third keeps eating the fallen frosting. The scene's feeling emerges from their willingness to keep helping, not from a speech about friendship.

## The audience still has to be able to tell what is at stake

A mixed comic tone requires careful orientation. If a joke follows a painful disclosure too quickly, the audience may hear the work retracting the disclosure. If a sincere moment arrives without preparation, it can feel like a demand for emotion that the story has not earned. The issue is not a fixed number of seconds between the laugh and the feeling. It is whether the characters' behavior gives us reason to believe both.

Performance matters. The cardboard moon might produce a cheap laugh if the performer drops it and rolls her eyes. It may produce tenderness if she repairs it gently while continuing the story. It might produce bitter irony if she throws it away the moment the audience stops applauding. Each action interprets the prop. A theory of tone cannot replace attention to the actor's body, the audience's expectation, or the time needed for a scene to change its direction.

There is also a political dimension to the demand for sincerity. Audiences do not grant every performer equal permission to be emotionally exposed. Some are expected to be cheerful, resilient, or perpetually ironic; others are encouraged to speak with authority about personal pain. The desire for sincerity can become another social demand. Criticism should ask who is allowed to risk sentiment, and who will be mocked for making the same attempt.

The labels may help identify tendencies, but they cannot settle such questions in advance. New sincerity names a problem of commitment after pervasive self-consciousness. Metamodernism proposes a cultural oscillation between enthusiasm and irony. Post-irony is a flexible, contested term for several ways of inhabiting that tension. None is a substitute for describing what a work actually does to an audience over time.

At the end of our opening performance, the cardboard moon finally lights up. The friend for whom it was made is in the audience. The performer says, 'I know this is an absurd object.' She looks at the moon. 'But it is bright enough to see from the back row.' The audience may laugh at the modesty of the claim. It may also understand why somebody built the thing. That combination does not need an exemption from irony. It needs a story in which an absurd object can still be worth carrying onto the stage.
