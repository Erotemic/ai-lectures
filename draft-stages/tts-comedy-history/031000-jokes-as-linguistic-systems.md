# Jokes as Linguistic Systems: Raskin, Attardo, and the Architecture of an Interpretation

**Status:** Substantive, audio-first manuscript

Imagine a museum guide showing a visitor an exhibit called The World's Most Reliable Clock. The visitor asks whether it keeps perfect time. The guide replies, "Perfectly. We set it to the correct time every Friday."

There is a small task for the listener in that exchange. A reliable clock ordinarily keeps the correct time as minutes pass. The guide treats reliability as the ability to display the correct time at one arranged moment. The sentence does not contain a nonsense word. Nobody falls over. The guide's answer is grammatically ordinary. Yet the meaning of the claim has changed underneath us, and we can reconstruct the change almost instantly.

What is a joke made of, if the crucial event happens inside interpretation? That question led linguists in the late twentieth century to develop models of verbal humor. Their central ambition differed from a comedian's ambition. Rather than ask how to make a room laugh tonight, they asked how a text can support incompatible readings, how a listener moves between them, and how jokes can be compared systematically. The distinction matters. A theory of a joke's structure is not a theory of why everybody will enjoy it.

## The intellectual change

Older theories of humor were often introduced as rival grand explanations: superiority, relief, incongruity. Those theories can tell us what an amusement means or what mental response it involves. But they can be surprisingly vague when given a single short joke. If we say the clock joke is incongruous, we still need to say which expectations conflict and how the last sentence rearranges them. If we say it mocks incompetence, we still need to explain how the words produce that judgment.

Linguistics supplied a more detailed toolkit. During the twentieth century, scholars described language not merely as strings of words but as an activity involving structured knowledge. We understand a sentence by activating expectations about situations. When someone says, "The customer returned the book," we imagine a shop or library, an object previously borrowed or purchased, and an interaction between people. Much of this knowledge is not spoken. We supply it because the situation seems recognizable.

Victor Raskin's Semantic Script Theory of Humor, developed in his 1985 book, applies this observation to jokes. A script is a structured body of background knowledge: the familiar sequence, roles, and expectations for a situation. A joke can activate two scripts that overlap in the words but oppose each other in some important respect. In the clock exhibit, one script is accurate continuous timekeeping. Another is accurate display at a selected appointment. The shared phrase "correct time" permits both. The guide's final sentence forces the audience to abandon a sensible interpretation of "reliable."

That account is more precise than saying merely that a surprise occurred. It also suggests a craft method. First establish a plausible script. Then design a later sentence that makes a competing script available and changes the significance of earlier words. But there are limits to the method. Two opposed interpretations are not automatically funny. In a detective story, a clue may support two interpretations and produce suspense. In a medical consultation, ambiguity may produce alarm. Interpretation is part of the mechanism, not a guarantee of comic amusement.

## What an opposition really does

Let's invent another example. A librarian puts up a sign reading "Quiet hours are strictly enforced." A patron asks what happens if someone talks. The librarian says, "We make an announcement over the loudspeaker."

The joke overlaps a script about preserving silence and a script about publicly administering rules. The institution tries to achieve quiet by creating noise. One source of pleasure is the quick recognition that an apparently sensible rule has defeated itself. But the deeper point is that the institution's response remains plausible. We have seen organizations that solve one problem by producing another. The joke borrows credibility from real procedures.

Suppose the last line were instead, "We turn the entire building into a giant cucumber." That would certainly violate an expectation. It might amuse in a surreal context. But it no longer produces the same neat reinterpretation of institutional enforcement. A script model helps identify what changed. The earlier version has a causally intelligible rule, an unforeseen consequence, and a target: procedural self-defeat. The cucumber version abandons the causal structure. It asks the audience to enjoy absurdity, image, or sheer disproportion.

This is why a structural theory needs to be tested on a range of humor forms. Puns offer relatively sharp ambiguities. Short narrative jokes often have clear turns. Character comedy, comic essays, satire, and improvised conversation can distribute their competing interpretations over minutes rather than a single line. Sometimes spectators know two interpretations from the beginning while the character knows only one. A theory designed around a punch line may not fully capture that dramatic arrangement.

## Six resources for describing verbal jokes

Salvatore Attardo and Victor Raskin expanded the approach in a 1991 paper, proposing what became known as the General Theory of Verbal Humor. They described six kinds of information, commonly called knowledge resources, that shape a verbal joke. The names are worth learning because they give us a practical way to compare variations without pretending to have discovered one universal humor formula.

First is the script opposition: which competing interpretive worlds does the joke bring together? For the loudspeaker librarian, quiet enforcement confronts noisy enforcement. Second is the logical mechanism: what apparent reasoning allows the opposition to connect? Here, a rule applied literally defeats its own purpose. A different joke might use a false analogy, an exaggerated inference, or a misunderstanding.

Third is the situation: the setting and its participants, objects, and activities. Our library contains a rule, a patron, and a loudspeaker. Fourth is the target, if there is one: the person, institution, behavior, or idea being mocked. The library's bureaucracy is the likely target; jokes without a specific target are possible too.

Fifth is the narrative strategy: the form in which the joke is organized. Is it a question and answer? A brief anecdote? A dialogue? A riddle? Sixth is the language: the particular wording, word order, and sound of the actual text. The theory lets us ask which resource changes when we rewrite a joke. Does moving the story from a library to a hospital change only its setting? Or does it also change the ethical stakes and, with them, the target and the mechanism?

The six categories are not a checklist that manufactures laughter. They are a representation scheme. Their value is diagnostic. If a translation preserves the situation and target but loses the double meaning of a word, the language resource may not survive. If an adaptation changes the mocked institution, the audience may attach a different social meaning even when the punch line's structure is nearly identical.

## A rewrite laboratory

Let us return to the clock museum and perform a controlled experiment. Version one ends, "We set it to the correct time every Friday." The joke depends on a clash between continuous accuracy and occasional calibration. Version two ends, "It has never been wrong in the museum's annual report." Now the mechanism has shifted toward institutional measurement and selective reporting. The museum can hide the clock's failure behind its reporting procedure. We have moved from a conceptual misunderstanding toward satire.

Version three ends, "We don't allow anyone to look at it on the other six days." The clock's actual performance is no longer the only issue. A person is using information control to manufacture the appearance of success. Our target is not an innocent error but a deliberate practice. The joke may become sharper, or less endearing, depending on the narrator's tone.

Version four removes the guide's dialogue entirely: "The museum's most reliable clock was right once a week, by appointment." It is compact and could work as a sentence in a comic essay. But we lose the exchange in which the visitor asks an ordinary question and the guide solemnly confirms the wrong interpretation. The dialogue helped sustain the fiction that an institutional representative believed the explanation. Removing it changes the narrative strategy and the character relationship.

The exercise teaches a subtle lesson. It is not enough to locate the two scripts. The comic force often depends on who believes which interpretation, when the audience recognizes the conflict, and which consequences are left implicit. The script model supplies a skeleton. Performance supplies breath and social life.

## The critics: conversation is not a sealed joke

One objection to structural joke theories is that they tend to treat a joke as an object with a stable text and a recipient whose task is to decode it. Actual conversation is interactive. A person can deliver a remark as a joke and discover that the other person does not take it as one. Listeners may interrupt, withhold laughter, respond sarcastically, or redirect the target. A sentence that counts as a joke among friends may become an insult in a workplace meeting. The words do not carry their own social permissions.

Linguistic pragmatics studies language as action in context: what speakers imply, what listeners infer, and how an utterance changes a relationship. Humor researchers working with conversation analysis ask when people laugh, whether laughter is invited or resisted, and how participants manage the fact that not everyone shares an interpretation. That is a different question from classifying a written joke. Both questions are legitimate, and neither replaces the other.

A related limitation is that verbal-joke categories may not describe a silent physical gag especially well. Imagine a performer carefully moving a chair away from a puddle, then sitting in the puddle instead. The audience understands the action, but no linguistic ambiguity is required. The comic event involves expectations about bodies, objects, and attention. We might extend the notion of scripts to cover it, but an extension that explains everything can become hard to test. A useful theory should be able to say what evidence would count against it.

The same care applies to cultural translation. A listener might perfectly understand the museum clock example but find it too predictable to laugh. Another listener might need familiarity with bureaucratic displays of excellence. A theory of meaning cannot infer comic pleasure from meaning alone. It needs help from psychology and sociology, which study different parts of the event.

## A writing method with modest claims

Here is a practical exercise you can use with an original scene. Start by writing a plain description of an ordinary situation in one sentence. "A committee is measuring the quality of public meetings." Record the script that makes this situation sensible: agendas, attendance, minutes, decisions. Now devise a second interpretation of the committee's goal. Suppose its members measure quality entirely by the number of meetings held. Their proposed solution to poor meetings is to schedule more meetings.

Draft three endings: one delivered as a statistic, one as a solemn announcement, and one as dialogue between a skeptical employee and the chair. Make each version specific enough to imply a person who would sincerely accept its logic. Then identify the six knowledge resources. Which are unchanged? Which shift? Next read each version aloud. You may find that a structurally elegant joke dies because the second script is obvious too early, because the target has no personality, or because the wording explains what the listener already inferred.

Finally, try making one character's mistaken interpretation partly defensible. Perhaps meetings are the only place where isolated workers can talk to one another. Now the committee's bad metric conceals a real human benefit. Comedy often improves when an error is not pure stupidity but a rational strategy taken past its proper limit.

The modern linguistic school contributed something durable: it made the internal architecture of verbal jokes available for close analysis. The disagreements that followed were equally valuable. A joke is not merely a code hidden inside a sentence. It is an invitation to an audience, an event in a social setting, and sometimes a work of art. If we can describe the two meanings without losing the people who move between them, we have learned an important part of what a joke does.

## Companion: the six knowledge resources

| Resource | Question for revision |
| --- | --- |
| Script opposition | Which two interpretive frames are put into tension? |
| Logical mechanism | By what inference or distortion does the joke connect them? |
| Situation | Which characters, props, roles and institutions are needed? |
| Target | Who or what is ridiculed, if anyone? |
| Narrative strategy | Dialogue, riddle, anecdote, caption, or other presentation? |
| Language | Which exact words, order or sound make the version work? |

The order and names derive from Attardo and Raskin's theoretical framework, not from a prescriptive comedic recipe. Museum, library, and committee examples are original inventions.
