# Satire After the Feed: Memes, Irony, and the Networked Public

**Course:** Comedy: Theory, History, and Craft  
**Section:** Modern schools of thought — digital media and public satire  
**Status:** Substantive, audio-first manuscript

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A fictional city posts an official photograph of a magnificent new pedestrian crossing. The crossing has been built across a dead-end road and leads directly into a brick wall. A resident copies the image, writes "At last, a direct route to progress," and shares it with friends. Within a day, other residents have added maps, mock travel guides, and imaginary reviews. Some of the images are sharp criticisms of municipal planning. Others are playful variations that have little to do with the city. A few circulate without any indication that the original crossing is real.

The comedy began with a physical incongruity. But its subsequent life is harder to explain as one joke performed for one audience. People imitate its form, transform its target, and distribute it into groups with different knowledge. Some recipients laugh without knowing the local issue. Others think the photograph is fabricated. Officials may share a version intended to prove that they have a sense of humor. The same object becomes satire, civic conversation, and entertainment, with no single author controlling its meaning.

What kind of comedy theory can follow an object through that process? Research on internet memes, networked media, public satire, and online antagonism gives us tools. It also forces us to separate the structure of a funny image from the social systems that make one version visible and another invisible.

## A meme is a family of transformations

Communication scholar Limor Shifman's Memes in Digital Culture, published in 2013, examined internet memes as groups of related digital items that circulate through imitation and transformation. That definition matters. It asks us not to treat a meme as simply one image with a caption, nor as a mysterious unit that travels unchanged from brain to brain. The object of study includes relations among versions and the practices of their makers and audiences.

In the pedestrian-crossing example, the first image is not the whole phenomenon. A second user adds a destination sign. A third makes a mock booking website. A fourth adapts the format to criticize a different town's public project. The group of versions shares recognizable features while allowing people to signal their own interpretations. Copying is not just repetition. It can be a public act of belonging, criticism, or playful display of ingenuity.

Shifman also distinguishes the spread of a single viral item from the participatory transformation characteristic of many memes. The difference helps explain why a joke written for a theater may not behave like a joke on a social network. A stage routine is usually controlled through a performer's timing and order. A meme invites rearrangement by people who may never encounter the first version. One can study which elements survive across copies, but the sequence in which a particular viewer sees them is less predictable.

A writer who wants to use this form should therefore ask two questions instead of one. Is the original unit intelligible and interesting? And does the form offer other people a productive space in which to make variations? A template that is too tightly tied to one narrow context may not travel far. A template so generic that nothing remains recognizable may lose the pleasure of shared invention.

## Satire has a target, but circulation can lose it

Satire is often described as comedy aimed at vice, hypocrisy, folly, or abuse of power. That is helpful, but not enough. A satire can imply a target without naming it, and the audience must infer the relationship between the fictional world and actual practices. In our crossing joke, the brick wall helps expose a planning failure. But if later users apply the image to every kind of obstruction, the target may grow more diffuse.

Suppose a resident creates a variation showing the mayor celebrating the opening with a ceremonial ribbon. It makes a pointed claim about misplaced official priorities. Another version shows a fictional astronaut crossing into a cartoon planet. The second may be more popular, but it no longer argues much about city government. Popularity and political precision are separate properties. The first version may make a specific grievance legible to neighbors; the second may reward broad recognition and quick amusement.

A further difficulty arises when a satirical item escapes its original audience. Someone who does not know the crossing is a joke may treat the image as evidence that a real city has built an absurd project. Someone else may assume the image is fabricated and dismiss a genuine problem. A creator can reduce ambiguity through labels, context, and clear targets, but cannot control every reuse. The distinction between intentionally fictional satire and factual reporting becomes important when circulation is detached from its source.

This is not a defect specific to digital media. Cartoons, pamphlets, and orally repeated jokes have long been reinterpreted. What changes with networked platforms is the speed, scale, variety, and visibility of adaptation, along with the use of engagement signals to determine which versions receive attention.

## Irony is a relationship with a reader

Irony depends on a distance between what is said and what is meant or understood. In the phrase "At last, a direct route to progress," the apparent praise contrasts with the image's evident failure. A reader who recognizes that contrast hears satire. A reader who sees only the caption without the image might not.

Online culture adds another difficulty: irony can be performed repeatedly until the creator's commitment becomes hard to identify. A person may say something extreme as parody, repeat it as a joke within a group, and later receive approval from people who believe it literally. The original speaker can retreat behind "I was only joking," while the audience may have understood a serious invitation. The label irony does not settle what social effects an utterance has.

Terms such as post-irony, meta-irony, and sincerity have been used in different communities to describe shifts in this relationship. They are not stable, universally agreed academic categories. It is more precise to ask, for a particular performance, which layers of belief are in play. Does a speaker say the opposite of what they believe? Do they mock the habit of saying the opposite? Do they sincerely endorse an absurd proposition while adopting the appearance of a joke? Does the audience know enough to distinguish these possibilities?

Imagine the town's communications office publishing a fake congratulatory post about the brick wall, complete with deliberately terrible slogans. Residents may interpret it as an admission of failure, a clumsy attempt at self-awareness, or a way to avoid accountability. The text may be identical in each reading. The institutional relationship is not.

A useful writer's test is to remove the presumed sympathetic audience. Would a stranger still know what is being criticized? If not, would the resulting ambiguity be a deliberate artistic resource or a predictable source of misrepresentation? There is no universally correct answer. Some comedy depends on insider knowledge. The point is to understand what that dependence costs.

## When provocation becomes a media system

In This Is Why We Can't Have Nice Things, published in 2015, media scholar Whitney Phillips investigated online trolling and its relationship to mainstream culture. Her argument challenges the easy story that trolls are a completely separate, deviant population who alone cause harmful online behavior. She examines how attention-seeking, sensationalism, and cultural habits can connect antagonistic online practices to broader media systems.

This perspective complicates the notion that any upsetting joke is simply edgy comedy. A person can deliberately provoke outrage to obtain an audience. Media organizations may then magnify the provocation, generating attention for the act and the story about the act. Participants can be rewarded for creating conflict even when many observers dislike what they did. The resulting cycle is not described adequately by asking whether the initial statement was clever.

The mechanism is visible in our fictional city if a user falsely claims that the crossing was intentionally built to punish a disliked group of residents. The claim circulates as a meme. Reporters amplify angry replies. Users add increasingly inflammatory captions. Some people treat the whole episode as an elaborate joke; others believe it is a factual revelation. The resulting publicity can eclipse the actual infrastructure problem. A comic format has become a tool for moving attention, and not necessarily toward accurate understanding.

This does not mean that satire should avoid difficult subjects or that laughter always undermines truth. It means that a creator who depends on circulation should consider what the artifact communicates when detached from its explanatory frame. The ethics of distribution is not exhausted by the ethics of intention. Nor should responsibility be assigned exclusively to the individual creator when platforms and institutions shape the reward system.

## Networks change timing

Stage comedy controls timing through a performer, a pause, an audience's shared attention, and the ability to respond immediately. Printed satire controls timing through the order of sentences and images. Networked comedy often delegates timing to feeds, reposts, and viewers who have arrived halfway through a conversation.

A joke may be received as a setup by one person and as a punch line by another who has seen earlier variations. A latecomer may encounter the parody before the original, making the original seem like an imitation. A callback can operate across hundreds of posts instead of within one script. A format can become exhausted when every possible variation has been made; then a deliberately bad or anticlimactic variation may become funny as an observation about exhaustion itself.

This creates a craft question. What must a viewer know for this particular post to work? One version may need no context: a wall at the end of a crossing is enough. Another may require knowing the city's earlier promises. A third may require familiarity with the jokes about those promises. The writer can layer jokes for different audiences, but each additional layer increases the risk that only insiders can read the work.

The online performer may also be rewarded for volume and immediate response. Where a stage comic can refine a routine over many nights, a creator working in fast-moving feeds may publish rapid variations before an idea cools. That can encourage playful collaboration, but it can also privilege speed over verification or craft. Again, there is no single destiny written into the technology. Platform incentives and creator choices interact.

## What does a laugh accomplish politically?

It is tempting to assume that satire exposing an injustice must weaken it. The evidence is more complicated. Laughter may inform an audience or help people share criticism. It can also produce cynicism, confirm a group's existing view, or allow powerful targets to appear approachable by joining in the joke. The effects depend on the work, audience, setting, and what happens afterward. A critic who claims a single satirical image changed public opinion needs evidence beyond the image's popularity.

Consider two variations of the city crossing. One invites residents to submit examples of inaccessible routes, creating a conversation about practical changes. Another simply labels every public employee incompetent. Both might be funny. The first potentially connects satire to specific problems and remedies; the second may invite a broader rejection of public institutions. Those are plausible consequences to investigate, not outcomes guaranteed by the form.

A good satire can also avoid becoming a disguised instruction manual for how the audience should feel. The audience should have room to discover the contradiction. If every caption explains that officials are hypocrites, the comic device may become a lecture wearing a costume. If nothing in the image identifies the target, the work may be entertaining without functioning as meaningful critique. The art lies in making a relationship visible while leaving the spectator work to do.

## A networked-writing workshop

Choose a fictional public problem that can be described without harming an identifiable individual. For example, a new city information kiosk directs visitors to another kiosk, which directs them back to the first. First write a conventional single-panel joke about the circular directions. The joke should work for a viewer with no background knowledge.

Next devise a template that other writers could adapt: "The official route to blank is blank." Test five versions. Some should be specific to institutions, some purely absurd, and at least one should challenge the format itself. Identify which pieces of the original are stable and which invite transformation. You have moved from a one-off joke to a small family of related items.

Now write two captions for the same image. The first should make the criticism unmistakable. The second should use strong irony and assume a sympathetic audience. Imagine the captions are reposted without the image, or sent to someone who has never heard of the fictional kiosk. What could they reasonably infer? Which version is more portable? Which is sharper? What information would need to travel with it to preserve the target?

Finally, invent an institutional response. The kiosk department adopts the meme as its new advertising campaign. How would the joke change if the target starts selling merchandise based on the criticism? Can the original writers recover the satirical force through a new observation, or has the institution successfully absorbed it? This is a creative problem, not a one-sentence moral rule.

The early twenty-first century did not invent irony, satire, or collaborative comic invention. It reorganized their circulation. Modern media theory helps explain why a joke cannot be studied only as a finished text and why neither loud laughter nor widespread sharing is sufficient evidence of successful critique. A meme is a practice of transformation. Satire is a relation to an object of criticism. Irony is an interpretive relation. And a platform is a system that shapes who sees what. Keeping these distinct lets us understand comedy that is simultaneously artistic, participatory, political, and commercially useful—sometimes to people its creators meant to mock.

## Companion: a circulation audit for satire

| Question | What to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Standalone meaning | Can a first-time viewer understand the primary joke? |
| Transformability | What recognizable elements invite remix? |
| Target | Is the satire aimed at a decision, institution, group, or vulnerable person? |
| Context loss | What changes when the image or caption circulates alone? |
| Institutional reuse | Can the object of critique adopt the joke and neutralize its target? |
| Evidence | Does popularity tell us anything about whether attitudes or actions changed? |

All city and kiosk examples are invented. Digital-culture scholarship supports the conceptual distinctions, not the fictional events.
