# Model fit and build the pattern

## Patterns, Ease, and Geometry

You measure a person around the chest and sew a fabric tube with exactly that circumference. The tube might fit while the person stands still, yet become uncomfortable or impossible to raise the arms in. The body is not a cylinder, and clothing must accommodate changing shape, bending joints and breathing. Pattern design solves a three-dimensional geometric problem using two-dimensional pieces of fabric, seam allowances, darts, pleats, curves and deliberate extra room called ease.

A paper pattern is a proposed set of surfaces that will join into a shape. Each piece has edges intended to meet other edges; markings encode orientation and assembly. Knowing how these pieces become a garment is more valuable than memorizing which number on a commercial envelope is your 'size.' Pattern size is a manufacturer's mapping between measurements and its draft, not a universal physical property of the wearer.

### Wearing ease and design ease are different

Wearing ease is the extra space that permits ordinary movement and breathing. Design ease is additional fullness chosen for style: a loose coat, wide trousers, or an exaggerated sleeve. Layering ease adds space for garments underneath. These categories overlap in real clothing, but they give us useful language. A fitted woven shirt and an oversized knit sweater can have very different relationships between body measurements and finished measurements.

Ease need not be distributed uniformly. A sleeve must accommodate arm rotation; a trouser seat needs space when sitting; a waist can be snug while the hip area requires room. Stretch fabric changes the problem because it can elongate under load. Some knit garments are intentionally drafted with negative ease, meaning the finished garment is smaller than the body dimension it covers and relies on appropriate stretch and recovery. Applying a woven pattern to a stretchy knit—or the reverse—without considering material behavior may produce an entirely different fit.

A garment can have plenty of circumference and still be uncomfortable if the armscye, crotch curve or shoulder slope is wrong. In other words, fit is not only about size but about where material is located when the body changes posture.

### Flat fabric meets curved anatomy

Imagine wrapping a sheet of paper around a sphere. The paper wrinkles or gaps because a flat sheet cannot cover a doubly curved surface perfectly without cuts, stretching or folds. Fabric is more compliant than paper, but the geometric challenge remains.

Darts remove a wedge of fabric and redirect fullness toward a curved area, commonly the bust or waist. Seams allow multiple shaped pieces to join along curves. Gathers, pleats and tucks manage additional material rather than cutting all of it away. A waistband, yoke and collar are examples of patterns that control fit and appearance by redistributing shape into multiple connected surfaces.

The grainline matters. Woven fabric typically behaves differently along warp, weft and bias directions. Two visually identical pattern pieces cut in different orientations may stretch, drape or twist differently. A long narrow panel cut off-grain can develop a persistent spiral after washing. Knit loops introduce further directional behavior. Pattern arrows are functional instructions about textile mechanics, not decorative markings.

### What seam allowances actually do

A seam line is where the pieces are intended to join. The cut edge lies some distance away: the seam allowance. If you measure a finished garment from raw cut edges and ignore the allowance, you will overestimate its final dimensions. If you 'fix' a tight garment by reducing seam allowance, you may change strength, finish and the available adjustment margin. Pattern alterations should distinguish moving a seam line from changing the allowance attached to it.

Notches, dots, balance marks and match points help align curved edges that have different shapes before sewing. Two curved pieces can have equal seam-line lengths even though their raw edge lengths look different. Walking the seam lines on paper helps check whether pieces are intended to match or whether one side includes deliberate easing or gathers. An experienced maker checks the geometry before forcing fabric under the presser foot.

### Test fit is an experiment

A toile, or muslin, is a trial garment made from inexpensive material with similar relevant behavior to the final fabric. It turns a pattern hypothesis into observable fit. You can pin or mark changes without risking the finished material. The test should cover movement, not merely appearance in a mirror. Sit, reach, bend and turn. Observe horizontal strain lines, vertical drag lines, twisting seams, restricted movement and unwanted pooling.

A common trap is changing several measurements at once. If the shoulder is too long and the sleeve binds, a huge side-seam adjustment may obscure the actual cause. A better alteration process changes one plausible geometric problem, tests again, and records which piece and seam line moved. The same principle appears in measurement and mechanical troubleshooting: preserve evidence of what the change actually did.

### Build a pattern-reading habit

Take a familiar shirt. Identify its front, back, sleeve, collar and any cuff pieces. Imagine flattening them into separate patterns. Where is fullness created or removed? Which seams support movement? Which grainlines matter for hanging correctly? Compare its finished measurements with body measurements at the chest and waist, but do not infer fit from those two numbers alone. The armhole, shoulder and sleeve geometry can dominate comfort.

Clothing construction becomes far more understandable once a pattern is seen as a model with explicit assumptions about body shape, movement, fabric behavior and assembly. Good sewing is the process of testing that model and revising it before the final cloth is cut.

## Drafting and Adjusting Simple Garment Patterns

We can now turn that model of fit into a draft. The fabric must move around the chest, under the arms, across the shoulders, and through the motion of reaching or sitting. Pattern drafting translates those three-dimensional body measurements and motion needs into two-dimensional pieces that can be cut and joined.

This begins with geometry, but not geometry alone. Woven fabric has different behavior along the lengthwise grain, crosswise grain, and bias. Knit fabric stretches through its loop structure, often in more than one direction. A pattern drafted for stable woven cotton will not necessarily fit or hang correctly in a highly elastic knit. Material behavior belongs in the drafting decision from the start.

### Measurements describe more than one dimension

A bust or chest circumference is a useful number, but it does not describe shoulder slope, posture, torso length or where fullness is distributed. Two people with the same circumference can need different shapes. For simple garments, start with measurements that locate the major structure: body circumference at intended levels, shoulder span, torso length, arm length, and target garment length. Mark the natural reference points consistently, use a relaxed posture, and document whether a measurement is of the body or a well-fitting garment.

A finished garment needs ease. Wearing ease is extra space required for breathing and movement. Design ease produces the intended silhouette. A close-fitting stretch garment may use less positive ease or deliberately negative ease, but only if the fabric's recovery and the garment's purpose support it. Do not add an arbitrary number of inches everywhere and expect a consistent fit.

### Begin with a block, then create a style

A simple pattern block represents body dimensions and essential shaping. A loose rectangular top can start from width and length, with a neck opening and shoulder line. A more fitted garment may need darts, curved seams, or panels to accommodate differences in circumference between neighboring regions. The complexity should follow the fit required, not the idea that every pattern must look sophisticated.

Take a basic skirt. The waist circumference is smaller than the hip circumference for many wearers. A rectangular tube sized to the hip might fall from the waist unless elastic, gathering, darts or shaped seams control the difference. If a woven skirt is shaped with darts, those darts remove excess fabric near the waist while preserving room around the hips. The pattern's geometry performs a physical job. A design with elastic can distribute that difference differently.

### Seam allowances are construction space, not fit space

A sewing line defines where pieces join. A seam allowance is fabric extending beyond that line to permit construction and finishing. If you accidentally include the seam allowance in the intended finished measurement, the garment can be too small. Label grain line, cut quantity, notches, fold lines, seam allowances and directional pieces. A pattern that fits only when held upside down or mirrored has a communication failure.

A seam consumes width when sewn. For two fabric panels joined on both sides, a finished circumference is the sum of panel widths measured between stitching lines, not between raw cut edges. The exact arithmetic changes when a design uses folds, facings, darts or stretch. Write the intended finished dimensions on the plan, then add construction allowances deliberately.

### Test with inexpensive material

A muslin or test garment reveals interactions that the flat drawing cannot show. It need not be beautiful. It should approximate the drape and stretch of the final fabric closely enough for the fit decision. Mark the center front and back, waist and intended hem; examine whether seams hang vertically and whether the wearer can move naturally. Check arm reach, sitting and bending, not merely standing still in front of a mirror.

Fit corrections should be systematic. A horizontal strain line may signal insufficient length or an incompatible shape; a diagonal drag line may indicate tension toward a restricted area. These are clues, not universal rules. Pin one change, check movement, and transfer the successful correction back to the paper pattern. Preserve the original so you can distinguish the base draft from a particular fitting experiment.

### Fabric changes the outcome

A loose woven fabric may stretch or distort along bias seams. A thick fabric changes how pleats, facings and hems lie. A knit may require a different seam treatment and allowance. Before cutting the final fabric, check grain, nap, print direction, shrinkage expectations, and the way the material behaves after laundering according to its care requirements. Patterning in the correct grain direction often matters as much as the accuracy of the cut outline.

A paper pattern is a model, not an oracle. It communicates expected shape under assumptions about body proportions, material behavior and construction. A successful garment emerges from drafting, trial assembly, observation and revision. The reward is not merely making clothes without a commercial pattern; it is learning to reason from the person and fabric to the seams that allow them to work together.

## Garment Alterations and Fit Diagnosis

The pattern’s assumptions can now be tested against the garment on a moving body.

A shirt pulls across the chest, yet there is spare fabric at the waist. Another shirt seems roomy enough until its wearer raises an arm; the whole body of the shirt rises. A third hangs crooked even though the measured chest and waist dimensions match the size chart. Alteration is not simply taking in or letting out a few centimeters. It is diagnosing how a three-dimensional body is interacting with a flexible surface whose shape, grain, and seams limit its motion.

### Fit is geometry under movement

Fabric rests against some parts of the body and spans space elsewhere. A garment needs enough room for the wearer to breathe, sit, walk, reach, and bend. Patternmakers distinguish body measurements from garment measurements. The difference is often called ease, though it includes several design choices: wearing ease for movement and comfort, design ease for intended silhouette, and fabric stretch or recovery where appropriate.

A woven garment with little mechanical stretch generally needs more dimensional allowance in certain directions than a close-fitting knit designed to elongate. A size label conceals these choices. Two garments with the same measured chest circumference can fit differently because of shoulder width, armhole shape, sleeve pitch, posture, and seam placement. The fabric's drape and stiffness change how those differences appear.

### Wrinkles can be evidence, but not a single answer

Diagonal drag lines often point toward areas of tension. Horizontal folds may indicate excess length or a region being pushed upward. Vertical folds can be extra fabric, or sometimes material constrained elsewhere. These are clues rather than universal diagnostic signs. A wrinkle that disappears when the wearer straightens their posture may have a different cause from one that remains in every pose.

Begin with the garment on the intended wearer, using the underlayers and shoes relevant to actual use. Observe the garment from front, side, and back. Ask the wearer to stand normally and move in the ways the garment is meant to permit. Identify whether the garment is too tight, too loose, or simply unbalanced around a seam. Then compare the observation with garment construction: seam allowance, lining, pockets, closures, and whether adjustment would distort another feature.

### The shortest route to a better fit may not be the obvious seam

Suppose trousers are loose at the waist but fit hips and thighs well. Taking in both side seams might shrink the hip circumference and shift pocket geometry. A center-back adjustment or a waistband alteration may sometimes preserve other relationships better, depending on construction. But a trouser with a low or high rise problem cannot always be corrected by changing waist circumference. The crotch curve and rise determine where the garment sits and how it moves through sitting and walking.

Consider a jacket that pulls when its wearer reaches forward. Increasing the chest measurement alone may not solve the problem if the back width, sleeve cap, armhole, or shoulder slope is responsible. Sleeve mobility is a system: the shape of the armhole and the sleeve's orientation determine how movement is distributed. A poorly fitting armhole can restrict movement even in a garment that looks oversized.

There are limits to alteration. Seam allowances provide a finite quantity of extra fabric. Once they are used, letting out further requires added material or reconstruction. A hem can often be shortened, but lengthening may reveal a crease, fading, or insufficient fabric. A tapered leg can be made narrower more readily than broader. Before cutting, identify which interventions are reversible and which permanently remove options.

### Fabric direction and construction constrain changes

Woven fabric has grain directions established by warp and weft. Bias directions behave differently because yarns can shift relative to each other. Moving a seam may change how the fabric hangs if the new line changes the relationship to grain. A knit can stretch significantly in one direction and little in another, and may recover poorly if overloaded. Alteration choices need to respect how the material carries load and returns to shape.

Seam type also matters. A lined coat, a flat-felled jeans seam, a coverstitched knit hem, and a simple plain seam require different handling. In some garments, a seam is also a decorative line or an internal structural member. Shortening sleeves may involve moving a cuff, placket, zipper, or button arrangement, not merely trimming an edge. A good estimate of work begins by inspecting both outside and inside construction.

### Diagnose with temporary changes

Pins, basting stitches, or temporary clips allow the fitter to simulate a proposed alteration before sewing permanently. Marking must account for symmetry, but bodies are not perfectly symmetrical, so mechanically identical left and right alterations do not always produce visually identical results. Look for how the proposed change affects an adjacent seam or opening. A waist adjustment that fixes the front but makes the back hem rise is not finished.

Consider shortening a dress hem. Have the intended wearer stand in appropriate footwear on a level surface. Establish a consistent visual reference, decide whether the hem should be parallel to the floor or follow a design line, mark with a method suitable for the fabric, and preserve enough allowance for the intended finish. A curved or bias-cut hem may hang differently after the fabric relaxes. Cutting immediately from the first measurement can produce an uneven result later.

For each alteration, preserve a record of the original dimensions and the proposed change. Try the garment in motion after temporary adjustment and again after sewing, before removing all remaining allowance. Pressing can reshape and settle seams, but it should not be used to conceal a fundamentally incorrect fit. A successful alteration improves appearance without sacrificing the movement the garment was intended to support.

### Fit is not a number

The most useful lesson from alterations is diagnostic restraint. A wrinkle is not a command to make a seam smaller. A size-chart mismatch is not proof of poor sewing. Fit emerges from pattern geometry, fabric properties, human posture, and movement. Once those are separated, alterations become deliberate experiments with observable outcomes rather than irreversible guesses made with a seam ripper.

## Fitting Sleeves, Armholes, and Moving Bodies

A jacket fits perfectly while someone stands still with arms at their sides. Then the wearer reaches forward for a steering wheel, and the fabric pulls across the back. Is the jacket too small? Perhaps. But a larger jacket may droop at the shoulders without improving the reach. Clothing fit is a problem of geometry in motion, not simply circumference measured on a motionless body.

The sleeve meets the body at the armhole, also called the armscye. That connection has a three-dimensional shape, yet a sewing pattern describes it with flat pieces. The shoulder cap, front and back armhole curves, sleeve pitch, and available ease determine how cloth moves when the arm rotates. Two shirts with identical chest and sleeve measurements can have very different mobility because their pattern geometry differs.

### Understand what is being fitted

Start with the shoulder point, the armhole seam, the grain direction, and the sleeve's seam line. A higher, closer armhole can sometimes permit better upward arm movement than a deep dropped armhole, because there is less fabric bridging from the lower torso to the moving arm. This sounds counterintuitive if one assumes that a larger hole always means more freedom. But when the arm rises, the cloth must rotate around its attachment. A low armhole may drag the body of the garment upward with it.

Sleeve cap height and width interact. A tall cap can create a refined shape with the arm at rest but may restrict certain movements if the surrounding garment is closely fitted. A shallower cap can change drape and available movement, but may also alter the appearance around the upper arm. There is no universally best cap: a tailored jacket, a work shirt, and an athletic top serve different postures and movements.

Fabric mechanics matter. A woven fabric with little stretch must provide movement through pattern shape, ease, pleats, gussets, or cut direction. A knit can recover after deformation, depending on fiber, structure, and finishing. Stretch is not free: fabric may distort, cling, or fail to recover. A fitting strategy that works with a stable woven may not transfer directly to a highly elastic knit.

### Read wrinkles as evidence

Imagine diagonal wrinkles running from the front armhole toward the upper chest. They can indicate that the sleeve is not aligned with the wearer's natural arm position, but they can also result from the shoulder width, garment balance, or the way the fabric has been pinned. A single wrinkle is not a diagnosis. Look at what changes when the wearer raises, reaches, bends, and lowers the arm.

If a sleeve twists around the arm, check sleeve pitch and grain alignment before adding circumference. If the garment rises markedly when arms lift, observe armhole depth, sleeve cap, and torso ease. If the back strains during a forward reach, look at back width, shoulder construction, and available motion across the upper back. The right alteration is the one that targets a measurable movement restriction rather than making the whole garment larger.

### Test with a muslin

A test garment, often called a muslin or toile, allows changes before expensive fabric is cut. Mark the actual seam lines, shoulder points, grain directions, and notches. Use the intended seam allowances. A mock-up assembled with poorly controlled allowances can misrepresent fit just as surely as a bad measurement can. Baste first so seams can be adjusted without damaging the fabric.

Ask the wearer to perform a short movement sequence appropriate to the garment: reach forward, place hands overhead, cross arms, sit, and return to neutral. Photograph the garment from front, side, and back with permission and consistent posture. Record where strain appears and which movement triggers it. The test is about the garment's job. A costume designed mainly for a static appearance and a shirt worn for lifting boxes require different movement allowances.

When altering the armhole, remember that the sleeve and bodice seam lengths must still correspond in the intended way. Changing only one pattern piece can create a mismatch. Ease distribution around a sleeve cap may be deliberately nonuniform. Move notches and reference marks coherently; do not simply stretch the shorter edge until the seam appears to fit. Excess stretching can create puckers and distort the garment.

### Solve one geometric problem at a time

A useful fitting procedure is incremental. First check overall balance and shoulder placement. Then check body circumference and upper-back movement. Then evaluate armhole height and sleeve pitch. Only then refine biceps width, cuff, and sleeve length. This order is not rigid, but it helps avoid compensating for one defect with a different adjustment.

For example, adding width to the biceps may solve tightness around the upper arm while doing nothing about a sleeve attached too far toward the back. Rotating sleeve pitch may improve forward reach without enlarging the jacket. Releasing a back pleat or adding a gusset may better suit a work garment than trying to reproduce a sharply tailored silhouette.

Fit also changes over time. Fabrics shrink or relax; bodies change posture; layers worn underneath alter the required ease. A good pattern retains useful reference marks and an alteration history. That makes future adjustments faster and more repeatable.

A garment should be judged where it lives: on a moving person. The right question is not only whether the seam line follows an attractive curve, but whether the combination of shape, fabric, and intended activity gives the wearer the mobility they need.

## Fitting Trousers: Rise, Seat, and Balance

Trouser fit is another application of that relationship among body shape, balance, fabric, and pattern geometry.

A pair of trousers fits well at the waist when the wearer stands still. After a few steps, the back waistband slides down and diagonal folds appear beneath the seat. Tightening the waist makes them uncomfortable without removing the folds. The problem may not be the waist measurement at all. Trousers are a three-dimensional structure around a body that bends at the hip, sits, walks and shifts its weight. Their fit depends on the relationship among waist, crotch curve, rise, seat, thigh and leg balance.

A flat pattern looks simple: front leg, back leg, curved crotch seam and waistband. But that pattern must create a spatial volume. The crotch curve is not merely decorative. It joins the two legs and gives depth for the pelvis and seat. An inadequate back extension, an incorrect rise or a misplaced inseam can make the garment pull even when the waist and hip circumferences seem correct.

### Circumference is only one dimension

Two people can have the same hip measurement and need different trouser patterns. One may need more depth at the seat; another may carry more volume forward at the abdomen. Body posture and pelvic tilt change how the fabric hangs. A waist circumference measures around the body; it does not tell you the length or shape of the crotch seam required to reach from front waist, between the legs and up the back.

Rise is often described as the distance from the crotch seam to the waist, with front and back rises measured separately. A comfortable rise depends on the style and the wearer's body, and the back generally needs enough length for sitting and bending. Lowering a waistline without adjusting the geometry elsewhere can alter the way the entire garment balances.

Consider a trouser that pulls down at the back only when the wearer sits. One hypothesis is inadequate back rise or crotch depth. Another is insufficient seat circumference. Another is a waistband shaped or positioned poorly for the person's posture. Releasing a small seam allowance at the seat may show whether circumference is limiting movement, but it will not repair a fundamentally short back rise. Diagnostic fitting must identify which dimension is constraining the movement.

### Learn to read folds

Horizontal tension lines can suggest insufficient room, but also appear where fabric is held by another part of the garment. Diagonal drag lines often indicate that material is being pulled from an area where there is inadequate space or a shifted seam. Vertical excess folds can indicate too much fabric, though their location depends on posture and fabric drape. A single wrinkle does not uniquely identify a correction.

The diagnostic habit is to pin or baste only one hypothesis at a time. If an area looks baggy, do not immediately take in every seam. The fabric may be hanging incorrectly because the crotch shape, waistband angle or leg balance is wrong. Temporarily unpicking, releasing or adding a small amount of fabric with a fitting insert can be more informative than permanently trimming an edge.

Trouser fitting is easiest with a test garment, sometimes called a muslin or toile, made from fabric with similar drape and stretch to the intended garment. Mark the grainlines, hipline, knee line and intended waist position. These become references. When the wearer stands in a neutral posture, vertical grainlines should reveal whether the legs hang as intended. An off-grain leg may twist while walking even if the circumference is generous.

### Seat shape and crotch extension

The back crotch extension contributes space for the seat. Changing it affects both the seam curve and the upper-leg geometry. A short extension can pull across the back and under the seat. Too much extension can create a drooping or folded region. Adjustments to the back fork should be evaluated with the inseam, side seam and crotch depth, not made in isolation.

Fabric behavior complicates things. A stretch woven fabric permits a closer fit than a firm non-stretch twill, while a knit can tolerate very different negative ease. If the pattern was designed for stretch and the fabric barely yields, the fitting symptoms may result from the material substitution. Conversely, cutting a rigid trouser pattern in a very soft fabric can create folds that were not apparent in the sample.

Seam allowances matter in alteration work. A pattern correction requiring more seat room is impossible to accomplish invisibly when the existing garment has no excess seam allowance. A tailor may change the waistband or redistribute small amounts of fabric, but certain changes require remaking major pieces. This is why fitting a new garment before cutting expensive fabric offers much more freedom than rescuing a finished one.

### A repeatable fitting session

Begin by recording the garment's intended style and fabric. Mark the body's actual waist and hip reference levels. Observe standing fit from front, back and both sides, then sitting and walking. Photograph the garment consistently if the wearer agrees. Note where the waistband moves, where tension appears and whether leg seams twist.

Choose one hypothesis, such as inadequate back rise, and make a reversible basted adjustment in a test garment. Try the same movements again. Has sitting improved? Did the front become too loose? Did the inseam shift? The successful correction is not the one that looks smooth in a single pose, but the one that improves the garment's overall balance across intended movements.

Once the shape is correct, transfer the change to the paper pattern. True the seams so corresponding pieces still join, restore grainlines and verify pocket and waistband placement. A pattern with only one corrected corner but mismatched seam lengths is not yet a reliable pattern.

Trouser fitting is ultimately about allocating fabric volume around a moving body. Waist, rise, seat and thigh interact. Learn to observe grainlines, test a change reversibly, and evaluate the garment in motion. That process replaces arbitrary pinning with a reasoned understanding of how flat cloth becomes a balanced three-dimensional structure.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Measurement distinction

`Finished garment circumference - body circumference = total ease` at a specified measurement plane, under a consistent measurement method. This arithmetic does not specify the distribution of ease, fabric stretch, garment posture or comfort. Wearable ease requirements depend on garment type and fabric.

##### Practical pattern audit

Record body measurements, finished garment measurements, fabric stretch/recovery, grainline, seam-line lengths, seam allowances, notches, dart legs and intended assembly sequence. For fitting trials, photograph front/back/side at rest and during movement; revise one construction hypothesis at a time. Use the same test fabric behavior when drawing conclusions from a toile.

### Garment Alterations and Fit Diagnosis

### 010000 — Garment Alterations and Fit Diagnosis

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Decision framework

1. Identify the intended use, underlayers, mobility, and design silhouette.
2. Compare body measurements with finished garment dimensions, including ease and stretch behavior.
3. Observe drag lines and balance in natural posture and relevant movement.
4. Inspect seam allowance, lining, pockets, seam finish, closures, grain, and stretch direction.
5. Pin or baste a reversible trial; re-evaluate adjacent features; only then cut and finish.

##### Worked example

A trouser waistband has 3 cm excess circumference but fits hips. Taking 1.5 cm from a single seam could move a pocket or affect seat shape. Compare candidate seam locations, alteration symmetry, allowance, and zipper/waistband construction before selecting a line of change.

##### Limits

Formalwear, lined jackets, high-stretch performance fabrics, and protective garments have different modification constraints. Altering certified protective clothing can void its tested performance. Threads Magazine fitting guidance and extension sewing resources support fitting concepts but do not prescribe a universal adjustment for a wrinkle pattern.

##### Motion-fit worksheet

Record garment purpose and layers, shoulder points, sleeve pitch, armscye depth, upper-arm ease, fabric stretch/recovery, grain line, and seam allowance. ##### Common false fixes

- Bigger chest circumference does not necessarily repair a low armhole.
- Extra biceps width does not correct wrong sleeve rotation.
- Pulling a mismatched sleeve cap into the armscye may create distortion instead of ease.
- A knit's mechanical stretch does not guarantee good recovery or long-term shape.

### 021000 — Fitting Trousers: Rise, Seat, and Balance

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Fit map:** Front/back waist level, high hip, fullest hip, crotch depth, front/back rise, front/back fork extension, thigh/knee, inseam/side seam, grainline, fabric stretch and intended ease. Document measurements with a defined method rather than comparing numbers from unrelated patterns.

**Diagnostic exercise:** Photograph/test standing, sitting, walking. Identify one symptom, list at least two competing causes, baste one reversible change, retest and transfer adjustments to pattern pieces with trued seam lengths. Examine how low-rise style and fabric stretch change interpretation.

**Limit:** Wrinkle patterns are evidence, not one-to-one diagnoses; body posture, garment design and grainline influence them. Structural changes to rise/crotch may not be feasible in a finished garment without sufficient seam allowances.

**Sources:** University extension apparel construction and fit teaching materials; Threads and established patternmaking resources for fitting trousers, crotch curve, seat and grainline; existing sewing-track references on ease, textile stretch and alterations.
