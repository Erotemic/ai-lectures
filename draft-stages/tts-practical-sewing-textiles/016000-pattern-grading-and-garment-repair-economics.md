# 016000 — Pattern Grading and Garment Repair Economics

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A shirt pattern fits one person well. To make it in a larger size, someone prints every pattern piece at one hundred and ten percent. The shoulder becomes too wide, the armhole shifts, the sleeve cap changes, and the collar no longer behaves as intended. The problem is geometric: a human body does not grow uniformly in every direction. Pattern grading is the controlled change of a garment's dimensions while trying to preserve its design and movement qualities.

## The pattern is not the body

A paper pattern represents flat pieces of material that will be joined around a three-dimensional shape. It includes seam allowances, ease, grain direction, darts or other shaping, and allowances for a closure or finish. Body measurements are inputs, not finished pattern dimensions. A woven shirt may need ease so the wearer can breathe, lift an arm, and sit comfortably. A stretch knit may achieve the desired fit with less positive ease or deliberate negative ease, depending on fabric recovery and design.

Grading rules decide how much a dimension changes between sizes and where those changes occur. A chest circumference increment must be distributed among front, back, and relevant seams, but sleeve, shoulder, neck, and length changes need their own logic. Enlarging a pocket, button, or collar by the same percentage as the torso can distort its function and appearance. The intended size chart and target fit are more useful than a uniform scaling factor.

Consider a simple woven shirt. Increase the total chest circumference by four centimeters while retaining the intended style. That does not necessarily mean adding one centimeter to every visible edge. The allowance must be allocated among the garment panels and seam locations, accounting for ease, plackets, side seams, and how the pattern pieces actually join. The armhole and sleeve also need coordinated changes. A change at one edge affects a neighboring seam: pattern pieces are a connected assembly, not independent drawings.

## Grain and material determine what is possible

Fabric has a lengthwise grain, a crosswise grain, and sometimes a direction-dependent stretch or nap. A pattern piece rotated to fit a scrap may hang differently or stretch under load. Printed fabric may need pattern matching. A bias-cut panel uses directional compliance intentionally; an accidental off-grain panel can twist after washing. Grading therefore starts with maintaining the intended grainline and balance, not merely tracing a larger outline.

The material also controls practical alterations. A woven shirt can often be taken in at a side seam if enough allowance exists. Making it larger is limited by material available inside the seams, adjacent details, and construction. A stretchy garment may recover after alteration or may have permanently stretched fibers. Interfacing, lining, and seam finish can constrain an otherwise straightforward change. Before cutting, inspect how the garment was assembled.

A repair has a similar dependency chain. Replacing a zipper may require removing topstitching, opening a lining, matching zipper length and strength, and reconstructing a finish. Replacing a button is much simpler, but the button may have failed because the fabric beneath it was torn or the garment is too tight. Repairing the visible component without fixing the loading problem invites another failure.

## Diagnose fit in motion

A garment can look perfect on a stationary mannequin and pull badly during normal movement. Ask the wearer to raise both arms, bend, sit, and reach forward. Identify whether tightness follows a specific seam, fabric stretch direction, or shape mismatch. A wrinkle pointing toward a strained area can be evidence, but not a universal diagram that uniquely determines an alteration. Fit problems may reflect posture and motion, not just circumference.

For a mock-up, mark the grainline and balance reference. Pin or baste one change at a time. Measure how much material was actually added or removed at each seam and transfer that measurement back to the paper pattern. If an armhole is changed, check the corresponding sleeve rather than assuming it will still sew in with the intended distribution of ease. Press and seam allowances affect the appearance, so inspect the garment after the intended construction steps rather than only during pinning.

## Make repair decisions with real costs

The economics of a repair involve more than the price of thread. Consider material cost, time, tools, skill, opportunity to damage the garment, replacement quality, sentimental value, and expected remaining life. A sturdy coat with a broken zipper may justify substantial repair. A thin fabric whose fibers have failed over a broad area may not become durable merely because a patch can hide the damage. Some repairs are valuable practice even when the financial calculation is neutral; some are not worth the time if they cannot address the underlying material failure.

Repairability can also guide new purchases. Look for accessible seam allowances, replaceable closures, abrasion-prone reinforcement, washable materials, and construction that permits future maintenance. A garment built with long-lasting fabric but a permanently bonded, irreplaceable closure has a different maintenance profile from one with ordinary replaceable parts. That doesn't make one categorically superior; it makes the tradeoff visible.

A good pattern or alteration is a geometrically coherent change to a system. A good repair restores function, not just appearance. Both require measurement, material knowledge, and an honest view of movement and wear. The craft improves when we stop asking 'Can this be made bigger or patched?' and ask 'Which constraints must remain true after the change?'

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Grading worksheet:** source size chart, garment finished measurements, intended ease, panel and seam locations, grainline, sleeve/armhole coordination, vertical length changes, button/closure placement, notches and seam allowance; compare against a test garment.

**Common error:** Uniform print scaling also scales seam allowances, buttons, notches, and parts that should not change proportionally. Grade the pattern geometry from a specified body measurement increment and check joining seam lengths.

**Repair decision matrix:** failure mechanism (closure / stitching / localized abrasion / widespread fiber failure); material and labor; access to seams and lining; durability of restored area; owner value; replacement quality; test fit in motion. An inexpensive material may be labor-intensive to install.

**Scope:** Manufacturer patterns differ in sizing, ease conventions, and seam allowances. Fabric behavior and fit must be checked on the intended garment, not assumed from paper dimensions alone.
