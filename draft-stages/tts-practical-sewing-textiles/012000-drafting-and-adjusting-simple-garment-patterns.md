# 012000 — Drafting and Adjusting Simple Garment Patterns

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A shirt pattern looks flat on the table. A person wearing the finished shirt is not flat. The fabric must move around the chest, under the arms, across the shoulders, and through the motion of reaching or sitting. Pattern drafting is the act of translating three-dimensional body measurements and motion needs into two-dimensional pieces that can be cut and joined.

This begins with geometry, but not geometry alone. Woven fabric has different behavior along the lengthwise grain, crosswise grain, and bias. Knit fabric stretches through its loop structure, often in more than one direction. A pattern drafted for stable woven cotton will not necessarily fit or hang correctly in a highly elastic knit. Material behavior belongs in the drafting decision from the start.

## Measurements describe more than one dimension

A bust or chest circumference is a useful number, but it does not describe shoulder slope, posture, torso length or where fullness is distributed. Two people with the same circumference can need different shapes. For simple garments, start with measurements that locate the major structure: body circumference at intended levels, shoulder span, torso length, arm length, and target garment length. Mark the natural reference points consistently, use a relaxed posture, and document whether a measurement is of the body or a well-fitting garment.

A finished garment needs ease. Wearing ease is extra space required for breathing and movement. Design ease produces the intended silhouette. A close-fitting stretch garment may use less positive ease or deliberately negative ease, but only if the fabric's recovery and the garment's purpose support it. Do not add an arbitrary number of inches everywhere and expect a consistent fit.

## Begin with a block, then create a style

A simple pattern block represents body dimensions and essential shaping. A loose rectangular top can start from width and length, with a neck opening and shoulder line. A more fitted garment may need darts, curved seams, or panels to accommodate differences in circumference between neighboring regions. The complexity should follow the fit required, not the idea that every pattern must look sophisticated.

Take a basic skirt. The waist circumference is smaller than the hip circumference for many wearers. A rectangular tube sized to the hip might fall from the waist unless elastic, gathering, darts or shaped seams control the difference. If a woven skirt is shaped with darts, those darts remove excess fabric near the waist while preserving room around the hips. The pattern's geometry performs a physical job. A design with elastic can distribute that difference differently.

## Seam allowances are construction space, not fit space

A sewing line defines where pieces join. A seam allowance is fabric extending beyond that line to permit construction and finishing. If you accidentally include the seam allowance in the intended finished measurement, the garment can be too small. Label grain line, cut quantity, notches, fold lines, seam allowances and directional pieces. A pattern that fits only when held upside down or mirrored has a communication failure.

A seam consumes width when sewn. For two fabric panels joined on both sides, a finished circumference is the sum of panel widths measured between stitching lines, not between raw cut edges. The exact arithmetic changes when a design uses folds, facings, darts or stretch. Write the intended finished dimensions on the plan, then add construction allowances deliberately.

## Test with inexpensive material

A muslin or test garment reveals interactions that the flat drawing cannot show. It need not be beautiful. It should approximate the drape and stretch of the final fabric closely enough for the fit decision. Mark the center front and back, waist and intended hem; examine whether seams hang vertically and whether the wearer can move naturally. Check arm reach, sitting and bending, not merely standing still in front of a mirror.

Fit corrections should be systematic. A horizontal strain line may signal insufficient length or an incompatible shape; a diagonal drag line may indicate tension toward a restricted area. These are clues, not universal rules. Pin one change, check movement, and transfer the successful correction back to the paper pattern. Preserve the original so you can distinguish the base draft from a particular fitting experiment.

## Fabric changes the outcome

A loose woven fabric may stretch or distort along bias seams. A thick fabric changes how pleats, facings and hems lie. A knit may require a different seam treatment and allowance. Before cutting the final fabric, check grain, nap, print direction, shrinkage expectations, and the way the material behaves after laundering according to its care requirements. Patterning in the correct grain direction often matters as much as the accuracy of the cut outline.

A paper pattern is a model, not an oracle. It communicates expected shape under assumptions about body proportions, material behavior and construction. A successful garment emerges from drafting, trial assembly, observation and revision. The reward is not merely making clothes without a commercial pattern; it is learning to reason from the person and fabric to the seams that allow them to work together.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Simple elastic-waist skirt calculation

1. Measure body hip and desired garment length; specify the intended ease separately.
2. Choose finished fabric width sufficient for hip clearance and gathering. Allow for elastic casing and hem separately from side-seam allowances.
3. Mark grain, front/back, side seams and any fold-on-fabric edges.
4. Test sitting and stepping in a muslin with similar material behavior before finalizing cut dimensions.

### Pattern annotations

| Mark | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Grain line | Alignment to fabric structure |
| Stitching line | Finished seam location |
| Cutting line | Includes planned seam/hem allowance |
| Notches | Alignment and assembly order |
| Fold line | Piece positioned on material fold, if required |

**Editorial boundary:** Drafting formulas depend on garment design, fabric, stretch recovery and body shape. These are methods for prototyping, not guaranteed individualized fit prescriptions.
