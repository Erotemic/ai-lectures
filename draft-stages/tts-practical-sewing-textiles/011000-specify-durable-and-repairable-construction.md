# Specify durable and repairable construction

## Choosing Fabrics for Durability, Drape, and Use

Two garments made from cotton can behave entirely differently. One holds a sharp crease and feels structured; another falls softly and moves with the body. Fiber is only one part of fabric behavior. Yarn construction, weave or knit pattern, density, finishing, and the direction of the cut all influence durability, drape, comfort, and the appearance of a finished seam.

Imagine choosing fabric for a pair of work trousers, a loose shirt, and a reusable shopping bag. All three must withstand use, but their priorities differ. Trousers need abrasion resistance and controlled fit; a shirt may prioritize breathability and how fabric hangs; a bag benefits from tear resistance, shape retention, and strong attachment points. Selecting by color and fiber content alone can leave you with attractive material that cannot perform the intended job.

### Fiber is a material, not a complete specification

Cotton absorbs moisture and is widely available in many fabric structures. Linen behaves differently because of its fiber morphology and stiffness. Wool can recover from some deformation and offers distinctive moisture and thermal behavior. Polyester can be engineered for many uses, often with different moisture handling and resilience from cotton. Blends combine properties, but the proportions and construction matter. No fiber is categorically best in all garments.

Even a label saying one hundred percent cotton can describe lightweight voile, dense canvas, soft flannel, crisp poplin, or elastic-feeling knit. Finishes and laundering change some properties further. When shopping, examine the fabric itself: its thickness, surface, elasticity, recovery, opacity, and tendency to wrinkle or crease. A swatch can reveal more about a particular project than a generic fiber guide.

### Woven and knitted structures respond differently

Woven cloth interlaces yarns along warp and weft directions. Many plain-woven fabrics stretch little along those yarn directions but deform more along the bias, approximately forty-five degrees to the grain. Knitted cloth is made from interlinked loops and can stretch substantially because the loops change shape. That is why a knit top can fit close to the body without the same seams and closures needed in a stable woven top.

Stretch alone is not the whole performance. Recovery describes how well fabric returns after being stretched. A knit that stretches easily but recovers poorly may sag at knees or elbows. A tightly woven textile may resist stretch but fray at cut edges. A fabric can also distort under its own weight, especially when cut on the bias. Pattern instructions for woven or knit fabrics usually assume characteristic behavior, so substituting another construction is a design change, not merely a shopping choice.

### Drape is stiffness interacting with weight

Drape describes how fabric falls and forms folds under gravity. A flexible lightweight material can hang in numerous fine folds; a stiff material may hold a projecting shape. Thickness, bending stiffness, mass per area, yarn structure, and surface friction all contribute. A heavy fabric does not necessarily drape poorly, and a very light fabric does not automatically drape well. The interaction matters.

For a structured bag, some resistance to bending may be desirable, especially at the base. For a loose summer blouse, too much stiffness can make the garment stand away from the body. A fabric that drapes beautifully on a mannequin may be frustrating to sew if it shifts or stretches as it is cut. Sewing difficulty is itself a project constraint, particularly for beginners.

### Durability has several failure modes

Abrasion resistance matters where surfaces rub, such as trouser knees and bag bottoms. Tear strength matters when a sharp load propagates a small cut. Tensile strength matters under distributed pulling. Seam slippage occurs when yarns move away from a seam even without thread breakage. Pilling appears when fibers and yarn ends form small surface balls after wear. Colorfastness, ultraviolet exposure, washing shrinkage, and heat damage add other aging processes.

A dense fabric with excellent abrasion resistance might still fail at a poorly designed seam. The seam may carry stress through a small region and require reinforcement or a different stitch. Heavy fabric also demands appropriate machine needle, thread, seam allowances, and handling. Choosing the strongest fabric on paper does not guarantee the strongest finished object.

### Test fabric before committing to the garment

Make a small sample with the intended seam, needle, thread, and pressing method. Wash and dry it according to expected care instructions, then compare dimensions. Observe puckering, needle damage, stretch recovery, and fraying. Hang a swatch over an edge to see drape, and rub it in an area representative of use. For serious durability decisions, standardized laboratory tests exist, but simple practical samples catch many problems before expensive cutting.

The direction of the pattern pieces also matters. Cutting a woven panel off grain can create unintended twisting or bias behavior. Nap, print direction, stretch direction, and fabric width influence layout. If you are making matched panels, ensure that each experiences the intended forces in the expected yarn direction. Two pieces of the same cloth need not behave identically if cut at different angles.

Choose fabric by writing a short use specification: required movement, comfort, silhouette, maintenance, climate, likely abrasion points, construction skill, and expected lifetime. Then compare sample behavior against those criteria. Fabric selection is engineering at the scale of the body. The right material is not a prestigious fiber name; it is a structure whose deformation, wear, and care properties fit what the finished object must do.

## Linings, Interfacings, and Reinforcing Stress Points

The chosen fabric still needs local support where forces or construction details concentrate stress.

A jacket can hang beautifully when finished, yet collapse around the collar after a few washes. A bag can be made from strong canvas and still tear where its strap meets the body. These are not necessarily problems with the main fabric. Garments and sewn objects are systems of layers, seams, and concentrated forces. A lining, an interfacing, or a small reinforcement may change the shape and durability far more than its weight suggests.

### Three fabrics can do three different jobs

The fashion fabric is the visible outer material. A lining is a separate inner layer that may protect the garment's inside, reduce friction against the wearer's clothing, conceal seam allowances, or influence how the garment hangs. Interfacing usually adds stiffness, shape retention, or support to a selected area such as a collar, waistband, placket, or buttonhole region. Interlining is another layer used for additional properties such as warmth or body. The terms can vary in particular construction traditions, so identify a layer by what it actually does in the project.

A lining does not automatically make a garment stronger. A silky lining inside a rough coat can help it slide over other clothing, but if it is cut too tightly it may tear when the outer garment flexes. The lining and shell can differ in stretch, shrinkage, and thermal response. Attaching two layers rigidly everywhere can cause puckers even when each layer was cut accurately on its own.

### Matching interface to movement

Suppose a soft collar should stand away from the neck. Without support, its fabric may fold under its own weight. Interfacing changes the bending behavior of that collar. But a very stiff interfacing on a light, flowing blouse can produce a cardboard-like edge. On a stretchy knit garment, a woven, non-stretch interfacing can unexpectedly prevent the neckline or placket from moving with the body.

Interfacing comes in woven, knit, and nonwoven constructions, with different weights and directions of stretch. Some versions are fusible, using an adhesive activated by heat and pressure. Others are sewn into place. Choosing between them involves the fashion fabric's heat tolerance, surface texture, expected laundering, stretch, and the desired final hand of the garment.

A fusible sample should be tested on actual fabric scraps with the intended pressing method. Too little heat or pressure may create a weak bond. Too much can flatten pile, shrink material, produce shine, or cause glue to migrate visibly through the fabric. An adhesive bond that survives the first press may still fail after repeated washing if the materials or process are incompatible.

### The seam is where forces gather

Consider a shoulder strap attached to a bag. The bag fabric may be strong in a broad sheet, but the entire weight of its contents must transfer through a relatively small stitched region. Threads carry tension; needle holes reduce some intact fiber area; concentrated stress can tear the fabric beyond the last stitches. Simply adding more closely spaced stitches does not always help. It can perforate the material more densely and create a line of weakness.

A reinforcement patch spreads load into a wider area. A stay tape can reduce unwanted stretch along a seam. A bar tack can concentrate durable stitching at an appropriate location when the surrounding material is strong enough. The right detail depends on where the force enters and how it can disperse through the layers.

For a garment, stress points may include pocket corners, the ends of a zipper opening, belt loops, crotch seams, underarm joins, and buttonholes. Each sees a different combination of pulling, bending, abrasion, and repeated use. Reinforcement should respond to that actual loading, not merely follow a habit of sewing another row everywhere.

### Grain, stretch, and shrinkage remain important

A woven lining cut on a different grain orientation from its outer layer may pull diagonally as the garment hangs. A knit lining may recover elastically while a non-stretch shell does not, changing where strain concentrates. Pre-treatment matters if the layers shrink differently when washed. It is often worth laundering and pressing a test sample in the way the finished item will be cared for before committing the final fabric.

Interfacing is often cut to avoid unnecessary bulk in seam allowances, depending on method and pattern. That detail influences how collars and waistbands turn at the edge. A heavy overlap at a corner can produce a lump that careful topstitching cannot erase. Trimming, grading seam allowances, and controlled pressing can improve the shape because they redistribute thickness, not because they cosmetically hide an error.

### Build a small test piece

Imagine making a shirt with a button placket. First construct one short section from the actual fashion fabric. Try the intended interfacing, buttonhole construction, and stitch length. Fasten and unfasten the button repeatedly. Observe whether the buttonhole distorts, whether the fabric puckers, and whether the placket folds where it should. Wash the sample if laundering will be part of normal use. This controlled test can reveal a poor pairing long before the shirt is finished.

For a coat lining, check ease at the shoulders and across the back while the wearer bends and reaches forward. The outer coat may have movement allowance that the lining must also accommodate. A small pleat or other designed ease can prevent tension on the lining when the arms move. The dimensions and location depend on the actual pattern and fabric.

For a bag strap, load a test seam gradually and inspect for stretching, thread failure, or tearing. Treat the test as a way to compare designs, not as certification of a product for safety-critical lifting. If a strap supports a person or hazardous load, a household stitching experiment does not establish the required rating.

Layers and reinforcements work by assigning jobs: appearance, comfort, shape, and load transfer. When those jobs are explicit, it becomes easier to choose materials, test the assembly, and repair a failure without unnecessarily replacing the whole object.

## Zippers, Buttons, and Other Closures

A coat can have beautifully constructed seams and durable fabric, yet become nearly unusable when its zipper fails. A waistband may be strong everywhere except at a button that pulls through the fabric. These failures are not merely defects of the small metal or plastic parts. A closure transfers force between two pieces of flexible material, and the surrounding cloth must be designed to receive that force.

Consider what happens when a jacket is pulled closed across the chest. The wearer stretches the garment, creating tension in the fabric. That force reaches the zipper tape and teeth, then transfers across to the other side. If the cloth around the zipper is poorly supported, it may pucker, tear, or deform while the zipper itself remains intact. The closure and its reinforcement together form the load path.

### Three different closure mechanisms

A button and buttonhole work by passing a protruding object through a controlled opening. The opening must be large enough for the button yet constrained enough to retain it under ordinary movement. A snap uses interlocking components that resist separation until a sufficient force is applied, often with a noticeable pop. A zipper uses two rows of interlocking elements that are joined or separated by a slider moving along the rows.

These mechanisms fail differently. A button may loosen because the thread holding it stretches or breaks. A buttonhole may fray because the surrounding stitches and fabric cannot maintain its edge. A snap may pull through a thin material because the base lacks reinforcement. A zipper may separate because the slider no longer forces the elements together correctly, because the teeth are damaged, or because the fabric has been pulled out of alignment.

### Zipper diagnosis begins with geometry

If a zipper closes and then opens behind the slider, the interlocking elements may not be fully engaging. The slider can be worn or distorted, but damaged teeth or misalignment can create similar symptoms. If the slider refuses to move, look for trapped fabric, a damaged element, distorted tape, or excess force before pulling harder. Forcing a jam may convert a repairable problem into a broken zipper.

Zippers come in different constructions, including coil and molded-tooth designs, and may be separating or closed-end. Their tapes, teeth size, slider type, and stops must match the intended garment. Replacing only the slider can be appropriate when the rest of the assembly is sound, but mixing incompatible slider and chain specifications is likely to fail. Manufacturer size designations are not always interchangeable across product families.

When sewing in a zipper, the seam allowance, placement, and support fabric matter. Lightweight cloth may need interfacing or stabilization so the zipper tape does not distort the garment. The zipper should not be forced to carry loads that a pattern's shaping and fabric should handle. If a garment is too small, repeatedly replacing the zipper does not remove the excessive tension.

### Buttons need more than strong thread

A button sewn directly tight against a thick coat can make the closure difficult to pass through the buttonhole. A thread shank creates room for the fabric thickness between button and garment. For lightweight fabrics, a smaller or flatter button may work without much stand-off. A backing button or reinforced patch can distribute load where the fabric is fragile.

Buttonhole direction also affects the way forces act on the opening. A horizontal buttonhole in a tensioned area can allow a button to settle toward one end under load; vertical orientation may be appropriate elsewhere. The correct choice depends on garment design, overlap, and intended stress direction. Interfacing at the opening helps prevent stretching and tearing, especially on fabrics that do not maintain their shape easily.

A useful test is to put the garment on and move naturally before deciding whether the closure is defective. If the fabric pulls hard across the buttons whenever the wearer sits, the fit or construction may be the primary issue. Adding stronger thread can make the next failure occur in the surrounding cloth.

### Repair should preserve the shape of the garment

Imagine a broken zipper on an otherwise good jacket. First assess whether the slider, stopper, individual teeth, tape, and attachment stitches are intact. Photograph the original alignment and measure the zipper length and type before removal. If the whole zipper requires replacement, the replacement should be compatible with the garment's thickness, curvature, and opening function. Removing and replacing the stitching without altering seam allowances helps preserve fit.

For a loose button, inspect not only the thread but also the base fabric and buttonhole. A torn button site may need a small reinforcement or a darned repair before the button is reattached. A worn buttonhole may require stabilizing the fabric before sewing its edge again. A snap that has torn out may need a properly reinforced mounting area; simply installing a larger snap in a damaged hole can worsen the tear.

### A small mechanism reveals a general craft principle

Closures are interfaces between parts that want to move relative to each other. A good closure provides enough restraint to do its job while allowing deliberate release. It transfers force into sound fabric, follows garment geometry, and can withstand repeated cycles. The same reasoning applies to straps, buckles, hook-and-loop tape, drawcords, and toggles.

The next time a zipper or button fails, resist assuming the visible hardware alone is responsible. Identify the direction of force, the first point of damage, the support around the closure, and whether the garment fits its actual use. A durable repair solves the entire interface, not just its most recognizable component.

## Sewing for Repairable Construction

A durable construction choice can also make later access and repair easier rather than conceal the next failure.

A jacket has a broken zipper, but reaching the zipper tape requires cutting through a permanently bonded lining. A second jacket was sewn so that the lining can be opened along a short seam, the zipper can be replaced, and the lining can be closed again. The first jacket may have been faster to manufacture. The second is easier to keep. Repairability is a property that can be designed into clothing before anything wears out.

### Decide which parts are likely to fail

A garment contains components that age at different rates: outer fabric, seams, thread, closures, elastic, lining, interfacing, and decorative trim. A strong outer cloth may outlast the zipper by years. Elastic can lose recovery. Buttons can be lost while the rest of a shirt remains sound. The construction should make these parts accessible and replaceable without destroying durable material around them.

Consider a child's coat. The shoulders and body fabric may be fine, but cuffs experience abrasion and soil. If the cuffs are separate pieces with accessible seam allowances, they can be replaced. If the coat is a single fused assembly with minimal seam allowance, repairing the worn edge can be much harder. A repairable design does not require making everything detachable; it requires anticipating where failure is most likely and preserving a workable path to the failed part.

Seam allowances are useful service margins. A seam sewn almost at the raw edge may be impossible to open and resew without losing significant garment size. A slightly larger, well-finished allowance can make alterations possible. But excess bulk in a delicate garment can be uncomfortable and may weaken its drape. The allowance should reflect fabric thickness, expected wear, and intended construction.

### Stitch type affects repair as well as strength

A straight lockstitch is familiar and often easy to unpick one segment at a time, but its limited stretch can make it unsuitable for some high-stretch seams. A zigzag or stretch stitch can tolerate movement, while an overlock seam finishes edges efficiently but may be difficult to open cleanly without cutting threads and some fabric. A flat-felled seam is strong and encloses raw edges, yet replacing one section may require opening more material than with a simple pressed-open seam.

This is a tradeoff, not a ranking. Workwear may benefit from durable enclosed seams despite repair complexity. A prototype garment or a garment expected to be repeatedly altered may benefit from simpler accessible seam construction. The appropriate stitch must still meet strength, stretch, abrasion, and wash requirements. Designing for repair cannot mean making the garment fail sooner during ordinary use.

Thread also matters. If thread is too weak for a stress point, seams burst. If thread is very strong relative to a fragile cloth, high loads can tear the fabric beside the seam instead. Reinforcing a point should spread load through a sound area, not create a hard edge that concentrates stress. A button reinforcement patch works only when the patch and stitching transfer force to enough surrounding cloth.

### Make access intentional

Linings often hide seam allowances and structural details. A repairable lining can include an accessible hand-closed turning opening or a planned section that can be opened and resewn. Closure tape can be installed so its seam is reachable without removing half the garment. Replacement hardware should use sizes and types that can reasonably be sourced. A beautiful proprietary clasp with no replacement option may shorten an otherwise durable item's life.

Repair access must also respect function. Opening a seam in waterproof clothing can compromise its seam seal. A structural load-bearing textile, such as a climbing harness, is not an appropriate place for improvised domestic stitching. Safety-critical fabric systems require the manufacturer's or qualified repair process, or retirement of the item. Design for repair is not permission to guess at the capacity of critical stitches.

Consider a zippered pouch. One version traps zipper ends between permanently glued layers; another uses sewn end tabs and an accessible seam. After hundreds of cycles, a worn slider or tape can be replaced on the second pouch without cutting the body. The difference is a small design choice made when the pouch is new.

### Build a repair plan into the pattern

Before cutting cloth, mark the intended service points: cuffs, hem, elastic casing, zipper, buttons, knees, elbows, and lining openings. Decide which seams must be removable and which can be permanent. Use compatible interfacing and reinforcement patches so the repairable part is not weaker than the surrounding garment. Where an elastic waistband will likely stretch out, a casing with accessible ends makes replacement simpler than elastic entirely captured by multiple inaccessible seams.

Document construction while sewing. A small note identifying the thread, seam allowance, needle type, fabric composition, zipper length, and available spare buttons can make a later repair far easier. For a handmade garment, a few spare matching scraps are more valuable than a photograph of every decorative step. For a child likely to grow, plan reasonable alteration allowances at hems or side seams where design permits.

Repairability should also be judged economically and materially. Some small components are cheap, but reaching them may take several hours of labor. If the garment was designed for service, that effort falls sharply. The environmental benefit depends on extending actual use, not on adding complicated construction features that never get used.

The governing question is simple: when the first component wears out, what must be destroyed to reach it? A repairable garment allows the weak part to leave without sacrificing the strong parts. Good sewing joins fabric for use; thoughtful sewing also leaves a route back in when life inevitably wears something down.

## Pattern Grading and Garment Repair Economics

A shirt pattern fits one person well. To make it in a larger size, someone prints every pattern piece at one hundred and ten percent. The shoulder becomes too wide, the armhole shifts, the sleeve cap changes, and the collar no longer behaves as intended. The problem is geometric: a human body does not grow uniformly in every direction. Pattern grading is the controlled change of a garment's dimensions while trying to preserve its design and movement qualities.

### The pattern is not the body

A paper pattern represents flat pieces of material that will be joined around a three-dimensional shape. It includes seam allowances, ease, grain direction, darts or other shaping, and allowances for a closure or finish. Body measurements are inputs, not finished pattern dimensions. A woven shirt may need ease so the wearer can breathe, lift an arm, and sit comfortably. A stretch knit may achieve the desired fit with less positive ease or deliberate negative ease, depending on fabric recovery and design.

Grading rules decide how much a dimension changes between sizes and where those changes occur. A chest circumference increment must be distributed among front, back, and relevant seams, but sleeve, shoulder, neck, and length changes need their own logic. Enlarging a pocket, button, or collar by the same percentage as the torso can distort its function and appearance. The intended size chart and target fit are more useful than a uniform scaling factor.

Consider a simple woven shirt. Increase the total chest circumference by four centimeters while retaining the intended style. That does not necessarily mean adding one centimeter to every visible edge. The allowance must be allocated among the garment panels and seam locations, accounting for ease, plackets, side seams, and how the pattern pieces actually join. The armhole and sleeve also need coordinated changes. A change at one edge affects a neighboring seam: pattern pieces are a connected assembly, not independent drawings.

### Grain and material determine what is possible

Fabric has a lengthwise grain, a crosswise grain, and sometimes a direction-dependent stretch or nap. A pattern piece rotated to fit a scrap may hang differently or stretch under load. Printed fabric may need pattern matching. A bias-cut panel uses directional compliance intentionally; an accidental off-grain panel can twist after washing. Grading therefore starts with maintaining the intended grainline and balance, not merely tracing a larger outline.

The material also controls practical alterations. A woven shirt can often be taken in at a side seam if enough allowance exists. Making it larger is limited by material available inside the seams, adjacent details, and construction. A stretchy garment may recover after alteration or may have permanently stretched fibers. Interfacing, lining, and seam finish can constrain an otherwise straightforward change. Before cutting, inspect how the garment was assembled.

A repair has a similar dependency chain. Replacing a zipper may require removing topstitching, opening a lining, matching zipper length and strength, and reconstructing a finish. Replacing a button is much simpler, but the button may have failed because the fabric beneath it was torn or the garment is too tight. Repairing the visible component without fixing the loading problem invites another failure.

### Diagnose fit in motion

A garment can look perfect on a stationary mannequin and pull badly during normal movement. Ask the wearer to raise both arms, bend, sit, and reach forward. Identify whether tightness follows a specific seam, fabric stretch direction, or shape mismatch. A wrinkle pointing toward a strained area can be evidence, but not a universal diagram that uniquely determines an alteration. Fit problems may reflect posture and motion, not just circumference.

For a mock-up, mark the grainline and balance reference. Pin or baste one change at a time. Measure how much material was actually added or removed at each seam and transfer that measurement back to the paper pattern. If an armhole is changed, check the corresponding sleeve rather than assuming it will still sew in with the intended distribution of ease. Press and seam allowances affect the appearance, so inspect the garment after the intended construction steps rather than only during pinning.

### Make repair decisions with real costs

The economics of a repair involve more than the price of thread. Consider material cost, time, tools, skill, opportunity to damage the garment, replacement quality, sentimental value, and expected remaining life. A sturdy coat with a broken zipper may justify substantial repair. A thin fabric whose fibers have failed over a broad area may not become durable merely because a patch can hide the damage. Some repairs are valuable practice even when the financial calculation is neutral; some are not worth the time if they cannot address the underlying material failure.

Repairability can also guide new purchases. Look for accessible seam allowances, replaceable closures, abrasion-prone reinforcement, washable materials, and construction that permits future maintenance. A garment built with long-lasting fabric but a permanently bonded, irreplaceable closure has a different maintenance profile from one with ordinary replaceable parts. That doesn't make one categorically superior; it makes the tradeoff visible.

A good pattern or alteration is a geometrically coherent change to a system. A good repair restores function, not just appearance. Both require measurement, material knowledge, and an honest view of movement and wear. The craft improves when we stop asking 'Can this be made bigger or patched?' and ask 'Which constraints must remain true after the change?'

### Zippers, Buttons, and Other Closures

**Track:** Sewing, Clothing, and Textiles. **Background:** pattern geometry, seams, material behavior, and machine troubleshooting. **Purpose:** treat clothing closures as small mechanisms that redirect force into fabric and must remain usable through repeated movement.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Closure inspection matrix

| Symptom | Candidate mechanism | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| Zipper separates behind slider | Slider wear, teeth not engaging, tape distortion | Examine slider and teeth alignment |
| Slider jams | Trapped fabric, damaged chain, bent slider | Stop forcing; inspect where resistance begins |
| Button detaches | Thread failure or torn base cloth | Inspect remaining thread and hole in substrate |
| Buttonhole enlarges | Fabric stretch, fraying, missing reinforcement | Inspect opening edge and support layer |
| Snap tears out | Concentrated load in thin material | Check backing and local garment tension |

##### Practice

Compare the load distribution of a button sewn to one fabric layer versus a reinforced patch on scrap textile; test only gentle hand loads. Try a sample zipper installation with stabilized and unstabilized fabric to observe distortion. Do not extrapolate to product strength ratings.

##### Source boundary

Use the chosen zipper and fastener manufacturer's specifications for size and fit. Sewing-extension resources explain seam, reinforcement, and fabric behavior but do not certify closures for fall-protection or other life-safety uses.

### Sewing for Repairable Construction

### 017000 — Sewing for Repairable Construction

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Repairability design sheet:** component, probable failure, replacement availability, opening seam, stitch type, allowance, reinforcement, resealing requirement, and verification after repair.

**Examples:** accessible elastic casing; zipper tape stitched to a removable facing; cuff attached at a known seam; spare buttons and matching cloth; hand-closed lining access. Constraints: bulk, drape, stretch, seam sealing and cost.

**Fabric/strength:** Choose thread and seam to match fabric and load. Avoid over-stiff patch edges on delicate cloth. For waterproof breathable garments, follow compatible seam-tape and manufacturer instructions after reopening seams.

**Limit:** Never improvise repairs to safety-critical load-bearing fabric (climbing, fall protection, automotive restraint, etc.) without the prescribed qualified process. Standard garment repair techniques do not certify structural textile performance.

### Pattern Grading and Garment Repair Economics

### 016000 — Pattern Grading and Garment Repair Economics

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Grading worksheet:** source size chart, garment finished measurements, intended ease, panel and seam locations, grainline, sleeve/armhole coordination, vertical length changes, button/closure placement, notches and seam allowance; compare against a test garment.

**Common error:** Uniform print scaling also scales seam allowances, buttons, notches, and parts that should not change proportionally. Grade the pattern geometry from a specified body measurement increment and check joining seam lengths.

**Repair decision matrix:** failure mechanism (closure / stitching / localized abrasion / widespread fiber failure); material and labor; access to seams and lining; durability of restored area; owner value; replacement quality; test fit in motion. An inexpensive material may be labor-intensive to install.

**Scope:** Manufacturer patterns differ in sizing, ease conventions, and seam allowances. Fabric behavior and fit must be checked on the intended garment, not assumed from paper dimensions alone.
