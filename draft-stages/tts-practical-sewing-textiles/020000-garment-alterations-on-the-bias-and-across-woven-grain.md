# 020000 — Garment Alterations on the Bias and Across Woven Grain

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

You shorten a woven skirt. You measure the same amount from its original hem all the way around, cut carefully and sew the new edge. On the hanger it looks even. When worn, one part hangs lower and twists toward the front. The measurement was consistent, but the fabric did not behave as a rigid sheet. Its weave direction and the way it stretches under its own weight mattered.

To alter a woven garment well, we need to understand grain. Woven fabric is constructed from lengthwise warp yarns and crosswise weft yarns. These create two principal directions. The diagonal direction, called the bias, can deform more readily because the yarns rotate relative to one another. True bias is approximately forty-five degrees from the straight grain. Even a fabric with little elastic stretch can extend and drape noticeably on the bias.

## Grain gives a garment its behavior

Think of a square woven cloth held by two opposite corners. The yarns form a grid. When you pull along the lengthwise grain, you are largely pulling along the warp structure. When you pull on the diagonal, the grid can shear into diamond shapes. That shear produces drape and movement without requiring individual yarns to stretch like rubber.

Designers exploit this property in bias-cut skirts, necklines, facings and binding. It can also create problems in alterations. A bias section may grow in length after hanging. A seam can pucker if one layer is stretched during feeding through the machine. A garment cut off grain may twist around the body even when the wearer stands still.

The selvage edge is often a useful clue to the lengthwise direction, but a finished garment may not retain its selvages. Find grain by examining the weave, seams, original cutting layout if available, and how the fabric hangs. Do not assume the side seam always traces the exact grain. Some designs deliberately angle panels.

## A skirt with alternating grain directions

Imagine an A-line skirt made of four panels. The center of a panel may hang close to straight grain while its sloping side edges approach bias. After a new hem is stitched, the diagonal parts may relax further and appear longer. If you use the old hem as a fixed measuring reference before the garment has hung, the result may not be even in wear.

A better alteration sequence begins with the garment on the person or a properly adjusted dress form, supported as it will be worn. Let the fabric settle, especially after major cutting or sewing. Mark the desired finished hem relative to the floor or another consistent reference while accounting for posture and shoes. Then check the marked length after the garment is removed. This measures the behavior of the actual garment, not only the width of cloth on a table.

Hem construction should also match the curve. The inner edge of a turned hem on a flared skirt has a smaller circumference than the original hemline. Trying to fold a deep straight hem can create excess fullness and puckers. A narrow hem, properly eased facing or suitable binding may fit the geometry better. Which method is appropriate depends on fabric weight, stretch, opacity and the original design.

## Fitting across the grain

Now consider taking in a woven shirt at the sides. Removing identical wedges from both seams may reduce circumference but also change where the garment hangs from the shoulders and how it crosses the hips. A dart transfers a region of flat fabric into three-dimensional shaping. Moving the dart can affect the strain paths around the bust or waist. Adjusting shoulder slope changes how the entire upper garment hangs, sometimes removing wrinkles that looked like a waist-fitting problem.

Wrinkles are evidence of tension and excess fabric, but not a complete diagnosis. Horizontal pulling across the back may indicate insufficient width, restricted movement or fabric trapped by another part of the garment. Diagonal wrinkles may show that cloth is being pulled toward a stress point. Release or pin one possible constraint at a time, then check sitting, reaching, walking and arm movement. Good fit is dynamic.

## Machine handling can introduce new distortion

A sewing machine's feed dogs move the bottom layer while the presser foot resists the top. On a bias seam, the layers can stretch differently during sewing. Pulling the fabric to help it through may create a permanent wave. Depending on the textile, stabilizing the seam before sewing, reducing handling or adjusting the foot and stitch setup can improve control. Pressing can reshape some fabrics, but aggressive steam and pressure can also stretch, shine or flatten the wrong areas.

For a delicate garment, practice on matching scraps. Mark grain and measure a test strip before and after sewing. Check whether the seam becomes longer and whether it recovers after pressing and resting. A clean line while sewing is less important than its final geometry on the body.

## A controlled alteration method

Begin by identifying what the original maker used the grain to accomplish. Photograph the garment on a body, mark reference points, and inspect seam allowances before cutting. Change one structural dimension at a time and baste major alterations. Test movement. Rehang when the garment includes bias sections. When the fit and hang are right, finish the permanent seam and hem using a technique compatible with the fabric.

If a side seam has become permanently off grain from wear or washing, moving the seam alone may not return the textile to its original shape. Sometimes the best repair is limited or requires reshaping more than one panel. A garment that is precisely measured on the table can still be badly fitted on a moving body.

Understanding grain explains why. Fabric is a structured material with directional movement. An alteration succeeds when its measurements and its material behavior agree.

## Non-spoken technical companion

**Direction sketch.** Warp runs parallel to the original selvage; weft runs across fabric; true bias is approximately 45 degrees to both. Woven bias extensibility often comes from yarn rotation, not elastic fiber strain. On some weaves, directionality and stability are more complicated.

**Alteration trial.** Mark a 100-mm square of matching scrap on grain and another at 45 degrees. Apply equal gentle loads, measure deformation, release and measure recovery; do not compare without controlling loads and fabric conditioning. A hem should be measured while garment weight is supported as worn and after settling when needed.

**References.** Sewing & Craft Alliance, *Preparing to Sew* grain and bias guidance; Utah State University Extension, grain and fabric handling; garment-fitting and seam-construction sources. Manufacturer instructions for machine tension, needles, and pressing temperatures take precedence for a particular textile.
