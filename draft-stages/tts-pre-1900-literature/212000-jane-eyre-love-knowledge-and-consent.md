# Jane Eyre — Love, Knowledge, and Consent

Jane Eyre contains two marriage proposals that test the heroine in opposite ways. Rochester loves Jane passionately but conceals an existing marriage. St. John Rivers proposes a formally legitimate marriage without romantic love, expecting Jane to become an instrument of his missionary calling. The arrangement can tempt us to announce that Jane must choose feeling over law, or law over feeling. Her actual argument is more demanding. She refuses to let either man's purposes replace her capacity for informed, responsible choice.

Rochester's attraction is not a simple act of condescension. He listens to Jane's intelligence, recognizes her wit, and finds companionship with someone who refuses to flatter him. Jane speaks to him as an equal in moral worth when society does not treat them as equal in station. Those conversations make the love convincing. But they also sharpen the betrayal. The more sincerely Rochester esteems her judgment, the worse it is that he denies her the facts on which judgment depends. He stages jealousy over Blanche, impersonates a fortune-teller, and conceals Bertha Mason. The problem is not that a fictional hero has a mysterious past. It is that Rochester proposes a marriage whose legal possibility he knows does not exist.

When he finally explains, he invites Jane to hear a history of coercion, disappointment, and long misery. A reader can recognize pain without granting him the exclusive right to define the persons involved. Jane is moved by him, but she does not conclude that compassion authorizes deception. Her refusal is costly. It drives her into deprivation. This is why the episode cannot be reduced to an easy triumph of Victorian propriety. She is not avoiding scandal at no personal cost; she is choosing an ethical boundary while fearing she may never recover from its consequences.

St. John offers a different kind of asymmetry. He is sincere about the mission he intends to undertake. Jane believes in his courage and his capacity for work. But he reasons about the marriage as a necessary instrument. She would be selected for duty, not known and loved as a partner. When she proposes to accompany him in a non-marital role, he rejects it. His moral vocabulary has turned their future into a choice between a wedding and supposed disobedience to God. Jane recognizes that the emotional life he asks her to suppress is not a trivial appetite to be discarded as a test of virtue.

Compare what each man asks Jane to disregard. Rochester asks her to disregard a legal marriage and the person bound to him by it. St. John asks her to disregard her own need for mutual affection. Neither can simply be dismissed as a villain. That is part of Brontë's achievement. The conflicts are generated by people whose needs and convictions can be felt, but whose demands on Jane must be judged from more than their own point of view. A novel can give a person sympathetic reasons and still find the person's conduct wrong.

The return to Rochester is often described as a neat restoration of balance: she is rich, he is injured, and therefore their former inequality has reversed. That arithmetic is too crude. Disability is not a moral penalty, and a dependent man is not necessarily an ethical man. Bertha's death and the destruction of Thornfield have not made the concealment harmless. What has changed is that Jane knows the truth of Rochester's circumstances, possesses independent means, and chooses to return without compulsion. The book represents their later life as companionship and care. Readers remain free to ask whether the story has fully accounted for Bertha, or whether its solution depends on an irreversible death that benefits the surviving couple.

The famous garden declaration insists on equal souls despite unequal bodies, money, and rank. It would be empty rhetoric if the plot did not put that claim under pressure. Jane's love is not proved by her willingness to endure any arrangement Rochester offers. Her love becomes a choice she can make again after she has practiced refusal. The novel's question is not which proposal a woman should accept. It is what she must know, possess, and be allowed to decide for acceptance to mean anything at all.
