# The Decameron — The Last Evening and the Road Back

Dioneo finishes the tale of Griselda. The listeners do not respond as a single chorus. Some find the narrative admirable; others criticize it. Their disagreement is one of the final acts of the storytelling company, and it is a fitting response to a tale that presents patience under treatment its narrator has already called folly. No single speaker receives authority to close the debate. Instead Panfilo, the king for this tenth and final day, looks at the hour and begins another kind of conclusion.

The company has been away from Florence for nearly fifteen days. Its members fled a city struck by pestilence, funerals and a collapse in the ordinary habits that had made urban life intelligible. In the country they have arranged a different rhythm. They have elected rulers for each storytelling day, obeyed conventions, eaten together, sung, danced and invented a space for judging stories without the immediate fear that dominates the city. Their refuge has had some success. Yet even a pleasant form of isolation cannot be made permanent without changing its character.

Panfilo argues that they should return. Their retreat has preserved their health and maintained their decorum. But if they stay indefinitely, the pleasure may become stale and their company may attract so many outsiders that it ceases to provide shelter. The group discusses the proposal and agrees. There will be no eleventh storytelling day. Panfilo retains the crown only until they leave the next morning.

That evening they dine, dance and sing. Fiammetta gives a song concerned with love and jealousy. Dioneo jokes about the lover who has inspired it. This is not a solemn farewell that turns the whole collection into a morality play. Ordinary playfulness survives almost until midnight. The music has accompanied joy, despair, tenderness and the comic fictions told through ten afternoons; now it fills the final hours of their temporary society.

At dawn the servants have prepared the goods. The company goes back to Florence and returns to Santa Maria Novella, the church where the young women first gathered amid the plague and where their journey began. The three young men take leave of the seven young women. The women return to their homes. There is no report that the plague has vanished, that the city has recovered or that all ten people are now safe forever. The narrative simply places them back in the world from which they withdrew.

This restrained ending is vital. The stories did not cure the Black Death. They did not establish a new political order, nor did the group agree on a single ethical explanation for everything it had heard. What they accomplished was more limited and more human: a period in which speech, hospitality, order and attention sustained a community under threat. Their creation was temporary; the act of making it mattered all the same.

The structure has also allowed contradictions to remain visible. The company has praised wit and exposed its cruelty; celebrated love and shown its deceptions; admired generosity and permitted a final story to question what admiration ought to mean. Its departure asks the reader to leave the protected frame without expecting those questions to be resolved. The book's last movement returns from stories to ordinary lives. Beyond the church door, history continues.
