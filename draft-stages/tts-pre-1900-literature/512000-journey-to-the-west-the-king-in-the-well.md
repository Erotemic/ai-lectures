# Journey to the West: The king in the well

## The visitor at the third watch

The lamp in Xuanzang's room had burned low. Beyond the walls of Baolin Temple, the countryside was quiet. His disciples had gone to sleep, the monks had finished their prayers, and the last voices in the corridors had faded. Xuanzang had been reading an old scripture, reminding himself of the teaching he had received before the journey. Now the page blurred beneath his eyes.

A gust of wind entered the room. The lamp bent sideways and almost went out.

Xuanzang looked up. A figure stood at the door.

At first he thought the abbey had sent someone to disturb him. Then he saw that the visitor wore the robes of a king, though they hung wet and heavy. Water ran from the hem. His face carried the exhausted sorrow of a person who had been calling for help long after hope ought to have left him.

The man bowed to Xuanzang with a humility no living monarch would have shown to an unexpected stranger.

He said he was the king of Wuji, the Black Rooster Kingdom, whose capital stood a short distance to the west. He had come because no courtier would hear him, no priest would plead his cause, and no member of his household knew the truth of his death.

Xuanzang was frightened. If the figure was a ghost, then some terrible wrong had been done. Yet the pilgrim's first instinct was to listen.

The king began five years earlier.

A drought had broken the country. Clouds passed overhead without rain. Fields withered, ponds shrank, and the people came to the royal city asking for food that the royal stores could not provide forever. The king had joined his subjects in prayer and fasting. He had not wished to be a monarch who ate while his people starved. As the famine worsened, he feared that nothing he did would be enough.

Then a Daoist practitioner appeared from the mountains of Zhongnan. He called himself a man of the Complete Truth and claimed to command weather through his cultivation. He performed rites, summoned clouds, and brought rain. The long dry earth drank. Crops began to recover, and the people praised both the stranger and the ruler who had welcomed him.

The king was so grateful that he took the visitor into his household. He treated the Daoist as a brother, trusted his advice, and allowed him access to the gardens and private rooms of the palace. For two years they moved together as friends.

One spring day they walked through the royal garden. The trees had flowered, and the newly repaired pools were full. The Daoist led the king toward an eight-sided well lined with glazed stone. He told him that marvelous light had appeared below, some heavenly sign revealed in the depths.

The king leaned over to see.

The Daoist pushed him in.

The water closed over the royal robes. No cry reached the palace guards. The murderer covered the well with a stone slab, concealed it beneath earth, and planted a broad-leaved banana tree to hide the opening. Then he went back into the palace and took the king's likeness.

For three years the impostor had sat on the throne.

He had spoken in the king's voice, worn his clothing, issued decrees under his seal, and received the ministers who had once served the man he killed. He kept the queen and the prince apart, using the routines of the palace to prevent the family from comparing what each had noticed. Whatever strange doubts arose could be explained away by the king's changed moods or the demands of government.

The drowned man tried to seek help from the powers who governed the dead. He said the false king had secured protection among divine and infernal officials. His complaints did not reach a court that would hear them. Only now, when his three-year ordeal had run its appointed course, had a night-wandering deity directed him to the Tang pilgrim at Baolin Temple.

'You are on a holy journey,' the king pleaded. 'You are not my subject. You have no reason to obey the man who sits in my place. Will you help me?'

Xuanzang was deeply moved. He had come to the west for scriptures, not to pass judgment on kings, but the face of the visitor made it impossible to think of the matter as another country's private concern. If the story was true, a whole household had been made to live with a murderer for years.

He asked what proof a stranger could offer against an image as convincing as the impostor's. The king answered that the false ruler might copy his robes and features, but he could not reproduce one thing the dead king had brought with him: a ritual jade tablet, white and finely worked, mounted with gold. Xuanzang must show it to the prince. The prince would have a chance to discover what had happened if he could be led to speak privately with his mother.

The king added that he would appear to the queen that night as well, so she might be prepared to hear the truth.

Then he withdrew into the darkness.

Xuanzang started awake beside the lamp.

His book was still open. A faint wind moved the pages. He could have believed the entire visit a dream, except that he remembered the water so clearly, the king's face, and the precise place where the jade tablet was to be found.

He woke his disciples. Bajie complained that ghosts were most inconsiderate about sleeping hours. Sha Wujing listened without mockery and suggested that they inspect the doorway.

There, on the stone outside, lay a white jade tablet edged with gold.

Bajie stopped talking. Wukong picked up the tablet and examined it. Dreams could be false, he said. This dream had left an object behind.

## The red box

The ghost had also told Xuanzang that the prince of Wuji would hunt in the country the next day with three thousand soldiers. Wukong understood the opportunity. They could not simply enter a royal city, accuse the king of murder, and expect the man sitting on the throne to surrender. They needed to put the evidence before someone with reason to care and the authority to investigate.

Wukong devised a plan that sounded more like a puppet show than a diplomatic visit.

He would hide in a small box made from a monkey hair, carried by Xuanzang with the jade tablet. The box would appear to be a marvelous oracle that knew events fifteen hundred years in the past and fifteen hundred years in the future. The prince, attracted by curiosity, would be made to listen before he could dismiss the story as the invention of a wandering monk.

Xuanzang objected to one part of the plan. Wukong wanted him to remain seated in his precious brocade robe and refuse to bow to the prince. It would offend every rule of courtesy he knew.

'You have to make him stop,' Wukong explained. 'If you bow and explain yourself like any other visitor, he will ride away with his hunting party. Make him angry enough to question you.'

Xuanzang looked unhappy, but agreed to trust his disciple.

Wukong flew into the countryside before the prince arrived. He saw banners, horses, hunting dogs, beaters, and soldiers. At their center rode a young man in royal hunting dress. The prince was not yet old enough to possess the hard composure of his father's ministers, though he had learned how to command a great company.

Wukong changed into a white rabbit and bounded across the grass where the young man could see him.

The prince raised his bow. The rabbit fled. His arrow flew, and the rabbit seemed to be struck. The prince spurred his horse after it, leading his hunting party away from the open field toward Baolin Temple.

At the monastery gate, the rabbit disappeared.

Wukong slipped back to Xuanzang and shrank into the red lacquered box he had prepared. The prince arrived with his soldiers, looking for the animal he had wounded. Instead he found a foreign monk seated before him, wearing a robe of remarkable beauty and making no attempt to rise.

The prince's attendants shouted at the discourtesy. Xuanzang kept his place. The prince demanded an explanation. How could a monk refuse to honor a royal heir?

Xuanzang answered that he carried three treasures: a robe, a staff, and a small box whose occupant could speak about things no ordinary person knew. The prince could inspect them if he wished.

The young man was suspicious. A robe might be expensive and a staff might be ancient, but no wooden box could know the hidden affairs of kings. He threatened to arrest the stranger if he continued to make sport of the court.

When soldiers approached, unseen protectors held them back. Hands met an invisible resistance. For a moment the prince was uncertain whether he confronted a criminal or a holy man.

The box stirred. A voice came from inside.

Wukong spoke as though from a distant age. He began not with the drowned king but with the duties between father and son, king and subject, husband and wife. These relationships, he said, could be copied in outward appearance without being honored in truth. A prince who loved his father must sometimes ask whether the man occupying his father's chair deserved that name.

The young man demanded that the box speak plainly.

Wukong came out, growing from a tiny monk into his familiar monkey shape. The prince recoiled. Here was a creature no court etiquette had prepared him to meet, who stood beside the seated Xuanzang and calmly accused the ruler of Wuji of being a false king.

Wukong related the drought, the rainfall, the friendship of king and Daoist, and the murder by the well. He told the prince that the real king had been dead for three years. His body lay under the covered well in the palace garden.

The prince would not accept it. He had seen his father every day. He had heard the royal voice. His father had judged disputes, received memorials, and issued orders. If all that was false, then three years of his own life had been stolen from him without his knowing it.

Wukong showed him the jade tablet.

At first the prince grew angrier. The object was familiar. If the monks possessed it, perhaps they had stolen it. Had they entered the palace? Had they bribed a servant? Were they trying to frighten the royal household with a stolen ornament and a marvelous story?

The monkey did not answer anger with anger. This time the difficulty was not a demon that could be defeated by a staff. It was the fact that a son had spent three years trusting the wrong face. Wukong asked him to test a claim only one person in the palace could confirm.

He must go to his mother alone. He must not ask the ministers, who had lived under the impostor's rule. He must not make his questions in a public court, where every ear might report them to the false king. He must go by a private palace entrance and ask his mother whether her husband had been the same to her before and after the change three years earlier.

The prince still doubted him. But the jade tablet was real, and there was no easy explanation for the stranger's knowledge of an abandoned well in the royal garden.

He ordered his soldiers to remain at the monastery and rode back toward the city alone.

Wukong watched the horse diminish along the road. For the moment, their success depended not on transformation or weapons but on a private conversation between a mother and her son.

## The question only a mother could answer

The prince entered the palace by a quiet gate. He passed familiar halls, avoiding the officials who might ask where he had been, and went toward his mother's apartments.

He found the queen distressed. She had slept badly, and that morning she had been weeping. A terrible dream had come to her: the man she had loved stood before her wet from head to foot, with the grief of death upon him. He had tried to speak, but she could not make the dream tell her everything she needed to know.

The prince sat beside her and asked what had changed in the palace three years earlier.

She looked at him in surprise. There were many possible answers. Ceremonies had changed, ministers had risen and fallen, the king had grown colder. She had hardly been allowed a private meeting with her son for a long time, though the pretexts for their separation seemed reasonable when delivered by royal officers.

'Not the ceremonies,' the prince said. 'Tell me about my father.'

The queen was silent.

Before the change, she said at last, her husband had been affectionate. He had spoken with her warmly and treated the palace as a place where he could lay down the duties of court. Since that spring he had withdrawn from her. His voice was the same; his face was the same. Yet he had become like a stranger, offering explanations about age and weariness whenever she sought the closeness they had once shared.

The prince took out the jade tablet.

The queen knew it. Her breath caught. There was no reason for the object to be in the hands of her son after such a long absence, and no reason for a stranger to know where it had been kept.

He told her what the pilgrim and the monkey had said. He described the ghost, the well, the Daoist brother, the covered stone, and the three years of impersonation. The queen heard enough to connect the tale with her own dream. She wept for the man she might have lost and for the years in which she had lived beside his likeness without his love.

The prince wanted to rush at once to the throne room. He could summon the guards, show the jade tablet, and demand that the king explain himself. His mother stopped him. If the story was true, the person who had deceived them possessed formidable arts. He had taken a throne by murder. A young man's anger would not make him harmless.

They agreed that the prince should return to the temple and tell the strangers what he had learned.

He rode out without attracting notice, carrying the knowledge that his father might lie dead under a stone in the garden, while the man who ate at the royal table was something else entirely.

Wukong listened to the prince's report and nodded. The queen's words matched the ghost's account. The evidence had grown stronger, but it was still not enough to force a dangerous being from the throne.

He told the prince to return before nightfall. The hunting expedition had lasted long enough, and the longer the prince remained missing, the more the court would ask what had happened. To make the hunt appear successful, Wukong called upon the local gods and sent a great collection of animals onto the road ahead. The soldiers who had spent the day waiting outside the temple would return with an impressive bag of game, though the prince had scarcely drawn his bow.

The prince thanked the pilgrims and rode home among the soldiers. The people who saw the procession would say that their young heir had enjoyed a fine day of sport. None of them could know that he was returning with a question that threatened the entire court.

Wukong watched until the banners disappeared.

'Well,' Bajie said, 'the young prince believes us. Why not go straight in and accuse the monster?'

'Because the monster can look exactly like the king,' Wukong answered. 'And a whole court may choose the man on the throne over strangers carrying a story. We need the king himself.'

Xuanzang looked toward the distant city.

'His ghost has spoken,' he said. 'What more can he tell us?'

'Not his ghost,' said Wukong. 'His body.'

## The garden at night

When the city had gone to sleep, Wukong called Bajie aside. He spoke quietly about the palace garden and a treasure hidden there. It would be valuable enough, he hinted, to provide food and comfort for a great many miles of travel.

Bajie looked interested at once. He had spent most of his life carrying packs for people who spoke constantly about leaving worldly desires behind. If this were a chance to obtain something useful, he wanted to hear more.

The monkey described a secret place beneath a tree, and Bajie agreed to help. He brought his rake, which was excellent for digging and prying. The pair left Xuanzang and Sha Wujing at the monastery and flew over the city walls.

The palace after dark had a very different face from the bright procession the prince had described. Guard fires burned at the entrances. Courtyards lay empty. The great official paths, where ministers walked in daylight, were silent. Wukong and Bajie crossed them without calling a guard to challenge their business.

They found the garden. Its plants had grown thick in an area no longer used. Beyond the walkways the banana tree spread its broad leaves over the stone paving. It had been placed there to conceal a crime, and for three years it had succeeded.

Bajie asked where the treasure was.

'Under that tree,' Wukong said.

Bajie began to dig.

He uprooted the plant, shoving its roots aside. Beneath the earth his rake struck something hard. He cleared the soil and found an eight-sided stone covering. He levered it up until they could see a dark opening. Cold air rose from below, carrying the smell of still water.

'What kind of treasure is kept in a well?' Bajie demanded.

'Come down and see.'

Bajie peered into the darkness. The water was deep, and there was nowhere to stand. The monkey lengthened his staff and lowered it through the opening as though it were a pole into the water.

'Hold on,' he told Bajie.

The pig clung to the staff and began descending. Halfway down, Wukong mischievously lowered the pole too far, dipping his brother into the cold water. Bajie spluttered and shouted curses up the shaft. His voice echoed from the stones.

'You said treasure!' he cried.

'There is treasure below,' Wukong answered. 'Keep looking.'

The water beneath the palace was not an ordinary reservoir. Bajie found a domain belonging to the Dragon King of the Well. The underwater ruler lived in a chamber below the shaft, beyond the sight of anyone leaning over the garden's edge. He knew the old king's fate and had kept his remains from decay.

The dragon recognized Bajie as a former celestial marshal and received him with respect. Bajie, who had expected a chest of gold, asked whether there were coins or jewels to take away.

The dragon led him to a figure dressed in royal clothing, lying motionless as though asleep.

The drowned king's face was intact. Three years in water ought to have destroyed a body. The Dragon King explained that a special pearl had preserved the man's appearance. The corpse was not a treasure to be spent in the market, but it was the proof Wukong required.

Bajie was disappointed. A wet dead king would not buy a bowl of rice. Worse, someone would have to carry him up the shaft, through the palace garden, and all the way back to the monastery. Bajie argued that Wukong ought to come down and do his own fetching.

From above, the monkey called that he would not lift Bajie out until the king came with him.

Bajie tried to negotiate. He had seen the body. He could report what it looked like. Surely that was enough. Why make an innocent pig haul a corpse for miles?

The Dragon King made the matter more difficult by keeping the preserving pearl rather than sending it along as a gift. What had been safeguarded in the well would have to be carried into the world of air, with no further promise of enchantment. The task was urgent now.

At last Bajie took the dead king onto his back. Wukong extended the pole. The pig struggled up through the narrow shaft, water streaming from his clothing and the royal robes alike. When he climbed onto the paving, he set the body down and announced that his part of the errand was complete.

Wukong disagreed. They had to return to Baolin Temple before the guards noticed the disturbed garden.

Bajie looked at the king, then at the distant wall, then at the king again. He realized that the treasure promised by his brother had been nothing but work. He was wet, cold, tired, and burdened with a dead man, while Wukong stood beside him looking entirely pleased with the plan.

The monkey gave him the same answer he had given before: the body was necessary, and there was no time to debate who ought to carry it. Bajie lifted the king again. Together they climbed back over the palace wall and returned to the monastery by night.

## The promise Bajie made for Wukong

Xuanzang and Sha Wujing were waiting when they arrived. At the sight of the drowned king, Xuanzang rose from his seat. Even after three years the body retained its features. The face was calm, the hands still, the robes soaked in water from the hidden well.

Xuanzang knelt beside him. The visitor in his dream had been no passing apparition. Here was the man himself, pulled from the place where his murderer had hidden him. The monk thought of the queen who had believed her husband altered rather than gone, the prince who had doubted what he saw every day, and the kingdom that had accepted the same face on its throne without knowing whose mind lived behind it.

He began to weep.

Bajie was not weeping. He was remembering the water down his neck, the long climb, and the promise of treasure that had become a journey through a well. He stood up and announced that a dead body ought not to be a problem. Wukong, he told Xuanzang, knew how to bring a man back to life.

Wukong turned toward him.

'What have you told our master?'

'Only what you are capable of,' Bajie replied. 'Why bring him here if you intend to leave him dead?'

Wukong protested. The king had been in the well for three years. His spirit had walked as a ghost. He was not a man who had merely fainted on the road. Even the Monkey King could not reverse every harm with a wave of his staff.

Bajie insisted. If the monkey had been clever enough to discover the king and send another disciple to carry him out, he was surely clever enough to finish the rescue. If he refused, perhaps their master should remind him of the vow he had made to serve with compassion.

Xuanzang listened. There was only one thing he knew that could make Wukong obey when he would not otherwise agree.

He began to recite the tightening spell.

The golden band around the monkey's head contracted. Wukong cried out. His confidence vanished beneath the sudden pain. He begged Xuanzang to stop, promising that he would find a way to restore the king without going into the underworld to demand back his soul.

Bajie watched with satisfaction. Wukong had turned his hunt for treasure into a night of hauling a drowned monarch. Now the monk's binding spell had turned the obligation back upon him.

The king lay between them, still cold and silent.

Wukong held his aching head and considered the impossible task that he had just been forced to accept.
