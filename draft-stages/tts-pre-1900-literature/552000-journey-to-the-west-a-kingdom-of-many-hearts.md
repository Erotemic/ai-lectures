# Journey to the West: A kingdom of many hearts

## The monk who had too many hearts

The royal soldiers brought Sun Wukong to the court of Bhikshu Kingdom dressed as Xuanzang. They believed they had seized the monk whose pure heart could cure their dying king. The king believed this too. He sat upon his throne, hollow-cheeked and anxious, with the old state father watching from beside him. In the passages outside, guards waited to see whether the visitor would yield peacefully or require a blade.

Wukong walked upright through the hall. The courtiers bowed to the throne, but the false monk was not much disposed to the humble ceremony. He asked why he had been summoned. The king spoke as though he were making a generous request. His illness had resisted ordinary medicines, he said, but the state father had prepared a remedy, wanting only one final ingredient. If the holy traveler would provide it, the grateful kingdom would erect a shrine in his name and honor him after death.

What ingredient? Wukong asked.

His heart and liver, said the king.

The false monk pretended to consider this as a technical difficulty. He possessed a great many hearts, he explained. Which sort did they require? The state father answered that the heart must be black. Wukong invited a knife to be brought. If there was a black heart within his chest, he would gladly hand it over.

The officers brought a blade. The court watched the monk open his robe and cut into his own belly. Instead of dying, he produced heart after heart, displaying them like an absurd merchant setting out colored goods. There were hearts of vanity, greed, envy, ambition, calculation, fear, cruelty, cowardice, pride, and innumerable other dispositions. Wukong held each up in turn. The ministers stared, the king grew pale, and the state father, who had expected a frightened victim, could not prevent the demonstration. None of the hearts was the black one he had specified.

Then Wukong dropped his disguise. He closed his belly and became the Great Sage again. The true black heart, he announced, belonged to the man who had recommended killing children and then a visiting monk to purchase longer life for a single ruler. Let the king examine that heart instead.

The state father understood that his deception had been exposed. He sprang into the air with a dragon-headed staff, and Wukong pursued him above the palace. The staff struck the iron cudgel with a sound the court could hear from below. They exchanged blows while the king hid among his rooms, and the ministers could no longer pretend that their learned counselor was an ordinary physician. After a hard fight the old man changed into cold light, swept into the palace, gathered up the beautiful consort who had first brought him royal favor, and fled beyond the city.

## Where the state father lived

Wukong landed and told the officials to find their king. He was less concerned with receiving their thanks than with preventing a new disappearance. The ruler had believed that the demon was saving his life; he might follow him if the illusion were not fully broken. At last servants brought the trembling monarch from his hiding place. He asked how the handsome monk from the morning had become a sharp-faced monkey warrior. Wukong explained the exchange of clothing and faces. Xuanzang, the real monk, was safe in the hostel with Bajie and Sha. The king, ashamed and anxious, sent for the pilgrims.

Xuanzang arrived still bearing the muddy monkey face Wukong had fastened upon him. The officials recoiled at the sight of this collection of supposed monsters: a long-snouted pig, a blue-faced warrior, and an apparent monkey who was addressed as the master. Wukong removed the disguise, returned the monk to his proper appearance, and made the truth visible to everybody in the hall. The king bowed and begged pardon.

But Wukong was not finished. The old state father had escaped, and his favored woman had vanished with him. The court must reveal where the stranger claimed to have come from. The king recalled a place south of the capital, a Willow-Grove Slope and a residence called Qinghua Manor. He had heard the name when the old man first arrived with his alleged young daughter. He now had to admit that he had taken her into the palace without learning whether either person was what they claimed to be.

Wukong took Bajie with him. Bajie insisted on eating first; it was difficult, he declared, to strike a great blow while hungry. After a full meal he found himself equal to the expedition. The two rose through the clouds and went south. The land below was green with willows, but there was no manor where the king had said to look. Wukong summoned the local earth god and asked for the building. The spirit said there was no Qinghua Manor, only a cave concealed beneath a particular willow, and that the supposed state father was too powerful for the small guardian to have challenged unaided.

The god gave the instructions for opening the entrance. Circle the tree three times one way, three times the other, strike it, and call for the door to open. Wukong performed the ritual. The trunk gave way to a hidden entry, and behind it appeared an enchanted household filled with soft air, flowers, and a painted-looking calm. A stone screen named it the Qinghua Immortal Residence. The place advertised serenity; its inhabitants had tried to turn a kingdom into a slaughterhouse.

Behind the screen Wukong found the old man clasping the woman who had been called his daughter. They were lamenting the destruction of their three-year plan. He called out before they could flee. The old man seized his staff, and the cave filled with the clash of weapons. Outside, Bajie grew impatient and began smashing the very willow that hid the entrance. Its damaged wood bled, another sign that it was bound to the demon's magic. The fighting swept out through the doorway toward him. Bajie raised his rake, and the old man found himself pressed by two opponents.

The demon became cold light once more and tried to escape. The disciples followed, until a new radiance appeared above the trees and a figure called for them to stop.

## The white deer and the white fox

The newcomer was the Old Man of the South Pole, the god of long life. He greeted Wukong, acknowledged Bajie, and asked them not to kill the fugitive. The supposed state father, he explained, was his own white deer, an animal that served as his mount and had wandered away while he was occupied in a game of chess with a celestial visitor. The deer had stolen his dragon-headed walking stick and, over time, acquired enough transformation magic to present itself as an immortal counselor.

Wukong was not inclined to accept an answer that began with the word 'mine' and ended with the rescue of a monster. Ownership did not erase the danger posed to the children or to the monk. He demanded that the god show him the animal's true form. The Old Man released the captive light and commanded it to transform. The grand court physician became a white deer, bowing its head and trembling before its master.

The Old Man of the South Pole began to depart with the deer, but Wukong stopped him. Another creature had helped the deer deceive the king. That woman must be found and shown to the court too. The god agreed to wait. Wukong and Bajie entered the cave again. The woman tried to escape through the screen as cold light. Wukong met her with his staff. She fell and changed into a white-faced fox. Bajie struck her with his rake and killed her before she could be taken alive. Wukong made him leave the body intact enough for the people in the palace to see what their splendid royal consort had been.

The two disciples returned with the fox's body. The Old Man of the South Pole came with the deer. Before the king and his ministers, the whole deception was laid out: the supposed immortal healer was a stolen celestial mount, and the lovely girl whose presence had led the ruler to neglect his people was a fox spirit. Wukong did not invite the court to imagine that the king had therefore been innocent. A demon had suggested the slaughter, but a king had signed the orders. Neither marriage nor medicine required him to forget his duties.

The king asked the visiting god for a way to recover his health. The Old Man said that his more elaborate medicines were not with him, but he offered three dates from his sleeve. The ruler ate them and felt his strength returning. Bajie asked for some dates as well; the god promised to bring more on another occasion. It was a typical end to a dangerous affair: a divine mount was reclaimed, a monster was dead, a monarch received a cure, and Bajie was still thinking about food.

## The children returned

The court held a feast of vegetarian dishes for the pilgrims and their celestial guest. The king offered silver and gold for the onward journey. Xuanzang refused to take payment, and Wukong gave the simpler advice the ruler had never wanted from his false physician: govern with restraint, do less harm, and do not demand another person's life as the price of your own ease. The Old Man departed upon the white deer. The kingdom prepared a public procession to honor the departing pilgrims.

But the greatest event had not yet happened. As the royal escort approached the road beyond the gates, a wind passed overhead. The gods Wukong had enlisted came back carrying the children's cages. They set down one thousand one hundred and eleven cages, and from them came the sounds of living boys. Wukong called for the families to identify their children and take them home. Parents who had spent a night believing the cages lost and many days fearing the royal decree ran forward. Fathers bent to lift boys who had been shut away. Mothers called familiar names. Brothers and sisters recognized voices through the crowd.

The people thanked the pilgrims with such force that the procession could hardly move. Some offered food, others clothes or shoes, and many simply reached toward the rescuers as though touch might make the children safer. For almost a month the pilgrims were detained by hospitality. Bajie, who often complained that kings gave them too little supper, now found more suppers than he could comfortably refuse.

When they finally continued west, the road behind them was no longer lined with captive children. The country still belonged to a ruler who had nearly committed an enormous crime, but the people had their sons back, and the ruler had seen that an appetite for immortality could be fed by those most powerless to resist it. Xuanzang had been willing to die rather than let the children be killed. Wukong had taken the risk upon himself and brought him home alive. Their different kinds of courage had been needed together.
