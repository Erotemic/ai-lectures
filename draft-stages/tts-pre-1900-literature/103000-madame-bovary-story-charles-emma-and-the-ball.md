# Madame Bovary — Story: Charles, Emma, and the Ball

Madame Bovary begins not with Emma Bovary but with a boy named Charles entering a classroom. His awkward clothing and speech invite the other pupils to mock him. He is not stupid, and he is capable of sustained work, but he is slow to grasp the social performance expected of him. The opening does not ask us simply to laugh at Charles. It establishes a world in which embarrassment, imitation, and received judgments matter from the first scene. Charles eventually becomes a medical practitioner, though the scope of his professional training is limited. His mother arranges a marriage to a widow named Héloïse Dubuc, whose supposed income proves disappointing. The union is not affectionate. During a professional visit to a farmer with a broken leg, Charles meets the farmer's daughter, Emma Rouault. After his first wife dies, Charles marries Emma.

Emma has been educated for a time in a convent, where religious life mixed with a powerful appetite for romantic images. She has read stories in which love arrives amid elaborate gestures, distant landscapes, noble suffering, and sudden transformation. The novels have taught her to expect an experience that domestic life does not consistently supply. This is not the entire cause of her dissatisfaction, and it is not a reason to treat her as incapable of thought. The books have given vivid form to hopes she cannot otherwise express. They have also given those hopes a style that ordinary days may be unable to satisfy.

Their wedding is a crowded country celebration, full of food, people, and local customs. Emma enters the marriage imagining that emotional life will now achieve the intensity promised by romance. Charles is delighted with his wife and is grateful for the comfort of a settled household. He finds pleasure in repeating routines and in knowing that she is there. Emma gradually discovers that marriage has altered her legal and domestic circumstances without producing the inner transformation she expected. The same meals follow one another. The roads remain familiar. Charles's contentment feels to her like a failure of imagination. She watches herself waiting for an event that can rescue life from repetition.

An invitation brings the couple to a ball at the château of La Vaubyessard. The Viscount and the fashionable guests inhabit a world of elaborate clothing, music, food, and apparently effortless elegance. Emma dances, observes the movements of servants and aristocrats, and is dazzled by an environment she imagines could contain a different version of herself. Charles remains on the margins of the event, awkward and happy enough to be included. Emma returns home with a sensation of exile from a world that briefly seemed to be her rightful home. A single evening grows in memory until it dominates the following seasons.

The ball matters because it reveals a mechanism the novel will repeat. Emma takes an image from a social scene, transforms it into a promise, and then judges the present against the promise. The château is not a complete account of aristocratic life; she sees selected surfaces. Yet those surfaces have real power. She has learned that wealth and rank create opportunities she does not possess. Her error is not noticing that difference. It is believing that the particular spectacle contains a guaranteed form of happiness. The stronger the memory becomes, the less ordinary kindness can count as evidence of a life worth living.

Emma's dissatisfaction deepens. She experiments with household arrangements, grows alternately restless and listless, and sometimes treats Charles's affection with coldness. He cannot explain the changes. His attempts to please her are sincere but clumsy. He recognizes that she is unhappy without discovering how she describes the unhappiness to herself. A doctor proposes that another place might improve her health, and Charles decides they should leave the village of Tostes for Yonville-l'Abbaye. Emma is pregnant when they move. In a different kind of novel, relocation and a child might form the unmistakable beginning of improvement. Flaubert ends the first movement by making the move possible without promising that a change of address will solve anything.

We should keep both people in view. Charles has limitations of perception and skill, but he is not secretly arranging Emma's disappointments. Emma is capable of imaginative intensity, but her hopes can make other people disappear as individual minds. Both are embedded in institutions: marriage, medical practice, provincial property, and social reputation. The domestic world is not a blank space waiting for Emma to fill it with correct desires. It is full of demands, habits, and material conditions that her romantic vocabulary does not tell her how to negotiate.

The story has barely begun, and yet its central conflict is established. Emma wants life to carry the color and force of a story she has imagined. Charles wants the life he already has to continue. Neither desire can by itself explain the other person. The novel will follow the distance between their expectations through love affairs, money, professional failure, and public disgrace. The first lesson for a listener is to resist deciding too soon who deserves contempt. Flaubert's close observation gives us reasons to question almost everybody, including the sensibilities with which we judge them.
