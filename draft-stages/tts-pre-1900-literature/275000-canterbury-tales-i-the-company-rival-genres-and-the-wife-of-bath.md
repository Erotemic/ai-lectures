# Canterbury Tales I: the company, rival genres, and the Wife of Bath

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Company at the Tabard

April has returned. Rain has loosened the winter's hold, and the earth begins to grow again. Birds wake early. People who have spent the colder months indoors begin to think of roads, inns, distant shrines. In Southwark, just across the Thames from London, a traveller has stopped at the Tabard Inn. He is bound for Canterbury, where pilgrims visit the shrine of Thomas Becket, archbishop and martyr. By chance a company of other pilgrims arrives, and the journey that follows will become one of the great collections of stories in English.

We meet this company not as a finished list of social categories but through someone's eyes. The narrator says he will describe the people as they appeared to him, their clothing, their occupations, their gestures and ambitions. A Knight comes first. He has fought in many campaigns abroad and is presented as experienced, modest and courteous. His son, the Squire, cares about love, music and dress as much as arms. Their Yeoman carries a bow, arrows and the equipment of a professional attendant. Even in this apparently honorable group, generations and kinds of service look different.

A Prioress named Madame Eglentyne speaks French learned in England rather than Paris. She has elaborate table manners, an elegant cloak and a brooch bearing a declaration about love. The narrator admires her tenderness toward little animals. Whether such refinement is evidence of religious holiness is another question. Nearby is a Monk who prefers hunting to the strict discipline of his order. His horses and fur-lined sleeves make the financial possibilities of religious office visible. A Friar knows taverns, innkeepers and profitable acquaintances better than the poor people whom he is supposed to help. He can make himself welcome almost anywhere.

A Merchant displays his prosperous appearance and talks of profitable trade; the narrator adds that no one knows he is in debt. An Oxford Clerk spends what he has on books and learning. A lawyer has achieved the reputation of ceaseless work, though the narrator quietly suggests that he may not be quite so busy as he seems. A Franklin is a wealthy landholder who enjoys open hospitality and a table supplied with fine food. A Haberdasher, Carpenter, Weaver, Dyer and Tapestry Maker represent prosperous urban trades, accompanied by a Cook whose skill does not prevent the narrator from noticing an infected sore on his leg.

A Shipman brings knowledge of ports and tides, along with habits that would look less admirable ashore. A Physician can diagnose disease through the language of medieval medicine, but loves money too. The Wife of Bath wears fine red stockings, travels widely, and has had five husbands, not counting other companions of her youth. A good Parson and his brother, a Plowman, stand apart: one teaches by living as he preaches, the other works hard and gives generously. The contrast is not between church and lay society but between service and exploitation.

Finally come the Miller, strong and noisy, fond of obscenity and willing to steal from grain; the Manciple, skilled at buying provisions for lawyers; the Reeve, a narrow and watchful estate manager; the Summoner, who trades upon people's fear of church courts; and a Pardoner with supposed relics, singing voice and a talent for persuading people to pay. Chaucer the pilgrim says he is reporting their appearances in good faith. Readers learn to notice the distance between what he notices and what he understands.

Then the innkeeper, Harry Bailey, makes a proposal. Travel can be tedious, he says, so why not hold a storytelling contest? Each pilgrim should tell tales going to Canterbury and returning. The best, judged for entertainment and instruction, will receive a supper paid for by the others. Harry offers to travel with the company and serve as judge. Everyone agrees, or at least is drawn into agreement. The Knight is selected to begin.

There is no finished account of this competition. We will not hear every promised tale or return to the Tabard for the prize. What survives is more interesting than a tidy sequence would have been: people argue, interrupt, answer insults, take over genres belonging to other speakers and sometimes refuse to tell the kind of story demanded of them. Chaucer gives the road a company of witnesses, but no single witness the authority to explain everyone else. The first speaker, the Knight, will offer a polished romance. It will be answered by a drunken miller who thinks refinement has had the floor long enough.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Knight’s Tale, Prison and Love

Theseus, duke of Athens, rides home after a victory, accompanied by his wife Hippolyta and her sister Emily. A group of women in mourning interrupts the celebration. Their husbands have been killed by Creon, ruler of Thebes, and denied proper burial. Theseus turns from homecoming to war, defeats Creon, and arranges honorable funerals. In the ruins his men find two wounded young Theban nobles, Palamon and Arcite. They are cousins, close as brothers, and now prisoners in an Athenian tower. This is the beginning of the Knight's long romance: public power has resolved one injustice by creating the captivity in which another story begins.

Years pass. One May morning Emily walks in a garden below the tower. Palamon sees her through the window and is struck by love before she knows he exists. Arcite looks down and falls in love too. Their old comradeship collapses into argument over who first saw her and who has the better claim. Palamon declares that Arcite has broken the law of brotherly loyalty. Arcite answers that love follows laws of its own. Neither can marry Emily, and neither seems interested in asking what Emily wants. The tower holds them, but their idea of love creates an additional prison.

A friend of Theseus secures Arcite's release under strict condition that he never return to Athens. To everyone outside the tower this must seem good fortune. To Arcite, banishment means losing the chance even to see Emily. Palamon envies his cousin's freedom, believing Arcite can gather military strength and come back. Each man calls the other's condition more fortunate than his own. Chaucer gives Fortune the power to make release feel like punishment and imprisonment feel like a remaining hope.

Arcite returns to Thebes, wastes away in love and changes so greatly that his face and voice become hard to recognize. After a dream of Mercury he travels back to Athens in disguise and enters service near Emily under the name Philostrate. His competence wins Theseus's trust. Palamon meanwhile escapes the tower, with the help of a drugged guard, and hides in a grove before fleeing the city. On a spring morning Arcite rides there to honor the season and gives voice to his anguish. Palamon hears him, recognizes the name and steps out of hiding. The two agree to settle their rivalry by combat. Arcite promises to bring weapons and provisions so they can fight properly the following day.

They meet again and fight so fiercely that both seem to have forgotten what victory would mean. Theseus happens upon them while hunting with Hippolyta and Emily. At first he orders both killed: one has broken his banishment and the other escaped prison. The women plead for mercy. Theseus relents, acknowledging how love can make intelligent people foolish. Yet his remedy is not to free Emily from the contest. Instead he promises a grand tournament a year hence. Each rival will bring a hundred knights; the victor will have Emily as his wife.

The Knight tells this with impressive control of time. Years are crossed in a few lines; a morning in a grove can be extended until every emotion has been exposed. The public ruler who settled a war now proposes to settle private desire by staging another, regulated war. Palamon and Arcite accept his plan eagerly. Emily has been seen, praised, desired and promised, yet has hardly entered the argument as a speaking agent. Before the tournament, she will go to Diana with a prayer different from either man's. And the divine answers will expose the fragile confidence with which humans assume that a tournament can make Fortune obey them.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Knight’s Tale, Tournament and Fortune

A year has passed since Theseus promised a contest between Palamon and Arcite. Athens prepares an arena of great expense and extraordinary detail. At one side is a temple of Venus, its walls crowded with images of lovers' pleasures and suffering. At another is Mars, surrounded by battle, terror and violence. At a third stands Diana, goddess of hunting and chastity. The buildings are beautiful, but the images also warn that the gods do not govern the same desires or grant compatible requests.

Palamon goes first to Venus. He does not care chiefly about winning the tournament; he wants Emily. Arcite prays to Mars for victory. Emily, caught between them, prays to Diana that she be permitted to remain unmarried. If she must marry, she asks that it be the one who truly loves her. The flames at her altar change strangely, and Diana makes clear that she cannot evade marriage. Palamon receives an ambiguous sign. Arcite hears a promise of victory. Above them, Venus and Mars contend until Saturn devises a way for both promises to be fulfilled. The audience is allowed to see the machinery of fate while the humans move through it unaware.

Teams arrive with hundreds of armored companions, splendid banners and animals, and an international cast of warriors. Theseus announces rules designed to limit killing. Weapons and tactics are restricted; defeated combatants are to be taken from the field rather than slaughtered. The tournament begins anyway with violence so intense that the distinction between sport and war is difficult to maintain. A mounted combatant falls, blood darkens the ground and warriors are dragged away. Palamon is captured, Arcite's party prevails, and Theseus declares Arcite the winner.

But Fortune has not completed her work. As Arcite rides in triumph, looking toward Emily, a supernatural disturbance strikes his horse. He falls and suffers terrible internal injuries. The doctors try to save him, but his condition worsens. His victory can neither be enjoyed nor exchanged. Facing death, Arcite commends Emily to Palamon, praising his cousin's worth and asking that she eventually consider him. He dies. The story has spent many scenes dividing the two men, then returns them to a bond made possible only by irreversible loss.

A great funeral follows. Theseus chooses the very grove where the cousins once fought, transforming the site of private rivalry into a public space of mourning. Trees are cut, a funeral pyre raised and elaborate rites performed. Emily mourns too; she has never been simply the prize imagined by the men. Only after years and an extended reflection on change does Theseus bring Palamon and Emily together. He argues that all things must pass, that individuals are parts of a larger order, and that wisdom accepts the limits imposed by time. The two marry, and the tale ends with a claim to happiness.

Should the ending satisfy us? The Knight offers a serious account of how rulers, institutions and ceremonies attempt to turn violent uncertainty into peace. Theseus certainly moves from rage to mercy, and he tries to make a broken social world livable. Yet he never gave Emily the freedom she requested, and his philosophical speech cannot undo Arcite's death. The tale asks listeners to respect chivalric order without forgetting the suffering that order organizes. It matters that the next pilgrim to speak is not another knight. The Miller, drunk and determined to be heard, will replace temples and noble tournaments with a carpenter's bedroom, a tub, a window, and a joke whose victims cannot explain it away as the working of Saturn.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Miller’s Tale, a Night of the Flood

The Host wants the next speaker to be someone respectable, but the Miller will not wait. He has drunk too much, boasts that he will tell a wonderful story and refuses to defer to the social order that made the Knight first. The Reeve, himself once a carpenter, objects when he hears the story will make a carpenter ridiculous. Chaucer the pilgrim warns his audience that this will be coarse material and offers readers the chance to turn the page. Then the tale begins and overturns the Knight's world at once.

In Oxford lives John, an aging carpenter with a young wife named Alison. John is possessive and afraid she will betray him. He rents a room to Nicholas, a clever student of astrology who has an eye for Alison. Nicholas courts her boldly. She agrees to an affair if they can evade her husband's watch. A parish clerk named Absolon also desires Alison. He sings beneath her window and brings gifts, but she has no interest in his rehearsed devotion.

Nicholas devises a plan from the learned astronomy that gives him authority in John's eyes. He locks himself away and pretends to have discovered a terrible secret in the stars: another flood, like Noah's, will drown the world that very night. John panics. Nicholas advises him to hang three large tubs from the roof, one for himself, one for Alison and one for Nicholas, stocked with food. At the appointed hour they will cut the ropes and float above the waters. John makes the preparations. When night comes, he climbs into his tub and falls asleep, certain that he is the one person in Oxford prepared for catastrophe.

Nicholas and Alison slip into bed. Absolon comes to the window and begs Alison for a kiss. She leans out in the darkness but offers him not her lips but her backside. He kisses it before realizing what has happened, and rushes away in disgust. Returning with a hot iron borrowed from a blacksmith, he asks for another kiss. Nicholas now sticks his own backside out the window and breaks wind; Absolon brands him. Nicholas shouts for water to cool the burn. From high above, John hears the cry, assumes the flood has come, cuts his tub loose and crashes to the floor.

The neighbors gather. John tries to explain the flood, while Nicholas and Alison declare him mad. His injuries are real, his fear is made ridiculous, and the audience within the tale believes the conspirators. The laughter at the end is both liberating and cruel. A pompous guardian has been deceived, but a frightened man has also been hurt and publicly humiliated. A student who boasted of his knowledge has been burned, and the elegant lover has lost his dignity in a very literal fashion.

The Miller has not simply told a dirty story after a noble one. He has seized the same concerns and turned them into their bodily opposites. In the Knight's Tale two men watched a woman through a high window; here lovers meet at a low one. In the romance, celestial powers arrange a tragic fall; here a student invents celestial knowledge and causes a carpenter to fall. The noble tournament has become a contest of practical jokes, and the confident boundary between learned wisdom and fraud has broken down. The Reeve, hearing his former trade mocked, insists on answering. The storytelling game has become personal.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Reeve’s Tale, the Mill and Revenge

The Reeve has reason to dislike the Miller's story. He is an older man who once worked as a carpenter, and the Miller has just made a carpenter the butt of a public joke. The Reeve responds with a tale about an arrogant miller, Simkin, who boasts of his wife, daughter and ability to cheat his customers. If the Miller thinks he can laugh at another craft, the Reeve will show him what happens when the laughter turns back upon its teller.

The story takes place near Cambridge. Two students, John and Aleyn, come from a college with grain to be ground. They have been warned that Simkin steals flour, so they plan to watch the work closely. Simkin is not alarmed. While their attention is diverted, his wife releases their horse, which runs free among the mares. The students spend the day trying to retrieve it. During their absence the miller steals their grain. By nightfall they are hungry, tired and stranded, too late to go back to the college. Simkin agrees to lodge them, enjoying his advantage over young men who imagined that education would protect them from a working tradesman's tricks.

The miller's house is small. In the darkness everyone must sleep close together: Simkin and his wife in one bed, their daughter Malyne nearby, and the students within reach. Aleyn decides to retaliate for the theft by sleeping with Malyne. John, unwilling to be left without a share in the night's revenge, moves the infant's cradle to confuse the positions of the beds and sleeps with the miller's wife. In the morning darkness Aleyn, returning from Malyne's bed, follows the misplaced cradle and climbs beside Simkin by mistake. He boasts of what he has done with the daughter, thinking he is speaking to John. The miller erupts in anger; a violent fight breaks out. His wife strikes with a staff, intending to hit a student but hitting her husband instead. The students escape, having recovered flour and taken other advantages from the household.

This is not a gentle victory of clever youth over corrupt power. The story treats sex as a means of revenge between men, and the consent and vulnerability of the women are not given equal attention. Its comic machinery depends on confusion of persons in a dark room, but the social violence does not disappear because the teller regards it as funny. Simkin steals; the students answer by treating his household as property that can be taken in payment. Replacing a stolen bag of grain with sexual humiliation reveals an economy of grievance in which other people bear the cost.

The Reeve has gained his revenge on the Miller inside the contest: his story is set in a mill, and a boastful miller is beaten and mocked. But he has also made the atmosphere of the road harsher. The Knight's Tale turned conflict into public ceremony; the Miller turned it into practical jokes; the Reeve turns it into vengeance against a family. There is no reason to assume that every pilgrim shares the same moral judgment, or that Chaucer wants us to make the victims vanish behind the punch line. The Cook is delighted by the rough entertainment. He begins a story about an apprentice, but the manuscript will not preserve a finished conclusion.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Cook’s Tale, Perkyn and the Broken Tale

After the Reeve's rough story, the Cook laughs with particular pleasure. He knows a good joke and promises to add one of his own. The Host teases him about his trade, because a cook who sells spoiled food and allows flies and dubious ingredients into his kitchen is already living in a comic story. The Cook replies in kind and begins his tale with a London apprentice named Perkyn Revelour, whose name suggests the revels that occupy too much of his time.

Perkyn is handsome, strong and lively. He is also unreliable in precisely the ways that trouble the people who employ him. He drinks, dances, joins street celebrations and spends more time seeking pleasure than serving his master. When he is sent to conduct an errand, there is always an opportunity for diversion. The other apprentices of his company begin to follow him into trouble. His master tries repeatedly to correct him, but discovers that warnings have no power against the next night's entertainment.

At last the master dismisses Perkyn, deciding that one bad apprentice will spoil the household's whole discipline. Perkyn finds a new home with an equally idle young man who lives on gambling and theft. The young man's wife, though she keeps a shop, also has a reputation for illicit activity. The surviving fragment stops at the point where this household might become the setting for the apprentice's next adventure. Chaucer does not tell us what Perkyn does next, how the friends fare, or whether the expected comic punishment arrives. We must not turn the end of the manuscript into an invented ending.

The brevity of the fragment is itself useful evidence of the larger project's unfinished state. The storytelling competition promises more tales than Chaucer completed. Here the Cook begins with an attractive setup, then the text breaks off. Later manuscripts sometimes attempted to fill such gaps with non-Chaucerian material. Those additions are historically interesting but should not be silently included as if Chaucer wrote them. The fragment stands on its own as an opening without an ending.

Even unfinished, the story adds a new London setting to the collection. The Knight's aristocratic Athens, the Miller's Oxford household and the Reeve's Cambridge mill are followed by a city of apprentices, trade, dancing and commercial discipline. The same question keeps shifting form: what is owed to the community by a person who wants pleasure or advantage? Perkyn wants to live as if every day is a holiday; his master needs work done and his household governed. The question is unresolved because the story itself is unresolved. On the pilgrimage road, however, the next surviving cluster will take us far beyond London, into the life of a woman repeatedly driven across the sea by the ambitions and fears of rulers.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Man of Law’s Tale, Constance at Sea

A Roman emperor has a daughter, Constance, admired for both beauty and Christian virtue. Merchants returning from Rome describe her to the Sultan of Syria. He falls in love with a woman he has never met and declares that he will marry her, even if converting to Christianity is the price. Arrangements are made and Constance is sent across the sea. The journey is described as a separation from everything familiar, not a triumphal bridal procession. She has little power over the reasons for her departure.

The Sultan's mother is furious at the prospect of her family's conversion. She secretly organizes the murder of her son and the Christians at a feast, then has Constance put into a rudderless boat and cast away. The tale's religious language characterizes the mother as an enemy of Christianity, but the plot is also one of court violence: a ruler's marriage plan, made without the consent of the people whom it will transform, becomes the occasion for massacre. Constance survives because her voyage, against ordinary expectation, carries her into another country.

She reaches the coast of Northumberland and is sheltered by a constable and his wife Hermengyld. Her piety gradually wins affection, and Hermengyld converts. A wicked knight attempts to pursue Constance and, when refused, murders Hermengyld while she sleeps beside her guest. He plants the bloody knife so that Constance will be blamed. In a public judgment his false oath is exposed by a supernatural sign. Constance survives a second plot in which the structures of power nearly accept evidence manufactured against her.

King Alla of Northumberland hears of her and marries her after converting to Christianity. They have a son, Maurice. But the king's mother Donegild plots against the marriage. While Alla is away, she intercepts letters. News of a healthy child becomes a false report of a monstrous birth; an order to care for Constance becomes a supposed command to put mother and child aboard another vessel. The letters serve the same purpose as the bloody knife: someone controls a written message and thereby controls what the powerful think they know. Once again Constance is set afloat, now with an infant.

Her boat survives other dangers at sea and eventually reaches the vicinity of Rome. Through a long sequence of recognitions, Alla comes to Rome, encounters Maurice, and discovers that his wife lives. Constance is finally reunited with her father. Alla and Constance are together again, though the tale has not offered its heroine a simple restoration of safety. Her children, her marriages and her faith have repeatedly been made instruments of other people's political plans.

This is a story of endurance shaped by providence, miracle and a particularly severe conception of virtue. Its geography is expansive, its religious divisions are represented from the Christian teller's side, and its heroine is praised most often for suffering without surrender. It is tempting to describe her as merely passive, but the tale values fidelity through situations in which ordinary choice has been removed. The questions worth retaining are who has the power to name innocence, who can falsify documents, and whether survival under such conditions counts as agency. The next major voice on the road will insist, very loudly, that experience itself can claim authority and that women can speak about marriage in their own names.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Wife of Bath’s Prologue, Five Marriages

Before telling a story, the Wife of Bath tells a life. She has had five husbands, and she knows that clerks armed with religious books will object. She does not apologize. Experience, she says, is her authority, even if it is not the authority of learned men. The prologue that follows is longer than many complete tales. It is an argument in which biblical quotation, jokes, memories, accounts of property and a history of violence compete with one another.

The first three husbands were older and wealthy. She describes how she gained advantage over them by accusation and performance: she could complain that a man was jealous, miserly or unfaithful, then insist that the husband prove his love by giving her control of money. Much of what she says is comic and deliberately provocative. It is also a record of a culture in which marriage governs economic survival. Her account of manipulation is inseparable from the possibility that men own the resources upon which a wife depends.

Her fourth husband has a mistress and causes her real pain. She retaliates by making him jealous. She admits that she too has taken pleasure outside the rules she applies to others. There is no stable position from which to identify her as either a victim or an all-powerful victor. Then she recalls her fifth husband, the younger clerk Jankyn. She loved him most deeply, she says, and he was the one who hurt her most. She had wealth by this stage; he brought youth, learning and books.

Jankyn owns a collection of antifeminist stories about wicked wives and female deception. Night after night he reads it aloud. Its learning becomes a household weapon, meant to make his wife ashamed of her sex and obedience seem the natural conclusion of scholarship. She tears pages from the book. He strikes her so hard that she suffers lasting hearing damage in one ear. She falls and claims he has killed her; when he approaches, she strikes back. Eventually he gives her what she calls mastery in their marriage, including control of his property and of her own speech. She says they lived happily afterward.

That happy ending should not make the violence disappear. She has exposed the contempt built into a tradition of male writing about women, but also described an intimacy that could become dangerous. Her voice is fearless and contradictory. She can repeat a church argument in order to mock it, praise a husband's love while recounting his cruelty, and claim that she has won a struggle which cost her bodily harm. The storytelling competition makes room for testimony that polished romance might omit.

Other pilgrims keep interrupting. The Pardoner says he is frightened to marry after hearing her account. The Friar and Summoner quarrel about how much she should be allowed to say. Their interventions prove that her story is not a private confession. It is being heard and judged by a social audience, some members of which are eager to correct her. When she finally begins her tale, she will move from lived experience to a world of Arthurian knights and transformations. Yet the central question will remain the same: what does a woman most desire, and who gets to answer on her behalf?

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Wife of Bath’s Tale, Sovereignty

The Wife of Bath carries her listeners back to King Arthur's time, when Britain is imagined as a place of fairies, wonders and knights. One knight violates a young woman by rape. The law says he should die. Arthur's queen and her ladies are given authority to decide his fate and offer a condition: he has a year and a day to discover what women most desire. If he returns with the right answer, he may live. The story begins with coercive violence, not with a harmless romantic misunderstanding. That beginning remains part of the meaning of the eventual marriage.

The knight rides everywhere and hears contradictory answers. Some women wish for wealth, others pleasure, fine clothing, praise, freedom, security or a reputation for virtue. A male listener might prefer the neat comfort of one answer. The knight's travel reveals that women are not interchangeable objects from which a general rule can be extracted. As his deadline approaches he meets an old woman in a clearing, where a group of dancing women seems to vanish before his eyes. The old woman promises the answer if he will grant her a future request. He agrees because his life is at stake.

She whispers that women desire sovereignty: the power to govern their own lives and their relations with men. At court the assembled women accept the answer, and the knight is spared. But the old woman now claims the reward. She demands marriage. The knight protests that she is old, poor and unattractive. She insists on his promise and the wedding takes place. The man who was ordered to learn about women's will is horrified when a woman uses that will to bind him.

On their wedding night the old woman challenges his contempt. She argues that true nobility comes from virtue rather than ancestry. Wealth is not proof of goodness, and age may possess experience and fidelity that youth cannot promise. Then she offers him a choice. She can be old and faithful, or young and beautiful with no assurance that he can control her conduct. He cannot decide. At last he yields the decision to her, asking that she choose what seems best. She rewards the surrender by becoming both beautiful and faithful. The story ends with harmony and the Wife's provocative prayer against husbands who refuse to be governed.

There are at least two important ways to hear the ending. Within the fairy-tale economy, the knight learns to abandon the right to command another person, and a marriage becomes possible only when he grants his wife choice. But the transformation also leaves the raped woman from the opening behind. She receives neither voice nor visible justice beyond the threat initially made against the knight. The new wife is rendered desirable according to conventional standards of beauty, although the tale has just challenged those standards. A promise of freedom has been bound to a fantasy of a perfectly accommodating woman.

The Wife of Bath is not presenting a courtroom argument free from contradiction. She is arguing from inside a world where men take women's bodies and voices for granted. Her tale makes power visible by giving it to the queen and an old woman, then makes the result attractive through magic. After it ends, the Friar and Summoner are quick to revive their own quarrel, turning the road from the question of marriage to the business of church officials who profit from other people's fears.
