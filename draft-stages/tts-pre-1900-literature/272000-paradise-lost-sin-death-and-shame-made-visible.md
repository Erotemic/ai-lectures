# Paradise Lost — Sin, Death, Shame, and the Body of Allegory

There is a scene in Paradise Lost that can surprise even a reader prepared for devils and angels. At the gates of Hell, Satan meets a figure named Sin, partly a woman and partly a grotesque creature whose body produces monsters. Beside her stands Death, a threatening figure who is also her child. Sin tells a history of violence within this family. The story is shocking, but it has more than the function of horror. Milton has made an abstract moral relation visible as kinship and a physical condition.

Personification is a literary technique in which an idea is given a body, a voice, and the ability to act. The poem does not ask us to believe that every instance of wrongdoing literally creates the same anatomical monster. It uses an invented genealogy to say something about consequences. Satan's first act of rebellious self-exaltation produces Sin; his relation to her produces Death; Death's violation produces further creatures that attack their mother. Injury generates injury, and the agents of harm cannot keep the effects safely beyond themselves.

Sin controls the keys of Hell. That position is an allegorical puzzle as well as a plot necessity. The barrier between rebellion and the created world opens not through brute force but through a promise of appetite. Satan persuades Sin to let him pass by offering new victims and a larger field for destructive action. The same structure will recur when the serpent offers Eve new powers and hides their cost. Desire is invited to mistake access for liberation.

The body's meaning changes throughout the poem. Before the Fall, Adam and Eve are unclothed without shame. Their sexual intimacy does not make them guilty. After the fruit has been eaten, they recognize one another's bodies differently. They feel exposed, cover themselves, and turn mutual attention into suspicion. This is not a claim that human bodies are intrinsically corrupt. Milton marks a change in how the people inhabit and regard their own bodies.

The punishments of Book Ten have bodily forms. Human beings will suffer labor, pain, aging, and death. The earth itself becomes more resistant. Satan's great apparent victory becomes a humiliating transformation when the fallen angels begin to hiss as snakes. They reach for fruit that disintegrates into ash. The symbol is exacting: they have reduced human flourishing to the satisfaction of appetite, and now appetite gives them no nourishment.

Sin and Death then build a road across Chaos. A relation that began as an episode at a gate becomes an enduring connection between Hell and the world. Milton turns the threat of evil from a single intruder's visit into a condition that will spread through human history. The road is a powerful image for consequences that become easier to repeat once a first passage has been opened.

There is a risk in reading these figures too mechanically. If Sin is given a female body and a history of violated maternity, one may ask what gendered assumptions the allegory draws upon. Those questions belong beside the poem's explicit hierarchy of Adam and Eve and its treatment of childbirth after the Fall. Personification does not remove a text from its social meanings. It can concentrate them in especially forceful images.

Try comparing Sin's story in Book Two, Eve's altered experience of nakedness in Book Nine, and the serpents' frustration in Book Ten. Each scene turns a choice or relation into an experience of the body. But they do not all assign guilt in the same way. Sin is harmed by Death's violation; Eve makes a choice under deception; Satan returns to suffer an image of the deceit he practiced. A serious interpretation must distinguish the positions of aggressor, target, and agent rather than treating everyone who suffers as equally responsible.

The result is a poem whose abstractions act and whose bodily actions carry moral meaning. Milton gives sin a genealogy, desire a sound, shame a covering, and the spread of death a road. By making these relations concrete, he lets the reader feel how a choice can move beyond an isolated instant and reorganize the world in which later choices are made.
