# Paradise Lost — Genesis, Classical Epic, and Milton's Inventions

The account of Adam and Eve in Genesis is remarkably compact. It tells of creation, a garden, a command, a serpent, disobedience, judgment, and expulsion. Paradise Lost expands this material into a poem of more than ten thousand lines, filled with angelic wars, political councils, intimate conversations, cosmic journeys, and a history of the future. The expansion is not a transcription of the Bible into longer sentences. It is a work of imaginative interpretation.

Begin by distinguishing the source from Milton's additions. Genesis does not give us a parliamentary debate in Hell, a conversation between Sin and Death at the gates, or Raphael sharing a meal with Adam and describing artillery in Heaven. Nor does it supply the detailed motives Milton gives Eve as she considers equality or Adam as he decides to join her in disobedience. These episodes reveal how the poet interprets theological questions through invented scenes. A reader can value their artistic power while remembering which assertions are scriptural and which belong to this particular seventeenth-century poem.

Milton also inherits a much older literary form: epic. From Homer and Virgil he takes an elevated invocation, action beginning in the middle of a crisis, large similes, catalogs of named figures, speeches before assemblies, divine intervention, journeys through perilous regions, and scenes in which a guide tells the hero about worlds beyond immediate sight. Satan's voyage through Chaos resembles an epic expedition. Michael's visions recall the prophetic and underworld journeys by which classical heroes discover their destinies.

These devices are not included simply to make the poem resemble a famous predecessor. They are often used against the expectations they create. A magnificent military leader turns out to be directing courage toward cruelty. A vast cosmic battle gives way to a household argument about the division of labor. The final action is not a warrior entering a conquered city but a couple walking out of a garden. The epic form teaches the reader to expect one kind of greatness and then asks whether a different greatness matters more.

The poem's time structure is another invention. Its opening occurs after Satan's rebellion but before his temptation succeeds. Raphael later recounts the war in Heaven and creation, while Michael speaks of events extending far beyond the lives of the first couple. Readers are repeatedly required to adjust what happened first, what has been foretold, and what is being remembered. The effect is not narrative confusion for its own sake. The present act in Eden is placed within both a prehistory of freedom misused and a future history of freedom continually tested.

Milton's classical names have complicated lives. Urania, the Muse invoked in Book Seven, carries the resonance of Greek poetry, while the narrator insists that his inspiration belongs to a heavenly rather than a pagan source. Mulciber, a name familiar from Roman mythology, appears among the fallen makers of Pandemonium. The devils' catalog uses the names of ancient gods later worshipped by human communities. This is an epic map of cultures interpreted through Milton's Christian commitments, not an impartial history of those religions.

Pay attention, too, to the difference between what characters say and what the poem endorses. Satan's arguments borrow noble ideals. Raphael's instruction is filtered through his concern for Adam's vulnerability. Adam's descriptions of Eve combine deep delight and assumptions about hierarchy. Milton gives these words dramatic positions; he does not turn every line spoken by a character into a timeless maxim by the author.

A practical reading exercise is to place Genesis chapter three beside Book Nine. List the events present in both, and then list Milton's expansions: the separate gardening proposal, Satan's extended rhetoric, Eve's thoughts after eating, Adam's conscious choice, and the struggle over blame. Ask what each addition makes the reader feel or judge. Do not assume that more detail always means greater fidelity to the source. The detail is Milton's principal means of turning a brief sacred narrative into a sustained drama of motives and responsibility.
