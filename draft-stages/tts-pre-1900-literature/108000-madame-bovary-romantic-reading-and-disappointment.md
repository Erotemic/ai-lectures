# Madame Bovary — Reading, Romantic Scripts, and Disappointment

Emma Bovary has read romances, but the claim that books alone cause her ruin tells us too little. She is also a farmer's daughter who has encountered convent education, aristocratic spectacle, provincial marriage, commercial goods, and a narrowly arranged set of adult options. Her reading supplies images for longings that have many sources. The question is how those images teach her to interpret an ordinary day. A sentimental novel can give a person words for desire; it can also teach a person to expect that desire will announce itself in a particular scene, with particular gestures, in a life already arranged for an audience.

At the convent Emma is drawn to stories of grand emotion, and the language of religious devotion mingles with literary glamour. Her imagination moves among beautiful settings, suffering lovers, and promises of transformation. She does not approach these images as examples among many possible lives. She treats them as standards by which her own experience ought to be measured. When Charles offers affection and reliability, she struggles to recognize those qualities as the substance of a relationship. They lack the theatrical force by which she expects love to identify itself.

The ball at La Vaubyessard expands the same structure from reading to social observation. Emma sees elegant clothes, music, refined food, and confident gestures. She imagines that the visible scene gives access to the hidden lives of its participants. The ball becomes a memory she polishes until it seems more complete than the life she actually lives. But a brief visit is not evidence of the feelings that occupy the guests on other days. Her experience is genuine; her conclusion is less secure. The novel exposes a common habit: using the most photogenic moments of someone else's life as a standard for the whole of our own.

Rodolphe understands Emma's preferred emotional language and uses it. He does not need to invent a new theory of seduction. He has to repeat the promises of exceptional love at moments when she is disposed to hear them. The agricultural fair gives us the scene in which romantic ideality becomes an effective technique of persuasion. Emma hears a declaration she can inhabit. Rodolphe hears a means to begin an affair. Their words are not empty for both people in the same way, and that inequality matters. She is not simply duped by beautiful language; she supplies a powerful need for the language to be true.

The promise to run away is the decisive collision between imagined form and actual commitment. Emma sees a future drawn in vivid outlines. Rodolphe calculates the inconvenience and withdraws. His letter performs sadness without taking on the burdens of the decision. The resulting illness is not a theatrical punishment for reading. It is the devastation of someone who had made the imagined future essential to the meaning of her present. The romantic vocabulary has offered her an absolute; the other person was never bound by the same absolute.

Léon, initially shy and sentimental, seems at first a gentler possibility. When the affair begins years later, however, novelty turns into habit. Emma experiences the same impatience and repetition that had troubled her marriage. A new person cannot permanently provide the first intensity of a new story. This repetition is part of Flaubert's argument. The problem is not merely that she selected the wrong lover. It is that she asks a succession of relationships to prove an entire theory of happiness, one that leaves little room for the other person's independent wishes.

Still, the novel's irony should not be allowed to vindicate the provincial world without scrutiny. The men who speak confidently of progress often fail at their responsibilities. The town judges women by standards it does not apply evenly to men. Debt is sold as convenience before it becomes coercion. A woman may have more reason than her husband to dream of another life, even when the dream is harmful. Emma's romantic scripts mislead her, but her dissatisfaction is not proof that the world around her deserves gratitude. An attentive reader must be able to criticize the scripts and the constraints together.

One useful comparison is between a remembered scene and a practiced habit. The ball occupies a few hours but dominates Emma's memory for years. Charles's care occupies many ordinary days but does not become a spectacular event. Flaubert makes duration compete with intensity. If we listen only for grand declarations, we reproduce Emma's method of judgment. If we listen only for the virtues of routine, we risk ignoring what routine denies. Madame Bovary teaches us to ask what an image can reveal, what it cannot show, and what happens when someone tries to live inside the image instead of the world that produced it.
