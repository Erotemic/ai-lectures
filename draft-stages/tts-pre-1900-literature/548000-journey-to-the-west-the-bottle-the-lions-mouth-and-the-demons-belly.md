# Journey to the West: The bottle, the lion's mouth, and the demon's belly

## Beyond the gate

Wukong had frightened the outer patrols of Lion-Camel Ridge into flight. Still dressed as Little Wind-Driller, he passed the first gateway and entered the heart of the demons' stronghold. The victory he had won by rumor did not prepare him for what lay within. The ground was littered with bones. Human remains lay in heaps like broken timber, and the air bore the smell of a place where killing had become routine work. Servants went about their tasks among the dead, cutting and cooking as if this were an ordinary household preparing dinner. It was one thing to hear that a kingdom had been eaten. It was another to walk between the remnants of the people who once lived there.

He went deeper. Beyond the grisly outer precincts lay a surprising inner garden with trees, flowers, and clear spaces. The demons had surrounded themselves with pleasure in the midst of ruin. Wukong passed through another gate, keeping his patrolman's clapper and bell in hand, and finally came into sight of the rulers. On their high seats sat three beings whose bodies alone explained the fear of their subjects.

The first was the blue-maned lion. His face and teeth were terrible, but more frightening was the way the air seemed to gather around his mouth, as though anything in reach might be drawn into it. Beside him sat the yellow-tusked elephant, huge and wrinkled, with a trunk that moved like a powerful arm. The third was a great roc, sharp-eyed and watchful, with the stillness of a hunter who could afford to wait because speed would be his whenever he chose to move. Beneath the three lords stood armed chiefs and officers. The patrolman was not supposed to be looking closely at the dais, and Wukong lowered his gaze.

He bowed and announced that he had returned from his circuit. Had he seen Sun Wukong? the kings demanded. He had, he answered, and the sight had nearly taken his courage. The Great Sage was sharpening an enormous iron staff upon the rocks and boasting that the three kings would be no better than firewood under it. The lion began to sweat. Perhaps they should never have planned to eat the Tang monk. Perhaps the sensible thing was to bar the doors and let the pilgrims go by.

A call went out to secure the gates. The demon officers returned with dreadful news: the outer soldiers had abandoned their posts. Wukong concealed his delight. He had not merely driven away the army; now he was using its disappearance to convince the rulers that their own power was failing.

## The fly in the closed hall

When the gates were shut, Wukong saw that his lie had created a problem. He was trapped in a room with the three beings he had come to investigate. Any further questions about his route or the order of the patrols could expose him. He therefore made the story more frightening. Sun Wukong, he told the lords, had a habit of changing into a fly and slipping through a crack. Closing the doors might do nothing at all. He might be present even now.

The lion ordered the guards to watch for flies. Wukong reached behind his head, plucked a hair, blew on it, and produced a bright buzzing insect. The insect flew straight toward the lion's face. At once the three rulers and their guards leaped up in alarm. Weapons, brooms, and hands swatted at the little visitor, with each blow producing still more confusion. Wukong, standing among them in his patrolman's clothes, found the spectacle irresistible. They were mighty kings, and all of them were dancing around a fly that he had made from his own hair.

He laughed. That was his mistake. When his face changed with amusement, the disguise failed for an instant and the familiar monkey mouth appeared. The roc saw it. He caught Wukong, called to his elder brothers, and declared that their supposed watchman was the Great Sage himself. The lion protested that this was a servant he knew. Wukong displayed his borrowed badge. The roc was not persuaded. He had seen the mouth change.

The guards seized the suspect, bound his arms and legs, and examined the body beneath the clothing. They found yellow hair, a tail, and all the other unmistakable signs of a monkey. Human and near-human disguises, the story explains, could deceive the eye without changing every part of Wukong's form. The third king had found the seam. Wukong's careful theater of ranks and badges was over.

The lion was pleased. If the most dangerous disciple had already been captured, the Tang monk himself would soon be theirs. The roc advised against celebrating too early. Wukong could escape from ropes, locks, and prisons. The third king owned something designed for precisely such an enemy. He ordered the precious yin-yang bottle brought from its storage room. Thirty-six lesser demons carried the vessel into the hall, though it was scarcely taller than a child. Its weight did not belong to its size. The forces sealed inside it demanded a company arranged according to the numbers of the heavens.

They unbound Wukong only to draw him into the bottle and seal the lid. The three kings retired to drink, confident that the troublemaker would soon dissolve within it. In the darkness, Wukong sat quietly, waiting to learn what would happen. At first the inside felt cool. He laughed at the name and reputation of a vessel that seemed more comfortable than the room outside. Perhaps the demons had paid too much for an empty jar.

## Three hairs when the strength is gone

The bottle heard his voice. Fire rose at once, filling the narrow space. Wukong used his power to resist flame. After a time, snakes emerged and struck at him. He caught them and tore them apart. Then came burning dragons, winding around his body. He could not command the bottle to stop. He grew tall, hoping to burst the sides, but the bottle grew with him. He shrank, and it shrank too. His oldest methods of escaping a tight place were useless here.

Then he felt pain in his ankle. The fire had softened part of his body. That frightened him more than the snakes. How could the Great Sage's body, tempered through calamity and heavenly furnaces, begin to fail? He had already been trapped by cymbals, bags, and golden light on this journey. Now he was sealed in a vessel that made itself larger or smaller to match him, while the people who held it planned to eat his master as soon as he was gone.

For a moment Wukong wept. He remembered accepting Guanyin's charge, joining Xuanzang, rescuing Bajie and Sha, and traveling through so many dangers toward the Western Heaven. There had been quarrels and dismissals, but he had come back every time. Would it end in a bottle, without even a chance to tell Xuanzang what had become of him?

As he reached behind his head, his fingers found three hairs unlike the rest. They were stiff, strong, and still intact. He remembered them: Guanyin had given him three life-saving hairs long ago, near the mountain where the dragon horse joined the pilgrimage. In an age of transformations and stolen treasure, he had almost forgotten the simplest gift kept on his own body.

He pulled out the three hairs, blew upon them, and changed one into a diamond-pointed drill, one into a piece of bamboo that could serve as a spring, and one into a cord. He rigged the little tools together and began boring at the bottle's base. The work demanded care. The demon's treasure could follow his body's size, but the hair-given drill found a weakness in its construction. A point of light appeared. The hole widened. The two energies that made the vessel deadly began to escape. When Wukong turned into a tiny insect and slipped through the opening, the fire had already failed.

Outside, the demons were still drinking. Wukong had survived their best prison, but he did not stay to boast in their hall. He escaped through the cave and returned to the place where Xuanzang waited. He found the master praying for his disciple's protection, not knowing how near that prayer had come to being for the dead. Wukong answered from beside him, and the relief in the camp was immediate. Bajie and Sha stared as their elder brother described the bottle, the three hairs, and the hole he had drilled through the bottom. The monkey who had earlier laughed at the demons' fear of a fly had learned how narrow the distance could be between a trick and death.

## The lion accepts a challenge

Xuanzang heard that the demons were still upon the road and refused to pass until the danger was dealt with. Wukong would rather not tempt the bottle again. But his master did not intend to leave a den of man-eaters behind without securing the way. Wukong took Bajie with him, leaving Sha Wujing to watch their master and the baggage. The two went up the mountain to call out the kings.

The lion emerged first. He was angry that the monkey had escaped a treasure expected to dissolve him. Wukong challenged him openly, displaying the iron staff whose description had driven thousands of soldiers away. The lion claimed he would grant passage if Wukong could survive blows to the head. Wukong agreed. The first strike achieved nothing. The second made the monkey appear to split into two figures, another piece of magic that left the lion uncertain whether he had wounded an enemy or merely multiplied him.

The fight moved from the cave entrance into the air. Sword and iron staff flashed in long arcs. Wukong recounted the staff's history and its victories; the lion answered with renewed blows. For a while neither gained an advantage. Then Bajie, watching from below, decided that the time had come for a second fighter to earn glory. He rushed forward with his rake raised high. The lion flinched at the sudden appearance of the huge, long-snouted warrior and retreated.

Bajie mistook the retreat for a rout. He chased the demon down the slope, shouting that the victory would belong to him. The lion stopped, took his true form, and opened a mouth vast enough to make the landscape seem small. Bajie saw the darkness waiting behind the teeth. His courage disappeared. He flung himself into thorn bushes and lay there trembling, willing to let every thorn tear at his clothing as long as the jaws did not close on him.

Wukong followed the retreating enemy. When the lion turned its mouth upon him, Wukong did not flee. He put away his staff, faced the open jaws, and allowed himself to be swallowed. The demon thought it had captured him. Bajie, from the bushes, believed the same. There was no time for the pig to study whether Wukong had leaped too willingly. He crept out, ran back to camp, and told Xuanzang that the lion had eaten their eldest brother.

Xuanzang collapsed in grief. Wukong had survived so many ordeals and had been a cause of so much anger; now the master saw all those quarrels in a different light. The Great Sage who could be exasperating and violent had also carried the party through danger after danger. Bajie, instead of consoling him, proposed that they divide the baggage. Sha could return to the Flowing-Sand River. Bajie could go back to Gao Village. They could sell the white horse and find a way to pay for their master's funeral. Sha objected, and Xuanzang cried out in despair.

## A passenger the lion cannot digest

Inside the lion, Wukong had not died. He could hear the monster's voice and the congratulations of the other kings. He spoke up from the belly. The lion demanded to know what he intended to do there. Wukong described the place as comfortable lodging. Perhaps he would stay for the winter. The lion threatened to go without eating until its prisoner starved. Wukong answered that he could make a meal of the liver, lungs, and other organs around him, and even set up a cooking pot upon the bones. The other kings, hearing this from the lion's own stomach, lost much of their confidence in the victory.

The lion ordered a strong salty drink so that he could vomit up the monkey. He swallowed it and retched until his whole body shook. Wukong held fast. The lion could not expel him. The demon tried another expedient, calling for a medicated wine that he believed would kill the intruder. But Wukong positioned himself in the throat to catch each swallow. The wine went to the passenger rather than to the belly that held him. The lion took cup after cup and found, to his astonishment, that the drink had no effect upon his own face.

Wukong, however, had drunk it all. He began to behave like a drunken acrobat. He kicked, swung, twisted, and turned somersaults among the organs. Each movement was felt outside as a wrench of agony. The great blue-maned lion doubled over upon the floor. He had swallowed an enemy he could not digest, could not dislodge, and could scarcely persuade to be still.

Beyond the cave, Xuanzang was mourning a disciple who was alive. Inside, the surviving disciple was making the monster suffer for the swallowing. None of the travelers yet knew whether this outrageous tactic would free the road or lead to another trap. The chapter ends with the lion writhing, and the Monkey King still very much inside him.
