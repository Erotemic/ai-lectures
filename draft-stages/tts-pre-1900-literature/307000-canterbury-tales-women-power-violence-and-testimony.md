# The Canterbury Tales — Women, Power, Violence and Testimony

The women in the Canterbury Tales are not a single class of characters. The Wife of Bath speaks at length about experience and the ownership of property. Griselda is praised for obedience. Dorigen seeks a marriage without tyranny but is placed under the force of a promise. Constance survives murderous plots and the manipulation of letters. Cecilia refuses an imperial officer's demand to abandon her faith. Virginia is killed by her father after a corrupt judge arranges to seize her. If the collection seems to ask one question about women's place, it will not permit one simple answer.

The first distinction to make is between speaking within a story and being spoken about by another. The Wife of Bath controls her own prologue and can make biblical learning answer to lived experience. Yet even she performs before an audience that interrupts and evaluates her. Virginia, by contrast, is introduced through the Physician's ideal of beauty and virtue; her death is told as evidence for a moral lesson in which she has limited control. The narrator's praise may conceal rather than correct the unequal distribution of agency.

In the Knight's Tale Emily is treated as the object of two men's rivalry and a ruler's settlement. She asks Diana to preserve her freedom to remain unmarried. The answer she receives does not grant that desire. A casual summary that calls her the happy prize of the victorious lover erases her only fully articulated preference. In the Wife's tale the queen commands the judgment of a rapist and an old woman demands the authority to decide her marital future. The contrast shows how the same romance tradition can question the conventions it employs.

Some protagonists demonstrate agency chiefly by endurance. The Man of Law's Constance moves from one forced separation to another. Her constancy is admired, but the tale's plot depends upon powerful people falsifying evidence and deciding where she may live. Griselda's extreme patience similarly draws the eye toward a husband whose power requires no justification until his conduct becomes unbearable. Those stories are morally interesting because the virtues they celebrate cannot be separated from the systems that make such endurance necessary.

The Physician's Tale is more severe. It makes a judge's sexual predation and a father's lethal honor appear as linked steps in a story of virtue. The young woman's death may fulfill the story's claim to chastity, but it also reveals a tradition in which the preservation of male reputation can be imagined as more important than a girl's continued life. We should be able to describe that logic without adopting it. The medieval teller's argument is an object for criticism, not an instruction to the present audience.

The Second Nun's Cecilia supplies another kind of religious agency. She refuses an official's command, persuades her husband to honor her vow, and uses speech to oppose a political order. She too suffers violence, but the hagiographic form interprets her refusal as a triumph rather than her obedience as a virtue. The Prioress, by contrast, is a female religious teller whose tenderness is bound to a story of persecution. It is not enough to sort narrators into good and bad on the basis of gender or religious role.

Chaucer's achievement is not that every voice receives justice. It is that the collection repeatedly places official moral claims beside the experiences they authorize or disregard. To read carefully is to identify who is allowed to narrate, who is allowed to decide, and which forms of suffering are explained away by a teller's chosen genre. The deepest argument is often not spoken openly in any one tale. It appears when we compare the lives that the stories make possible with the lives they make expendable.
