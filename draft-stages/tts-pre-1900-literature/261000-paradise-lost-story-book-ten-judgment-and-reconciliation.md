# Paradise Lost — Book Ten: Judgment, Serpents, and Reconciliation

The guardians of Paradise return to Heaven in distress. The human pair has fallen despite the warnings and watchfulness. God does not accuse the angels of having failed to make choice impossible. Adam and Eve were left free; the catastrophe is not explained as a defect in the patrol. The Son descends to judge them. Milton carefully distinguishes this role from the Son's future mission to redeem, though the judge also acts with compassion.

Adam and Eve cannot meet the divine visitor with the openness they had shown before. They hide, and when called to answer, they speak through excuse and accusation. Adam points toward the woman given him; Eve identifies the serpent. They tell parts of the truth, but each uses the chain of events to move responsibility away from the self. The Son pronounces consequences upon the serpent, upon Eve, and upon Adam. For the humans these include painful childbirth, gendered authority, hard labor upon a resistant earth, and eventual death. The poem's words about women and subjection come from a seventeenth-century theological world, and they remain morally difficult for readers who reject its assumptions.

The judgment also carries a promise. The serpent's victory will not be permanent. A descendant of the woman will ultimately defeat the power that has wounded humanity. The Son clothes the ashamed couple in skins. This practical kindness matters because their innocence cannot simply be restored. Grace in the poem begins to operate within an altered condition, not by pretending that the Fall never occurred.

Elsewhere Sin and Death have sensed an opportunity. Across Chaos they build a broad causeway from Hell toward the world of human beings. Satan's solitary journey can now become a route for spreading destruction. He encounters the two figures, who celebrate his success, and proceeds proudly home. There he expects to receive a conqueror's welcome from the host in Pandemonium.

He rises to announce that he has brought down humanity. Instead of applause he hears a mass of hissing. The fallen angels, including their leader, have been transformed into serpents, the shape Satan used for his greatest deception. A grove of tempting trees appears before them; they reach for the fruits and find that what they bite turns to ashes. They have celebrated appetite and cunning as liberation. Now their bodies enact the futility of a hunger that cannot satisfy itself. The audience is invited to compare this spectacle with Satan's earlier eloquence on the burning lake.

Meanwhile the created world experiences alteration. Seasons become harsher, the relations among creatures less peaceful, and the conditions of life more dangerous. Milton dramatizes a universe in which human moral disorder has physical reach. Whether we understand that as theology, poetry, or both, it is part of the scale of his catastrophe. Human choice has consequences that the choosers cannot contain.

Adam now confronts what he believes he has done to all his descendants. He wishes he had never been made. He imagines innumerable future sufferers reproaching their ancestor. This is a terrible enlargement of guilt: one person's act seems to have burdened people who did not consent to it. Adam's anger falls upon Eve, who approaches him humbly. He rejects her at first, turning remorse into accusation again.

Eve then offers herself as the one who might bear the punishment alone. Adam begins to relent. Their new language is no longer the claim that somebody else caused everything. They can admit injury, speak of mutual responsibility, and seek mercy. They consider the prospect of death and the suffering of children not yet born; then decide against destroying themselves or refusing a future. They pray together. Their repentance does not reverse the judgment. It changes how they will face it.

The ending of Book Ten is one of the poem's most consequential turns. Humanity has lost Edenic innocence, yet the two people have recovered the possibility of a relationship that includes confession and forgiveness. Satan returns to a palace and finds his triumph reduced to animal noise. Adam and Eve remain on the ground, still mortal, but can speak honestly together. The poem has begun to separate possession of power from the capacity for hope.
