# The Decameron · Day 2: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Story: Day Two Begins, Fortune Turns

Morning comes to the country retreat. The members of the company rise, walk through the gardens, eat, and enjoy the ordered pleasures that now mark their days. The beauty of the setting is no accident. Boccaccio places this calm against the memory of Florence, where the plague had interrupted the most ordinary acts of trust. These ten people cannot remake the city by telling stories. They can create, temporarily, a household in which a meal follows work, an invitation can be accepted, and one person's speech receives another's attention.

The wreath has passed from Pampinea to Filomena, the new queen. Instead of leaving the subject entirely free, she asks for stories about people who are beset by dangers and misfortunes but unexpectedly reach a happier outcome. The theme is Fortune: the instability of possessions, health, rank, travel, and desire, and the possibility that a change which looks disastrous might lead somewhere no human plan could have reached. The day's tales will move through roads and seas, palaces, markets, burial chambers, exiles, shipwrecks, and changing political regimes.

It would be a mistake to suppose that this theme makes every person happy in the same way. One traveler escapes a robber; another becomes rich by piracy before losing everything again. Some men and women survive because a stranger gives shelter. Others are brought into a wealthy marriage by coincidences so dramatic that the storyteller's hand is nearly visible. A reunion that the tale names fortunate may depend on a character's humiliation or lost freedom. The ending cannot erase every injury suffered along the way.

This is also the point at which the group's internal rules become more distinct. Dioneo is permitted to speak last and to take greater liberty with the subject assigned to the others. That privilege is a formal piece of playfulness: even a carefully governed company finds room for a voice that resists its pattern. Each other narrator must work within the queen's theme, though the ways they understand it differ. The story that feels most like an adventure may be the one that most clearly shows Fortune mocking the confidence of merchants and kings.

We have already heard that clever words can rescue a person from danger. Now we will hear how little cleverness sometimes matters when a ship breaks apart, a ruler dies, or a rumor reaches the wrong ears. But we will also find people who act resourcefully amid uncertainty. The distinction between chance and choice will become less tidy the more we listen. Neifile begins in a city excited by miracles, with a performer who thinks he can deceive a crowd and discover that the crowd is much more dangerous than he imagined.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 1: Martellino and the False Miracle

Three friends, Martellino, Stecchi, and Marchese, travel to Treviso. Martellino makes his living through physical comedy and impersonation; he is good at inventing a face or a manner and convincing other people that the performance is real. The city is crowded with people visiting the body of a man regarded as holy, Saint Arrigo. Pilgrims believe that miracles have occurred near his remains, and the crowd pushes eagerly toward the shrine.

Martellino decides that the place is perfect for a joke. If he pretends to be unable to move, his friends can carry him through the press of people and lay him near the holy body. Then he will suddenly stand as if cured. The plan depends on the crowd believing that a convincing physical appearance is evidence of a miracle. He twists his limbs, lets his friends handle him as a suffering patient, and succeeds in reaching the shrine. When he rises, the spectators are delighted. They have witnessed, or think they have witnessed, proof of divine power.

But someone who knows Martellino recognizes him. The man shouts that the crippled posture was an act. Delight turns at once into anger. The people who were ready to celebrate a miracle are now ready to punish a sacrilegious fraud. They beat the performer, and his friends see that mere explanation will not save him. The crowd is too angry to hear anyone who appears to be defending the joke. Stecchi and Marchese resort to a second accusation: they claim that Martellino has stolen something. That draws the attention of officials, who take custody of him and remove him from the mob.

The second trick brings its own danger. The authorities are willing to prosecute a supposed thief, and Martellino might be hanged for an offense he did not commit. The friends must now arrange to tell their story to the proper person. They reach a powerful official who can see the absurdity of the whole sequence. Amused by the affair, he releases Martellino, and the three leave with relief and gifts of clothing.

The tale has two reversals. The false invalid becomes a miraculous patient, then a hateful fraud; the man saved from a crowd by being labeled a thief is threatened by the charge that saves him. Performance can turn on its performer. The story is not an eyewitness report proving that miracle cures never occur. It is a comedy about human spectators, the speed with which excitement becomes certainty, and the equal speed with which certainty becomes violence. Martellino has spent his life observing how other people can be fooled. For once, the consequences escape his control. His survival belongs less to his skill as an actor than to his friends' improvisation and the willingness of an official to laugh.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 2: Rinaldo and Saint Julian

Rinaldo d'Asti is traveling on business when he falls into conversation with men he meets along the road. They ask how he prepares for a safe night's lodging. He tells them that he is accustomed to commend himself to Saint Julian, a saint associated with hospitality. He believes the prayer has helped him find shelter in many difficult journeys. His companions hear this with less devotion than interest. They are robbers, and they now know that Rinaldo has money worth taking.

The men wait for a favorable place, seize what he carries, strip him of his goods and clothing, and leave him exposed in the winter cold. One thief mocks Saint Julian, suggesting that Rinaldo may now test whether the saint can provide comfort. Rinaldo staggers toward Castel Guglielmo, searching for any opening into safety. A gate is closed. He finds what shelter he can and trembles outside a house. His prayer has apparently failed at the very moment when he needed protection most.

Inside, a lady is alone. She had expected a lover to visit her, but he has been delayed. Hearing the stranger shivering outside, she sends to discover what has happened. Rinaldo is brought in, warmed, clothed, and fed. The lady is attracted to him and invites him into a more intimate companionship. A night that began in fear and deprivation becomes, from Rinaldo's point of view, an astonishing reward. He is not made prosperous by his own planning. He has found hospitality because another person's plans went wrong, while the stranger's decency makes a place for him.

Meanwhile, the robbers are caught. Their crimes are discovered, and Rinaldo's goods are recovered. In the morning he can travel again with both his property and a story about how well Saint Julian has provided for him. The thief's mockery has been answered, at least to Rinaldo's satisfaction. The saint did not stop the robbery; the favorable end came by a chain of human actions and accidents.

Filostrato tells an adventure of providence and chance without forcing a reader to accept an explanation. One character interprets the entire episode through devotion. Another might see thieves who were unlucky, a lady who was generous and lonely, and a road on which one person happened to approach the right house. The narrator allows Rinaldo's gratitude to stand. The larger comedy lies in the mismatch between what he expected when he prayed for a comfortable lodging and the strange path by which he received it. Fortune can take a blessing that looked like protection and turn it into rescue after injury. The original loss is real, even though the ending restores more than possessions.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 3: Alessandro and the Disguised Abbot

Three Florentine brothers inherit wealth and squander it through lavish spending. For a time they seem to enjoy the signs of success; then credit fails and their resources disappear. Their young nephew Alessandro has been helping to conduct their business abroad, in England. His own hopes now become entangled in political disorder there. With the old arrangement no longer secure, he decides to leave the country and make his way toward Rome.

On the road he joins a traveling party led by a person dressed as an abbot. The supposed abbot is young, commanding, and surrounded by attendants who treat the traveler with extraordinary respect. Alessandro's courtesy and competence make him welcome. An attraction grows between him and the person he believes to be a churchman. The abbot finally finds a moment to speak privately with him, and the two arrange to share a room. Alessandro is disturbed by what seems to be an inappropriate proposal from a superior cleric. The stranger then reveals the secret: the abbot is in fact a woman disguised in religious clothing.

She is a daughter of the king of England, fleeing a marriage that has been arranged for her. Her costume and her household's obedience have allowed her to travel without being recognized. She chooses Alessandro as the man she wishes to marry. Once he understands who she is and what she intends, their connection changes from bewildering intimacy into a plan for marriage. They arrange the necessary steps, and political circumstances that had ruined Alessandro's household become the route by which he enters a royal family.

The pair eventually return to England. Her father accepts the marriage and Alessandro gains position and wealth enough to repair the misfortunes of his uncles. A family that had moved from extravagant prosperity into ruin has found an ending so favorable that no business calculation could have predicted it. Where the brothers exhausted their own resources, the nephew's fortunes turn on flight, a disguise, a chance meeting, and a woman's decision to trust him.

Pampinea's tale is full of the conventions of romance: hidden identity, royal parentage, perilous travel, and marriage as restoration. It is also a story about who has the power to choose. The princess must take on the public appearance of a man and a religious superior to evade a marriage she does not want. Alessandro must learn to distinguish a frightening surface from the person behind it. The story treats the revelation as liberating, but its happiness also depends on a royal parent's eventual willingness to recognize a match made without his initial permission. Fortune brings the lovers together, but the woman's escape from a planned marriage has created the possibility that Fortune can act upon.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 4: Landolfo and the Floating Chest

Landolfo Ruffolo of Ravello has wealth enough to live comfortably, but he cannot be content with it. He loads a ship with merchandise and sails in hope of a much larger profit. The venture fails. Market conditions turn against him, and his carefully calculated trade leaves him poorer than before. Disappointed and desperate to recover his standing, he converts his vessel into a pirate ship. In that dangerous trade he prospers, taking wealth from other merchants by force.

His success is fragile. Genoese ships capture him, and the booty he has amassed is lost. Then a storm destroys the vessels carrying him. Landolfo, who has moved from legitimate commerce to piracy to captivity, now faces the sea with nothing left but his life. He clings to whatever floating object offers survival. A chest becomes his refuge. He cannot know what it contains. He knows only that it is large enough to keep him above the water while the wreckage drifts.

The sea finally carries him toward Corfu. A woman on the shore discovers him, takes pity on his condition, and helps him recover. She gives him warmth, food, and protection. In this household, generosity is not a reward for his deeds; the stranger does not know what he has done. When Landolfo opens the chest, he finds jewels of enormous value. The object that was merely a means of staying alive has also carried the fortune he thought gone forever. He returns home rich and sends a gift to the woman who helped him. This time he decides not to risk everything again in pursuit of still more wealth.

The story's reversals are exceptionally clear. Landolfo's first commercial judgment fails. Piracy makes him rich but destroys his moral claim to superiority over other thieves. His captors appear to control his future, until the storm makes their power meaningless. At the point of greatest helplessness, he acquires the goods that restore him. His prize has not been earned by shrewder trading or more violent plunder. It has come through an accident of wreckage and water.

Lauretta does not ask us to forget the people robbed along the way. Their losses do not disappear simply because Landolfo ends happily. The tale exposes a frightening uncertainty built into mercantile life: ships sink, cargo changes hands, markets refuse to obey the plans of traders. But it also gives a significant place to care. A woman on the shore treats an exhausted stranger as a person in need. The chest brings jewels; she brings the conditions under which a man can live to use them. The final change in Landolfo is not only that he has money again. He has learned, at least for now, what he did not know when the first ship departed: there can be enough.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 5: Andreuccio’s Night in Naples

Andreuccio comes from Perugia to Naples with money to buy horses. He is inexperienced in the great city's ways, and he takes too little care to conceal how much he is carrying. A young woman learns of his money and arranges a deception. She claims to be a relative, draws him into her house, tells a story calculated to win his confidence, and offers him a place to stay. He believes himself fortunate to have found family in an unfamiliar city.

During the night he steps into a privy and falls through a badly placed opening into filth below. The house is closed against him, and his purse is gone. He knocks, protests, and discovers that the woman who welcomed him has no intention of admitting him again. His good clothing, his money, and his sense that he understands the situation have all disappeared. He wanders through Naples dirty, frightened, and angry.

A pair of men encounter him and propose a new venture. They plan to rob the tomb of an archbishop recently buried with valuables, but first they insist that Andreuccio wash away the smell of the latrine. They lower him into a well. When watchmen approach, the thieves scatter and leave him trapped. Other men come to draw water; the sudden weight upon their rope, and then the unexpected sight of a person rising from the well, sends them fleeing in terror. Andreuccio escapes yet again and somehow becomes involved in the planned robbery after all.

At the church, the thieves open the tomb and send him inside to search the body. Andreuccio has learned something from his earlier betrayal. He takes a valuable ring and conceals it before passing other objects up. His companions, suspecting that he is withholding treasure, close the tomb and abandon him in the dark beside the dead archbishop. He calls out in fear. Then a second group of thieves arrives, intending to plunder the same grave. One of them opens the tomb. Andreuccio seizes the man's leg, terrifying the intruders, and climbs free while they run.

By morning, he is battered, filthy, exhausted, and still alive. He returns home without the horses he meant to buy, but with the archbishop's ring. The adventure is sometimes called a lucky escape, though it has involved repeated robbery, fear, and a narrow passage through death. Fortune has not protected Andreuccio from humiliation. It has allowed him to survive it and to leave with a costly object he did not acquire honestly either.

Fiammetta makes Naples almost a second character. The city is a place of sharp distinctions between those who understand its hidden arrangements and those who do not. A stranger's claim to kinship is a trap. A well is both danger and means of rescue. A grave becomes a temporary prison, and the dead man's ring becomes the sign of life continuing. The tale has the pace of a long comic chase, but its humor depends on the transformation of a sheltered merchant into someone who has learned, by one terrible night, to distrust the apparent explanation of every door he enters.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 6: Beritola and the Lost Sons

Madonna Beritola's troubles begin not with a careless commercial wager but with political defeat. Her husband, Arrighetto Capece, has held office in Sicily. When the ruling order changes, he is imprisoned and his family is exposed to danger. Beritola flees with her children, hoping to reach a place of safety. Then pirates separate her from her two small sons. She is left alone on a deserted shore, deprived of her household, her country, and the children for whose lives she had risked escape.

The story pauses over her isolation. Beritola lives among wild creatures, nursing young deer as if they were children. Her care has nowhere else to go. Travelers eventually discover her. Corrado Malaspina and his wife bring her to their household, offering protection to a woman they cannot fully place within their world of names and relations. She returns to human society, but not to the life she knew.

Meanwhile, the two boys have survived in separate and difficult circumstances. The older has taken service under another name. Because years have passed and both mother and child have changed, the two can come astonishingly close without recognizing one another. The young man forms a secret relationship with his master's daughter. When their intimacy is discovered, he is imprisoned. A new catastrophe seems to repeat the family's first loss: someone powerful can suddenly decide the fate of a person who has few means of defense.

But politics turns again. News arrives that the rulers whose victory had ruined Beritola's family have lost their grip, and that her husband may be restored. The prisoner reveals the identity he has been concealing. Circumstances that looked like shame and disorder become signs by which separated members of the household can recognize each other. The older son is reunited with his mother and receives permission to marry the young woman he loves. The younger son, found after years of hardship, is restored to them. Arrighetto returns to honor and the family is brought together.

The structure is deliberately elaborate. Beritola's apparent loss is not one accident but a chain of divisions: from husband, children, position, and even ordinary human company. Recovery likewise needs several kinds of reversal. A change in the government matters, but so do private acts of hospitality, confession of a name, and the recognition of kinship. Fortune has made the family nearly invisible to itself and then brought the pieces together again.

Emilia's tale risks making political upheaval look like a harmless device for restoring love. It is worth resisting that simplification. Beritola has suffered years that cannot be undone by a final reunion. The deer she nurses are not merely a decorative emblem; they mark how radically her human relationships have been broken. The ending gives her back her sons, but the power of the story depends on feeling what it meant to lose them. In a collection framed by the plague, this is one of the tales most concerned with what happens when family itself becomes the missing world a person struggles to recover.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 7: Alatiel’s Long Voyage

Alatiel is the daughter of a powerful ruler whom the story calls the Soldan of Babylon. Her father sends her by sea to marry a king in the western Mediterranean. The marriage has been planned as a public alliance. But ships do not obey court plans. A storm breaks the voyage, and Alatiel is cast into a succession of foreign households. She travels across regions where she cannot easily communicate and where the men who take charge of her often treat her beauty as something to possess.

The ship runs onto the shore of Majorca. The sailors who try to escape by boat drown; Alatiel and some attendants remain aboard and survive. A nobleman named Pericone takes her in. They cannot speak one another's language, and her refusal of his advances is clear even without common words. Pericone serves wine to a woman unused to drinking it and takes advantage of her intoxication. His brother Marato then kills him and carries Alatiel onto a ship. Two Genoese captains murder Marato by throwing him overboard and quarrel violently over her themselves. One dies and the other is badly wounded. A succession of men who claim to protect her prove unable, or unwilling, to distinguish care from possession.

The survivors reach the eastern Mediterranean. Alatiel is taken into the household of a prince in the Morea, then becomes the object of the Duke of Athens's desire. The duke murders the prince and abducts her. When war threatens and the duke must defend himself, Constantine, a relative of the duke's wife, visits Alatiel, becomes consumed by the same passion, and secretly carries her away by sea. She reaches Chios and later Cyprus. Political rivalries and household intrigues repeatedly change who controls her movements. The names and ports matter less than the accumulating pattern: a royal woman has become the prize in a series of private betrayals, and the violence does not stop because a new captor claims to love her.

At Cyprus, Antigono, a man who once served her father, sees her and recognizes her. For the first time in years Alatiel meets someone who knows her earlier identity without having obtained it through possession. She entrusts him with the history she has concealed from strangers. He arranges her return, but he also teaches her the account she must give the royal households on the journey home. Boccaccio's tale moves with the energy of romance and adventure, but that movement is not the same thing as freedom for the woman at its center.

Four years pass before a means of returning to her father's court emerges. The woman who left home as a royal bride has lived through a history that an official marriage negotiation would never willingly acknowledge. She faces a further dilemma. The arrangements that govern her father's household assume a sexual history she no longer has. A trusted adviser helps her produce a version of events that can be accepted. She claims to have remained untouched through her travels. The tale's final reversal is therefore not simply a safe arrival: it is the successful substitution of a persuasive account for an experience that would expose her to rejection.

Her father believes the explanation and renews the old marriage plan. Alatiel goes to the intended husband, with the public appearance of the bride originally promised. The narrator presents the outcome as fortunate. The ironic distance between what the audience knows and what the father believes is the source of much of the tale's humor. But the humor is built upon lost autonomy, coercion, sexual double standards, and the capacity of public reputation to erase a complicated private life.

Panfilo's story is one of the longest and most uncomfortable examples of Fortune's reach. Its sheer geographical movement can make Alatiel seem like a precious object shipped from one owner to another. In the world of dynastic marriage she is already the subject of an agreement made before the wreck. The men she meets often intensify that condition rather than free her from it. To call every encounter a romantic adventure would conceal the injury present in the plot; to say the heroine possesses no resourcefulness would conceal her struggle to survive.

The ending matters because a story saves her a second time. The first rescue takes place through travel and shelter; the second through the account of herself she presents to her father. The listener, knowing far more than the king, must decide what kind of happy ending this is. It is safety within a social order that has not changed its demands. Boccaccio allows the company to laugh, but a reader can also hear the cost of making a woman acceptable to a world whose categories leave little room for what she has endured.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 8: The Count of Antwerp in Exile

A trusted nobleman serves the royal household in France. In the tale he is the Count of Antwerp, a man whose office and reputation depend on the belief that he can be entrusted with matters of great importance. The wife of the king's heir desires him. When he refuses her advances, she turns disappointment into accusation. She claims that the count behaved improperly toward her. Because her place in the household gives her words immediate force, his defense is not enough to protect him.

The count must flee. He takes his children away and journeys to England, where he cannot keep them all beside him. He arranges care for them in different places and assumes a lower station. Eventually he finds service in Ireland. The man who once moved among rulers now lives by disguising his rank and working under another name. He can still exercise judgment, loyalty, and skill, but he has lost the public recognition that formerly gave those qualities their reward.

Years pass. His children grow older, separated from the story of their own origins. By a chain of encounters and changes of fortune, they find security and promising futures. Their father returns to England without revealing himself, observing how much has happened while he was absent. His long exile has made him a stranger to his former life. At the same time, the woman's false allegation has never stopped being the cause of his disgrace. The accusation has endured longer than the desire that produced it.

At last the queen who made the charge becomes seriously ill. Confronting death, she confesses that she lied. The truth reaches the king, and measures are taken to find and restore the injured man. The count's loyalty and innocence are publicly acknowledged, his standing returns, and the separated family is reunited. The outcome is not achieved by a brilliant escape from one single danger. It takes many years and several lives moving through different countries before the false story loses its power.

Elissa's plot is built on a sharp injustice: the word of someone close to the throne can destroy the life of a person who has committed no offense. It borrows an old narrative pattern of rejected desire turned into false accusation. That pattern should not be confused with a general truth about women's testimony. In this particular fiction, a powerful accuser abuses a position inside the ruling family. The reader has been shown her motives, so the reader can recognize the gap between what the court believes and what happened.

The children and the father's disguises extend the same question into a second generation. Who are people when titles, names, and histories have been stripped away? What makes recognition possible again? Fortune places the count among strangers, but character is the thread by which the tale guides him back. The long-awaited confession repairs his public reputation. It cannot give him back the time that accusation and exile consumed. The happy ending names a restoration; the story has made us feel how precarious the conditions of that restoration were.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 9: Zinevra and the Merchant’s Wager

Merchants gathered away from home begin arguing about their wives. Bernabò of Genoa insists that his wife, Zinevra, is faithful and capable. Another man, Ambrogiuolo, claims that no woman can be trusted to resist temptation. What begins as idle male conversation becomes a wager. Bernabò stakes money on the proposition that Ambrogiuolo cannot seduce his wife. The men bargain over her reputation as if she were a costly object to be tested and owned, though no one has asked her to take part.

Ambrogiuolo goes to Genoa and discovers that Zinevra will not yield to his approaches. He needs evidence convincing enough to make Bernabò believe that he has succeeded. Through a woman's assistance and a concealed chest, he gets into Zinevra's private chamber while she sleeps. He observes identifying details of her body and takes small objects that belong to her. Returning to the merchants, he describes what he has seen and produces the objects. Bernabò treats this as proof of adultery, pays the wager, and orders a servant to kill his wife.

Zinevra persuades the servant to spare her. She changes her clothes and identity, takes a male name, and enters service in places far from Genoa. Through ability and steadiness she rises into the household of the Soldan in Alexandria. Her life under another name is not merely a disguise to escape pursuit. It is evidence that the capacities her husband discounted can make her valuable in a demanding public world. She travels, manages responsibilities, and learns how to speak with people who have no idea what she once endured.

Chance brings Ambrogiuolo within reach. Recognizing signs of the stolen objects, Zinevra questions him carefully and reconstructs his fraud. She arranges to bring both the deceiver and Bernabò before the Soldan. In a court where she has earned trust, the story of the false evidence can at last be tested by people who are willing to hear her. Ambrogiuolo's trick is exposed. He is punished, Bernabò admits his wrong, and Zinevra reveals her identity. The husband and wife are reunited and return home wealthy.

Filomena gives us a heroine who does much more than wait for Fortune to lift her. She survives a murder order, adapts to another social position, acquires competence that others recognize, and produces evidence stronger than the original fraudulent display. But the return to Bernabò is a difficult ending for a modern reader to accept without question. A man who treated his wife's life as collateral in a gambling conversation and ordered her death has been allowed to reclaim his marriage after repentance. The story's own audience may welcome the restoration; we can still ask what it costs.

This tale also resembles the first day's stories about persuasive testimony, but the stakes are harsher. A description of a sleeping woman's body is persuasive because it is private, yet privacy can be violated without consent. The appearance of knowledge is not the same as the truth of the accusation. Bernabò fails because he confuses evidence of access with evidence of willingness. Zinevra lives because she refuses the identity assigned to her by that error and ultimately constructs a public occasion in which the real chain of events can be spoken aloud.

## The Decameron — Day Two, Tale 10: Bartolomea and the Pirate

Dioneo's concluding story returns to marriage, but with a sharper and more openly bawdy voice than the other narrators have used. Ricciardo di Chinzica is a prosperous older judge who marries a much younger woman, Bartolomea. He has made a socially advantageous match. He also finds reasons to avoid intimacy with his wife. A great many calendar days, he tells her, are reserved for religious observance or some other prohibition. His legal learning supplies restrictions when she seeks affection.

During a trip along the coast, Bartolomea is seized by a pirate, Paganino da Monaco. The abduction is an act of force, even though the tale proceeds to shape their subsequent relationship as one of mutual sexual pleasure. Ricciardo discovers where she has been taken and travels to seek her return. Paganino receives him and agrees to restore Bartolomea if she wishes to go. The judge may have expected his position as husband, and perhaps his status as an educated and wealthy man, to make her decision automatic.

Bartolomea refuses. She describes a marriage in which her desires were routinely postponed behind a calendar of exemptions, and contrasts it with the attention she receives in Paganino's household. She sees no reason to return to a husband who made a solemn legal bond but neglected its emotional and physical substance. Ricciardo can argue, bargain, and invoke the language of duty, but he cannot persuade her to leave. He goes home alone, dies not long afterward, and Bartolomea eventually marries Paganino.

The final tale of the day is called fortunate from the woman's point of view, at least as its narrator chooses to present it. Yet the plot begins with a kidnapping. That fact should not vanish merely because later scenes give Bartolomea a choice and pleasure. The story's bawdy humor depends on a medieval literary convention that makes sexual satisfaction a way of judging a marriage. Its comic justice falls on the husband who has taken the benefits of marriage without attending to his wife's needs. It does not offer a careful account of the power relations through which the pirate acquired his partner.

Dioneo is deliberately testing the boundaries of the company's assigned theme. The other narrators have given us shipwrecks, exile, threatened death, lost children, and identity concealed or recovered. Here the storyteller makes the unhappy turn appear as a rescue from a lawful marriage. What counts as good fortune depends on whose life is being described. Ricciardo believes he is the injured party. Bartolomea describes herself as the one who had been deprived. Paganino profits from violence which the narrator's ending does not judge with equal severity.

The tale closes Day Two with a provocation rather than a settled moral. The book's orderly company has granted Dioneo the right to speak from an angle that makes their other stories newly arguable. One man's restoration may be another woman's captivity; a judge's legal claim may coexist with his failure of care; a satisfying comic ending may rest upon acts that remain troubling. The question of Fortune has grown complicated. It can rescue the endangered, but its gifts are not distributed according to virtue, and storytellers do not agree on how to decide who has been lucky.
