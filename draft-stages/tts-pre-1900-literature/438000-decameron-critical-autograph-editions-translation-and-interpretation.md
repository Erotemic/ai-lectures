# What Does It Mean to Read Boccaccio's Words?

Most modern readers encounter the *Decameron* through a printed book or a screen. The experience is so ordinary that we may forget how long the work lived before either existed. Boccaccio composed it in the years after the plague of 1348, when books circulated as handwritten copies. A scribe could introduce a new reading or omit a passage. A reader might encounter the tales through a manuscript with a particular arrangement of initials, margins, and headings. The object was part of how the writing reached its audience.

In this case scholars possess a particularly important witness: Berlin Staatsbibliothek manuscript Hamilton 90, dated to approximately 1370. It is recognized as written by Boccaccio himself, in the later period of his life. The manuscript contains corrections and alternative readings in his hand and small illustrations. It also has losses and later interventions. An autograph is not automatically a flawless transcript; indeed the mistakes and later changes once helped obscure the fact that the book was in his own handwriting. These are reasons to study it closely, not reasons to ignore it.

The surviving copy was made well after the work's first composition. That distinction matters. We should not imagine that every reader in the early 1350s held the exact text of Hamilton 90, or that every later manuscript preserves one stable version. A critical editor compares witnesses, evaluates variants, and decides how best to present the work. The renowned editor Vittore Branca used the evidence of Boccaccio's autograph in the study and editing of the *Decameron*. Brown University's text project identifies its online Italian base as Branca's 1992 Einaudi edition.

A reader listening in English has another layer between the author and the ear. J. M. Rigg's translation of 1903 is freely accessible and deliberately close to the Italian in many respects. Brown's project describes it as literal but dated in style. Literalness is not neutrality. An English sentence must choose a register, idiom, and rhythm; it cannot reproduce every implication of a fourteenth-century Tuscan phrase. A comic double meaning may require explanation even when the basic plot has been conveyed accurately.

Proper names themselves require care. Different English versions may spell a character's name differently, change the form of a title, or choose a familiar modern equivalent. Translations can also vary in how explicitly they render erotic jokes or accusations. If a critical interpretation depends on the exact force of one word, reading the Italian and comparing editions is better than arguing from a single convenient English adjective. A retelling, however faithful to the sequence, is not a replacement for the original wording.

The title's alternative designation, Prince Galeotto, deserves attention as well. Galeotto belongs to the wider tradition of romance and serves as a mediator of desire in the literary imagination. An audience knowing Dante's treatment of Paolo and Francesca may hear a significant echo in a book addressed to those suffering from love. The title therefore does more than count ten days. It signals that storytelling itself can bring people into emotional relationships, with pleasure and risk both present.

The book's rubrics, its miniature statements of what happens in each tale, also belong to the authored design. Cambridge scholarship points to their presence in the autograph and to the way they present the book as a deliberately ordered literary work. A modern editor who treats the headings as dispensable labels removes a way Boccaccio taught readers to approach stories whose outcomes were often known in advance. The exact division of paragraphs, spelling, and punctuation may belong to a later editorial convention, while the structural fact of the rubrics is much older.

A responsible listening edition can distinguish three kinds of work. There is the primary Italian text, for which historical and critical editions make the best claims to textual fidelity. There is a translation, giving access to that text while introducing choices. And there is a lecture or adaptation, which retells and analyzes selected material in a new voice. Each has its use, but they do not have identical authority. A claim about what the original says should cite the original or a reliable textual witness; a claim about what an interpreter thinks should be identified as interpretation.

The manuscript history gives a final reason to resist the fantasy of a single transparent story. Boccaccio was a writer, reader, and scribe who revised his work and shaped its physical presentation. Later readers and printers shaped its passage through new media. When we listen to the *Decameron* today, the sound is distant from a handwritten page of around 1370, yet the route connecting them is traceable. Textual scholarship does not diminish literary pleasure. It explains how the words and their meanings survived long enough for us to hear them.
