# The Decameron — Abraham in Rome

Neifile tells of two friends in Paris. Giannotto is a Christian merchant who wants his friend Abraham, a Jew, to join his religion. He presses him with repeated arguments and encouragement. Abraham, unwilling to accept a new faith merely on another man's recommendation, announces that he will travel to Rome and look at the people responsible for governing the Christian church. He will judge the religion by what he sees at its center.

Giannotto is alarmed. He knows stories about the greed, vanity, sexual misconduct, and bargaining that flourish around the papal court. The journey, he fears, will be disastrous for his purpose. If Abraham wants an example of holy Christian life, Rome is not where Giannotto would send him. But Abraham is firm. He leaves, sees the behavior of the clergy for himself, and returns. Giannotto asks how the visit went, expecting to hear that his friend has rejected the whole proposal. Abraham's report is devastating. The men whose office should demand exemplary conduct seem, in his telling, occupied with worldly appetites and mutual corruption.

Then comes the reversal. Abraham says he wishes to become a Christian. Giannotto can scarcely believe it. Abraham explains that a religion with leaders so harmful to its own supposed values should have collapsed long ago if it were maintained by human management alone. Its survival, despite the behavior of those charged with sustaining it, persuades him that it must have divine support. Giannotto's worst evidence has become, by Abraham's reasoning, the strongest possible argument for conversion.

It is a feat of comic logic, but we should hear both its sharpness and its limits. The tale is not Boccaccio's proof that religions are equally valid, nor an independent account of what medieval Jewish travelers thought of Rome. It is a Christian conversion story that places words in the mouth of an imagined Jewish merchant. Within that framework, the Jewish character is observant, intellectually serious, and able to draw a surprising conclusion. The clergy, not Abraham, bear the main force of the satire. The story's claim about divine preservation belongs to its speaker and narrative design; a listener does not have to accept it as a conclusion of history.

Neifile's story also deepens the question raised by Panfilo. In the first tale, a false confession made a false saint, and the sincerity of worshippers was left unresolved. Here, defective churchmen unexpectedly become witnesses to the strength of the religion they fail to practice. The author uses the difference between institutional claims and institutional conduct as comic material. Giannotto gets the result he desired, but not through the arguments he expected to succeed. The friend who inspected the center of authority has judged it corrupt and then chosen to join it. The paradox is the story, and its discomfort is part of the pleasure and the argument.
