# Journey to the West: The seven sisters and the web of silk

## A simple errand

The king and queen of Zhuzi had followed the pilgrims out with a farewell procession. For the moment there were no soldiers chasing them, no bells of fire and smoke, and no demand for an audience with a frightened ruler. The road opened into country where the seasons seemed to have turned while the travelers were passing from adventure to adventure. The winter chill gave way to spring. Green growth brightened the hills, and the pilgrims saw houses scattered near a little stone bridge.

Xuanzang stopped the horse. He wanted to go and ask for a meal himself. This was a small request, almost comic in the mouth of a man whose disciples had just carried him across half the world. Wukong offered to go. Bajie insisted that the youngest and strongest members of a household ought to do the work. Xuanzang answered that he was not asking them to climb a mountain or search an unfamiliar wilderness. A house stood plainly in sight. Surely he could knock on its door and request a bowl of vegetarian food.

Sha Wujing ended the argument. When their master had made up his mind about something harmless, arguing only made him more obstinate. Let him go, said Sha, and they could stay with the horse and baggage until he returned.

Xuanzang changed his outer robe and took his begging bowl. He crossed toward the bridge. At first he saw four young women sitting by the house, embroidering patterns of birds and flowers. They looked like daughters or sisters working together on an ordinary afternoon. He hesitated. There seemed to be no older householder, no man who might receive a monk in the customary way. He waited beneath a tree, trying to decide whether to call out.

The longer he waited, the more he imagined Wukong mocking his unsuccessful errand. At last he went forward. In another part of the courtyard he saw three more women playing a ball game, passing and kicking the ball with the skill of people who had played together for years. Their sleeves fluttered as they turned. No one seemed to be hiding a weapon or guarding a prison. There were seven women in all, and they looked entirely at ease.

He called from the path and asked whether any food might be spared for a traveling monk. They put down their needlework and ball, greeted him politely, and invited him inside. He was relieved. Perhaps, he thought, the road was growing kinder as the western land drew nearer.

## The door that led into rock

The house changed character as soon as they passed the entrance. Beyond the inviting courtyard rose cold stone walls and a dark opening among the rocks. Stone tables and benches furnished a chamber much more like a cave than a farmhouse. Xuanzang felt the air grow colder. He would have been glad to make an excuse and leave, but the women were already arranging seats around him.

They asked what project had brought him there. Was he collecting money for a monastery, a bridge, a tower, printed scriptures? Where was his list of donors? He explained that he wanted no money. He was on the long journey from the Tang empire to obtain Buddhist texts; for that afternoon he wanted a little plain food. The women exclaimed that a visitor from so far away ought to be treated with special generosity. They called for a meal.

Then a different expression passed among them. They knew what the monk had not told them, or what his name was enough to tell. A holy pilgrim of extraordinary merit was coming west. To demons who hoped to prolong their own lives, his flesh was said to be more valuable than any medicine. Here he was, walking into their cave of his own accord. An invitation to lunch had supplied what years of hunting might not have won.

Xuanzang stood and attempted to leave. The women blocked the door. They threw him to the floor, bound him with several ropes, and hung him from a beam with his body awkwardly suspended. He could not stand, sit, or rest his limbs. Tears rose in his eyes. He had wanted to prove that a master could obtain a meal without dispatching disciples. Instead he had supplied his captors with a victim, and his disciples with a fresh reason not to let him go off alone.

The women loosened their clothes. Xuanzang feared they were preparing some new humiliation or an immediate killing. Instead, each revealed a strand of silk emerging from her body. The threads thickened and spread outward until a web covered the approach. Within moments the inviting house became an enclosed mass of shining lines, sticky and layered like the fabric of a thousand looms. The seven sisters departed, leaving the monk hung up in the cave.

## The mountain's old witness

Back on the road, Wukong had been making his own entertainment among the branches. He noticed that the house ahead had turned into a field of white brightness. Sha and Bajie could see it too. A familiar pattern had returned: a simple errand had lasted far too long, and an ordinary household had become a demon's dwelling.

Wukong ran to the bridge and examined the web. A blow of the iron staff would break a wooden gate or shatter stone. Would it help against soft threads that could bend, cling, and wrap around the weapon? He touched them. The substance yielded but refused to separate neatly. A person who rushed straight in could find himself entangled while the captors took their time killing him.

He summoned the local earth god. The god was reluctant to appear. At home his wife asked why he was turning and trembling. A certain Great Sage had called him, he explained, and the Great Sage was known to strike before listening. His wife suggested that an old man ought to be spared. Her husband was less certain. He went all the same, bowed low, and was relieved to find Wukong more interested in questions than in assault.

The land was Spider-Silk Ridge, the god said, and the cave beneath it was Spider-Silk Cave. Seven female demons lived there. The god did not know the limits of their magic, but he knew they had taken over a hot spring nearby. It had once been the bathing place of seven heavenly maidens, who had apparently preferred to abandon it rather than quarrel with its new occupants. The sisters visited the spring regularly. Their usual bathing hour was drawing near.

Wukong dismissed the frightened god and became a fly. He waited near the cave. The threads slowly withdrew, drawn back into the hidden dwelling. Then the seven sisters emerged, laughing together, arm in arm, and went toward the hot spring. Wukong settled upon one of their hairstyles and listened.

One proposed that after their bath they should return and steam the fat monk. The others approved. Wukong, who could not resist criticizing a bad plan even in an enemy, wondered why steaming was preferable to boiling if the object was to save fuel. But he did not reveal himself. He followed to the bathing place to discover what the seven intended.

## At the Cleansing Spring

The spring lay in a walled enclosure filled by naturally hot water. Its channels flowed into the land beyond, warming the surrounding ground. The tale gives it a mythic ancestry: the many ancient suns, the legendary archer who brought all but one down, and springs that preserve something of the fallen suns' heat. Whatever its origin, the water was clear and warm. The women set their garments on racks and entered the pool.

Wukong perched nearby in his insect form. He considered attacking them as they bathed. One sweep of the staff through the water could injure all seven. He rejected that plan. Killing naked women at their bath, even women who had seized his master, would diminish his own standing. Instead he became a great bird, seized all seven sets of clothes from the racks, and flew away with them.

He returned to Bajie and Sha carrying the stolen garments. Bajie examined the bundle with delight and immediately wanted to know how his brother had obtained so many clothes without doing battle. Wukong explained that the women were still in the pool and were too embarrassed to come out. The master hung in their cave. They should free him while their captors remained stranded.

Bajie disliked the plan. The sisters might possess other clothes. Once dressed, they could pursue the pilgrims, or wait to take revenge on the return journey. Better to kill them now. Wukong refused. He had already decided not to strike them in the water. If Bajie wanted the deed, he would have to undertake it himself.

Bajie went to the spring. The women cried out when he came through the gate. They had been robbed of their clothing and now a strange man stood looking at them. They demanded that he leave. Bajie addressed them with mocking politeness, removed his own robe, and jumped into the pool in spite of their protests. When they came toward him he turned into a slippery catfish and swam between them, eluding their attempts to catch him and making them more frantic.

This is one of the pilgrimage's episodes in which the humor is inseparable from a violation. The spider sisters have tried to turn a guest into a meal, but Bajie's refusal to respect the privacy of their bath is no less deliberate. He makes a game of their fear. The text's later account from the sisters' point of view will remember this encounter as a grave injury, not as a harmless joke.

At last Bajie came out, took up his rake, and identified himself as the former Heavenly Marshal, now a disciple of the Tang monk. The women knelt in the water and begged to be spared. They offered money for the journey and promised to release their prisoner. Bajie would have none of it. He had come to destroy the threat, and the seven understood that his rake was not a ceremonial ornament.

They released more silk from their bodies. The threads filled the pool and the courtyard, tying and confusing Bajie. When he struggled free, the sisters escaped toward their cave, calling for help. Their promised meal had become a siege, but it was the disciples who had chosen to carry the fight from the cave to the bath.

## Seven kinds of children

At Spider-Silk Cave the women called upon a group of young demons whom they treated as sons. They could not overcome Wukong or Bajie by physical force, so the smaller creatures attacked in their own form: biting, stinging, and swarming. Bees, horseflies, and other winged insects rose in thick clouds. The heads and faces of the disciples were quickly covered. Bajie protested that the western road was growing absurd. First there were demons large enough to swallow armies; now even insects were determined to stop them.

Wukong had no intention of being defeated by a swarm. He plucked hairs from his body, breathed upon them, and sent them into the air as several kinds of hawks and hunting birds. The birds descended upon the insects. Each strike cleared another patch of sky. Soon the buzzing had ceased, and the ground was littered with the bodies of the little demons. The seven sisters had escaped, but the force they summoned could not protect their dwelling.

Wukong, Bajie, and Sha pushed through the remnants of the silk and entered the cave. Xuanzang was still suspended from the beam, shaking with pain and indignation. Bajie suggested that their master must have come here for the pleasure of hanging about, since the rest of them had spent the day suffering to rescue him. Sha told him to leave the joke until after the ropes were cut.

They brought the monk down, steadied him, and searched the chambers. The seven women were gone. Wukong found no immediate pursuit. The cave itself, however, remained. Bajie proposed smashing it with his rake so that the sisters could not return to their old home. Wukong offered a faster solution: gather dry branches and burn the place. The disciples set fire to the dwelling until the rooms that had received Xuanzang so courteously were consumed.

Xuanzang was once more placed on the white horse. The party moved west, believing the danger behind them. But the sisters were neither dead nor reconciled. They had lost their cave, their smaller defenders, their garments, and the assurance that a familiar place was safe. They had an elder companion elsewhere who might be willing to take revenge. The next religious house on the road would receive the pilgrims with tea and all the formalities of hospitality. It would not be a place of peace.
