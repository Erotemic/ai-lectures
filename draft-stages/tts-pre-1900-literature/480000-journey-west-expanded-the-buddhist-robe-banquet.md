# The Banquet for a Buddhist Robe

The flight from the burned monastery to Black Wind Mountain took Wukong very little time. It was the country below that demanded attention. The mountain was beautiful in the new spring: gullies ran between wooded heights, cliffs rose over flowering slopes, and birds moved through quiet groves. It did not look like a hideout designed only for evil. A person could have spent a happy day walking among its trees.

Wukong was not in the mood for scenery, but a sound from below made him stop. Three figures were sitting together on a grassy slope, talking as though they had arranged a peaceful afternoon of learning. One was a dark-complexioned man, one a Daoist dressed as a religious adept, and the third a scholar in white. Their conversation concerned furnaces and elixirs, the practices by which seekers of long life tried to refine the body and escape ordinary death.

Wukong hid among the rocks to listen. He had once devoted himself to precisely such arts. He knew enough of them to judge a boast, and he was curious to hear what the strangers would say.

Soon the dark man announced that the anniversary of his birth was approaching. Would his two friends come to celebrate? The scholar in white said he would, as always. Then the host mentioned the particular pleasure he had prepared. The previous night he had acquired a rare embroidered Buddhist robe, a beautiful article worthy of a banquet. They could hold a celebration devoted to the garment itself, a festival of the Buddhist robe.

The phrase was enough for Wukong. The robe had belonged to Xuanzang. The night of its disappearance was also the night of the monastery fire. The black-clad speaker had supplied the connection himself.

The monkey sprang from concealment with his staff raised and demanded the stolen robe. The gathering dissolved in an instant. The black figure escaped as a gust of wind. The Daoist fled into the sky. The scholar in white was not quick enough. Wukong struck him down and discovered that the body was that of a white-flowered snake spirit. The monkey broke the body and left it behind, then hurried after the black figure into the mountain.

He came to a cave entrance with stone doors. Over them was a sign naming Black Wind Mountain and Black Wind Cave. Wukong knocked with his staff. A small attendant opened the door and asked what visitor presumed to strike an immortal's dwelling so violently.

Wukong wanted none of the ceremony. Tell your Black King, he said, that the owner of the robe has come. If the robe is returned promptly, the cave may be left standing. If not, its master will discover what happens to stone when the Great Sage is angry.

The attendant ran inside. The Black King, who had scarcely recovered from his interrupted conversation on the slope, put on armor, took up a black-tasseled spear, and stepped through the entrance. Wukong laughed at his imposing appearance and began with insults rather than introductions. The Black King returned them. Neither looked inclined to surrender the treasure.

When Wukong accused him directly, the creature made a revealing answer. He had indeed taken the robe. He had seen the fire, come to the monastery, and found a guest sitting upon a roof stirring the flames. The robe had been left where he could take it. What right did this roof-top arsonist have to demand it now?

Wukong explained his own claim, including the fact that he served the scripture-seeking monk from the Tang court. He then offered an elaborate account of his past exploits. He had found the path to immortality, taken a staff from the Dragon Kings, fought heavenly armies, survived execution, endured a furnace, and escaped it. The story ended with the Buddha pressing him beneath a mountain and Xuanzang releasing him after five centuries.

The speech was intended to terrify the opponent. Instead, the Black King recognized the famous rebel by the title Wukong most hated: the lowly keeper of Heaven's horses. The creature asked, with evident pleasure, whether this was that very stable officer.

Wukong's anger flared. The insult reached back through all the pride that had made the monkey rebel. He struck at once. The bear-king met the staff with his spear, and the two fought through the mountain air and across the rocks outside the cave.

Neither gained a decisive advantage. The sun climbed toward noon. At last the Black King proposed a pause so he could eat. Wukong was offended all over again. He had spent five hundred years under a mountain without a proper meal, he declared. Was this creature already asking to stop for lunch?

The black figure feinted with the spear, swept back into the cave, and closed the stone door. The attendants withdrew with him. He had no reason to risk his life for Wukong's convenience. Inside, he could eat, write invitations, and arrange the banquet he had promised his friends.

Wukong beat against the closed entrance, but the cave would not open. He could fight the Black King if the creature came out; he could not easily extract the robe from a sealed house whose owner had no intention of cooperating. For the first time on this errand, the problem demanded something other than another stroke of the staff.

He flew back to the monastery to tell Xuanzang that the thief was found. The monks, now sheltering in the few surviving rooms, greeted him with frightened reverence. Their master was alive, the robe's location was known, and that was enough to make them offer food and thanks.

Xuanzang asked the only question that mattered to him. Had Wukong brought the robe?

Not yet, the monkey said. But the robber had admitted taking it, and the monastery could no longer be blamed for hiding it. The Black King was strong, and his cave was locked. Wukong would return with another plan.

The selected text calls the creature a black man before revealing its bear identity; treat racial imagery and insults as part of the narrator and characters, not ethnographic description.
