# Journey to the West: Two monkeys before the Buddha

## A mountain with two kings

Sha Wujing had seen the impossible with his own eyes. He had gone to the southern sea to accuse Sun Wukong of striking Xuanzang and stealing the pilgrimage bundles, only to find Wukong standing beside Guanyin. The bodhisattva said the monkey had been with her for four days. And when Sha told him that another Wukong was enthroned on Flower-Fruit Mountain, reading the emperor's travel document and preparing an imitation pilgrimage, the real Wukong was as furious as Sha had expected him to be.

Yet the two disciples did not fly straight to the mountain as comrades who trusted one another. Wukong's somersault cloud could have left Sha far behind. Sha insisted that they travel together. If his elder brother flew ahead, who could say what he might arrange before the witness arrived? Wukong bristled, but agreed. Their company had already been torn apart by mistrust. Speed alone would not mend it.

They came to the eastern sea, found the familiar mountains rising beyond it, and looked down toward Water-Curtain Cave. There, amid drinking and revelry, sat a second monkey king. He wore the same golden headband, the same tiger-skin garment, the same boots. His eyes had Wukong's gold; his face had Wukong's sharp angles; his staff could have been the iron staff taken from the Dragon King's treasury.

The sight undid every attempt at restraint. Wukong leapt down, raised his weapon, and demanded to know what spirit dared steal his face, his mountain, and the loyalty of his children. The figure sprang from the stone seat and met him stroke for stroke. Two iron staffs crashed in the air. The mountain monkeys scattered. Each combatant accused the other, and each claimed to be the disciple charged with protecting the Tang monk.

Sha could not help. He lifted his demon-subduing staff, saw an opening, and pulled it back. One wrong blow might strike his real brother. He turned his anger upon the cave instead, overturning stools and vessels, scattering the false king's attendants, and searching for the stolen bundles. He could not find them. The waterfall that concealed the cavern and its hidden ways confused even one who had been there before.

Above the cave the fight rose higher. Both monkeys shouted to Sha that he should return to Xuanzang, explain the situation, and leave them to ask Guanyin to decide which was which. They spoke with the same voice at the same instant. Sha went westward, carrying no answer, while the two kings raced toward the southern sea.

## No difference under the headband

Guanyin had anticipated a crisis; she had not expected to see two apparently identical Wukongs pulling at one another before her sanctuary. They knelt, rose, accused, and repeated one another's story. Each reported his dismissal over the killing of human robbers, the attack upon Xuanzang, and the counterfeit pilgrimage. Each insisted that the other was a demon who had stolen a disciple's honor.

The bodhisattva looked closely. Her attendants gathered around the two figures. In other circumstances Wukong's headband would have settled the question. The real disciple wore a binding fillet, and the tightening spell caused him agony. Surely the impostor would be free of it.

Guanyin placed an attendant beside each monkey and recited the spell quietly. Both clasped their heads. Both rolled upon the ground, crying out for her to stop. When she ceased, they were upright again, still quarreling. The fillet, which had caused so much anguish between master and disciple, could not now identify the disciple at all.

Guanyin sent them to Heaven. The gods had known Wukong long before the journey, when his rebellion had shaken their palaces. Surely someone there would recognize him.

At the Southern Gate the guards heard the shouting before they saw the combatants. The two demanded an audience with the Jade Emperor. They thrust their way into the celestial court, each making the same appeal: he had reformed, had taken religious vows, and now asked the emperor to distinguish him from a creature abusing his name. The emperor ordered the demon-revealing mirror brought out. Li Jing, the Pagoda-Bearing Heavenly King, held it up. In its reflection stood two monkeys, each complete in dress, headband, and face. The court could see no difference. The emperor sent them away.

The two monkeys descended to the road and found Xuanzang under the shelter of the family that had been caring for him. Sha had returned ahead of them and told his master that the original attacker had been a false figure. Xuanzang was ashamed of his earlier certainty. The sight of the two combatants, however, only renewed his helplessness.

Bajie was delighted at first. Here was a battle he could join. But when he shouted to the elder brother, both monkeys answered him. Sha suggested that they seize one apiece and let Xuanzang use the headband spell. This time the master recited it, and this time both monkeys bent double in equal pain. The monk who had been so ready to condemn could no longer tell what he was condemning.

Around them the household stared upward in alarm. They had begun by sheltering a beaten stranger; now flying monks and monkey warriors filled their sky. Bajie boasted that traveling twenty thousand leagues and back was a small day's work for such men. The frightened family had no useful opinion about whose headband was genuine.

## The one who could hear

Still fighting, the two carried their dispute into the realm of the dead. They appeared before the Ten Kings of the Underworld and demanded that the registers settle their identities. The books that listed the living and the dead could not help. Wukong had long ago struck his name from the records; the false monkey was no ordinary dead soul awaiting judgment. Even here their likeness resisted an easy test.

Kshitigarbha, the bodhisattva who presided over suffering beings in the underworld, possessed a creature called Diting, a marvelous listener. Lying with its ear to the ground, it could distinguish beings throughout the world and hear what others could not.

The animal listened. It raised its head and told its master that it knew the creature's name. But it would not speak the name aloud while the combatants stood in the court. If exposed, the impostor could unleash such violence that the underworld might suffer, and the spirits around them were not strong enough to seize it. The listener had knowledge, but knowledge did not make the court safe.

Kshitigarbha understood the hint. He told the pair to go to the Buddha at Thunderclap Monastery. Their case could be decided only where there was power enough to act on the decision. So the two monkeys left the underworld and fought their way westward across the sky.

At Thunderclap Monastery the Buddha was teaching an assembly of bodhisattvas, arhats, monks, nuns, and lay followers. The noise of the two iron staffs rose through the sacred precinct. The guardians attempted to stop the combatants; neither would be kept out. They knelt together before the Buddha and told the same tale once more, from the human robbers to the disputed mountain and the humiliating failure of Heaven's mirror.

The Buddha looked at them and spoke not first of a culprit, but of a divided mind. Where the entire court saw two disciples, he saw a struggle of two wills. Guanyin arrived to report that she, too, had been unable to make the distinction.

Then the Buddha explained that the beings of the world did not fit every simple category the assembled gods had learned. There were five classes of immortals and five kinds of living creatures; beyond them were four extraordinary monkeys. The intelligent stone monkey could know transformations and the ways of heaven and earth. The red-rumped horse monkey had its own powers of perception and escape. The long-armed gibbon could grasp celestial forces and understand good fortune and danger. And there was a fourth: the six-eared macaque, a being able to hear distant speech, grasp secrets, and know what others had said far away.

The one wearing Wukong's face was that six-eared macaque, the Buddha said. He had heard enough of the pilgrimage to counterfeit not only the monkey's appearance but his knowledge, voice, and conduct. For this reason so many witnesses had been fooled.

The false monkey understood that his name had been given and sprang away. The assembly closed in around him. He changed himself into a tiny flying insect, hoping that a body too small to see might escape the ring. The Buddha lifted a golden alms bowl and cast it over the creature. The bowl settled on the ground. There was no fluttering escape.

The assembly gathered around. The Buddha raised the bowl, and beneath it crouched the six-eared macaque in his own form. Wukong needed no second invitation. His iron staff came down on the exposed creature's head. The impostor died there, before anyone could attempt to place him under another vow or make him a pilgrim instead. The Buddha expressed pity. Wukong replied that the thief had beaten his master and taken the pilgrims' property, offenses serious enough to deserve punishment. The violence did not disappear because the truth had been established.

## One company again

The Buddha told Wukong to return to Xuanzang. The monkey knelt and asked for something else first. Xuanzang had dismissed him. If he went back and was rejected again, he would only humiliate himself before another divine court. Let the Buddha remove the golden fillet and release him from this impossible service.

The Buddha refused. He promised that the journey still offered Wukong a place and a reward. Guanyin would escort him back and require Xuanzang to receive him. It was not the freedom Wukong had requested. He went anyway.

At the household on the western road Guanyin explained the deception. The figure that struck Xuanzang and carried off the travel documents had been the six-eared macaque, not the disciple he had sent away. The real Wukong had remained at her sanctuary until Sha brought word. The Buddha had identified the impostor, and Wukong had killed him. Xuanzang bowed and accepted the bodhisattva's instruction that he must keep Wukong with him: the road ahead was still dangerous.

Even before the discussion ended, Bajie returned in a rush of wind with the stolen bundles. He had gone back to Flower-Fruit Mountain after the others and fought his way into the false company. A counterfeit Tang monk and a counterfeit pig disciple had proved to be monkeys once struck. He had searched the cave until he found the packages, and he had inspected them. The travel document and the rest of their belongings were there.

The family that had housed Xuanzang watched the four travelers prepare to go. The distinction between the true and false monkey had been settled; the distinction between a dependable companion and a dangerous one remained more difficult. Xuanzang had been wrong to assume that the attacker was Wukong. He had not invented Wukong's earlier killings, nor the terror the headband could inflict. Wukong had recovered his position, but the Buddha had not lifted the band. Sha and Bajie could see the road opening before them without believing all quarrels would now be easy.

They thanked their hosts, fastened the bundles to the white horse, and set out west. Once more there was only one Wukong in the company, one monkey voice calling ahead from the road. Autumn was approaching, and the next obstacle had been waiting for them since the days of Wukong's first great rebellion in Heaven.
