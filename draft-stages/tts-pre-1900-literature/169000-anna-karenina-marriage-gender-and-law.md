# Anna Karenina — Marriage, Gender, Law, and Social Punishment

The novel begins with an affair committed by a husband and becomes famous for an affair committed by a wife. That structural choice exposes a double standard more effectively than a simple speech about hypocrisy could. Stiva betrays Dolly and continues moving through society with considerable ease. His wife bears the emotional and practical cost, but he remains charming, well connected, and socially useful. Anna's affair, by contrast, threatens her access to her son, her public standing, and the recognized shape of her future. Comparing the two stories reveals that moral language and social enforcement do not fall equally on the participants.

The difference is not that every man is free and every woman powerless. Karenin suffers humiliation; Vronsky's career and social standing are affected; Kitty can make a consequential choice in refusing Levin; Dolly exercises considerable authority within her household. But the legal and social arrangements give these people different options. Anna cannot simply end a marriage and enter another recognized union by declaring her wishes. The rules surrounding divorce and custody involve religious and civil institutions, personal bargaining, and the husband's ability to cooperate or refuse.

Anna's relationship with Seryozha is the sharpest measure of these constraints. She continues loving him, but she does not control whether she can visit or raise him. The birthday visit is painful partly because affection survives separation. Her daughter with Vronsky belongs to a different legal and domestic arrangement. The children are not symbols of abstract purity. They are people whose lives make the consequences of adult desire and institutional power impossible to treat as private matters.

Dolly's point of view complicates easy moral sorting. She understands betrayal from within a marriage and cares for a household that her husband has destabilized. When she visits Anna, she can see the attraction of a luxurious home and passionate attachment, but she also perceives the insecurity beneath them. Dolly does not need to approve of every decision Anna has made to see how harshly society has narrowed her options. In her own life, financial worries and childcare make departure from Stiva far more difficult than a purely romantic view of marriage would suggest.

Kitty's development gives another comparison. She initially judges love through the visibility of courtship and Vronsky's attentions. After humiliation she learns to distinguish public admiration from durable care. Her marriage to Levin still involves jealousy, misunderstanding, sickness, and daily labor. Social approval does not automatically make a relationship ethical or happy. What sustains the marriage is not the absence of problems but the possibility of shared practical responsibility, and even that possibility is supported by property and class privilege.

Anna and Vronsky's conflict has multiple causes. Her suspicion can become cruel, and his impatience can be damaging. Their relationship also takes place under circumstances that make an ordinary quarrel dangerous. He can imagine political life, property management, and a career; she is increasingly restricted to the question of whether he still loves her. The dependency intensifies her fear. A reading that treats her jealousy as a self-contained personal flaw misses the conditions that make love carry the weight of an entire future.

Tolstoy's novel is not a modern legal treatise, and its depiction of nineteenth-century Russian institutions should not be substituted for a complete history of Russian divorce law. Nor is every authorial judgment easy to distinguish from a character's view. The careful critic can nevertheless ask what the narrative shows: who gets to preserve a reputation, who can claim a child, who owns property, who manages work, and who is expected to absorb a scandal silently.

For a practical comparison, return to Stiva's household at the opening and Anna's attempt to visit Seryozha. List the formal and informal powers each adult exercises. Then read Dolly's visit to Anna's estate, where sympathy and criticism coexist. The goal is not to prove that the characters' actions were equally harmless. It is to understand how a society can call itself moral while distributing the consequences of its rules so unequally.
