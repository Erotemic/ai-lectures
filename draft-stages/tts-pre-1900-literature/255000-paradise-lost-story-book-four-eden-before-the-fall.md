# Paradise Lost — Book Four: Eden Before the Fall

Satan reaches the heights of Mount Niphates and sees Earth spread before him. The beauty of the world does not quiet him. Instead it forces an inward reckoning. Alone, with nobody to impress, he remembers his former place in Heaven and acknowledges that pride drove his revolt. He has opportunities for remorse, but cannot imagine surrendering the position he has established among his followers. He fears the humiliation of repentance more than the pain of remaining evil. His defiance begins to look less like liberty than like an imprisonment he must continually renew.

He enters Paradise by leaping its boundary and takes the form of a cormorant to observe the garden from the Tree of Life. Milton fills Eden with flowing water, shade, fruit, animals, and living abundance. This is not a wilderness that human beings will someday tame into usefulness. It is a place already plentiful, entrusted to its inhabitants for enjoyment and care. The garden has paths and work, but labor here is not punishment. The first human pair tends a living environment rather than conquering a hostile one.

Adam and Eve appear. They are unclothed, but nakedness does not trouble them. They possess no reason yet to fear being seen by another person. Milton describes a hierarchy between them, assigning Adam a form of preeminence even in their innocence. Yet he also gives Eve speech, affection, perception, and the capacity for delight. The reader need not accept the hierarchy to understand that the poem is trying to imagine a relationship without betrayal or coercive fear. This couple lives under one prohibition: they must not eat from the Tree of Knowledge of Good and Evil.

Satan listens as the two discuss their happiness and the command that limits it. The restriction gives him a plan. He can present transgression as the achievement of a power unjustly withheld. He feels an occasional stab of sympathy for the creatures he intends to corrupt. They have caused his misery in no way. Their happiness simply reminds him of what he has lost. Rather than abandon the revenge, he turns their innocence into one more reason to proceed.

Eve tells Adam of the first moments of her waking. She had been attracted to her reflection in a pool and then directed toward the man with whom she would share her life. This scene will matter later, when Satan learns to praise her beauty and suggest that she deserves a greater station. The attraction to an image is not yet a sin. It is part of the poem's interest in how a creature learns to recognize both herself and another person.

Night comes. Adam and Eve praise their Creator, rest together, and love one another physically without shame. Milton refuses the claim that sexuality in itself began with the Fall. In his Eden, married affection belongs to goodness, although the poem also distinguishes love ordered by mutual care from appetite that can master judgment.

Angels are guarding Paradise. Uriel has noticed, too late, that the supposed visitor's expression betrayed hatred, and has warned Gabriel. The guardians search for the intruder and discover Satan in the shape of a toad near the sleeping Eve's ear, attempting to stir troubling images in her dreams. An angel touches him with a spear and forces him back into his proper form. Satan exchanges threats with Gabriel. A sign from Heaven interrupts the confrontation, and the intruder withdraws.

No fruit has yet been eaten. Yet the enemy has entered the garden, and Eve's imagination has been touched during sleep. The book closes with temporary safety rather than final victory. Milton gives us time to know what can be lost: work without drudgery, bodily life without shame, companionship not yet ruined by suspicion, and a world experienced as a gift. Against this, Satan offers a vision of enlargement that is already shrinking his own soul.
