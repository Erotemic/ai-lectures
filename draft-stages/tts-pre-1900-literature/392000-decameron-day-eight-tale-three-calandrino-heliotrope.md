# The Decameron — Calandrino and the Invisible Stone

Calandrino is a painter in Florence, easily fascinated by promises of wealth and hidden powers. His acquaintances Bruno and Buffalmacco know this and often amuse themselves at his expense. One day another wit, Maso del Saggio, allows Calandrino to overhear what sounds like a learned discussion of wondrous stones. The talk passes through fanciful lands and marvelous properties until it reaches a prize Calandrino cannot resist: the heliotrope, a stone said to make its possessor invisible.

The supposed source of the stone is conveniently local. It can, Maso says, be found among the pebbles of the Mugnone, a stream outside Florence. Calandrino rushes to tell Bruno and Buffalmacco. They pretend to be excited and make plans for the hunt. They also begin privately planning a much more practical marvel: convincing their companion that he has acquired the power and can no longer be seen.

The three go to the stream, where Calandrino gathers dark stones until his clothing is heavy with them. Bruno and Buffalmacco move a little apart. They speak as though their friend has disappeared and loudly wonder where he has gone. Calandrino hears everything. Because they do not address or look toward him, he reasons that one of his stones must have succeeded. The companions then throw stones at him as they continue pretending to see no one. Rather than recognizing an assault by his friends, he treats the blows as accidental and keeps quiet, fearful that speaking will dissolve his miraculous condition.

He hurries home, delighted at the chance to use invisibility for profit. His wife, Tessa, naturally sees him and asks why he has come carrying such a ridiculous load. To Calandrino this proves that a woman has broken the spell. He turns his anger against her and beats her. Bruno and Buffalmacco arrive and hear his furious explanation. They know precisely why the magic failed: it never existed. But they allow his mistaken account to stand, reinforcing the misogynistic superstition that makes the wife responsible for what his friends have done.

The narrator makes Calandrino ridiculous, but the joke has a dark edge. Three men manufacture a false belief, two of them inflict painful blows, and a woman who did not participate becomes the target of the fool's anger. Calandrino is gullible not because he is simply ignorant of stone lore, but because he urgently wants a power over the people around him. That wish makes him believe the performance of invisibility even as he feels the stones hitting his body.

The sequence inaugurates a group of recurring comic characters. Bruno and Buffalmacco will return in further stories, and Calandrino will again mistake staged evidence for a discovery. Their world is not populated by abstract tricksters. It is a small network of working Florentines with knowledge of one another's habits. We need not assume any encounter happened to historical painters of the same names. The literary characters are complete in this scene: Maso supplies the legend, the friends supply false witnesses, and Calandrino supplies the longing that makes the lie work.
