# Othello — Story: Jealousy and the Handkerchief

Cassio leaves Desdemona after asking her help. When Othello approaches, Iago draws attention to Cassio's departure with a brief remark of apparent surprise. He refuses to state an accusation clearly. Instead he asks what Othello saw, pauses, and makes his reluctance to speak seem like loyalty. Othello must begin supplying the suspicion himself. This is the first mechanism of the trap: a planted question can make the victim believe that the conclusion originated in his own judgment.

Desdemona innocently presses Cassio's case. She believes that restoring a deserving officer would be a kindness to both men. Othello initially receives her requests as a husband rather than an investigator. Then Iago asks whether Cassio knew about the courtship. He has, Othello says. Iago's response makes that history appear newly dangerous. The ensign counsels against jealousy even as he manufactures it, and repeatedly suggests that a husband would be wise to watch a wife who once concealed marriage plans from her father. His performance of caution gives his unsupported hints the appearance of reluctant expertise.

Othello asks for proof. This should be a moment of resistance to the plot. But Iago can control what counts as proof because he has access to the general's confidence and to the household. Desdemona tries to soothe Othello when he complains of pain. A handkerchief, one of Othello's gifts to her and a valued token in their relationship, falls to the ground. Emilia picks it up. She knows that her husband has long asked her to obtain it, but she does not know what he intends to do with it. She gives it to Iago, who dismisses her questions and plans to place it where Cassio will find it.

At first glance, a handkerchief is a small piece of cloth. Onstage it is an extraordinarily mobile piece of evidence. Othello gave it to Desdemona; she carries it; Emilia finds it; Iago takes it; Cassio discovers it in his room; and Bianca will later handle it. Each transfer can occur without the next person understanding its significance. The audience sees the route that the object takes. Othello will be invited to interpret only its later appearance. A thing that is genuine can be made to support a false claim when its history is concealed.

Iago intensifies the deception with another false report. He says he once heard Cassio speak of Desdemona in his sleep and behave as if sharing intimacy with her. This is hearsay offered by the person most interested in the conclusion. Iago also claims to have seen Cassio use the distinctive handkerchief. Othello treats the combination as decisive. The two men kneel and commit themselves to vengeance. Othello elevates Iago to the position Cassio lost. The promotion that Iago wanted is now secured through Othello's misjudgment, and his success makes him even more dangerous.

Desdemona does not know that her efforts for Cassio have been made into an accusation. She cannot produce the handkerchief when Othello demands it, and she does not understand why the missing cloth has become a test of marital loyalty. Othello tells her a story about its history and powers, heightening the sense that loss of the token might signify loss of love. The story may reveal his genuine investment in the object, but it cannot make Desdemona guilty. She continues to speak about Cassio, not knowing that his name has become a trigger.

Cassio, meanwhile, has found the handkerchief in his lodgings and asks Bianca, a woman who cares for him, to copy its embroidery. Bianca is suspicious that it might be another woman's gift. Her reaction is not proof of Desdemona's involvement. It is another person's reasonable but incomplete reading of an unexplained object. The handkerchief has reached the point at which Iago can cause innocent people to produce the gestures and words he needs. The audience understands the mechanism, but the general, who believes he is testing his wife, is now almost entirely inside the false story made for him.
