# The Dragon Prisoner and the Monkey Beneath the Mountain

Guanyin and Hui'an were traveling east when they heard cries above them. A dragon was suspended in the air, bound and awaiting execution. He had the shape and voice of a powerful creature of the waters, yet he could do no more than call to someone passing by. Guanyin stopped and asked why he had been condemned.

He said he was the son of the Dragon King of the Western Sea. A fire had broken out in his father's palace, damaging a precious pearl. His father had complained of his conduct to the heavenly court, and the Jade Emperor had ordered him beaten and sentenced to death. He had already received the blows. Now he waited for the sentence to be completed.

Guanyin did not promise a rescue that she could not grant. She went to Heaven and petitioned the Jade Emperor himself. The scripture seeker, she explained, would have to travel a great distance through difficult country. A human monk would need a strong mount. If the condemned dragon were spared, he could serve as the pilgrim's horse, and by that service turn punishment into merit. The emperor consented. The dragon was released and sent to a deep ravine to wait for the traveler. Later, when the moment arrived, he would take the form of a white horse.

The detail is easy to miss among the bright battles of Wukong's rebellion. The pilgrim's future mount was a prisoner rescued from execution. So were several of his companions, in different ways. The great journey was being staffed out of Heaven's failures, people and creatures whose strength had become a danger without a new purpose.

The travelers went on until a distant mountain glowed with signs of supernatural power. Hui'an recognized it. This was the Mountain of Five Elements. At its summit the Buddha had placed the sacred written formula, and beneath it lay Sun Wukong. Guanyin paused there. She remembered the monkey who had caused the banquet scandal and the war in Heaven. She looked upon the great rock and spoke of the captive whose fame had once shaken the court.

A furious voice rose from below. Who, it demanded, was standing over the prison reciting its occupant's disgrace? Wukong had not lost the ability to hear an insult. He called up at the unseen visitor, and Guanyin descended to face him. The monkey recognized her at once. He greeted her with sudden enthusiasm and asked whether she had come to set him free.

He had been under the mountain for more than five hundred years. His strength remained, but he had no use for it. He could see a little of the world and hear the guardians, yet there was no way to reach the forests of his home or the monkeys who had once shouted his title. He begged Guanyin to speak on his behalf. He wanted to be released.

Guanyin knew better than to free him merely because he asked. This was the monkey who had wrecked a heavenly feast, stolen from the furnaces, and tried to seize the imperial throne. If he returned to his old habits, his freedom would become trouble for every place he passed. She told him that his former conduct had been serious, and that release without amendment would accomplish little.

Wukong answered that he had had time to regret his folly. He would be willing to cultivate himself properly if someone opened a path for him. These were the words Guanyin had been waiting to hear. She explained the mission. Somewhere in the Tang lands a pilgrim would be chosen to travel west and obtain the true scriptures. When that pilgrim reached the mountain, Wukong could become his disciple. He would protect the human monk and accompany him through the trials of the journey. His labor would make atonement possible.

Wukong accepted before she had finished. He wanted to go; he wanted a road, a purpose, and the chance to use his arms and legs. Guanyin asked his name and offered to give him a religious one. Wukong was pleased to explain that he already had a name from his former teacher: Sun Wukong. She was delighted that the name belonged to the same pattern as those of the other future disciples. She need not rename him.

She did not lift the mountain. That was not the arrangement. The pilgrim who would become his master was to come, meet him, and release him. The monkey would then owe his freedom to the relationship he was about to enter. Guanyin promised that the opportunity was on its way, but she could not tell him when. Wukong settled back under the stone, not comfortable, perhaps, but no longer without a reason to endure it.

The two travelers came at last to Chang'an. They did not enter as splendid supernatural visitors. Guanyin and Hui'an assumed the appearances of poor, afflicted monks, their clothes ragged and their bodies marked by disease. A city that admitted ministers in silk might turn aside a pair of shabby wanderers. That was useful to them. They were not there to announce themselves but to watch.

They found shelter at a small shrine of the local earth deity. The deity knew who they were and hurried to welcome them; neighboring protective spirits came as well. Guanyin told them to keep silent. She had come with a commission from the Buddha and needed somewhere to wait while she searched for a person of the right character. The local gods withdrew, leaving the travelers in their humble hiding place.

In Chang'an, other lives were already taking shape. One of them belonged to a young scholar whose hopes lay in a court examination. He could not know that decisions in a western paradise, a river full of bones, a monster's mountain cave, a dragon's prison, and a rock under which a monkey waited would eventually converge upon a child of his family.

The scripture mission had reached the east. The person who would carry it back west had not yet been named.
