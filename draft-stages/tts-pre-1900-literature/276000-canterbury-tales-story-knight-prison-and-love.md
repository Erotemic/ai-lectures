# The Canterbury Tales — The Knight’s Tale, Prison and Love

Theseus, duke of Athens, rides home after a victory, accompanied by his wife Hippolyta and her sister Emily. A group of women in mourning interrupts the celebration. Their husbands have been killed by Creon, ruler of Thebes, and denied proper burial. Theseus turns from homecoming to war, defeats Creon, and arranges honorable funerals. In the ruins his men find two wounded young Theban nobles, Palamon and Arcite. They are cousins, close as brothers, and now prisoners in an Athenian tower. This is the beginning of the Knight's long romance: public power has resolved one injustice by creating the captivity in which another story begins.

Years pass. One May morning Emily walks in a garden below the tower. Palamon sees her through the window and is struck by love before she knows he exists. Arcite looks down and falls in love too. Their old comradeship collapses into argument over who first saw her and who has the better claim. Palamon declares that Arcite has broken the law of brotherly loyalty. Arcite answers that love follows laws of its own. Neither can marry Emily, and neither seems interested in asking what Emily wants. The tower holds them, but their idea of love creates an additional prison.

A friend of Theseus secures Arcite's release under strict condition that he never return to Athens. To everyone outside the tower this must seem good fortune. To Arcite, banishment means losing the chance even to see Emily. Palamon envies his cousin's freedom, believing Arcite can gather military strength and come back. Each man calls the other's condition more fortunate than his own. Chaucer gives Fortune the power to make release feel like punishment and imprisonment feel like a remaining hope.

Arcite returns to Thebes, wastes away in love and changes so greatly that his face and voice become hard to recognize. After a dream of Mercury he travels back to Athens in disguise and enters service near Emily under the name Philostrate. His competence wins Theseus's trust. Palamon meanwhile escapes the tower, with the help of a drugged guard, and hides in a grove before fleeing the city. On a spring morning Arcite rides there to honor the season and gives voice to his anguish. Palamon hears him, recognizes the name and steps out of hiding. The two agree to settle their rivalry by combat. Arcite promises to bring weapons and provisions so they can fight properly the following day.

They meet again and fight so fiercely that both seem to have forgotten what victory would mean. Theseus happens upon them while hunting with Hippolyta and Emily. At first he orders both killed: one has broken his banishment and the other escaped prison. The women plead for mercy. Theseus relents, acknowledging how love can make intelligent people foolish. Yet his remedy is not to free Emily from the contest. Instead he promises a grand tournament a year hence. Each rival will bring a hundred knights; the victor will have Emily as his wife.

The Knight tells this with impressive control of time. Years are crossed in a few lines; a morning in a grove can be extended until every emotion has been exposed. The public ruler who settled a war now proposes to settle private desire by staging another, regulated war. Palamon and Arcite accept his plan eagerly. Emily has been seen, praised, desired and promised, yet has hardly entered the argument as a speaking agent. Before the tournament, she will go to Diana with a prayer different from either man's. And the divine answers will expose the fragile confidence with which humans assume that a tournament can make Fortune obey them.
