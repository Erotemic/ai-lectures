# Anna Karenina — Story: Estates, Jealousy, and Two Households

Life in the countryside might seem far from Anna's exposed position in fashionable society, but Tolstoy makes the two worlds part of one system. Levin and Kitty are establishing a household while Anna and Vronsky have settled at his country estate. Both homes contain affection, work, guests, servants, money, and the small frictions of intimacy. The differences become clearer when we ask who belongs to the household in law, who can make decisions, and who can imagine a future without asking permission from an estranged spouse.

Levin's estate is full of concrete problems. There are fields to supervise, books to read, hunting trips to organize, and relationships with workers that do not conform to his theories. His attempts to improve agriculture remain vulnerable to the opinions and interests of people who do not see him as a philosopher. Kitty's presence, along with visiting relatives and friends, changes the atmosphere. Levin can grow jealous or irritated even in the marriage he so long desired. An encounter with an excessively attentive guest unsettles him; his response reveals that moral seriousness does not free him from possessiveness.

Kitty's marriage is also affected by her pregnant body and by the demands of the extended family. Tolstoy describes households as systems of work, not isolated pairs exchanging declarations of love. Women manage illness, meals, visits, children, and the atmosphere of rooms where men may discuss history or agriculture. The value of this labor is visible even when the people benefiting from it do not fully recognize its cost. Levin's ideal of a true family life must become more practical if it is to deserve that name.

Dolly visits Anna and Vronsky. She is weary of Stiva's infidelity and the burdens of a crowded home. Anna's household initially appears attractive: beautifully appointed rooms, leisure, attentive servants, and a confident lover who appears willing to supply material comfort. Yet Dolly soon observes strain beneath the display. Anna lacks the security that marriage would confer, and her dependence on Vronsky is growing. She cannot simply compare the advantages of two domestic arrangements as though they were freely available consumer choices.

The problem of divorce remains unresolved. Anna would like a legally recognized future, but the process is controlled by Karenin and by religious and social constraints that do not operate equally upon both spouses. Her daughter is with her, while her son remains in Karenin's household. Vronsky has ambitions for his estate, local political affairs, and his public standing. Anna is not incapable of social thought; she simply confronts a shrinking range of actions that society will tolerate. In the same rooms they can disagree over what it means to be free.

Vronsky's estate provides a stage for comparison with Levin. Vronsky brings money, technical order, and managerial assurance to his property. Levin tends to be more troubled by the labor system's inner contradictions. Neither man's estate is outside class power. Their land and income depend on social arrangements that permit owners to make decisions and workers to bear their consequences. Tolstoy presents both men sympathetically in places without requiring readers to accept their economic position as natural or just.

When Dolly returns to her own life, she has not found a simple answer to the problems of marriage. She can feel solidarity with Anna, judge parts of her conduct, and recognize the burdens of her own household. This complex perspective is valuable because the novel can otherwise seem divided into a condemned woman and a morally approved landowner. Dolly sees dimensions of both lives that their protagonists overlook. Her exhaustion may make a luxurious house look desirable, but her observation allows her to notice how little control Anna actually possesses.

Toward the end of this phase Anna becomes more suspicious of Vronsky's absences and interests, while he grows impatient with constant examination of his loyalty. Neither can make the other feel secure by one more explanation. The problem is not simply a defect of temperament. Anna's precarious legal and social position has made the relationship carry an impossible weight. Vronsky's continued freedom becomes, for her, a reminder that he could imagine a life after the affair more easily than she could.

The two households therefore offer neither a perfect ideal nor a perfect cautionary tale. Levin and Kitty must learn how to sustain everyday commitments amid irritability and work. Anna and Vronsky must confront a society that recognizes his status more readily than hers. The central question is how people can create a stable life when their desires are intense but their responsibilities and freedoms are so unevenly distributed.
