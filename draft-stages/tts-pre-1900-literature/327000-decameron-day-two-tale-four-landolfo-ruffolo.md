# The Decameron — Landolfo and the Floating Chest

Landolfo Ruffolo of Ravello has wealth enough to live comfortably, but he cannot be content with it. He loads a ship with merchandise and sails in hope of a much larger profit. The venture fails. Market conditions turn against him, and his carefully calculated trade leaves him poorer than before. Disappointed and desperate to recover his standing, he converts his vessel into a pirate ship. In that dangerous trade he prospers, taking wealth from other merchants by force.

His success is fragile. Genoese ships capture him, and the booty he has amassed is lost. Then a storm destroys the vessels carrying him. Landolfo, who has moved from legitimate commerce to piracy to captivity, now faces the sea with nothing left but his life. He clings to whatever floating object offers survival. A chest becomes his refuge. He cannot know what it contains. He knows only that it is large enough to keep him above the water while the wreckage drifts.

The sea finally carries him toward Corfu. A woman on the shore discovers him, takes pity on his condition, and helps him recover. She gives him warmth, food, and protection. In this household, generosity is not a reward for his deeds; the stranger does not know what he has done. When Landolfo opens the chest, he finds jewels of enormous value. The object that was merely a means of staying alive has also carried the fortune he thought gone forever. He returns home rich and sends a gift to the woman who helped him. This time he decides not to risk everything again in pursuit of still more wealth.

The story's reversals are exceptionally clear. Landolfo's first commercial judgment fails. Piracy makes him rich but destroys his moral claim to superiority over other thieves. His captors appear to control his future, until the storm makes their power meaningless. At the point of greatest helplessness, he acquires the goods that restore him. His prize has not been earned by shrewder trading or more violent plunder. It has come through an accident of wreckage and water.

Lauretta does not ask us to forget the people robbed along the way. Their losses do not disappear simply because Landolfo ends happily. The tale exposes a frightening uncertainty built into mercantile life: ships sink, cargo changes hands, markets refuse to obey the plans of traders. But it also gives a significant place to care. A woman on the shore treats an exhausted stranger as a person in need. The chest brings jewels; she brings the conditions under which a man can live to use them. The final change in Landolfo is not only that he has money again. He has learned, at least for now, what he did not know when the first ship departed: there can be enough.
