# The Decameron — What Is Great-Heartedness?

Panfilo, the final ruler of the ten storytellers, has assigned a subject that sounds like the culmination of everything they have heard: magnificence, or great-hearted actions. The word does not mean only splendor, expensive clothing, enormous buildings or lavish hospitality. It means a readiness to act generously where something of real value is at stake. A ruler can give money or land, but a person without royal wealth can also make a costly gift. By choosing this subject for the final storytelling day, Panfilo asks his companions to measure how people use power rather than simply how much power they possess.

At daybreak, clouds are still colored by the rising sun. Panfilo wakes the company and leads a walk through the grounds. The friends talk about the future as they go, for the retreat cannot continue forever. They have escaped the plague-stricken city and have managed their days through elected leadership, meals, work performed by servants, music and stories. Now their carefully organized interlude is coming to its natural conclusion. They return to the palace, drink by the fountain, take breakfast, rest and assemble again in the shade.

Neifile begins. Her tale concerns a knight who believes a king has rewarded him unjustly. Other stories will test the meaning of giving: an outlaw unexpectedly honors a captured abbot; a man offers his own life to someone who comes to murder him; a rescuer surrenders the woman he has saved; a suitor relinquishes a promise that he has secured at immense cost. Kings, lovers, friends and powerful men will all face situations in which refusing to take something may count for more than acquiring it.

There is an argument hidden inside this apparently noble theme. A gift made from abundant wealth may cost its giver little. An act by a poor man or a vulnerable woman may cost nearly everything. A gesture praised by other men as generous may involve the disposition of a woman who was never asked to agree. The last day's stories therefore require a distinction between magnificence as a reputation and generosity as something actually experienced by the recipient.

The sequence has a deliberate escalation. After the royal stories, Filomena tells of friendship carried to the point of surrendering a marriage. Panfilo offers an expansive tale of a host who unknowingly shelters a foreign sultan and is later rescued by him. Then Dioneo, who enjoys the final speaking turn, tells the story of Griselda and the Marquis of Saluzzo. It will look at first like an extreme example of patience. Yet Dioneo himself calls the marquis's conduct a kind of mad folly and does not offer it for imitation.

That is an important distinction as we enter the ending. The ten stories do not demonstrate that every praised sacrifice is good. They invite us to ask who chooses the sacrifice, who bears its cost, and whether a powerful person can honestly call himself generous while demanding submission from another. The frame around these tales retains the brightness of a leisurely afternoon, but the idea of magnificence will not remain simple for long.
