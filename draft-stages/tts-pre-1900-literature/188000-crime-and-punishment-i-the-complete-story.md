# Crime and Punishment I: the complete story

## Crime and Punishment — A Theory and Two Deaths

Crime and Punishment begins in the heat of a Petersburg summer. Rodion Romanovich Raskolnikov, a former student, lives in a cramped garret and avoids his landlady because he owes rent. His poverty is real, but it does not explain everything about him. He moves through the city with an idea that has grown more dangerous as his isolation has increased. He believes some exceptional people may be justified in breaking the rules that bind ordinary people, if their acts serve a larger historical purpose. The novel will test that thought through consequences no theory can hold still.

He visits Alyona Ivanovna, an elderly pawnbroker who lends small sums against the possessions of desperate people. He has been to her before. This visit is partly a rehearsal: he studies the route, the apartment, the door and the timing of its occupants. He despises the woman and imagines that taking her money might enable him to do good. Yet he recoils from the thought of murder. We hear self-disgust and rationalization in alternating bursts. Nothing in the novel presents her unpleasantness as a legal or moral permission to kill.

In a tavern he meets Semyon Marmeladov, an impoverished clerk who talks about his drinking and the suffering of his family. His daughter Sonya has been forced by economic desperation to earn money through prostitution to help sustain them. His wife Katerina Ivanovna is ill and struggling to care for children. Marmeladov tells his history with guilty eloquence but returns to destructive behavior. Raskolnikov walks away knowing that the city contains suffering beyond the kind he has selected as an excuse for his plan.

A letter arrives from his mother, Pulcheria Alexandrovna. His sister Avdotya Romanovna, called Dunya, plans to marry Pyotr Petrovich Luzhin, a man who promises material security while displaying a desire to have a wife indebted to him. Raskolnikov believes Dunya is sacrificing herself for her brother's education and future. He is furious that family love should be converted into a transaction. He is also unable to admit how his own wish to be a rescuer turns people into instruments of his pride.

The letter recounts another danger. Dunya previously worked in the household of Arkady Svidrigailov, whose advances threatened her reputation. His wife later helped clear Dunya's name, but the episode has shown how little protection a dependent young woman may possess against a man with money and status. The reader is being prepared to compare several forms of domination: Luzhin's respectable bargain, Svidrigailov's predatory desire, and Raskolnikov's theory of exceptional entitlement.

Raskolnikov dreams of childhood cruelty: a horse is beaten to death while adults jeer and watch. The child in the dream is horrified. The scene shows that pity has not vanished from him. His theory is not the whole of his character. Yet pity alone will not prevent the act he is considering. He learns by chance that the pawnbroker's gentler half-sister, Lizaveta, is expected to be out at a particular time. What he takes as opportunity does not amount to necessity. He chooses to proceed.

Carrying an axe, he goes to Alyona's apartment under a pretext, strikes her, and begins searching for valuables. The crime becomes chaotic immediately. Lizaveta unexpectedly returns and sees him. He kills her too. She is not the object of his theory and has not even been represented by him as an obstacle to social good. Her death exposes the emptiness of his effort to separate an abstract operation from particular human beings.

Visitors arrive outside the door. Raskolnikov narrowly escapes detection by hiding and seizing a chance to leave the building. His hands and clothing have been exposed to blood. He returns the axe and retreats to his miserable room. He has killed two people, taken possessions he scarcely understands how to handle, and gained none of the freedom he imagined the act would prove he possessed.

We have reached the crime before the end of the first part of the book. The remaining novel is not a conventional mystery about the identity of a murderer: the reader has watched the killings. The question is what the murder does to the mind that sought to justify it, and what obligations remain to the people who survive. Before we can ask whether Raskolnikov will be caught, we must ask what it now means for him to live among others.

## Crime and Punishment — Fever and the City

After the murders, Raskolnikov is not relieved. He is frightened by every sound and astonished that other people continue the routines of everyday life. He is summoned to a police office and immediately imagines that the crime has been discovered. The summons concerns unpaid rent. Yet when officers discuss the murders in his presence, he faints. The distance between what the authorities know and what he fears becomes almost unbearable to him.

He has stolen objects and money but cannot bring himself to use them. Instead of buying food or helping the people whose suffering he cited as justification, he hides the loot beneath a stone. The supposed economic objective has disappeared. The theft does not serve a benevolent plan, and the murder has not yielded a stable new identity. His actions increasingly suggest that the experiment was less about enrichment than about whether he could step across a moral boundary without being destroyed by the knowledge of crossing it.

He falls into illness and delirium. His friend Dmitri Razumikhin comes to help, arranging practical care and trying to make sense of his erratic behavior. Razumikhin is energetic, frequently untidy, and not without vanity, but he does what Raskolnikov's abstractions cannot do: he looks after someone in a room, provides food, arranges a doctor, and returns when needed. His ordinary reliability will become a moral counterweight to the supposedly extraordinary theory.

Raskolnikov alternates between craving company and driving it away. He tries to follow what the police know and what journalists say. He behaves recklessly with people who might suspect him, partly because he wants reassurance that he is invisible and partly because he seems compelled to expose himself. He returns to the neighborhood of the murder and asks questions that would alarm anyone familiar with the case. The city becomes a theater in which every stranger might be an accuser.

The conversations around him are crowded with arguments about social conditions. People discuss poverty, reform, crime, and whether improved institutions can make wrongdoing disappear. Dostoevsky places these ideas near real suffering but does not offer a simple opposition between caring about poverty and taking moral responsibility. Raskolnikov has killed Lizaveta, a poor woman who had no power over him, while telling himself that the exploitation of poverty required a bold response. The contradiction remains present even when his speech turns toward grand philosophical questions.

He meets Luzhin and reacts with hostility. Luzhin insists on the respectable logic of his proposed marriage to Dunya, presenting economic advantage as proof of good sense. Raskolnikov sees condescension but is himself rude and controlling. His family's dependence and his sister's threatened choices wound his pride, yet he is in no position to claim moral superiority merely because he dislikes another man's bargain.

Walking through the city, he encounters despair in other forms, including an attempted suicide by a woman in the water. His impulse to help struggles with a feeling that the human world has become intolerable. His isolation is not lack of information about other people's pain; it is inability to remain answerable to them. The feverish passages make the world feel both crowded and unreachable.

Late in this movement, a carriage accident fatally injures Marmeladov. Raskolnikov helps bring the injured man home and gives money to the family. Sonya arrives as her father is dying. Katerina Ivanovna, exhausted by poverty and illness, must confront yet another loss. Raskolnikov's generosity is genuine. It does not cancel the two murders. The reader now sees a person capable of immediate care and terrible violence without being invited to regard one act as a receipt balancing the other.

When he leaves the family, he experiences a sudden sensation of renewed life and hope. It is fragile and confused. He has met actual people whose needs demand particular responses, but he has not yet admitted what he has done. The tension between public appearance and private guilt can no longer be contained inside his garret. His mother and sister are about to arrive, and the police investigation will become more pointed. The supposed solitary experiment has drawn everyone close to him into its consequences.

## Crime and Punishment — Family and Porfiry

Raskolnikov's mother and sister arrive in Petersburg hoping to see a recovered son and to make arrangements for Dunya's marriage. Instead they find him feverish, abrupt, and unwilling to explain himself. Their affection is intense; it is not enough to make him available to them. Raskolnikov wants to direct the decisions of people he loves while concealing the most consequential decision he has made.

Razumikhin becomes invaluable to the family. He has already cared for the sick young man and now helps the two women navigate lodgings and acquaintances. He is attracted to Dunya, but his behavior is a contrast with Luzhin's. He offers help without assuming that it entitles him to own her future. The contrast is not absolute—Razumikhin can be foolish and excited—but the capacity to provide practical care is becoming a central moral difference among the men.

Luzhin's demand for obedience grows clearer. He tries to arrange meetings so that Raskolnikov is excluded and suggests that Dunya should choose between her brother and her prospective husband. He frames control as respectability. Dunya has not agreed to become an instrument of his quarrel. She can perceive what her future would cost if a marriage began by accepting that ultimatum.

The murder investigation moves inward. Porfiry Petrovich, the investigating magistrate, has learned about an article Raskolnikov wrote before the crime. The essay proposed a distinction between ordinary people and exceptional people who may supposedly have the right to transgress accepted laws in pursuit of historically significant aims. The idea is often associated with figures such as Napoleon. It is not simply a claim that clever people deserve special privileges; it is a test of whether one person's vision can license violence against another.

During an interview, Porfiry treats the article with a mixture of curiosity, amusement, and pressure. He does not need to accuse Raskolnikov directly to make every answer feel dangerous. The investigator understands that a person who has built his identity around a theory may want to defend the theory even when speaking would be safer. Raskolnikov tries to stay composed but becomes intensely conscious of his own reactions. The interview is a contest over who controls the meaning of his words.

The talk about exceptional individuals exposes the moral structure of the case. If a great man may kill because he imagines some future benefit, how can anyone decide beforehand who is great and which future will count? The violence against Lizaveta provides an answer more concrete than a philosophical counterexample: the victim of an improvised theory does not stop being a person when the theory fails to account for her.

Raskolnikov also receives attention from people who notice his strange conduct near the murder scene. One stranger addresses him as a murderer. The encounter unsettles him profoundly because he cannot tell how much has been discovered. His imagination begins to substitute for evidence. He is surrounded by people who know fragments of truth and fragments of his behavior; his mind fills in the spaces with persecution.

His dreams and waking thoughts become difficult to distinguish. In one disturbing dream he tries to repeat the murder and finds that the woman will not yield to the action he imagines. The dream is not a reliable account of an external event. It reveals how impossible it has become for him to return to the clean, controllable calculation he held before the killing. His victim exists in memory as something he cannot erase.

At the end of this movement a new figure appears: Arkady Svidrigailov, the former employer who harassed Dunya and whose presence threatens her independence. His arrival suggests that the novel's conflict over exceptional power will not remain confined to Raskolnikov. Other men also believe that desire, money, or self-definition can excuse the use of people. The story is about to force these competing forms of entitlement into the same room.

## Crime and Punishment — Lazarus and the False Confession

Svidrigailov's appearance brings a new kind of threat. He has money and knows how to make misconduct seem like charm or eccentricity. He says he wants to assist Dunya, but his earlier pursuit of her makes his generosity suspect. The reader should not accept his self-description as the truth of his intentions. His influence works by offering people a bargain in which he controls the conditions.

Dunya confronts Luzhin's demand that her brother be excluded from the family. He resents having his authority questioned and uses financial advantage as though it should silence criticism. Dunya refuses to accept the marriage on those terms and dismisses him. The scene restores an element of agency that her earlier circumstances seemed to threaten. The rejection does not solve the family's poverty, but it establishes that need does not erase a person's right to judge the person who proposes to relieve it.

Raskolnikov continues moving between his family and Sonya Marmeladova. Sonya lives under the burden of supporting Katerina Ivanovna and the children after Marmeladov's decline and death. Her work exposes her to humiliation, and she has no social power comparable to the men who speak so confidently about moral theory. Yet she has retained a way of understanding people that does not reduce them to categories of useful and useless.

Raskolnikov goes to her room and questions her harshly about her life, her faith, and how she can continue under such pressure. He asks her to read the New Testament story of the raising of Lazarus. The reading is not a magic cure. It places before them an account of life restored after what appears final. Raskolnikov remains divided between a longing for renewal and the pride that makes confession feel like surrender. Sonya does not yet know the full truth of his act.

Svidrigailov has taken rooms from which he can hear conversations in Sonya's quarters. He will use this access later. It is another form of listening without consent, in a novel where information is constantly sought for purposes of control. Porfiry investigates through pressure and inference; Svidrigailov gathers knowledge that may become leverage over Dunya.

At a further interview, Porfiry keeps Raskolnikov uncertain about the extent of the evidence. He brings up his behavior at the murder scene and seems ready to produce a witness. Raskolnikov resists the insinuations. At the crucial moment a house painter named Nikolai, sometimes called Mikolka in translation, suddenly appears and declares himself guilty of the killings. Porfiry is startled. The confession seems at first to provide the escape Raskolnikov needs, but it is not reliable evidence of the painter's guilt.

Raskolnikov survives the interview without an accusation that can be proved publicly. Yet the false confession makes his position morally worse. Another person is now standing in danger of punishment for crimes he committed. Even if Raskolnikov never spends the stolen goods and never openly repeats the theory of exceptional power, his silence is capable of harming someone he has not previously considered.

The structure of the story is revealing. Raskolnikov reads about resurrection with Sonya; another man falsely confesses to his crime; and Porfiry refuses to treat the apparent solution as satisfactory. Religious hope and legal procedure have not yet converged. A confession can be true or false; an act of faith can be sincere or demanded; a declaration can conceal as much as it reveals. The novel insists on more than words.

Raskolnikov has repeatedly tried to make other people serve his experiment without their knowing it. Sonya's compassion, Dunya's refusal, and the painter's peril force him to confront different forms of responsibility. The next movement will bring his crime into speech. Speaking will not yet mean that he understands it.

## Crime and Punishment — Confession and Loss

At the opening of Part Five, Luzhin has lost Dunya's consent to marry him. Instead of questioning his demand to control her, he looks for a way to disgrace the family and reassert his superiority. He turns to Sonya, the least powerful person among the people gathered around Raskolnikov. His plan will demonstrate how easily public judgment can be manipulated when a victim has already been marked as disreputable.

At a gathering connected with Marmeladov's funeral, Luzhin accuses Sonya of stealing a banknote. He has placed the money where it can be discovered and attempts to convert the discovery into proof. Sonya is frightened and cannot defend herself through social standing. But Andrei Lebezyatnikov has seen Luzhin's action and exposes the trick. The accusation fails because a witness supplies the missing information. We are asked to distinguish proof from a display designed to look like proof.

The incident also demonstrates the cost of poverty and sexual stigma. Luzhin chooses Sonya because the listeners may find a charge against her plausible before hearing it. Her position as a woman supporting a family through prostitution makes her vulnerable to moral contempt from people who have benefited from or ignored the conditions that produced it. The novel condemns Luzhin's deliberate falsification without asking the reader to imagine that Sonya's social difficulties vanish once he is exposed.

Raskolnikov goes to Sonya and tells her what happened in Alyona Ivanovna's apartment. He confesses that he killed the pawnbroker and Lizaveta. The second name matters profoundly: Lizaveta was Sonya's friend. The confession is therefore made not to a neutral vessel of forgiveness but to someone who has a personal relation to one of the victims. Sonya is shocked and grieves. She does not accept his theory as permission. She urges him to acknowledge the crime publicly and to submit to punishment rather than continue using others as shelter for his secret.

Raskolnikov tries to explain himself. He speaks of money and good deeds, but these explanations break down under questioning. He speaks of Napoleon and of wanting to know whether he could step across an accepted boundary. At other moments he despises the pride that drove him. His words shift because he has not settled on a truthful account of himself. Confession of the act does not produce a stable confession of motive.

Sonya's response is central. She does not say that the killings are harmless because Raskolnikov suffers. She asks him to live in truth with what he has done. Her willingness to remain near him is a form of care that does not depend on falsifying the crime. That combination is difficult for him to accept. If he could believe the act was justified, he would not need forgiveness. If he could believe himself beyond repair, he would not need to change.

The Marmeladov household meanwhile reaches catastrophe. Katerina Ivanovna, ill and humiliated, tries to make her children perform in the streets for money. Her desperation becomes public spectacle. She collapses and dies, leaving the children without the parent who struggled to care for them. Svidrigailov uses his wealth to arrange material support for the orphaned children and help with funeral expenses. The help is real. It does not erase his predatory conduct toward Dunya, any more than Raskolnikov's earlier gift erased the murders.

Svidrigailov has overheard enough through the wall of Sonya's room to know Raskolnikov's secret. This creates a new danger. A confession intended for moral truth can be converted into leverage by someone who has no intention of using it responsibly. Dunya's future and Raskolnikov's legal exposure are now linked through a man whose motives remain hard to trust.

At the end of Part Five, the family arguments, the legal investigation, and the poor household's suffering no longer look like parallel episodes. Luzhin's false accusation rehearses what unreliable proof can do. Sonya's knowledge makes the murder personal rather than theoretical. Svidrigailov possesses the secret for his own purposes. Raskolnikov has spoken the truth to one person. He has not yet taken responsibility before the society whose law he broke.

## Crime and Punishment — Porfiry, Svidrigailov, and the Last Choice

In the sixth part of Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov's possibilities narrow. He can continue hiding behind the painter's false confession, surrender to the authorities, or try to persuade himself that no moral surrender is required. The investigation is no longer principally a question of what evidence a police officer can assemble. Several people know parts of the truth, and Raskolnikov must decide what kind of person he will become after the crime.

Porfiry Petrovich visits him without the theatrical pressure of their earlier encounters. The investigator explains that he suspects Raskolnikov and does not believe Nikolai the painter committed the murders. He advises a voluntary confession. He also tells Raskolnikov that a future remains possible beyond punishment. Porfiry's confidence is not identical to possession of courtroom proof; his insight and the available legal evidence are different things. His appeal joins procedural calculation to psychological understanding.

Raskolnikov reacts angrily because confession threatens the image of himself as someone entitled to overrule ordinary human limits. He is still capable of calling his act a failure of courage rather than a moral violation. At the same time, his fear and isolation reveal that the theory has never made him invulnerable. The conflict is not between a criminal who feels no remorse and a magistrate who instructs him to feel it. It is between incompatible ways of interpreting his own suffering and responsibility.

Svidrigailov takes a parallel path. He knows of the murders through the conversations he overheard and hopes the information will bring Dunya under his control. He lures her into a situation where he can threaten to expose her brother. The demand is coercive, not romantic. Dunya resists, and when trapped she draws a revolver and fires. The encounter ends when Svidrigailov recognizes that he cannot make her love him; he allows her to leave. His decision to release her does not undo the threat he has made.

Afterward Svidrigailov wanders through the night. He makes arrangements involving money and others' future support, then experiences bleak dreams and an increasing sense of emptiness. In the morning he kills himself. The novel makes his final hours unsettling rather than presenting him as a tidy philosophical example. Like Raskolnikov, he has been tempted to regard other lives as available for private desire. Their choices and possible futures are not the same, but the parallel is intentional.

Raskolnikov says farewell to his mother without telling her everything. He admits the crime to Dunya, who urges him toward responsibility. His family love remains real, but concealment has made every encounter another burden for the people who care for him. Razumikhin remains with the family, providing a steadier form of protection than Raskolnikov's grand claims to rescue them ever did.

He visits Sonya. She gives him a cross, and she continues to insist that acknowledging guilt cannot be delegated to somebody else. He goes into the public square and performs a gesture of abasement, bowing and kissing the ground. People around him do not see a heroic spiritual transformation; some mock or misunderstand him. His gesture is incomplete because he has not yet said the truth to the authorities.

At the police station he almost withdraws. News of Svidrigailov's suicide unsettles him, and he walks away from the office rather than confess immediately. Then he sees Sonya, still watching and still willing to share the consequences of his decision. He returns and states that he killed the pawnbroker and Lizaveta with an axe and stole from the apartment.

This is not the novel's last page, and that is essential. A confession to police can begin legal accountability, but it cannot instantaneously provide repentance, restore the victims, or repair relationships. Raskolnikov has finally made the truth public. The epilogue will ask whether he can learn to understand that truth as something more than the record of a failed experiment.

## Crime and Punishment — The Epilogue

When Crime and Punishment reaches its epilogue, the legal question has an answer. Raskolnikov has confessed and has been sentenced to eight years of penal labor in Siberia. The court has considered his confession and circumstances, including evidence of earlier acts of generosity and his disturbed state around the murders. But if the book ended with a sentence, it would confuse the administration of punishment with the completion of a moral life. Dostoevsky continues beyond the courtroom because the problem that organized the crime is still alive in Raskolnikov.

Sonya follows him to Siberia and settles near the prison where he serves his sentence. She writes to the family in Petersburg and visits when she can. Her care is neither effortless nor a theatrical guarantee of salvation. She lives under restrictions and must build relationships in a new place. Other prisoners respond warmly to her, while Raskolnikov remains proud, withdrawn, and resentful. He cannot understand why people value her or why they are hostile to him. His isolation has survived the confession.

Dunya and Razumikhin marry. Razumikhin continues to support the family and hopes that Raskolnikov may someday rejoin them. His mother Pulcheria Alexandrovna declines and dies, never possessing a fully candid explanation of her son's situation. The story does not distribute consequences evenly. The murder has reshaped people whose love could neither prevent nor justify it.

For a long time Raskolnikov still does not repent in a straightforward way. He recognizes that he has been caught and disgraced, but at times regards his failure as the inability to bear an act that a truly exceptional man would have endured. That is a crucial distinction. Shame at a failed performance is not the same as grief for the people killed. If we say he was spiritually restored the moment he entered the police station, we erase much of what Dostoevsky has written in the epilogue.

During illness he dreams of a plague spreading across the world. People become convinced that their own views are unquestionably correct and turn on one another. The dream enlarges Raskolnikov's theory into a social nightmare: each person acting as the final authority on truth produces violence rather than liberation. It is a dream, not a historical forecast supplied by an impartial narrator. Its force comes from the relationship between his private experiment and a world unable to acknowledge another person's claim.

Sonya becomes ill for a time, and Raskolnikov discovers an unexpected urgency in his concern for her. When they meet again, his defenses begin to loosen. In a moment of overwhelming feeling he falls before her and weeps. She understands that he loves her. The novel presents this as the beginning of a transformation rather than proof that everything necessary has already occurred.

A copy of the Gospels is nearby, associated with Sonya and the earlier reading of Lazarus. Raskolnikov has not simply become a theologian capable of solving the questions that once troubled him. He has begun to imagine a life organized around attachment to another person rather than the need to prove his exemption from ordinary moral bonds. Dostoevsky closes by suggesting that the long story of renewal lies beyond the book's pages.

What remains unresolved? Alyona Ivanovna and Lizaveta are still dead. Their lives cannot be restored by the criminal's suffering. Sonya's care has a cost. Raskolnikov still has years of punishment to serve. The people in Petersburg must live with the consequences of what has happened. The epilogue offers hope without undoing any of these facts.

The title Crime and Punishment includes law, but it also includes the inward experience of a crime and the possibility that punishment might become part of change. The sequence matters: a murder, an attempted theory of exception, fever and fear, encounters with people who refuse reduction to ideas, disclosure, legal accountability, and only then the tentative start of a different life. The novel ends at a threshold. Its final achievement is to make the reader see how much work must follow crossing it.
