# Journey to the West: The queen, the bells, and the golden-haired beast

## One more visit to the queen

Sai Taisui kept the gates of Xiezhi Cave closed. He ordered watchmen to beat gongs and knock clappers through the night, guards to stand with weapons ready, and officers to search the corridors for a thief who could look like anyone. Yet he had not found his thief. A fly rested where no one thought to look. It was Sun Wukong, much smaller than the terrible warrior whose name the guards had been taught to fear.

A creature that small can go where a man with an iron staff cannot. When the watch grew loud around the outer gates, Wukong flew back into the Golden Sage's chambers. He found the queen leaning over her table and weeping. Her grief had a new edge. Yesterday a messenger from Zhuzi had come with a bracelet and the promise of rescue. Today that messenger appeared to have been killed or captured by the mountain lord. She believed hope itself had been a trick played upon her.

A whisper rose beside her ear. The monkey was alive. He had made a mistake with the bells, he admitted. They had released their powers as soon as he examined them. The demon had taken them again. Wukong had hidden in the shape of a fly rather than abandon the queen. Now he had returned to ask for another chance.

The queen could not easily believe a voice with no body. She spread her hand, and a little fly settled in her palm. Wukong spoke again. His smallness was absurd beside the seriousness of the pledge, but she believed him at last. He wanted her to invite Sai Taisui into the room once more, to offer him drink and act as though her wish to be his wife had not changed. Wukong would be close enough to steal the bells by some less foolish method.

The queen asked how she could endure such an evening. Wukong did not pretend that she ought to enjoy it. He promised that he would remain nearby. She was to summon Chun Jiao, a fox-shaped attendant whom the demon trusted, and direct her to prepare the procession. Wukong flew onto the maid's head and turned a hair into a sleep-inducing insect. Soon the servant was stumbling with tiredness. She went behind the screen and fell asleep. Wukong took her form, the face, the clothes, and the obedient manner that would let him stand at the queen's side.

The masquerade would succeed only if two people performed it. Wukong could supply the false maid. The Golden Sage had to speak the lines that would persuade her captor to lower his guard.

## Wine, dancers, and fleas

Sai Taisui welcomed her invitation with delight. He told her about the intruder who had brought fire into the pavilion. The creature was Sun Wukong, he said, a monkey who had broken the spear of his advance guard and killed his messenger. The mountain lord had locked all the gates, yet the thief had vanished. The queen answered with the calmest voice she could find. Since the intruder seemed to have escaped, why spend another night chasing shadows? Come and rest instead.

In her room a table was set with fruit, meat, and wine. The false Chun Jiao poured the cups. The queen offered a ceremonial exchange of drinking vessels. Wukong urged more toasts and called the other servants to sing and dance. With music, wine, and the prospect of a marriage that existed only in the demon's hopes, Sai Taisui began to relax. He still could not touch the queen; the strange garment he had given her three years earlier punished every attempt. Yet he was delighted to be seated near her and addressed with courtesy.

When the dancing ended, the queen asked whether his treasures had been damaged by the thief. The bells themselves were too powerful to break, Sai Taisui said. Only the wrapping had burned when the foolish intruder pulled the cotton from their mouths. Now he wore them securely beneath his clothes, close to his skin. No one would steal them again.

The disguised maid was listening. Wukong tore hairs into tiny pieces, chewed them, and breathed magic across Sai Taisui's body. The bits became fleas, lice, and bedbugs. They crawled under the collar, into the seams, between the layers of the demon's garments. Sai Taisui twitched. He scratched once, then again. The itch became unbearable. He found a louse and stared at it as though his own household had insulted him.

The queen expressed sympathetic embarrassment. Perhaps his undergarments needed laundering. What man, even a ruler, had never found an insect in his clothes? Let him remove the layers and she would help inspect them. Sai Taisui, pleased by this apparent concern, began to undress. Fleas leaped from every fold. Then the purple-gold bells appeared, still close to his body.

The false Chun Jiao offered to clean the insects from them. Sai Taisui, distracted and ashamed, handed them over. Wukong pretended to examine every little edge. He concealed the real bells and transformed hairs into three perfect imitations. The fake ornaments were returned to their owner. Sai Taisui passed them to the queen and urged her to lock them carefully in her cabinet this time. The queen obeyed. She knew exactly what kind of bells she had shut away, but the demon did not.

More wine was poured. The queen proposed that they prepare her bed. Sai Taisui, still unable to endure the enchanted spikes, declined with extravagant politeness. He would spend the night elsewhere, with another servant. The Golden Sage was left alone in her chamber, having once again acted a part to which she had never consented.

Wukong cast off the maid's shape. He restored the sleeping Chun Jiao, so that the real servant would wake without understanding what had happened. Then he slipped through the corridors invisible, opened the sealed gate with a spell, and reached the outside of the cave. There, instead of quietly returning to Zhuzi with the bells, he shouted his challenge.

## The battle of the two sets of bells

He called Sai Taisui out. He declared that he had been sent by Zhuzi and demanded the release of the Golden Sage. Through the night the guards took his messages inward. They were terrified of waking their master. At dawn, after Wukong had begun striking the gate with his staff, Sai Taisui appeared and asked who had been shouting.

The reply came back that the stranger was the queen's grandfather from Zhuzi. Sai Taisui was puzzled. Was there a family name that meant grandfather? He questioned the Golden Sage, who answered with scholarly seriousness about words in old books. Between them they supplied an explanation that satisfied the demon, though the audience knows the monkey was simply enjoying the chance to call an enemy his nephew.

Sai Taisui armed himself and came out carrying a decorated axe. A small monkey stood outside and saluted him as a nephew who had at last responded to his grandfather's summons. Sai Taisui demanded a name. Wukong answered with the old account of the stone that gave him birth, the Dragon King's staff, the heavenly appointment that insulted him, his title as Great Sage Equal to Heaven, the peaches, the furnace, the Buddha's hand, and the centuries beneath the mountain. It was both a boast and a reminder that he had faced far greater powers than the lord of this cave.

The demon asked why such a famous creature would lower himself to work for a human king. Wukong rejected the accusation. The king had asked for help with proper honor. Xuanzang's passage required passports, food, and the goodwill of the country. Accepting hospitality did not make Wukong a servant in the contemptuous sense his enemy intended. And in any case, the abduction of a queen and the terrorizing of a population were matters that ought to concern anyone able to intervene.

The two fought. Staff struck axe, cloud rolled against cloud, and Sai Taisui discovered that he could not easily force the monkey back. After many exchanges the demon asked to return for breakfast. Wukong knew the real purpose: the three bells. He agreed, feigning the generous patience of a fighter willing to face an opponent at full strength.

Inside, Sai Taisui demanded that the queen unlock the cabinet. She hesitated. If the bells had been stolen again, what would become of her? If she refused, what would he suspect? At last she handed him the imitation set. She could only hope that Wukong's plan had succeeded.

Back outside, Sai Taisui climbed to a favorable position and waved the first bell. Nothing happened. He waved the second. No smoke came. He shook the third and found no sand. Incredulous, he tried again. Wukong brought out the true bells and offered a ridiculous explanation: perhaps one set was male and the other female, and Sai Taisui's bells were frightened by the presence of their partners. The demon objected that bells were not living creatures. Wukong suggested a test. The mountain lord's bells had failed; now let the monkey try his own.

He shook all three at once. Fire rose across the slope. Smoke rolled like a storm wall. Sand swept through the trees and over the terrified army. Wukong called up wind to give the flames greater force. Sai Taisui, who had threatened to burn the people of Zhuzi with these very treasures, found himself caught within their fury.

## Guanyin and the debt of three years

Before Wukong could strike the trapped demon, a voice called from above. Guanyin had arrived with her willow branch and pure-water vase. She sprinkled drops over the mountain. The fire died, the smoke thinned, and the sand ceased to fly. Wukong fell to his knees and asked why she had come to save the monster.

The answer transformed the story. Sai Taisui was not a wild creature who had wandered from nowhere. He was Guanyin's golden-haired mount, a powerful beast that had broken free when its keeper fell asleep. Guanyin also supplied a history that the king of Zhuzi had never told his visitors. As a young prince he had been fond of hunting. On an excursion he shot a male peacock and wounded its female companion. They were the offspring of the Peacock Wisdom King, a revered Buddhist figure. A judgment followed that his union with his wife should be broken for three years and his body afflicted by sickness. The mount had overheard this judgment, escaped, and acted as its instrument.

Wukong did not accept the explanation as a vindication. The beast had seized a woman, forced other women into its service, and threatened a city. What part of that was harmless correction of an old wrong? Guanyin said that the allotted three years had ended and that the monster would now be reclaimed. Wukong asked at least to give it a beating. She refused. A few strokes from the iron staff would destroy the creature. If he respected her presence, he would leave it alive and let her take it home.

The monkey obeyed, but not without complaint. A powerful creature had been permitted to turn a private punishment into public suffering. He asked Guanyin to keep better watch when she returned to the southern sea.

The monster rolled into its true form, the golden-haired beast. Guanyin prepared to mount it and noticed the bells were missing from its neck. Wukong claimed ignorance. She told him plainly that without the stolen bells, not even ten Wukongs could have approached so easily. He continued pretending. She threatened to recite the spell of the tightening fillet. The bells appeared at once. Guanyin replaced them on her mount and departed across the sky.

The greatest power in this confrontation had belonged neither to Sai Taisui nor to Wukong. The creature had been dangerous because of treasures given in a divine household. Its final escape from punishment also depended on that household. Wukong had rescued the queen, yet he had not been allowed to decide the beast's sentence.

## Returning the Golden Sage

Wukong went back into the cave. He attacked the remaining demons, who could no longer rely on Sai Taisui or his bells, and cleared the rooms until the queen could leave. He told her that Guanyin had recovered her mount and that the three-year ordeal was finished. For her journey home he made a little grass dragon, had her ride it with her eyes closed, and carried her by magic toward Zhuzi.

When she opened her eyes, the Golden Sage saw the roofs, courtyards, and gates of her own palace. The king rushed toward her. After years of separation his first movement was to take her hand. Immediately he cried out in pain and fell back. The enchanted garment still produced sharp points; what had protected the queen from Sai Taisui now prevented the reunion for which she had endured so much. Bajie laughed at the king's luck, a joke that did not improve the mood in the hall.

Then another immortal appeared, Zhang Ziyang, also known as Zhang Boduan. He explained that he had seen the danger three years before. He had turned an old brown garment into the splendid robe whose spikes defended the queen's body. Now that the demon had been returned, he came to lift the protection. With a gesture, the enchanted robe fell away. The queen stood as she had before her abduction, and the king could at last hold her without being hurt.

The palace filled with gratitude and ceremony. Wukong recovered from Xuanzang the declaration of war he had hidden in his master's sleeve and showed it to the king. Only now did he explain the full sequence: the advance guard, the messenger called Come-and-Go, the bracelet, the false courier, the first failure, the second theft, and Guanyin's arrival. The king heard that the creature which had made him hide beneath the earth was a divine mount and that the cause of his suffering was linked to a hunting crime committed long ago. Whether this explanation satisfied every person harmed by Sai Taisui was a question the celebrations did not ask.

The court wanted the pilgrims to remain. Xuanzang thanked the king but refused. Their western mission was unfinished. Passports were stamped, gifts offered, and an escort gathered. The king and his recovered queen accompanied the four travelers toward the road. Soon Zhuzi lay behind them, no longer hiding from a visitor out of the sky. There would be other kingdoms and other monsters. But for this family, the reunion had finally happened.
