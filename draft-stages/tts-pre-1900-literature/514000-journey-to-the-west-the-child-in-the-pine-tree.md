# Journey to the West: The child in the pine tree

## A kingdom returned to its king

In Wuji Kingdom, the king had his life again, but not yet his crown.

He stood among his wife, his son, and his ministers in the clothes of a man who had carried a foreign monk's luggage through the palace gates. Before them lay the dead impostor's abandoned throne. His rescuers had uncovered a murder that had lasted three years; they had learned that even the impostor had been an instrument of a bodhisattva's punishment. Now the question was what to do with a living man who had already watched his kingdom continue without him.

The monks from Baolin Temple appeared bearing the royal garments they had kept safe: the high crown, the belt of jade, the yellow robe, and the shoes of state. The prince brought forward the ceremonial tablet by which the king could be recognized. Wukong arranged the pieces about him as though completing a picture whose true subject had been missing. The prince lifted the tablet into his father's hand. The ministers urged him toward the throne.

The king wept and refused. Three years dead, and restored by monks who owed him nothing: how could he presume to take his old place without first acknowledging what they had done? He asked Xuanzang to rule in his stead. He would gladly withdraw beyond the city with his family and live as an ordinary subject.

Xuanzang's answer was immediate. He had not come to establish a government. He had come for the scriptures.

Then the king turned to Wukong.

The monkey grinned. He had been offered kingdoms before, and had treated the court of Heaven as something between an employer and a nuisance. If he had wanted to be a king, he said, there were more kingdoms in the world than he could count. An emperor had to grow his hair, keep watch through the night, answer news from distant borders, and worry over hunger, flood, and war. A monk could get up, eat what was offered, and walk away. The duties of a throne seemed a very poor bargain.

That settled it. The king returned to his seat, pardoned offenders, honored the monks whose temple had sheltered the pilgrims, and had portraits of Xuanzang and his disciples made for the palace. A banquet filled the rooms that had been ruled by a stranger.

But the king could not repay his visitors with a kingdom, and Xuanzang refused precious gifts. Gold and silk were laid before him. He would not take a thread. He asked only that the travel document be properly endorsed and that the party be allowed to continue west.

When they left, the court came with them to the road. The king, queen, prince, and ministers escorted the pilgrims beyond the city, pressing near the wheels of the departing carriage as though a longer farewell could delay the parting. The king asked Xuanzang to visit again when he returned from India. Xuanzang agreed. Then the white horse carried him out through the fields, and the figures in royal silk grew smaller behind them.

Autumn had almost passed. Frost touched the leaves. The warm roofs of the capital gave way to the exposed ridges of the westward road.

## A voice that would not be left behind

After more than half a month, the travelers came to a mountain so high that it appeared to divide the sky. Clouds clung to its cliffs; dark streams threaded the valleys; animal cries sounded from woods where a rider could see only a few steps ahead. Xuanzang gathered the horse's reins and asked Wukong to be vigilant. They had crossed too many mountains whose caves had hungry owners.

Wukong said he was watching.

Then a red cloud rose from a hollow below the path. It was not the blush of sunset. It burned upward like a column of heat, and Wukong, whose eyes were trained to see through disguises, understood that something alive was concealed within it. He seized his master's leg and hauled him off the horse.

Bajie raised his rake. Sha Wujing stepped in front of Xuanzang with his staff. Wukong scanned the ridges until the red vapor scattered and the creature vanished from sight.

'Get back on the horse,' he said.

Xuanzang's patience snapped. A moment ago the monkey had warned him of a monster; now he was expected to ride on without explanation. Did Wukong mean to throw him from the saddle whenever a cloud moved? Xuanzang began to remind him of the tightening spell. Sha Wujing intervened, and the company resumed its climb.

Not far ahead came a cry.

'Help me! Please, save me!'

They heard it again, young and frightened. Xuanzang turned toward the sound. He believed he could distinguish a real human voice by its echo. He had not yet seen the child, but he was sure there was a person in danger.

Wukong urged him to pass by. Spirits could learn voices. Creatures that spent centuries in mountain grass could learn names, prayers, and cries for help. Some knew how to call out a traveler's childhood name and seize the spirit of whoever answered. A dangerous place was no place for a monk to make decisions based on sound alone.

Xuanzang would not accept this. What was the point of seeking sacred teaching, he asked, if they stepped around a person in distress?

Wukong tried another method. He fell back a few paces, pointed his staff, and used a spell that compressed the road between them and the next crest. In a few steps the pilgrims had passed beyond the place from which the voice came. Wukong thought he had left the trouble behind.

The voice followed, now sounding from the slope behind them.

Xuanzang stopped. The poor child had been left behind, he said. They had missed their chance to help. Bajie suggested the wind had shifted, although he knew very well that Wukong had hurried them away.

Above the ridge, the red cloud reappeared.

Its owner was watching. He had chosen the monk precisely because the monk could not bear to ignore another's suffering. He had waited long in the mountain for news of a holy man who had cultivated goodness through ten lives. Anyone who ate a portion of that monk's flesh, the stories promised, could prolong life almost without limit. But the man came guarded by three formidable disciples. The creature's first approach had failed. Its second would have to be more convincing.

It floated ahead of them and changed shape again.

This time Xuanzang looked up and saw what he had been hearing: a little boy, perhaps seven years old, naked, bound at wrists and ankles, hanging by a hemp rope from the branches of a pine. He twisted in the wind and cried out to the passing strangers.

'You see?' Xuanzang said to Wukong. 'A child!'

Wukong saw the child. He also saw the demon. But Xuanzang was looking directly at them both, and the threat of the tightening spell still hung over the monkey's head. He lowered his eyes and let his master speak.

The boy began a long, carefully rehearsed account. His grandfather had possessed a fortune. His father had inherited it and lent money at interest, until dishonest borrowers brought ruin upon the household. A band of robbers had seized the family's remaining possessions, murdered the father, and carried off the mother. Because she had begged them not to kill her son, the robbers had left him tied to this tree instead. He had hung there for three days and nights, alone, cold, and close to death.

The tale was shaped for Xuanzang's compassion. There was a slaughtered father, a kidnapped mother, a boy punished for his parents' wealth, and a promise of gratitude if anyone would help. The monk did not ask how a seven-year-old had survived three days suspended in the cold, or why the child's story contained so many convenient details.

'Cut him down,' he told Bajie.

Bajie did. Freed from the ropes, the boy collapsed theatrically on the ground. He could not walk, he said. His limbs were too weak.

Xuanzang considered placing the child on the horse, but the horse was the only way he himself could travel. He asked whether one of the disciples would carry him.

Wukong volunteered.

He spoke kindly enough, and helped the boy upon his back. Yet as he walked he studied the weight of the body and the movement of the spirit within it. If the child was truly a demon, a monk's mercy had handed that demon a place against Wukong's shoulders.

## The body and the storm

The boy felt Wukong's suspicion and drew in four great breaths. His body grew heavier with every step. Soon the monkey seemed to carry a mountain on his back.

'You think you can pin me to the earth with a little borrowed weight?' Wukong asked softly. 'I know what you're doing.'

The creature, afraid of being crushed along with its disguise, slipped its spirit out of the boy's body and rose unseen above the treetops. The weight remained on Wukong's shoulders. He took hold of the body, swung it down upon a roadside boulder, and smashed it so completely that it could never again be mistaken for an injured child. He tore the limbs apart as well.

Xuanzang was horrified. He had commanded a rescue, and the disciple he mistrusted had answered with terrible violence. Wukong insisted that the life had already left the body, that there had never been an ordinary boy for him to murder. His master could see only the wreckage.

In the sky, the spirit watched what had happened to its discarded shape. The monkey had correctly judged the deception; the spirit had escaped his blow. Its anger turned into a new plan.

A wind came down the mountain.

It tore branches from the trees and lifted stones from the path. Dust shut out the sun. The horse screamed and bucked. Bajie threw himself behind a rock; Sha Wujing crouched low over the luggage. Even Wukong had to narrow his eyes against the sand. When he sprang toward Xuanzang, the monk was gone.

The storm passed as quickly as it had risen. Light returned to the road. The white horse stood trembling beside the discarded baggage. Bajie emerged from his hiding place and congratulated himself on surviving a dreadful wind. Sha Wujing looked about for their master.

No one could find him.

Wukong said the demon child had raised the storm and seized Xuanzang. He had warned them. He had tried to steer them around the danger. His master had insisted on taking the child's side, and now they faced another rescue that might have been avoided.

'Perhaps this is where we part company,' Wukong said. 'You each go your own way.'

Bajie seized the suggestion eagerly. They had been going west for years, and every valley contained another creature eager to eat the monk. If the leader was lost, why not end the expedition? Surely there was food somewhere east of here.

Sha Wujing stared at them. Had they forgotten why they were traveling? They had made vows before Guanyin. They had accepted their names and their duties. For them, protecting Xuanzang was not an arrangement that lasted only while the road was easy. If they abandoned him now, they abandoned the chance of correcting their own former lives.

His reproach struck home. Wukong admitted that his anger had made him speak rashly. Bajie, hearing the monkey change his mind, immediately declared that his own wish to go home had been a passing joke. They would remain together. They would find their master.

They took up the bags, steadied the horse, and climbed toward the place from which the red cloud had risen.

Wukong summoned the mountain and land spirits and demanded an account of the resident demon. The gods appeared frightened and worn down. The creature had made them into servants. They gathered wood, watched the roads, and supplied whatever the cave required; they could do little to resist its magic.

The monster was known as Red Boy, the Holy Infant King. He lived at Fire Cloud Cave beside Withered Pine Ravine on Mount Hao. His mother was the Rakshasi woman, and his father was the mighty Bull Demon King.

At this last name Wukong grew cheerful. Long before his imprisonment beneath Five Elements Mountain, when he had wandered through the world making alliances, he had sworn brotherhood with six other demon kings. The Bull Demon King had been the eldest among them. Wukong had been the youngest. If Red Boy was his old companion's son, then he was, by the conventions of sworn kinship, Wukong's nephew.

'At last,' said the monkey, 'we have good news. The boy is family. A nephew cannot very well eat his uncle's master.'

Bajie was skeptical of this sudden rescue through genealogy. Sha Wujing was more pointed. A friendship left untended for five hundred years was not the same thing as a family that knew your face. Wukong had not visited his sworn brother, sent gifts, or shared a meal with him in all that time. What reason had the son to recognize an uncle he had never met?

Wukong refused to be discouraged. A floating leaf might be separated from another by the whole breadth of the ocean and still meet it again. He expected Red Boy to acknowledge their connection, or at least to return Xuanzang unharmed when he learned who had come to claim him.

After a long search the disciples found the ravine, its running stream, the stone bridge, and the dark entrance to a cave among the cliffs. Wukong ordered Sha Wujing to guard the baggage and horse beneath the pines. Bajie would go with him.

The two brothers crossed the bridge with staff and rake in hand. From beyond the cave mouth came the sounds of a household preparing a feast.

Wukong was about to ask his supposed nephew for a family favor. Inside the cave, Xuanzang was already bound and waiting to be cooked.
