# Guanyin Revealed and Xuanzang’s Oath

The monastery was crowded with monks and officials when the interruption came. Xuanzang sat on the teaching platform, reciting texts and explaining their meaning to the assembly. The emperor had arranged the ceremony to relieve the suffering of the dead. Every detail was dignified: the prepared hall, the ranked officials, the incense, the sound of chants, the visible authority of the selected religious teacher.

Two shabby wanderers stood among the listeners. One stepped forward and called out. Xuanzang was teaching a limited doctrine, the stranger said. Did he know the greater teaching? Could he explain the scriptures that truly delivered souls from suffering?

The interruption was startling. Courtiers did not come to a royal ceremony to hear a ragged monk criticize its leader. Yet Xuanzang did not respond with anger. He came down from the platform and bowed to the stranger, asking what instruction he might receive. He knew the texts he had studied, he said, but if there were deeper teachings, he wished to hear of them. He would not defend his own reputation at the expense of learning.

The monk told him of a collection in the far west, at the Great Thunderclap Monastery of the Buddha. These were the great Mahayana scriptures, the teachings by which living people might learn and wandering souls might be relieved. Xuanzang listened with growing interest. He wanted to know where such writings could be found and how they might be brought to the Tang lands.

The officials, however, heard disturbance. Word went to the emperor that wandering monks had interrupted the ceremony. They were brought before Taizong. The emperor recognized the shabby visitors as the people who had offered the embroidered robe and staff. He asked why they had returned only to disrupt Xuanzang's teaching. Guanyin, still under the poor monk's appearance, repeated the claim. The present recitations could not perform the greater work the emperor hoped for. The scriptures of the west were needed.

Taizong asked the crucial question. Where were those scriptures, and who could obtain them? The wandering monk named the western region and the Buddha's monastery, so far from Chang'an that the journey was spoken of as one hundred and eight thousand li. The emperor asked the stranger to demonstrate the teaching. For an instant Guanyin's disguise had served its purpose: the court had considered the proposal seriously enough to request proof.

Then she rose from the platform into the air. The shabby monk's appearance fell away. Before the astonished crowd stood Guanyin in her radiant form, holding her vessel and willow branch, with Hui'an beside her. The people saw that the rebuke had not come from an ordinary passerby. Ministers, monks, and common citizens bowed. Taizong himself watched as the bodhisattva ascended and a message descended from the sky, confirming that the scriptures of the west could be sought by someone willing to make the journey.

The emperor ordered the ceremony suspended while the question was settled. He turned to those gathered and asked who would take on the task. No one rushed forward. The distance was immense, the route unknown, and the stories of the western road were full of peril. To seek the scriptures meant abandoning the safety of a monastery and the honors of the capital. A person might travel for years and never return.

Xuanzang stepped forward. He did not claim to know how he would cross the deserts or where the monster-filled roads would lead. He said that if the undertaking might benefit the people of the Tang realm and the souls who suffered, he would accept it. The emperor was delighted. Here was the man he had already found worthy to lead the rite, now willing to take the work beyond the walls of the capital.

Taizong offered him an extraordinary honor. Xuanzang would be accepted as the emperor's sworn brother, the royal younger brother who could carry the authority of the Tang while seeking the scriptures. They went before the Buddha's image and made their vows. Xuanzang promised not to return empty-handed. If the true scriptures could not be obtained, he would rather perish upon the road than pretend the journey had succeeded. The emperor arranged for the necessary papers and preparations for departure.

For the first time, the mission that had begun at Vulture Peak had a human traveler. Guanyin had found him in Chang'an, tested his humility by interrupting his public teaching, and placed the robe and staff into his hands. The emperor's fear of the dead had created the ceremony; the ceremony had brought Xuanzang before the court; and the question of how to deliver souls had turned a monk's learning into a journey.

Xuanzang's own life had prepared him in another way. He had been a child without a secure home, rescued by a monk, raised among the scriptures, and reunited with a family marked by violence. He knew that the world could be cruel, even when the forms of government and ceremony appeared orderly. He also knew what a stranger's mercy could do. A man who had once floated helplessly down a river would now travel by choice toward dangers he could scarcely imagine.

He did not know all the companions already waiting for him. At the River of Flowing Sands, a former heavenly general had accepted a new name. At Mount Fuling a pig-faced warrior had set aside his old habits for the promise of a future. A condemned dragon was waiting to become a horse. Beneath the Mountain of Five Elements, Wukong counted the years until a scripture seeker might release him.

Those preparations were separated from one another by hundreds of miles and by histories of sin, punishment, violence, and hope. Xuanzang carried no knowledge of their full stories. He had accepted the mission because he believed the scriptures mattered. Before he could approach the Buddha's monastery, he would have to learn to live and work among beings very different from the monks of Chang'an.

The Tang emperor had sworn a bond with him. The capital was preparing its farewell. The staff's nine rings would sound on the road, and the embroidered robe would be carried far beyond the court where it had first astonished the officials. The pilgrim had been chosen. Now there was only the western road, with everything that road concealed.
