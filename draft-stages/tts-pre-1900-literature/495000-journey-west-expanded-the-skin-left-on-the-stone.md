# The Skin Left on the Stone

With Wukong's staff coming toward him and Bajie's rake rising from below, Tiger Vanguard began to lose the fight. He gave way, stumbled over the broken ground, and fled downhill. Both disciples pursued. Neither intended to let a creature who hunted travelers escape into the mountain and ambush someone else.

Then the demon changed shape. He rolled across the earth and appeared as a great tiger again. Stripes flashed among the rocks. Wukong and Bajie chased the animal, making their way toward a narrow place where a boulder rested beside the path. The tiger turned, seemed to crouch upon it, and suddenly stopped moving.

Wukong struck. The iron staff hit something so hard that the shock ran back into his hands. Bajie brought down the rake and felt its teeth jar against stone. They looked closer. A tiger skin had been spread over the boulder. There was no living creature beneath it.

Wukong recognized the device. Tiger Vanguard had used the skin as a substitute for himself. He had shed his appearance to leave the pursuers a false quarry. While the brothers were spending their strength on the empty hide, the real demon had slipped away in a rush of wind.

And Xuanzang had been left alone.

The disciples raced uphill. They found the white horse, the luggage, and the place where their master had been sitting. The monk was gone. Bajie's anger dissolved into alarm. Where would they search? Which of the many folds in the mountain had swallowed him?

Wukong refused to let panic settle into despair. Xuanzang had vanished in the space of a few moments. It was unlikely that Tiger Vanguard had carried him beyond the entire mountain. They would find the lair, demand his release, and fight whatever stood between them and the monk. He told Bajie to bring the horse and bags and stay close.

In the same hours that the disciples searched the ridges, the captive was being borne toward a cave. Tiger Vanguard had returned to the road on the wind, found Xuanzang reciting the sutra, seized him, and carried him away. The monk had scarcely seen the danger arrive. One moment his companions were shouting somewhere below; the next the ground had disappeared beneath him.

At the entrance to a guarded cavern the demon resumed his ordinary monstrous form and instructed the sentries to announce that Tiger Vanguard had brought a prisoner. The cave belonged to Yellow Wind King. Its entrance was cut into rock, and behind it servants, warriors, and lesser animals moved under the command of the cave's ruler.

Tiger Vanguard presented Xuanzang with the satisfaction of a soldier who had fulfilled his orders. The monk was not simply any traveler, he explained. He was the religious brother of the Tang emperor, bound for the western scriptures. His disciples included a long-snouted warrior with a rake and the famous monkey named Sun Wukong. Tiger Vanguard had deceived them both. Here was the prize of his patrol.

Yellow Wind King was startled rather than delighted. Sun Wukong was not a name to be dismissed. News of that little monkey's impossible strength had traveled far beyond Flower-Fruit Mountain. The king wanted to know how his officer had managed to bring Xuanzang in while such a guardian stood beside him. Tiger Vanguard described his struggle, his feigned escape, and the trick with the skin.

The subordinate urged that the prisoner be cooked at once. Yellow Wind King refused. He had not captured Xuanzang himself, and he feared the noise that Wukong and Bajie would make if they reached the cave. Better to wait several days, he said, until the disciples gave up or departed. Then they could prepare the monk without interruption.

Orders were given. Attendants bound Xuanzang to a post in the rear garden, a place named for calming the wind. The ropes tightened around his body. There was no meal, no welcome, and no sign that he could persuade these captors to let him continue his journey. The only reason he had not yet been eaten was that the king feared the men outside.

Xuanzang called helplessly for his disciples. He remembered how readily Wukong fought, how much noise Bajie made, and how weak he himself had been when the tiger first appeared. He did not know that the two of them were moving through the mountain in search of him. He knew only that the next days would decide whether his pilgrimage ended in a demon's kitchen.

At last Wukong and Bajie came upon a cavern beneath the cliffs. It was set among trees and wild growth, too fortified and too full of strange activity to be the dwelling of ordinary shepherds. Wukong studied its entrance. The two brothers had been tricked once by rushing after their prey. This time they would divide their work. Bajie would watch the horse and keep the baggage out of sight; Wukong would challenge the guards and learn whether their master was inside.

Bajie did not object. The same strength that had carried him through the fight would now be needed to hold a position, and he had begun to understand that guarding the luggage and the white horse could be as necessary as striking a blow.

Wukong left him in a sheltered hollow and walked to the entrance with his iron staff. He had no intention of being fooled by a loose skin a second time.
