# Middlemarch — Three Love Problems

When Peter Featherstone dies, the people who have anticipated his money come to his funeral as though a calculation were nearing its conclusion. Instead the will unsettles them. Fred Vincy does not receive the estate on which he has counted. Stone Court goes to Joshua Rigg, an unexpected heir. This arrangement will have consequences beyond Fred, because Rigg's stepfather John Raffles brings a history that another influential Middlemarch man would rather keep buried.

Fred's disappointment is severe, but the novel does not ask us to mourn the loss of an unearned fortune as though it were an injustice. A path he should have abandoned is now closed to him. Mary Garth remains steady in her refusal to marry a man without useful work. Fred will have to discover whether he loves her enough to change his way of living when there is no inheritance to remove the effort.

Lydgate and Rosamond become engaged and arrange to marry. Their courtship carries real warmth, but both treat marriage as confirmation of the future they already want. Rosamond imagines refined drawing rooms, visitors of consequence, and a doctor who spends money without the vulgarity of asking where it comes from. Lydgate imagines a peaceful domestic retreat that will accommodate his medical research and hospital labor. He is confident that his income and plans can simply adapt. They commit themselves without fully understanding each other's expectations.

A substantial house and furnishings begin to translate these assumptions into debt. Rosamond regards quality as necessary to dignity; Lydgate postpones the unpleasant conversation that might restrict their choices. Their failure is interpersonal, but it is also economic. Credit makes a life possible before the money exists to pay for it. In a town governed by visible respectability, the pressure to appear prosperous can be relentless.

Dorothea continues to negotiate a difficult marriage. Will Ladislaw is now increasingly connected with Mr. Brooke's reform-minded political activity. Brooke has acquired an interest in the Pioneer newspaper and is drawn into a campaign for Parliament. Will helps as an editor, bringing energy and a conviction that change may be possible. Brooke speaks the vocabulary of reform while remaining a careless manager of the estates under his control. The contrast is a public version of the gap Dorothea has seen in private life: principles praised in conversation may disappear when they require sustained work.

Casaubon is distressed that Will will live nearby. He fears the judgment of his young cousin and of his wife, though neither has acted as he imagines. He also believes his research is imperiled by illness and time. Dorothea's attempts to support him cannot fully dislodge the suspicion that his authority is diminishing. Will's regard for her grows, and the situation becomes painful precisely because no one has announced an affair. A reader can see attachments forming while the participants remain constrained by loyalty, pride, and social convention.

The new medical institution remains a site of rivalry. Lydgate wants to modernize diagnosis and treatment but cannot live outside the town's systems of money and patronage. Bulstrode supports the hospital, and that support makes the banker more powerful. His public language of religious duty conceals a private history that may become known through Raffles. We do not yet have all the evidence, but the story is teaching us to notice how financial dependency can make apparent integrity vulnerable.

The title Three Love Problems invites several answers. Fred's problem is how to make affection compatible with responsibility. Lydgate and Rosamond's is how to reconcile intimacy with the practical facts of life. Dorothea's is how to be loyal to someone whose demands are constricting without surrendering her capacity for independent judgment. Eliot refuses to make love a universal cure. Affection can motivate people to change; it can also provide a pleasing excuse not to examine what change would cost.

The later tragedy in Middlemarch will grow partly from small decisions made here: a loan, a house, a newspaper, a conversation withheld, and an inheritance imagined. Nothing has the theatrical finality of a murder or a battle. Yet the future is already being made.
