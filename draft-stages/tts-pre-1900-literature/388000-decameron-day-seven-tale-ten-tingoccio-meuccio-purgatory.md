# The Decameron — A Message from the Dead

Two men in Siena, Tingoccio Mini and Meuccio di Tura, are close friends with a shared curiosity about what follows death. No one can return from the grave to give an ordinary account, so they make a pact. Whichever dies first will come back, if it is possible, and tell the survivor about the other world. It is the kind of promise that sounds solemn until the practical question arises: what information would these friends most like to know?

Tingoccio becomes intimate with the mother of his godchild. The spiritual relationship matters. A godparent is joined to the child's family by a religious bond, and sexual contact across that connection is treated as an offense in the moral system the men have learned. The same relation was invoked by Fra Rinaldo earlier in the day, when he used his status as godfather to gain access to a married woman. Tingoccio's affair gives the pact a particular point of inquiry.

Eventually Tingoccio dies. One night, when Meuccio is alone, a voice comes to him, and the dead friend appears. Tingoccio says he is in purgatory and describes various punishments. The apparent miracle is narrated without offering independent proof; it is the premise of Dioneo's fiction. Meuccio listens, asks questions and finally reaches the subject that troubles him. Does Tingoccio suffer a special punishment for having slept with the mother of his godchild?

No, Tingoccio answers; in the place where he now finds himself, such relations do not count for the extra punishment Meuccio imagines. Meuccio is at once astonished and regretful. All the opportunities he has refused from fear of this supposed sin now seem, by the report of a ghost, to have been lost without necessity. The moral and metaphysical question of the afterlife has been converted into a calculation about missed sexual occasions.

Dioneo ends the seventh day with a joke that breaks its apparent rules. The protagonists are not a wife tricking her husband, and their most important deception is not enacted in a household room. Instead, a dead man communicates what the living cannot verify. The device returns to religious authority, which appeared in the first tale as a prayer that was actually a coded instruction. At the end, the claim is even more difficult to test. It arrives from beyond death.

The story should not be mistaken for a report of what medieval Christian theology established about spiritual kinship. It is a bawdy fictional argument built around a supernatural messenger. Tingoccio's testimony is authoritative to Meuccio because Meuccio wants it to be. Desire, which prompted the question, also shapes the answer's reception. Dioneo ends with a laugh, but the structure is exact: a visitor from the other world offers the precise exemption that an eager friend would most like to believe.
