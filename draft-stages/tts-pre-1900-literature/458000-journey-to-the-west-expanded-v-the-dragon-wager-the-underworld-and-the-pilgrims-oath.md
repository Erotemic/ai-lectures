# Journey to the West expanded V: the dragon wager, the underworld, and the pilgrim’s oath

## Journey to the West — The Fisherman, the Woodcutter, and the Fortune Teller

Outside Chang'an, near the Jing River, a fisherman and a woodcutter walked home together. They had sold their day's goods in the city and shared a little wine. The fisherman was Zhang Shao. The woodcutter was Li Ding. They were friendly men, pleased with their work and with the chance to argue over which of them led the more fortunate life.

Li Ding loved the mountains. He spoke of trees, quiet paths, mountain winds, and the freedom to cut wood far from the duties that occupied important men. Zhang Shao loved the water. He spoke of his small boat, the fish that filled his nets, and the pleasure of returning to the river each morning. Neither could bear to let the other win. They exchanged verses about their trades and the changing seasons, praising the gifts of mountain and stream. Their poems were not the weapons of a serious quarrel. They were the entertainment of two friends who had time to talk.

At last they reached the place where their roads separated. Zhang Shao warned his friend to beware of tigers in the mountains. Li Ding took offense and replied that storms could overturn a boat as easily as beasts could seize a woodcutter. Zhang laughed. He said he had a way to keep ahead of the dangers of his trade. Each day in Chang'an he visited a diviner named Yuan Shoucheng, gave him a golden carp, and received a prediction about where he should cast his nets. The predictions had been so accurate that the fisherman could fill his basket almost without failure.

The friends said farewell and went their ways. Their conversation had been playful. But it had been overheard.

A water spirit serving the Dragon King of the Jing River had listened from beneath the bank. He hurried to the underwater palace and told the king that a diviner in Chang'an was directing fishermen precisely where the river's creatures could be found. The spirit worried that if the man could name every favorable pool and current, the fish under the Dragon King's protection would soon be taken. The king was furious. A human fortune teller, trading predictions for carp, was interfering with his dominion.

The Dragon King reached for his sword and prepared to go up to Chang'an and destroy the diviner. His advisers restrained him. If he rose in his true form, with rain and clouds around him, the city would be terrified and the heavenly court might hold him responsible. Better to transform himself into an educated gentleman and investigate. If the diviner was indeed a fraud, the king could humiliate him. If not, he would at least know what he faced.

He changed into a scholar in pale clothing and went to the western street where Yuan Shoucheng received visitors. The fortune teller sat quietly with his writing materials and implements of divination. The stranger asked a simple question: would it rain tomorrow? Yuan said yes. At what hour would the clouds gather? When would thunder sound? When would the rain begin, and how much would fall? Yuan gave a precise schedule and a precise depth. The Dragon King listened with growing amusement. He governed the river and its rain. What human could dictate such things to him?

The disguised dragon offered a wager. If the prediction proved accurate, he would bring fifty pieces of silver. If it proved false, he would break the diviner's sign and drive him from the city. Yuan accepted without fear. The visitor left, returned to the river, and told his household that the matter was settled. They laughed together. The master of the river, surely, could choose how much rain to give.

While they were laughing, a messenger came down from Heaven bearing an imperial decree. The Dragon King bowed and opened it. Tomorrow he was ordered to provide rain to Chang'an. The decree specified the very hours and the very quantity that Yuan had just named.

The Dragon King's pleasure vanished. He had made a wager against a man whose prediction appeared to be known in Heaven itself. To honor the Jade Emperor's decree would be to lose his wager. To win the wager would require him to alter what Heaven had commanded. His advisers offered the kind of cunning that wins a small argument at the cost of something larger. Change the starting time of the storm by one hour, they suggested. Shorten the rainfall by a small amount. Then Yuan's prediction would be wrong and the king could smash his sign.

The Dragon King listened. He knew the decree had come from the Jade Emperor. He knew it was not a suggestion. But he did not wish to kneel before a human fortune teller, and the court of fish and crabs around him was watching. The risk began to seem tolerable. He decided to adjust the rain.

The next day he ordered the wind, thunder, clouds, and lightning into position. They obeyed him, as they always had. But he did not let them act at the commanded hour, and he did not let the promised quantity fall. The city received its rain; Yuan's exact prediction was wrong. The king had won the wager in the narrowest possible sense.

He dressed again as a scholar and strode into Yuan Shoucheng's room. Without waiting for explanation, he threw down the sign, scattered brushes, and shattered the instruments. He shouted that the fortune teller was a liar. He had predicted rain that did not fall according to his schedule. The diviner ought to leave Chang'an and stop deceiving its people.

Yuan remained calm. He told the visitor that he knew who stood before him. This was no ordinary scholar. It was the Dragon King of the Jing River, who had altered a decree of the Jade Emperor to win a wager. The error in the prediction did not prove that Yuan lacked knowledge. It proved that the dragon had broken the law of Heaven.

The Dragon King dropped his arrogance. The punishment for such an offense, Yuan said, would be execution. The king knelt and asked for help. He had wanted to destroy a fortune teller's reputation. Now he needed that same man to tell him how to live.

Yuan said he could not cancel the heavenly order. But the executioner appointed for the following day was a human official, the Tang minister Wei Zheng. If the Dragon King could persuade the emperor to keep Wei Zheng from carrying out his assigned duty, perhaps he might escape. That night, the Dragon King would have to seek the mercy of the ruler of Chang'an.

The river creature left the broken room and returned beneath the water. Over a dispute that began with two friends praising their work, an imperial death sentence had been set in motion. The road to the scriptures was now winding through the smallest of human vanities: a wager made because someone could not endure being proved wrong.

## Journey to the West — The Minister Who Slept and the Ghost at the Gate

That night the Tang emperor Taizong dreamed of a dragon. The creature bowed before him and begged for his life. He had broken a heavenly command and was to be executed at the following day's appointed hour by the emperor's own minister Wei Zheng. If the emperor could keep Wei Zheng occupied, the dragon said, he would be saved. Taizong was astonished. A ruler of human beings was accustomed to petitions about land, taxes, offices, and criminal judgments, not requests from dragons. But the petitioner was terrified, and the emperor promised to help.

When morning came, Taizong told his courtiers about the dream. Wei Zheng was not among them. An adviser suggested summoning him and keeping him at court for the whole period during which the execution might take place. The emperor agreed. Wei Zheng received the summons and could not refuse, although he already knew from a heavenly command that he had another duty at precisely that hour. He had prepared himself for it and feared the consequences of neglecting either his earthly sovereign or the Jade Emperor.

Taizong welcomed him without mentioning the dragon's petition. They discussed affairs of government until the hour drew near. Then the emperor proposed a game of go, the board game of territories, encirclement, and patient calculation. He wanted his minister in sight, his hands engaged with the black and white stones, his attention occupied until the dangerous hour had passed.

The pieces clicked onto the board. One player extended a position; the other defended a corner. It was a fine game and an absorbing one. But as the appointed moment approached, Wei Zheng grew drowsy. He had been working without rest, and the strain overcame him. His head lowered over the table. He fell asleep in the emperor's presence.

Taizong saw the minister sleeping and was amused by his weariness. The emperor did not want to wake him. A man who had labored so hard in the service of the state should be allowed a brief rest. Besides, what danger was there in a man sleeping beside his sovereign? He was not leaving the room. He held no sword. The dragon would surely be safe.

Before long Wei Zheng woke and apologized for falling asleep. The emperor forgave him and was ready to resume the game. Then a disturbance sounded outside. Officers brought a severed dragon's head, bloody and immense. It had fallen from the sky near the street. No one understood how such a thing could happen. Taizong turned to Wei Zheng.

The minister told him what had occurred during sleep. His spirit had gone to the heavenly execution ground, where the Dragon King waited bound. Wei Zheng had carried out the sentence given by the Jade Emperor and cut off the dragon's head. His body had remained beside the go board throughout. Taizong realized with horror that his scheme had failed through the one circumstance he had interpreted as harmless. A minister did not need to leave the room in order to act for Heaven.

The emperor had not meant to betray the dragon. He had given his word and taken what seemed an effective precaution. But promises did not depend entirely on intention. The dragon was dead, and he had reason to think the ruler's promise had not been kept.

That night the dragon returned as a ghost. He carried his own severed head and cried out at the palace, demanding justice from Taizong. He accused the emperor of promising to save him and then permitting Wei Zheng to strike. Taizong struggled in terror, unable to explain away what had happened. A figure came from the south bearing a willow branch, and the ghost withdrew. It was Guanyin, who had been staying in Chang'an while searching for the future scripture pilgrim. Her intervention drove the immediate danger away, but it did not heal the emperor's fear.

The palace became a place of noises after dark. The king who had faced armies in battle now feared the sound of a door. Courtiers summoned physicians, and ministers tried to reassure him. He could speak of conquering territories and protecting his people, but he could not sleep through the accusations that rose from the unseen world.

Two commanders, Qin Shubao and Yuchi Jingde, offered to stand guard. They dressed in their armor, took up their weapons, and stationed themselves at the palace gates. In their presence the disturbances ceased. For several nights the emperor slept more peacefully, and he thanked them. Yet watching through the night was exhausting. He did not wish to impose the duty upon trusted generals indefinitely.

The court had portraits made of the two commanders in their armor and placed the paintings on the doors. These images continued the protective work. The novel pauses over this extraordinary origin of familiar door guardians: two human warriors, first standing through the night with their weapons, are transformed into painted figures whose faces can repel what a living monarch cannot see. When another entrance caused trouble, Wei Zheng was assigned to watch it with the sword he had used in the dragon's execution.

Even with the gates guarded, Taizong became weaker. The physicians could offer little hope. His body wasted while the court made preparations for the possibility of his death. At last Wei Zheng brought a final proposal. He had a letter for an old acquaintance who now served in the underworld as a judge, Cui Jue. If the emperor took it with him when he died, perhaps the judge would help him return.

The emperor accepted the letter. He had intended to save the Dragon King's life by keeping an official at a game. Now he himself would depend on an official's favor in another jurisdiction. He put Wei Zheng's message away, closed his eyes, and died. The palace mourned. Beyond the world of soldiers and ministers, the emperor's spirit was already moving toward gates where his own authority would need to be recognized anew.

## Journey to the West — Taizong Before the Ten Kings of the Dead

Taizong was dead in the palace, but the story did not leave him there. His spirit found itself outside the world he had ruled. At first it seemed that he was still surrounded by soldiers on an imperial excursion. Then they vanished, and the landscape became strange and empty. He did not know where he was going. A figure called to him from a distance, addressing him as the emperor and asking him to come closer.

The stranger was Cui Jue, the judge to whom Wei Zheng had written. He wore the clothing of an official, carrying the assurance of someone who understood the records and procedures of the underworld. Taizong produced the letter from his minister. Cui read it and welcomed him warmly. He knew Wei Zheng, and he promised to help the emperor return to life if the laws and ledgers of the dead allowed it.

They went together toward the city of ghosts. At its gate Taizong saw figures he had not expected to meet again: his father Li Yuan and his brothers Li Jiancheng and Li Yuanji. The brothers came toward him demanding what had been taken from them. In the human world, Taizong's power had survived the violent struggle that made him emperor. In the underworld, the dead remembered it differently. They seized at him, and he could not simply command them to stand aside. Cui called upon an attendant to drive them back, and only then could Taizong continue.

Past the gate stood halls and offices of astonishing grandeur. The underworld was not a wilderness where ghosts wandered without order. It had officials, petitions, clerks, summonses, registers, and judges. The king of the living world was being led into another government. Before its throne stood the Ten Kings of the Dead, each responsible for a portion of the vast administration of souls.

They received Taizong with elaborate courtesy. He was a king among human beings, and they were kings within their own realm. Yet the formal welcome did not mean that he could decide his own case. The dead Dragon King had brought a complaint against him. Why, asked the judges, had Taizong promised rescue and allowed the execution to proceed?

The emperor explained everything. The Dragon King had come in a dream. Taizong had promised help. He had summoned Wei Zheng, kept him in attendance, and engaged him in a game of go. Wei Zheng had not left the room. The execution had nevertheless occurred while the minister slept. The emperor had tried to keep his word and had failed to understand that the appointed executioner could act through his spirit.

The Ten Kings were satisfied with the account. They already knew the Dragon King's allotted death had been recorded in the heavens. The dragon's complaint had required Taizong's appearance for a formal hearing, but it did not make him the murderer. The matter was closed. Still, a legal question remained. The emperor was dead. Was his time among the living truly finished?

The judges ordered the records consulted. Cui Jue went to examine the book that named the spans of mortal reigns. The entry for the Tang emperor appeared to allow only thirteen years, the very number of years Taizong had ruled. Cui recognized the danger. If this entry stood unchanged, the underworld officials would have no reason to release his guest.

So Cui performed an act that belonged as much to the world of clerks and documents as to the world of miracles. He took his brush and added strokes to the written number. Thirteen became thirty-three. The court saw the amended record and concluded that twenty years of earthly life remained to Taizong. The judges declared that he should return.

Taizong expressed his gratitude. Before he departed, he asked about the members of his household. The judges reassured him about most of them but hinted that a royal woman's life would soon be cut short. They also accepted his offer of a gift from the living world. Asked what fruit the underworld lacked, they answered that they had eastern and western melons but no southern melons. Taizong promised to send them the fruit they desired.

This was a strange scene for an emperor. A decision about his life rested upon an altered character in a ledger. He was invited to send produce as a token of thanks. The dead did not seem bound by the dignity that surrounded him in court; they treated him as a guest, a petitioner, and a debtor, sometimes all at once.

Cui Jue and another underworld official were assigned to take him back. But the route home did not pass directly from the court to the palace. The judge would show him what the emperor's former wars and the living world's misconduct had left behind. There were more gates to cross, and not all the people waiting beyond them would bow at the sight of imperial robes.

## Journey to the West — The Hells, the City of Wrongful Death, and the Borrowed Money

Cui Jue led the Tang emperor away from the halls of the Ten Kings. Taizong had won permission to return to life, but the way back was not a quiet corridor. It passed through regions where the dead were gathered according to their deeds, their griefs, and their unfinished business. The emperor could no longer rely on the courtesies of a royal audience. He had to see what lay beside the road.

First came the dark country around the mountains of the underworld. From one direction rose wailing; from another came the clamor of punishments. The judge explained that the realms before them were places of consequence. People who had lied, stolen, tormented others, exploited the poor, or slaughtered living beings were made to endure suffering fitted to their conduct. Taizong saw the eighteen hells, with their instruments of violence, their terrible wardens, and their desperate inhabitants.

The names passed before him like places on a route he would never choose: prisons of fire, cold, grinding, knives, and cruel devices. He heard the cries of those whose lives had ended but whose wrongdoing had not been forgotten. The judge did not ask him to settle their cases. The message of the journey was to remember what he had seen and govern differently when he returned.

The path then reached the bridges associated with passage and rebirth. A golden bridge carried those of high spiritual achievement; a silver one served upright and worthy people. Another bridge stretched above a dreadful river. The water beneath it was dark and violent, and spirits struggled amid the current while fearsome guards watched. This was the bridge over the Nai River, the place where the cost of crossing could no longer be paid by rank or familiar privilege.

Taizong looked from one passage to another and asked where each led. Cui answered patiently. The emperor tried to absorb the details, but the sights were overwhelming. He had fought battles and ordered punishments in life; now he walked through an administration that remembered every injury and judged people by standards of its own.

Then came the city of wrongful death. Here the dead were not silent. Many had fallen in war, slaughter, and political violence. They recognized Taizong, or thought they recognized the cause of their suffering. As he approached, crowds moved toward him, crying for their lives back. They named him and called upon him to answer. Some were maimed, some carried the marks of wounds, and others reached from the crowd with the blind urgency of hunger.

Taizong recoiled. Here were lives that had disappeared inside proclamations of victory and accounts of campaigns. They did not praise the emperor who had secured a realm. They saw the man under whose armies they had died. He called out to Cui for protection. The judge said the spirits had no means to support themselves and no religious ceremonies to release them. They could not be driven away by imperial commands. Taizong would have to provide them with money and promise to perform rites for them in the world above.

The emperor had arrived in the underworld with nothing. He possessed neither treasury nor purse. Cui explained that someone living in the city of Kaifeng had credit in the realm of the dead: a man named Xiang Liang, who through offerings and good works had accumulated large deposits there. If Taizong would sign a document of borrowing, the judge could arrange payment from that account. The emperor agreed and gave his name to the debt.

The money was distributed. The hungry spirits took what they were given, and the crowd opened enough for Taizong to pass. Cui instructed him to remember the promise. He was to hold a great ceremony upon his return, with prayers and offerings for those who had died without care. The ritual would address the suffering that an ordinary court order could not reach. Taizong promised again. This promise, like the one made to the dragon, would matter beyond the moment when it was spoken.

Farther on he saw the paths of rebirth. Different classes of beings moved toward their appointed destinations. Some souls might pass into fortunate lives; others descended into harder conditions. There were humans, animals, spirits, and many kinds of travelers gathered for their next passage. Taizong watched their movement and was told to carry the lesson home.

At last Cui Jue took leave of him. Another underworld guide led the emperor toward the border of the living world. A saddled horse waited. They rode until they reached the Wei River, where bright fish moved in the water. Taizong paused, delighted by the sight after the misery he had seen. He lingered. The guide urged him onward, but the emperor would not move quickly enough. At last the guide pushed him from the horse into the river.

Taizong cried out as he fell. Water seemed to close over him. He felt the shock of drowning, the loss of balance, the rush of panic. Then the place around him changed. He was no longer beside an underworld river. He was in the body that had been laid out in the palace.

The court officials had been mourning for three days. Some wanted to proclaim a successor, while Wei Zheng insisted that they wait a little longer. They had begun to speak of the dead emperor in the past tense when a cry burst from the coffin. The voice shouted that someone was drowning him.

The mourners fled in terror. Only a few ministers kept their courage and approached the coffin. They opened it. Taizong was sitting up, dazed, still protesting about the river and the man who had pushed him from the horse. He had returned to life. He could once again speak to the people he ruled. And, as he would soon discover, he had brought back several obligations that neither a sword nor a royal seal could discharge.

## Journey to the West — The Emperor’s Return and the Messenger with Melons

When Taizong spoke from inside his coffin, the palace did not immediately recover its composure. The emperor's attendants had prepared for a funeral. The officers had argued over succession. The court women had put on mourning garments. Now the man they were ready to bury was sitting upright, describing a river, a horse, and the sudden shove that had brought him back to his body. The ministers steadied him and summoned physicians. After food, rest, and medicines, the emperor regained enough strength to tell the story.

He described the city of the dead, the officials of the Ten Kings, the complaint of the Dragon King, the intervention of Cui Jue, and the register that allowed him twenty more years to live. He described the halls of punishment, the bridge above the dark river, and the crowd of victims in the city of wrongful death. His ministers heard how a ruler might have honors in one world yet stand helpless among those killed in the wars that founded his kingdom.

When he had recovered, Taizong began to act upon the promises he had made. He issued proclamations urging mercy and good works. The story says that he released a great number of condemned prisoners so that they might return home before appearing again for their appointed punishment, and arranged for thousands of women in the palace to leave its service. The court's proclamations imagined a kingdom in which acts of generosity might lighten the sufferings that filled the underworld.

Yet declarations were not enough. Taizong had promised the Ten Kings a gift of southern melons, and he had borrowed a fortune in the underworld from a man he had never met. Both debts still waited. He sent an officer to find Xiang Liang in Kaifeng and deliver the money corresponding to what had been borrowed. He also asked for someone who would carry melons into the land of the dead.

A man named Liu Quan volunteered for the second task. The circumstances of his life were painful. His wife Li Cuilian had given food to a monk in charity, and Liu Quan had berated her for acting without his approval. In distress she had died by suicide, leaving young children. Liu Quan could not endure the household after her death. He offered himself for the embassy into the underworld, knowing that the undertaking required him to die and carry the fruit as a spirit.

The court received his offer. He was given the melons and ritual instructions, and he took a fatal draught. His soul reached the gates of the dead with the promised offering. When a ghostly guard challenged him, Liu Quan answered that he was the emperor's messenger. The Ten Kings accepted the fruit and praised Taizong for keeping his word. They asked the messenger's name and reason for coming. He told them about his wife, his sorrow, and the children he had left.

The judges ordered the records of Liu Quan and Li Cuilian examined. They learned that the appointed span of their lives had not yet run its course. Liu Quan's journey, intended as a final offering, had brought him to the one realm where his lost wife could be found. They were reunited in the court of the dead and given permission to return to the living world together.

But another difficulty arose. Liu Quan's body had only recently died, and it could receive his spirit again. Li Cuilian had been dead longer; her body was gone. Where could her spirit live? The judges consulted the registers and determined that a woman of the Tang royal household, Li Yuying, was due to die. Her body could be used. The decision was carried out through the strange procedures of the underworld.

The story moves between tenderness and unease. Liu Quan and his wife find one another, but their return depends on taking over the body of a princess. The narrative treats this as a miraculous arrangement by the judges. It does not ask the living woman for her approval. The royal household will discover that a familiar body now speaks as someone else.

The emperor meanwhile awaited news, having sent one man to settle a monetary debt and another to carry fruit into a place where no ordinary courier could return. He had seen enough of the underworld to believe that government included obligations to the dead. He was learning that even in a tale of miracles, duties accumulated. A promise to one frightened dragon had started the chain of events. A promise to hungry spirits and the Ten Kings would now draw all of Chang'an into a solemn religious undertaking.

When Liu Quan returned, he would bring more than word that the melons had been delivered. He would bring a wife in a borrowed body, and a new question for the emperor about the reach of earthly authority. The city of the living was about to receive a story that was as strange as anything in Taizong's own account of death.

## Journey to the West — The Princess’s Borrowed Body and the Debt of Silver

Liu Quan returned to the Tang court alive. The emperor had sent him to the underworld with southern melons, and now the messenger stood before him giving a report. The Ten Kings had accepted the offering. They had recognized the emperor's good faith. And Liu Quan, who had gone down burdened by the death of his wife, had met her again and been told that both of them could return to the human world.

There was one part of the account the court could scarcely understand. The wife whom Liu Quan had met in the underworld had no body to return to. The judges had therefore assigned her spirit to the body of the emperor's sister, a princess named Li Yuying, whose life had reached its appointed end. The princess had fallen unconscious and then awakened speaking with the memories and manner of a different woman.

Taizong summoned the royal woman. She came before him with the conduct of someone who belonged to Liu Quan's household, not to the imperial family. Asked whether she knew her husband, she pointed to Liu Quan as naturally as if she had seen him that morning. Liu Quan recognized her words, her memories, and her account of their marriage. But the face before him was not the face of Li Cuilian. He hesitated. It was his wife, and yet it was the princess.

The emperor heard them both and accepted the underworld's arrangement. He sent the pair away with gifts from the princess's possessions, together with a decree relieving them of certain burdens. Husband and wife returned to their children. In the telling, the household is reunited. The miracle is not smooth if one thinks about the body whose owner has been displaced. The novel, however, moves its characters onward: what matters within the episode is that the underworld's promise has been executed and the messenger has returned with evidence of it.

The other debt now came due. Taizong had borrowed money in the city of wrongful death from an account held by a man named Xiang Liang. The emperor ordered a minister to find him and pay the obligation. In Kaifeng the official discovered not a wealthy creditor but a poor man who made a living selling water and pottery with his wife. He had no great stores of silver in his house. When the emperor's representative presented a large payment, Xiang Liang was terrified.

He insisted that he had never loaned the monarch money. To accept wealth that he had not earned would be a dangerous act. The official explained that the loan had been made in the realm of the dead, where Xiang Liang's small offerings to monks and spirits had accumulated as treasure. The poor man's generosity in the living world had made him rich in the underworld. The emperor had borrowed one treasury of that invisible wealth and wished to repay it in silver.

Xiang Liang and his wife refused. They considered the proposed gift far beyond anything they had given. No argument about an invisible ledger could make them comfortable taking the money. The minister reported their refusal, and Taizong found another way to fulfill his obligation: the funds could support religious construction and charitable purposes in the man's name. In this way the emperor honored the debt without forcing the reluctant couple to become rich by command.

Taizong had now heard two extraordinary reports. A spirit could return in another person's body, and offerings that appeared insignificant in the world above could possess value in the world below. More important, he remembered the crowd of spirits who had stopped his own passage near the city of wrongful death. He had promised them a great rite of deliverance. Unlike the melons, that promise could not be carried by one messenger. It needed the religious institutions of the capital, monks, temples, texts, ceremonies, and the participation of the government itself.

He ordered the work begun. Officials would choose monks with learning and discipline. The court would provide support. A great gathering would be organized to bring prayers and teaching to the souls of those who had died without care. The emperor who had previously feared a dragon at his door could now act without turning the next fear into another execution. He would call upon religious knowledge, not merely the force of his soldiers.

Among the monks of the Tang lands was Xuanzang, the River Child who had grown to manhood at Gold Mountain Monastery. The officials had not chosen him yet. He had his own history of loss, a rescue by the river, and a decision to remain in religious life after finding his family. None of that was known to the emperor as the shape of a future pilgrimage. The decree for the ceremony would bring him to the palace, and the search Guanyin had been conducting in secret was about to come to its end.

## Journey to the West — Xuanzang and the Great Water-and-Land Ceremony

The emperor's summons went out through the Buddhist communities of the Tang lands. Taizong wished to hold a great ceremony for the dead, especially the spirits he had seen crowding the city of wrongful death. The court put out notices, and monks traveled toward Chang'an from distant temples. Yet before the rite could begin, the emperor's choice raised a dispute in the very chamber where his officials were to approve it.

The official Fu Yi was asked to help select religious teachers. Instead he presented a memorial opposing the undertaking itself. Buddhist teaching, he argued, had come from the west. It encouraged men to leave their families and could weaken the duties that bound children to parents and subjects to rulers. What profit was there in invoking unseen rewards and punishments when a good ruler could govern the living? He asked the emperor not to lend his authority to the monks.

The memorial passed among the ministers. Xiao Yu, one of the emperor's senior officials, spoke in reply. Buddhism had long been practiced in the country, he said, and its teachings encouraged good conduct and restrained evil. The two men argued about filial duty, foreign origins, and what counted as a worthy teaching. When the emperor summoned further opinions, other officials defended the value of religious practice. Taizong accepted that advice. The story says he forbade renewed attacks on Buddhism and threatened those who slandered monks with the loss of an arm. A ceremony intended to relieve suffering would thus proceed under a decree enforced by imperial punishment.

The court then searched among the assembled monks for a leader whose learning was real, whose discipline was respected, and whose conduct would not dishonor the undertaking. They found a man who had grown up in a monastery, mastered the texts placed before him, and shown little interest in rank or riches.

His name was Chen Xuanzang. The emperor remembered the family. Xuanzang was the son of Chen Guangrui, the examination champion who had been murdered on the river and restored to life through the help of a Dragon King. The young monk was also the child saved on a plank by Faming. The same boy who had been sent away with a blood-written letter was now being considered to lead the capital's religious ceremony.

Taizong approved the choice and gave Xuanzang responsibility for the great gathering. Xuanzang accepted with formal humility and set about the work. The ceremonial site was prepared at Huasheng Monastery. Monks were assembled from far and wide. Places were arranged for teaching, ritual recitation, and offerings. The gathering was to run for forty-nine days, a period set out carefully in the novel rather than a single afternoon's prayer.

The capital filled with anticipation. Officials brought their gifts. Religious teachers took their places. The emperor and the people of the city came to see the ceremony begin. Xuanzang sat at the center of the gathering, not as a hero of battles but as a scholar and monk whose voice would guide others through the rites. The boy who had once known nothing about his family was now publicly trusted with the suffering of thousands of unknown dead.

Guanyin had been watching. Still disguised as a poor wandering monk and accompanied by Hui'an, she learned of the emperor's plans. She had wandered through the Tang lands searching for a seeker worthy of the scriptures. Now the name of the ceremony's leader reached her. She knew his spiritual origin, which lay deeper than his birth at the river, and recognized him as the person for whom she had been waiting.

She took the embroidered robe and nine-ringed staff given by the Buddha and carried them into the streets. Her appearance was shabby, but the robe shone with precious work. It drew attention immediately. An ordinary monk asked the price. Guanyin named a sum so high that he thought the seller mad: five thousand pieces of silver for the robe and two thousand for the staff. He laughed and went away.

The strange merchants continued until they encountered the minister Xiao Yu. He stopped to examine the robe and asked why it should cost so much. Guanyin answered in riddling terms: there were circumstances in which she would demand the price, and circumstances in which she would give the treasures away. Someone who despised the teachings and wanted to own a sacred robe for status alone would be made to pay. Someone who truly honored the purpose of the robe and could make proper use of it might receive it without payment.

Xiao Yu understood that the ragged stranger's poverty was deceptive. He told her of the emperor's ceremony and the virtuous monk chosen to lead it. Guanyin agreed to go with him to court. Before Taizong she repeated the offer. The emperor, pleased by both the splendor of the objects and the stranger's willingness to give them for a worthy purpose, accepted them for Xuanzang.

The monk was summoned. He put on the embroidered robe and held the ringed staff. The silk and jewels caught the light, and the staff's rings gave off a clear sound whenever he moved. Courtiers marveled. Taizong had him pass through the streets so the people could see the honors being shown to the leader of the rite. The former abandoned child became for a moment the most celebrated monk in the capital.

But the robe and staff had traveled from Vulture Peak with a purpose that neither the emperor nor Xuanzang yet knew. They were meant for the journey west. Guanyin had not sold them to a rich collector; she had placed them upon the person who would have to carry them through the country of demons, mountains, and rivers.

The ceremony continued. Xuanzang chanted and taught, and the emperor returned to listen. The multitude assembled because of the dead; the ritual was a response to an unseen world whose suffering had frightened its ruler. But the longer the proceedings continued, the more clearly a question emerged. Could the scriptures available in Chang'an accomplish everything the emperor hoped for? Were they enough to address the dead and transform the living, or was there another body of teaching to be sought far beyond the empire?

A shabby monk and his companion moved among the listeners. Their appearance offered no reason for the court to attend to them. Guanyin had waited for this moment since accepting her commission in the west. She had chosen the person, delivered the objects, and watched the great ceremony unfold. What remained was to interrupt the most solemn religious gathering in Chang'an and tell its leader that the teaching he was giving was not the whole of the path.

## Journey to the West — Guanyin Revealed and Xuanzang’s Oath

The monastery was crowded with monks and officials when the interruption came. Xuanzang sat on the teaching platform, reciting texts and explaining their meaning to the assembly. The emperor had arranged the ceremony to relieve the suffering of the dead. Every detail was dignified: the prepared hall, the ranked officials, the incense, the sound of chants, the visible authority of the selected religious teacher.

Two shabby wanderers stood among the listeners. One stepped forward and called out. Xuanzang was teaching a limited doctrine, the stranger said. Did he know the greater teaching? Could he explain the scriptures that truly delivered souls from suffering?

The interruption was startling. Courtiers did not come to a royal ceremony to hear a ragged monk criticize its leader. Yet Xuanzang did not respond with anger. He came down from the platform and bowed to the stranger, asking what instruction he might receive. He knew the texts he had studied, he said, but if there were deeper teachings, he wished to hear of them. He would not defend his own reputation at the expense of learning.

The monk told him of a collection in the far west, at the Great Thunderclap Monastery of the Buddha. These were the great Mahayana scriptures, the teachings by which living people might learn and wandering souls might be relieved. Xuanzang listened with growing interest. He wanted to know where such writings could be found and how they might be brought to the Tang lands.

The officials, however, heard disturbance. Word went to the emperor that wandering monks had interrupted the ceremony. They were brought before Taizong. The emperor recognized the shabby visitors as the people who had offered the embroidered robe and staff. He asked why they had returned only to disrupt Xuanzang's teaching. Guanyin, still under the poor monk's appearance, repeated the claim. The present recitations could not perform the greater work the emperor hoped for. The scriptures of the west were needed.

Taizong asked the crucial question. Where were those scriptures, and who could obtain them? The wandering monk named the western region and the Buddha's monastery, so far from Chang'an that the journey was spoken of as one hundred and eight thousand li. The emperor asked the stranger to demonstrate the teaching. For an instant Guanyin's disguise had served its purpose: the court had considered the proposal seriously enough to request proof.

Then she rose from the platform into the air. The shabby monk's appearance fell away. Before the astonished crowd stood Guanyin in her radiant form, holding her vessel and willow branch, with Hui'an beside her. The people saw that the rebuke had not come from an ordinary passerby. Ministers, monks, and common citizens bowed. Taizong himself watched as the bodhisattva ascended and a message descended from the sky, confirming that the scriptures of the west could be sought by someone willing to make the journey.

The emperor ordered the ceremony suspended while the question was settled. He turned to those gathered and asked who would take on the task. No one rushed forward. The distance was immense, the route unknown, and the stories of the western road were full of peril. To seek the scriptures meant abandoning the safety of a monastery and the honors of the capital. A person might travel for years and never return.

Xuanzang stepped forward. He did not claim to know how he would cross the deserts or where the monster-filled roads would lead. He said that if the undertaking might benefit the people of the Tang realm and the souls who suffered, he would accept it. The emperor was delighted. Here was the man he had already found worthy to lead the rite, now willing to take the work beyond the walls of the capital.

Taizong offered him an extraordinary honor. Xuanzang would be accepted as the emperor's sworn brother, the royal younger brother who could carry the authority of the Tang while seeking the scriptures. They went before the Buddha's image and made their vows. Xuanzang promised not to return empty-handed. If the true scriptures could not be obtained, he would rather perish upon the road than pretend the journey had succeeded. The emperor arranged for the necessary papers and preparations for departure.

For the first time, the mission that had begun at Vulture Peak had a human traveler. Guanyin had found him in Chang'an, tested his humility by interrupting his public teaching, and placed the robe and staff into his hands. The emperor's fear of the dead had created the ceremony; the ceremony had brought Xuanzang before the court; and the question of how to deliver souls had turned a monk's learning into a journey.

Xuanzang's own life had prepared him in another way. He had been a child without a secure home, rescued by a monk, raised among the scriptures, and reunited with a family marked by violence. He knew that the world could be cruel, even when the forms of government and ceremony appeared orderly. He also knew what a stranger's mercy could do. A man who had once floated helplessly down a river would now travel by choice toward dangers he could scarcely imagine.

He did not know all the companions already waiting for him. At the River of Flowing Sands, a former heavenly general had accepted a new name. At Mount Fuling a pig-faced warrior had set aside his old habits for the promise of a future. A condemned dragon was waiting to become a horse. Beneath the Mountain of Five Elements, Wukong counted the years until a scripture seeker might release him.

Those preparations were separated from one another by hundreds of miles and by histories of sin, punishment, violence, and hope. Xuanzang carried no knowledge of their full stories. He had accepted the mission because he believed the scriptures mattered. Before he could approach the Buddha's monastery, he would have to learn to live and work among beings very different from the monks of Chang'an.

The Tang emperor had sworn a bond with him. The capital was preparing its farewell. The staff's nine rings would sound on the road, and the embroidered robe would be carried far beyond the court where it had first astonished the officials. The pilgrim had been chosen. Now there was only the western road, with everything that road concealed.
