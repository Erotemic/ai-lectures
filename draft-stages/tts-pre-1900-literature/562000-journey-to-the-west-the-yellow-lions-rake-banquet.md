# Journey to the West: The Yellow Lion's rake banquet

## Three empty places

At the palace forge the smiths rose to a sight that no explanation could improve. The space where the gold-banded cudgel had stood was empty. So were the places for the nine-toothed rake and the demon-subduing staff. They had kept the fires burning and the hammering going for days, but they had slept through the hour in which every model disappeared. They searched behind the furnace, under the benches, and through the courtyard, as though a staff heavy enough to shake the sea might have slipped behind a basket of coal.

The three princes came to see the work. The smiths knelt and told them the truth. Afraid that their masters were playing a trick, the princes ran to the guest pavilion. Wukong was awake but unarmed; Bajie was barely out of bed. When he heard that the rake had vanished, the pig sprang up in a panic that no royal etiquette could contain.

"My rake? Where? Give it back!"

He immediately accused the smiths of theft. They protested that ordinary men could scarcely lift one of the sacred weapons, let alone carry all three through a sleeping palace. Bajie replied that many men could drag a heavy thing together. He demanded that they be beaten until they confessed. Wukong stopped him. The tools were his disciples' responsibility. They had placed their treasures where anyone with power could see them. The light that seemed so marvelous to the apprentices would have been an advertisement to every greedy spirit in the surrounding country.

This recognition did not tell them where to look. From the city there were roads and hills in every direction. Wukong climbed high and surveyed the countryside, watching for a trace of unnatural cloud. Then he found two wolf-faced demons traveling toward a market, carrying silver and talking freely in the confidence that their errand was nobody else's concern.

He made himself a small flying creature and listened. The pair were called Crafty Oddity and Odd Craftiness, names so alike that a careless listener would lose track of which demon was speaking. Their master, the Yellow Lion of Tiger Mouth Cave, had acquired three extraordinary weapons. To celebrate he meant to hold a banquet, with particular honor for the brilliant nine-toothed rake. The two servants had been sent out to purchase pigs and sheep for the feast.

Wukong heard more than enough. A treasure that could stir the covetousness of a lion demon had also made him foolishly proud. Instead of hiding his theft, he was inviting guests to admire it.

## The invitation

Wukong stepped into the path of the two servants. Before they could raise a weapon he froze them with a spell. They remained upright with their eyes open, unable to move or speak. He took their silver and the identification tablets hanging at their waists. Then he went back to Jade Flower and told Xuanzang, the princes, and his brothers what he had learned.

Bajie was indignant that the demon had named the feast after his rake. "At least he recognizes quality," he said, "but he has no right to serve dinner in its honor while its rightful owner goes hungry."

The plan was simple enough to delight Sha. Wukong and Bajie would transform themselves into the two missing servants. Sha would play a trader bringing animals for the feast and insisting on collecting the remaining payment. The royal household would buy the animals. By the time they entered the lion's home, their enemy would think them part of the banquet preparations.

Bajie had not seen the two servants closely and worried that he would change into the wrong face. Wukong coached him through the disguise and gave him one of the tablets. Sha dressed as a cattle dealer and took charge of the animals, while the other two swaggered ahead in the manner of creatures pleased to be trusted with their master's money.

On the mountain road they met a third demon carrying a lacquered invitation case. He asked where the livestock came from and explained that he was going to Bamboo Joint Mountain to invite the Yellow Lion's grandfather. This elder bore a name that sounded grand even among demon royalty: Ninefold Numinous Primal Sage, Jiuling Yuansheng. The invitation asked him to take the place of honor at the rake banquet. Many lesser lion spirits would accompany him.

Wukong read the note and returned it with an expression of polite interest. He had gone out to recover three weapons and had learned that the thief belonged to a larger family. Sha later asked what kind of monster this grandfather might be. Bajie decided that a yellow lion was probably the sort of creature he could defeat with a rake. Wukong told him not to judge by the younger creature. A name like Ninefold Numinous Primal Sage suggested a far more dangerous presence.

## A merchant in the cave

Tiger Mouth Cave was well appointed. Flowers grew around the entrance and the stone galleries were tidy. Servants came out to seize the pigs and sheep, and the Yellow Lion himself appeared to ask about the cost. The disguised Wukong explained that the seller had been brought along for the small sum still unpaid. That much the demon accepted. But when they said the merchant wished to admire the treasures for which the feast was being held, the Yellow Lion grew suspicious.

"Why should a stranger see what I have taken from the royal city? He could tell the princes and bring trouble to my door."

Wukong answered that the merchant lived far away and was concerned only with getting paid. Bajie added that a master ought to be proud of such treasures. A little flattery, a little appetite, and the urgency of the coming banquet carried the argument. The Yellow Lion ordered the difference paid and allowed the supposed merchant to enter.

In the inner hall, displayed like a shrine to stolen splendor, stood the weapons. Bajie's nine-toothed rake occupied the place of greatest honor. Wukong's cudgel was set to one side and Sha's staff to the other. Every light that had drawn the Yellow Lion from his mountain still shone, but the hall was now filled with the demon's servants, many of them already imagining how magnificent tomorrow's feast would be.

Sha made a show of bowing to the precious rake. Bajie eyed his weapon with the possessive devotion of a man reunited with a close relative. Wukong waited until the three were in position. Then he gave the signal.

The merchant caught up his demon-subduing staff; the two servants shed their disguises. Bajie seized the nine-toothed rake with a shout loud enough to shake the cave. Wukong took his cudgel from its place and whirled it between the astonished attendants and the exits. The Yellow Lion had invited his enemies inside and politely shown them where to recover the means of destroying his house.

The battle ran through the hall, the side rooms, and the galleries. Creatures who had been carrying pots and dishes snatched up clubs and knives. They could not stand against the three disciples now fully armed. The Yellow Lion fought fiercely, but three opponents came at him with a knowledge of one another's movements that the young princes had only begun to learn. After losing the hall he fled from the mountain, leaving his servants to face the consequences.

Wukong, Bajie, and Sha broke open the remaining rooms and scattered the defenders. Their attack did not end at retrieving the weapons. They set fire to the cave and burned the household, a harsh decision that the novel does not disguise as a tidy recovery of stolen property. The feast would never be held. A cavern that had been decorated for celebration was left smoking among the trees.

## The grandfather

The Yellow Lion ran to Bamboo Joint Mountain, to the Nine-Bend Winding Cave, where his grandfather lived. He fell before the old ruler and poured out his grievance. He had found treasures, he said, and taken them home to celebrate. Three strange monks had tricked him, slaughtered his attendants, destroyed his cave, and forced him to flee. He wanted vengeance and a home in which to live again.

The elder did not applaud him. He asked who these monks were. The Yellow Lion described a long-eared pig, a dark-faced man with a staff, and a small monkey with a face like a thunder god.

The old lion's expression changed. He knew the monkey. Sun Wukong had rebelled against Heaven five hundred years earlier and had never been easily restrained. If the Yellow Lion had known anything of the Great Sage, he would not have touched his staff. The younger creature had made a quarrel with a man famous for breaking into heavily guarded places and scattering everything within them.

Still, the elder agreed to help his descendant. He summoned a company of lion spirits: white and tawny, shaggy and swift, armed with axes, clubs, and spears. Their magical winds traveled across Leopard Head Mountain toward the smoking remains of Tiger Mouth Cave. There they found the two servants Wukong had frozen. The pair had only just recovered movement and were weeping beside the wreckage. Their report confirmed the Yellow Lion's story: Wukong had stolen their tablets and their silver, and the three monks had destroyed the home while wearing their faces.

The Yellow Lion broke down. He cried for his dead household, for the companions who had prepared his banquet, for the years of work lost in a single day. Some of the demons tried to quiet him. In his grief he struck at the rocks, wishing himself dead rather than dispossessed. The old ruler pulled him away and told him that mourning would not restore the cave. There was one course left, he said: march on Jade Flower and take the travelers and the royal family who sheltered them.

The army moved under blackening cloud. Wind whipped dust into the streets. Outside the city, parents grabbed their children and ran for the gates. Watchmen shouted for the doors to be shut. The prince and his sons had been eating with the pilgrims when the alarms reached the pavilion.

Wukong understood at once. This was not a solitary thief returning for his stolen goods. It was an elder power accompanied by many lions, come to hold the entire city responsible for what had been done to one den. He ordered the gates secured, told the royal household to remain on the walls, and called Bajie and Sha to stand with him.

The three teachers rose into the air again. The first time the city had watched them perform in wonder. This time those same motions would be used against an army approaching through the dust.
