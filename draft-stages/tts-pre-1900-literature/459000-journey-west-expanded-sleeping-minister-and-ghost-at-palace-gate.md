# The Minister Who Slept and the Ghost at the Gate

That night the Tang emperor Taizong dreamed of a dragon. The creature bowed before him and begged for his life. He had broken a heavenly command and was to be executed at the following day's appointed hour by the emperor's own minister Wei Zheng. If the emperor could keep Wei Zheng occupied, the dragon said, he would be saved. Taizong was astonished. A ruler of human beings was accustomed to petitions about land, taxes, offices, and criminal judgments, not requests from dragons. But the petitioner was terrified, and the emperor promised to help.

When morning came, Taizong told his courtiers about the dream. Wei Zheng was not among them. An adviser suggested summoning him and keeping him at court for the whole period during which the execution might take place. The emperor agreed. Wei Zheng received the summons and could not refuse, although he already knew from a heavenly command that he had another duty at precisely that hour. He had prepared himself for it and feared the consequences of neglecting either his earthly sovereign or the Jade Emperor.

Taizong welcomed him without mentioning the dragon's petition. They discussed affairs of government until the hour drew near. Then the emperor proposed a game of go, the board game of territories, encirclement, and patient calculation. He wanted his minister in sight, his hands engaged with the black and white stones, his attention occupied until the dangerous hour had passed.

The pieces clicked onto the board. One player extended a position; the other defended a corner. It was a fine game and an absorbing one. But as the appointed moment approached, Wei Zheng grew drowsy. He had been working without rest, and the strain overcame him. His head lowered over the table. He fell asleep in the emperor's presence.

Taizong saw the minister sleeping and was amused by his weariness. The emperor did not want to wake him. A man who had labored so hard in the service of the state should be allowed a brief rest. Besides, what danger was there in a man sleeping beside his sovereign? He was not leaving the room. He held no sword. The dragon would surely be safe.

Before long Wei Zheng woke and apologized for falling asleep. The emperor forgave him and was ready to resume the game. Then a disturbance sounded outside. Officers brought a severed dragon's head, bloody and immense. It had fallen from the sky near the street. No one understood how such a thing could happen. Taizong turned to Wei Zheng.

The minister told him what had occurred during sleep. His spirit had gone to the heavenly execution ground, where the Dragon King waited bound. Wei Zheng had carried out the sentence given by the Jade Emperor and cut off the dragon's head. His body had remained beside the go board throughout. Taizong realized with horror that his scheme had failed through the one circumstance he had interpreted as harmless. A minister did not need to leave the room in order to act for Heaven.

The emperor had not meant to betray the dragon. He had given his word and taken what seemed an effective precaution. But promises did not depend entirely on intention. The dragon was dead, and he had reason to think the ruler's promise had not been kept.

That night the dragon returned as a ghost. He carried his own severed head and cried out at the palace, demanding justice from Taizong. He accused the emperor of promising to save him and then permitting Wei Zheng to strike. Taizong struggled in terror, unable to explain away what had happened. A figure came from the south bearing a willow branch, and the ghost withdrew. It was Guanyin, who had been staying in Chang'an while searching for the future scripture pilgrim. Her intervention drove the immediate danger away, but it did not heal the emperor's fear.

The palace became a place of noises after dark. The king who had faced armies in battle now feared the sound of a door. Courtiers summoned physicians, and ministers tried to reassure him. He could speak of conquering territories and protecting his people, but he could not sleep through the accusations that rose from the unseen world.

Two commanders, Qin Shubao and Yuchi Jingde, offered to stand guard. They dressed in their armor, took up their weapons, and stationed themselves at the palace gates. In their presence the disturbances ceased. For several nights the emperor slept more peacefully, and he thanked them. Yet watching through the night was exhausting. He did not wish to impose the duty upon trusted generals indefinitely.

The court had portraits made of the two commanders in their armor and placed the paintings on the doors. These images continued the protective work. The novel pauses over this extraordinary origin of familiar door guardians: two human warriors, first standing through the night with their weapons, are transformed into painted figures whose faces can repel what a living monarch cannot see. When another entrance caused trouble, Wei Zheng was assigned to watch it with the sword he had used in the dragon's execution.

Even with the gates guarded, Taizong became weaker. The physicians could offer little hope. His body wasted while the court made preparations for the possibility of his death. At last Wei Zheng brought a final proposal. He had a letter for an old acquaintance who now served in the underworld as a judge, Cui Jue. If the emperor took it with him when he died, perhaps the judge would help him return.

The emperor accepted the letter. He had intended to save the Dragon King's life by keeping an official at a game. Now he himself would depend on an official's favor in another jurisdiction. He put Wei Zheng's message away, closed his eyes, and died. The palace mourned. Beyond the world of soldiers and ministers, the emperor's spirit was already moving toward gates where his own authority would need to be recognized anew.
