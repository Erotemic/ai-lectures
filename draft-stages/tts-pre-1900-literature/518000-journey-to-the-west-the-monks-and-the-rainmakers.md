# Journey to the West: The monks and the rainmakers

## The carts beyond the gate

The Black Water River had closed behind them, and the pilgrims resumed their journey west. Winter passed in a succession of cold camps and narrow roads. When spring returned, the first green shoots appeared along the streams. Sun warmed the fields, birds began calling above the paths, and the riders looked forward to an easier stretch of country.

Then they heard a roar ahead.

It was not a beast, although it was loud enough to suggest one. The sound rose and fell in great collective shouts, hundreds of voices straining together. Xuanzang pulled his horse to a stop. Bajie thought a mountain might be splitting. Sha Wujing said it sounded like thunder. Wukong jumped into the air to look.

Below him stood a city. No battle flags flew above its walls. No army waited at the gate. Yet outside the eastern entrance, on a strip of sandy ground, a mass of men hauled wooden carts up a steep embankment. The loads were piled with bricks, timbers, clay, and stone. The road passed through narrow turns and sharp inclines, a cruel route for anyone pulling heavy building supplies. As the men strained at the ropes they shouted together, trying to make their own voices supply strength that their bodies no longer had.

They were monks.

Their robes were ragged. Many had little strength left. A few overseers dressed as Daoist priests came out of the city, and the laborers immediately pulled harder. The sight troubled Wukong. Monks and Daoists both lived as religious men; he could think of no reason why one group should be driving the other like draft animals.

He changed his shape into that of a traveling Daoist, hung a small basket from his arm, and approached the two young priests with a drum and an easy greeting.

They welcomed him. A wandering Daoist was fortunate, they said, to have arrived in Chechi Kingdom. Here the rich admired Daoist learning, officials showed it respect, and the king himself gave the highest honor to the order. A visitor need not go hungry. He might even receive a place at court.

Wukong asked which teachers had won such favor.

The priests described three great masters: Tiger Strength Immortal, Deer Strength Immortal, and Goat Strength Immortal. In a year of severe drought, local Buddhist monks had prayed without producing rain. These three Daoists had arrived with powerful rites, summoned the weather, and relieved the kingdom. The king had rewarded them with titles and estates. He had come to believe that they alone could preserve the country's health and perhaps extend his own life.

And the laborers?

Those, said the priests, were the monks who had failed. Their monasteries had been torn down. Their images had been destroyed, their religious papers withdrawn, and their persons assigned to the three masters as servants. They were building the new religious complex with the materials of the old world their patrons were replacing. Some worked in kitchens; some swept courtyards; these were the men left to haul the carts. The young priests had come to count them and make sure none had escaped.

Wukong adopted a mournful expression. He could not follow the priests into the city just yet. He had an uncle, a monk, lost to the family for years. Perhaps the old man had been brought here. Would his two gracious new friends permit him to inspect the laborers for a relative?

The priests, flattered by his manner, agreed. He would only have to count the workers and report whether any were missing.

Wukong approached the carts alone.

Every monk immediately knelt. They assumed he had come to inspect the work. One by one they called out that they had not been idle, that five hundred men were present, that the loads were moving as quickly as possible.

'Get up,' Wukong told them. 'I'm looking for family.'

At once they crowded forward. Each hoped the stranger might recognize him. A man with a relative among the favored priests might be permitted to go home. Wukong looked from face to face and asked why men sworn to a spiritual life had become slaves to other religious men.

Their answer was a history of fear.

## Five hundred who could not leave

It was not merely the work that held them. The king had ordered drawings of monks to be displayed throughout his country. Officials who captured a monk could be promoted; ordinary people received silver for turning one in. A traveler with a shaved head was in danger. Even someone with thinning hair might be mistaken for a monk and hauled before an officer. The five hundred laborers could not hide their clothing or alter their faces enough to escape.

More than two thousand monks had been brought to this place. Hundreds had died from cold, disease, and exhaustion. Others, driven past endurance, had sought death for themselves. A smaller number remained. These five hundred, the survivors said, were not allowed even that final release. Ropes broke when they tried to hang themselves. Blades failed, water would not drown them, and poison seemed unable to take effect.

Wukong called them fortunate to be protected from death. One laborer answered that they had been protected from the end of suffering, not from suffering itself.

At night the monks slept exposed on the sand. Spirits guarded them. Sometimes, in dreams, they heard that a mighty traveler was coming from the east: the disciple of a holy monk, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, who would restore them to freedom. An elderly celestial figure had even described his face, his staff, and his proud, mischievous manner.

Wukong listened. He was flattered that anyone in Heaven had been telling his story. He was less delighted that the description was so accurate. He asked whether they would recognize the Great Sage if they saw him.

They said they would.

He announced that he was Sun Wukong, but the monks protested. He looked like an ordinary Daoist. Wukong told them to glance toward the far road, where the true Great Sage was approaching. While they turned, he threw off his disguise. When they looked back, the monkey with the golden eyes and iron staff stood before them.

The monks fell to the ground again, this time from relief.

Wukong went to the two young overseers and asked for all five hundred laborers to be released. He called them his family now: one hundred neighbors from one side of the house, one hundred from the other, another hundred from his father's relations, another from his mother's, and the last hundred his closest friends. The priests laughed at the absurdity. The monks belonged to the three masters under royal decree. Not even two could be freed without permission.

Wukong asked again. They refused again. The third refusal brought out his iron staff.

He struck the two young priests dead.

The monks recoiled. To them these overseers had been powerful and feared, but also protected by the king. Killing them could mean reprisals. Wukong had not yet overturned the law that imprisoned the laborers; he had made its enforcers disappear. What would happen when the deaths were discovered?

He told the monks not to panic. He broke the loaded carts, hurled the timber and bricks aside, and announced that no one need pull another load. But the men were still afraid to run. A road guarded by officials, reward notices, and wanted pictures could carry them only from one punishment to the next.

Then Wukong plucked hairs from his body, chewed them, and distributed a fragment to every monk. Each was to keep the hair beneath the nail of a finger. If anyone tried to seize him, he must close his fist and call for the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. A defender in Wukong's likeness would appear.

One of the monks tested it. A figure with the monkey's staff sprang into being beside him. Others called the name, and suddenly the sand was crowded with guardians. When Wukong instructed them to speak a second word, the guardians vanished back into the hairs.

Now the monks dared to leave. Wukong told them not to flee too far. He intended to confront the king, and they would be summoned again when it was safe to return and reclaim their lives.

He returned to Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. The company entered the city as evening fell. The surviving monks led them to Zhi Yuan Temple, where they were fed and given shelter. Its residents had dreamed of Wukong's arrival too. They recognized him with the exhausted delight of people who had spent too long waiting for the sound of footsteps outside a locked door.

Xuanzang slept. Wukong did not.

## The Three Pure Ones at midnight

From his room Wukong heard bells and ritual music. He climbed into the night air and saw the great Daoist temple glittering with lamps. The three royal teachers were conducting prayers in honor of the Three Pure Ones, the revered figures represented on the temple's principal altar. They wore ceremonial robes. Students chanted scriptures. Offerings of steamed buns, rich pastries, rice, fruit, and other food filled the tables beneath the images.

Wukong went back to wake Sha Wujing. There were offerings, he said, too fine to leave uneaten, and the priests seemed unlikely to finish them all. Sha Wujing was reluctant to go prowling at night. Bajie, however, heard a half-asleep mention of food and sat up at once. He would certainly accompany them.

The three disciples flew to the temple and waited for the worship to end. Bajie wanted to descend immediately and begin eating. Wukong restrained him. He breathed out a gust of wind that scattered lamps and upset the ceremonial arrangements. The priests, alarmed by the disturbance, decided to conclude the night's rites and return in the morning.

Once the hall was empty, the three visitors entered.

Bajie reached for the largest buns, but Wukong struck his hand away. If they were going to steal a feast from a sacred altar, they must at least do it with a proper plan. Three images occupied the honored seats: the central figure represented Yuanshi Tianzun, and the other two represented Lingbao Tianzun and Taishang Laojun. The disciples would take their shapes, sit above the offerings, and eat without being disturbed.

Bajie took the form of Laozi. Sha Wujing became Lingbao Tianzun. Wukong sat in the central position as Yuanshi Tianzun.

The original statues lay on the floor. Wukong pointed out that a priest entering before dawn might trip over them and discover the trick. They must be hidden. He directed Bajie toward a nearby chamber, a place Wukong described with exaggerated elegance as the house where the five grains completed their cycle.

Bajie carried the heavy statues there and found a latrine.

He complained loudly about Wukong's fondness for grand names. This was a privy, not a hall of mystical transformation. Then he threw the statues inside, splashing himself with foul water, and returned to the altar grumbling that an immortal ought not be asked to perform such work.

The disguised disciples settled to their feast. Bajie ate enough for several people. Sha Wujing joined him. Wukong preferred fruit, and watched his brothers make the offerings vanish.

A young Daoist had left a handbell in the hall. He woke, remembered it, and came to retrieve it. In the dark he heard a breath, then a muffled laugh. Startled, he stumbled on a fruit stone, fell, and shattered the bell. The sound brought him running to the rooms of the three masters.

Tiger Strength Immortal, Deer Strength Immortal, and Goat Strength Immortal gathered their attendants. Carrying lamps, the priests entered the hall and found the offerings eaten while the holy figures appeared undisturbed on their seats. There were peeled fruits and discarded seeds, signs that someone had enjoyed the food. Yet no thief could be seen.

The masters decided that the Three Pure Ones themselves must have descended to accept the gifts.

They bowed and asked for a favor in return. If the august beings were truly present, would they bestow a little sacred elixir or holy water? The king would treasure such a gift as a means of lengthening life. The Daoists prayed, chanted, and waited.

Bajie whispered that they had gone too far. Wukong pinched his arm to keep him silent. Then the monkey answered in an awful, solemn voice, saying that they had descended from a celestial banquet with no pills on hand. The priests begged again. At last Wukong told them to bring vessels and leave the hall while the divine liquid was prepared.

Tiger Strength brought a great jar. Deer Strength provided a basin. Goat Strength took a flower vase from its place among the offerings. They withdrew, closed the doors, and knelt in expectation.

The three disciples rose from their seats.

Wukong relieved himself into the vase. Bajie, delighted by the opportunity for a particularly vulgar trick, filled the basin. Sha Wujing contributed to the larger jar. They resumed their positions and called for the priests to collect their reward.

The teachers accepted the vessels reverently. Then they tasted the contents.

The flavor was not what they expected. One noted a strange harshness; another recognized the smell. The explanation was too humiliating to say aloud. Wukong supplied it from the altar. He declared that their sacred water was nothing but the monks' urine, and that he and his brothers had eaten the offerings intended for the gods.

The priests seized staves, brooms, and whatever weapons were near. Wukong caught Bajie and Sha Wujing under his arms and flew out before the mob could reach them. They returned to Zhi Yuan Temple and slipped into bed, careful not to wake Xuanzang.

By dawn the kingdom's three religious masters had a new grievance, and the emperor would soon hear it in full.

## The king's court

Xuanzang rose in the morning, dressed in his ceremonial robe, and prepared the travel permit. The little party approached the palace, asking only for the official endorsement that would allow them to cross the border. A royal officer announced that foreign monks had arrived from the eastern Tang country.

The king initially thought the matter simple. Monks were to be arrested. But a senior minister reminded him that these travelers had crossed an enormous distance under diplomatic protection. Whatever the king thought of local monks, it would be rash to insult an important distant ruler by seizing his envoys without hearing them. The king allowed them to enter and began examining the documents.

At that moment the three Daoist masters arrived. They did not bow to the monarch. The king rose from his seat to honor them, had cushions provided, and asked why they had come without invitation.

They pointed to Xuanzang's group and announced that the criminals were before him. These foreign monks had murdered two of their disciples, freed five hundred captive laborers, destroyed the carts, desecrated the temple images, stolen the offerings, and mocked the Three Pure Ones by giving the priests filthy liquid to drink.

The king was furious. Wukong stepped forward and asked permission to answer.

His defense was brazen. Who had witnessed the killings? If two monks had to die for two killings, he said, the remaining two should be allowed to complete the mission. If the freed laborers and broken carts constituted another offense, perhaps one more monk could be held responsible, leaving one to go west. As for the temple incident, how could travelers newly arrived in the city know the layout of the building so well? Why had the priests not seized the intruders during the night? They might simply be accusing whoever happened to be at court that morning.

The argument did not make the acts disappear. The king wavered. Wukong had made uncertainty where the Daoist masters had demanded immediate punishment.

Before the court decided, village elders arrived to petition for rain. The new spring had been dry. Fields that should have been growing green stood in danger. The people needed water, and the masters had a reputation for commanding it.

The king seized upon the petition. Here was a way to judge the strangers. Years before, he explained, he had turned against Buddhist monks because they could not bring rain, while his Daoist masters could. Let the pilgrims compete in summoning a storm. If the travelers succeeded, they would receive their permit and depart. If they failed, they would be punished.

Wukong agreed. Xuanzang looked toward him. The monk knew no ritual for commanding the weather. The monkey smiled and told him to trust the arrangement.

## The first strokes of the command tablet

Tiger Strength Immortal went first. A raised platform had been prepared with banners for the heavenly constellations, a ritual sword, incense, burning papers, jars of water, and the names of thunder officials. The master announced that his command tablet would mark five stages: wind at the first stroke, clouds at the second, thunder and lightning at the third, rain at the fourth, and clear skies at the fifth.

He performed his rites. The first stroke sounded, and wind began to move.

Bajie shivered. The Daoist really could summon weather. But Wukong had already taken a hair and left an exact likeness of himself standing by Xuanzang. His true spirit rose into the sky to see who was bringing the wind.

There he met the officials of the air, preparing to release their forces. Wukong announced that he had come with Xuanzang on the scripture mission and that the men below were competing for their lives. Why were the wind officials helping the wrong side? The officials bowed and closed the bag from which the wind would have poured.

On the platform, Tiger Strength struck his tablet again. Clouds began to gather, then vanished under the monkey's orders. A third stroke brought thunder officials, but when they reached the sky above the city they found Wukong waiting. The thunder god explained that Tiger Strength's ritual was genuine: he had filed the proper petitions, and Heaven had issued its command. Wukong did not deny the authority of the papers. He simply told the officials to wait until he gave them a different signal.

The fourth stroke summoned the dragon kings. Ao Guang and his brothers arrived ready to answer the formal request. Wukong greeted them like old colleagues. He reminded Ao Shun of the rescue at the Black Water River and thanked him again for his son's service. Then he explained the contest. They were to hold their rain until Xuanzang took the platform.

Below, Tiger Strength waited for the clouds. The sky remained blue. At last he descended, obliged to admit that no rain had fallen. He claimed the dragon kings must have been away from home.

Wukong declared that the dragons were not absent. They were simply unwilling to answer him. Let Xuanzang pray, and the king would see.

The monk climbed the platform and sat quietly. He did not know the talismanic forms of the Daoist rite. He knew the words of the Heart Sutra, which he began reciting with steady attention.

High overhead, Wukong organized his own instructions. He would use the iron staff as a signal. One upward point would release wind. A second would bring cloud. A third would summon thunder and lightning. A fourth would release rain. A fifth would clear the sky. The officers agreed, although several recoiled when they first heard that the monkey planned to use his staff; they remembered too well what that weapon could do when it was meant for striking.

Wukong returned to his place, reclaimed the borrowed hair that stood in his likeness, and waited for Xuanzang to finish reciting.

Then the staff rose.

Wind roared through Chechi. Roof tiles shifted. Dust filled the streets. Wukong pointed again and clouds rolled in until the sun disappeared. At the third signal thunder broke above the palace, and lightning flashed across the city. The people shut their doors and burned incense in fear. At the fourth signal the dragons emptied rain over the rooftops and fields.

Rain fell so hard that the streets began to flood. The king sent a messenger to ask the pilgrims to stop before the crops were destroyed by the very water intended to save them. Wukong lifted the staff for the fifth time, and the downpour ended. The clouds opened. Sunlight filled the square.

The king rejoiced. The Daoist masters sometimes brought rain, but they could not clear the weather so precisely. He ordered Xuanzang's travel permit brought forward.

The three masters objected. They claimed that Tiger Strength's earlier ritual had indeed summoned the gods, but that the gods had arrived late, just as Xuanzang began. The Buddhist monk had merely enjoyed their master's success.

Wukong proposed a test. If Tiger Strength had called the dragon kings, let him invite them to appear openly. The king approved. Tiger Strength called. No dragon showed himself.

Then Wukong raised his head toward the sky.

'Ao Guang! Brothers! Show yourselves.'

Four dragons emerged among the clearing clouds. Their scales shone above the palace, their horns caught the sunlight, and their great bodies circled in full view of the king and his court. The king bowed and thanked them. The dragons returned to their seas.

For a moment the matter seemed settled. The rain had come; the dragon kings had acknowledged Wukong; the permit could be stamped and the travelers released.

But the masters who had ruled Chechi by their command of the weather had just watched their power publicly challenged. They had more ways to test a monk than asking for rain. They were not ready to yield their places, or to accept that the five hundred laborers might go free.

The first contest was over. The struggle at the royal court was not.
