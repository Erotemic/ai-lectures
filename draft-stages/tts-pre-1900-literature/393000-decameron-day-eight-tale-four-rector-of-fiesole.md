# The Decameron — The Rector of Fiesole

A widow in Florence is being pursued by the rector of Fiesole, a cleric whose persistence has become unwelcome. She has made it clear that she does not want him. He continues anyway, relying on office, proximity and repeated requests. What he takes as courtship she experiences as pressure. Having found direct refusal ineffective, she works out a way to expose him before someone whose judgment he cannot easily disregard.

The widow pretends to yield. She tells the rector that he may come to her bed in darkness, where they can meet without scandal. The priest, excited by the apparent change, fails to imagine that the setting might have been designed for an audience. The woman arranges for a maid, described by the narrator as unattractive and old, to take her place. The maid is paid for the role. The substitution depends on darkness, silence, and the priest's certainty that he is entitled to the encounter he believes he has finally won.

At the appointed time the rector enters the darkened room and takes the substitute for his hostess. Then the widow's brothers bring the bishop to witness the scene. The rector's powerful religious superior sees the priest in circumstances that contradict the decorum of his office. The affair is exposed not by an argument, but by directing authority to an already staged piece of evidence. The cleric who ignored a woman's refusal is made answerable to the person who can discipline him.

The bishop rebukes and punishes the rector, and his humiliation circulates as a local joke. For the widow, the result is release from harassment. She has not persuaded the priest by moral reasoning. She has used the social mechanism he respects—ecclesiastical supervision—to stop his pursuit. Emilia's narrator invites us to take pleasure in a person of power being trapped by his own appetite.

Yet the maid's role is also worth examining. She is not merely a neutral item of scenery but a working woman recruited, for pay, into the most physically intimate part of the plot. The widow secures an outcome she needs by directing risk toward someone below her in the household hierarchy. The narration treats the substitute's age and appearance as a joke at the priest's expense. A listener can see the critique of a predatory cleric without repeating that contempt toward the servant.

The story is one of several in which Boccaccio turns a would-be investigator into the person being investigated. The priest thinks he has secured a private encounter, but his privacy is theatrical. The bishop's arrival makes the private public. Unlike the clever confessor episode of the preceding day, a religious official does not successfully impersonate authority here. He is caught by it. The widow's victory does not depend on proving that all priests behave similarly. It depends on making one persistent man unable to escape the meaning of his own actions.
