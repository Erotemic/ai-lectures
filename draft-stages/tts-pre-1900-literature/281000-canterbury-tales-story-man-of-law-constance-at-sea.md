# The Canterbury Tales — The Man of Law’s Tale, Constance at Sea

A Roman emperor has a daughter, Constance, admired for both beauty and Christian virtue. Merchants returning from Rome describe her to the Sultan of Syria. He falls in love with a woman he has never met and declares that he will marry her, even if converting to Christianity is the price. Arrangements are made and Constance is sent across the sea. The journey is described as a separation from everything familiar, not a triumphal bridal procession. She has little power over the reasons for her departure.

The Sultan's mother is furious at the prospect of her family's conversion. She secretly organizes the murder of her son and the Christians at a feast, then has Constance put into a rudderless boat and cast away. The tale's religious language characterizes the mother as an enemy of Christianity, but the plot is also one of court violence: a ruler's marriage plan, made without the consent of the people whom it will transform, becomes the occasion for massacre. Constance survives because her voyage, against ordinary expectation, carries her into another country.

She reaches the coast of Northumberland and is sheltered by a constable and his wife Hermengyld. Her piety gradually wins affection, and Hermengyld converts. A wicked knight attempts to pursue Constance and, when refused, murders Hermengyld while she sleeps beside her guest. He plants the bloody knife so that Constance will be blamed. In a public judgment his false oath is exposed by a supernatural sign. Constance survives a second plot in which the structures of power nearly accept evidence manufactured against her.

King Alla of Northumberland hears of her and marries her after converting to Christianity. They have a son, Maurice. But the king's mother Donegild plots against the marriage. While Alla is away, she intercepts letters. News of a healthy child becomes a false report of a monstrous birth; an order to care for Constance becomes a supposed command to put mother and child aboard another vessel. The letters serve the same purpose as the bloody knife: someone controls a written message and thereby controls what the powerful think they know. Once again Constance is set afloat, now with an infant.

Her boat survives other dangers at sea and eventually reaches the vicinity of Rome. Through a long sequence of recognitions, Alla comes to Rome, encounters Maurice, and discovers that his wife lives. Constance is finally reunited with her father. Alla and Constance are together again, though the tale has not offered its heroine a simple restoration of safety. Her children, her marriages and her faith have repeatedly been made instruments of other people's political plans.

This is a story of endurance shaped by providence, miracle and a particularly severe conception of virtue. Its geography is expansive, its religious divisions are represented from the Christian teller's side, and its heroine is praised most often for suffering without surrender. It is tempting to describe her as merely passive, but the tale values fidelity through situations in which ordinary choice has been removed. The questions worth retaining are who has the power to name innocence, who can falsify documents, and whether survival under such conditions counts as agency. The next major voice on the road will insist, very loudly, that experience itself can claim authority and that women can speak about marriage in their own names.
