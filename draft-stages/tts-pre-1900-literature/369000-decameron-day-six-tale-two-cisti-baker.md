# The Decameron — Cisti the Baker

Cisti earns his living as a baker in Florence. His trade is humble by the social standards of the men who pass his shop, but his business has prospered, and he owns exceptional wine. When Messer Geri Spina and visiting papal envoys begin walking past his doorway on their way to discussions, Cisti wants to offer them something worthy of their company. He fears that an open invitation from a working man might seem presumptuous to distinguished guests.

So he arranges a small performance. He takes a seat outside in spotless clothing, sets out shining cups and a fine flagon, and drinks a little wine with visible appreciation. The sight and his evident pleasure attract the attention of the passersby. Eventually they ask to taste it and discover that his wine is excellent. For several days he offers them refreshment, and the difference between baker and gentleman seems briefly suspended by shared appreciation.

The exchange becomes more delicate when Messer Geri wants to serve some of Cisti's wine to guests at his own house. He sends a servant with a large container. Cisti refuses to fill it and sends the man back. When Geri asks what happened, the baker makes clear, by implication rather than an insult, that such wine is not meant for an indiscriminate serving as though it were ordinary table drink. The nobleman understands the correction. He sends a more fitting vessel and receives the wine. Cisti later offers the remainder of his supply as a gift, and Geri treats him with enduring respect.

Pampinea asks us to see a noble soul concealed by a supposedly low occupation. That is the narrator's social vocabulary, not proof that baking is inherently base. In the action itself, Cisti's accomplishment lies in knowing the quality of what he makes and what he owns. He is willing to share, but he will not allow generosity to be confused with an absence of judgment.

Cisti's reply is effective because he makes Geri reconsider the request without forcing him to lose face in public. The exchange moves from hierarchy toward recognition without pretending the hierarchy disappears. Geri can issue instructions through a servant; Cisti can decline and reveal why the instructions were ill-conceived. The men meet at the level of discernment.

The wine is more than an expensive object. Its fragrance, small cups, and careful serving furnish the language in which Cisti can speak to people who would not ordinarily consult a baker about dignity. A lesser teller might finish with the servant being humiliated. Pampinea ends instead with the gentleman capable of recognizing a rebuke and the craftsman capable of making it.
