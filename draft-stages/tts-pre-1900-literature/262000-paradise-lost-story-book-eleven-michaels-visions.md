# Paradise Lost — Book Eleven: Michael's Visions of Human History

Adam and Eve's prayers rise to Heaven. The Son presents them to the Father, and mercy is granted. But mercy does not mean that the couple may remain indefinitely in the garden. They have become mortal and must learn to live in a world transformed by their choice. The angel Michael is sent to tell them they must leave Paradise. He is also instructed to give Adam a vision of the future, so that grief at losing one place may be joined to an understanding of a much longer human story.

When Michael approaches, the couple see signs that something serious is coming. Adam receives the news, and Eve laments the loss of the flowers, trees, and familiar places she has tended. Her grief is specific. Paradise is not just a theological location; it is home. Adam worries about losing the place where he has spoken with God. Michael explains that divine presence is not confined to Eden. Their life outside the garden may still be one in which they seek God and do good.

Eve is sent to rest, and Michael leads Adam to a height from which visions unfold. The first is a field with two brothers. One makes an offering, then kills the other. Adam has never seen murder. He must learn what death by violence means through a scene that gives it form. The killer is Cain, and his victim Abel, though Michael's teaching initially matters more than the names. The first human child who dies does so at another human's hands. The family that began with mutual companionship becomes capable of murderous envy.

Adam is then shown suffering less spectacular but just as inexorable: disease, old age, the frailty of bodies, and the work of death over time. He recoils from the prospect that his descendants will carry bodies vulnerable to so many forms of pain. Michael does not conceal the misery; he instructs Adam in moderation and care for the life he has been given. The visions are not a plan for avoiding all injury but a preparation for recognizing it.

More scenes follow. Adam sees human communities that pursue pleasure without restraint, social arrangements in which strength and appetite replace responsible judgment, and warfare that enlarges private violence into public destruction. The poem draws on early chapters of Genesis but expands them into a survey of possible human corruption. History will not be one continuous fall from innocence without exception. There are righteous individuals, and their fidelity can appear small against a crowd.

Enoch stands apart from widespread wrongdoing. Noah too is described as a man who resists the corruption of his age. Adam sees the construction of the ark and the waters that cover the world. Here the epic reaches another apparent undoing of creation: land disappears under floodwater and the human population is almost extinguished. Yet the ark preserves living creatures and a family from which life can begin again. When the waters subside and a rainbow appears, the spectacle suggests both judgment and promise.

Michael's visions do not allow Adam to hold every descendant as a moral extension of himself. He sees people choosing brutality, faithfulness, greed, restraint, and obedience. The burden of inherited mortality does not erase the differences among their acts. This is important for the poem's difficult claim that Adam's Fall affects the whole race. Milton makes universal consequences visible, but he also gives future human beings lives in which choices continue to matter.

Book Eleven ends at the Flood, not with the final departure from Paradise. Adam has been instructed by images he could scarcely have imagined while innocent: a brother murdered, bodies wasting, cities fighting, and an entire world drowned. His grief is wider now, but so is his understanding that humanity's story will contain both judgment and preservation. In the final book Michael will move from visions to a spoken account of nations, prophets, law, and the promised redeemer.
