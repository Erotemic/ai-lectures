# Journey to the West expanded IV: the scripture mission and the River Child

## Journey to the West — The Buddha’s Scriptures and the Westward Mission

For five hundred years the Mountain of Five Elements stood over Sun Wukong. Its stone was crowded with the weight of the Buddha's hand, and a strip bearing a sacred formula held the mountain fast. Below it, the former Great Sage Equal to Heaven could look out and speak. He could not get up. What he had wanted most in the world was freedom to go wherever his somersault cloud could carry him, and now he could not turn his body beneath the rock.

Far away in the western paradise, the Buddha returned from settling the trouble in Heaven. The assembly at Vulture Peak greeted him with banners and flowers. He told them what had happened: a monkey born from stone had collected marvelous powers, stolen from Heaven, survived heavenly weapons and the furnace, and finally accepted a contest whose size he did not understand. The Buddha had left him beneath the mountain. The gods were safe again. Time resumed the unhurried rhythm of the western realm.

Centuries went by. Then, at a festival, the Buddha gathered the holy company and ordered a banquet of flowers and fruit. Ananda and Kasyapa brought the offerings before the assembly. After the celebration came teaching. The Buddha spoke of beings in the four great continents, of cruelty and kindness, of the difficult passage from ignorance toward understanding. He looked toward the eastern continent of human beings and described the violence, greed, and quarrels that clouded the lives of its inhabitants.

He possessed scriptures that could be carried to them. There were three great collections: one concerned the teachings of the law, one reflection and reasoning, and one the sacred texts that could guide souls. The novel gives the collection an enormous number of scrolls. Yet possessing the scriptures in the west was not enough. They could not simply be dropped like rain on people who did not know their value. Someone would have to come and seek them.

The Buddha asked for a messenger. Such a messenger must travel east, find a suitable pilgrim, and convince that person to journey westward over mountains and rivers. The traveler would have to endure exhaustion, temptation, hunger, fear, and the dangers of a road populated by creatures whose powers belonged to more than the human world. At the end, the pilgrim could receive the true scriptures and take them back to the Tang lands. The undertaking would require both courage and a mind capable of learning.

Guanyin stepped forward. She was the bodhisattva associated with compassion, the one whom the distressed called upon for rescue. If someone had to search for such a pilgrim, she would go. The Buddha was pleased. She would be accompanied by the guardian Hui'an, also called Muzha, whose strength and obedience made him a fitting escort. But he gave Guanyin an instruction that was strange for a being who could cross the sky. She was not to fly high above the earth and arrive in Chang'an without seeing what lay between. She must travel low enough to examine rivers, mountains, dangers, and distances. How else could she prepare the person who would have to pass that way with ordinary feet?

He gave her gifts for the future traveler: a magnificent embroidered robe and a staff with nine rings. The robe was not a piece of court clothing, though it shone with jewels and costly threads. It was an emblem of the faith and a protection for one who deserved to wear it. The staff could support the traveler through a country where an imperial order would mean nothing to monsters on a mountain path.

Then the Buddha gave her three circlets. They were small objects, easy to hide in a sleeve. Their use was not gentle. Each had a corresponding spell; when placed upon a disobedient supernatural creature, a circlet would bind itself to the head, and the spell could inflict a terrible pain. These were not decorations for the pilgrim. They were instruments for bringing powerful and unruly companions into a discipline that they might not accept by persuasion alone.

Guanyin accepted the robe, the staff, and the three circlets. She bowed and set out with Hui'an. The west was luminous behind them. Ahead lay the road that the scriptures themselves had never taken, a succession of places with different inhabitants and different laws. Guanyin had to recognize whom she met, not merely defeat an obstacle and fly onward.

As she traveled, she could see how the journey would be made. Rivers could rise so high that a boat was useless. Mountains sheltered monsters whose hunger had no human limit. Some of those monsters were not born evil; they had once held places in Heaven, had broken its rules, and were still suffering the consequences. A person born to the human world could not pass them by simply believing that a journey was good.

But Guanyin had a different way of looking at these scattered disasters. The pilgrim's problem might become a disgraced spirit's opportunity. A criminal could be given work and judged by what he did afterward. A creature that had spent years attacking travelers might one day protect one. Even a monkey pinned under a mountain might possess the very strength that a quiet scholar would lack.

At first there was no pilgrim in sight. There was only a destination in the west, a search in the east, and the road between them. This was the beginning of the other great movement of the story. The Monkey King's rebellion had been about rising against every authority he encountered. The undertaking now being prepared would ask whether enormous power could serve something beyond its owner's pride.

Guanyin and Hui'an descended toward the first great obstacle. A terrible river lay across their way, and something living in it was already moving toward them.

## Journey to the West — The River of Sands and the Pig Demon

The river seemed to have no good shore. Sand stretched outward into it, water crashed and rolled over itself, and the distant banks disappeared into haze. It was called the River of Flowing Sands. In the descriptions of the novel, even a feather could not float upon it. For a pilgrim who could neither command the clouds nor breathe beneath the water, this river would be a grave barrier.

Guanyin stopped to look. Hui'an stood beside her with his iron staff. They had not waited long when the surface burst open and a creature rose from the water. His hair was red and tangled, his face fierce, his mouth broad, and in his hand he held a heavy weapon. He rushed toward the two figures, eager for another meal. Hui'an intercepted him. The guardian's iron staff met the creature's weapon, and they exchanged blows along the river's edge. Water flew, dust rose, and neither fighter yielded easily.

At last they paused long enough to ask one another's names. Hui'an identified himself and told the attacker that Guanyin was waiting nearby. The effect was immediate. The monster had known the bodhisattva's name before his exile. He followed the guardian to her, bowed, and began to explain himself.

He had once been the Curtain-Raising General in the Jade Emperor's palace, one of the servants whose duties brought him close to the imperial presence. During a heavenly gathering he had dropped and broken a precious glass vessel. The punishment had been severe: hundreds of blows, banishment from Heaven, and a recurring torment in which swords pierced him. Thrown down into the river, ravenous and embittered, he had begun to seize travelers. Hunger had become murder, and murder another burden to add to his original offense.

He had eaten many people, he said, including travelers who had come seeking scriptures. When their bodies vanished, their skulls sank like other bones, except for nine skulls that continued to float. He had threaded those strange remains together and sometimes wore them as a grisly necklace. He showed no expectation that anyone could make such a history clean.

Guanyin did not pretend that it was clean. She told him that being punished for an offense did not give him permission to kill. But there was another possible future. A pilgrim would travel to the western realm and bring back scriptures. If he would stop harming living beings and undertake the journey as a disciple, his labor could become part of his repentance. She would see that the recurring punishment ceased. The skulls, which he thought only a curiosity, would prove useful when the pilgrim arrived.

He agreed. Guanyin gave him a new religious name, Sha Wujing. The surname Sha recalled the sands, and the name marked a wish for awakened purity. He was to wait at this river and join the person seeking the scriptures. The weapon was still in his hands. His strength had not vanished; what it would be used for was what changed.

Guanyin and Hui'an moved on. Soon a mountain lay ahead of them, thick with a dark and threatening atmosphere. From its slopes came another huge creature, this one carrying a rake with nine iron teeth. His ears were enormous; his snout jutted forward; his body had the build of a warrior who had been given the wrong animal's face. He rushed to attack. Hui'an raised his staff again, and iron cracked against iron. The stranger fought hard, but when Guanyin dropped a lotus between the weapons and broke the struggle apart, the creature recognized whose power had intervened.

He threw down his rake and begged to be heard. His old name and rank, he said, were not those of a wild boar. He had been Marshal Tianpeng, commander of the heavenly waters. He had drunk too much at a heavenly celebration and behaved improperly toward a celestial woman. For that he had been beaten and banished. When his spirit sought another body in the mortal world, it had entered a sow rather than a human mother. He emerged in this humiliating form, killed the sow and her litter, and took to the mountains.

On Mount Fuling he had settled at Cloud-Path Cave. A woman called Second Sister Egg had taken him into her household; after her death he remained there with the property and the cave. He had found no life that satisfied him and had returned to eating human flesh. None of this made his present conduct acceptable. Guanyin asked why, having suffered for wrongdoing, he continued to injure others.

The pig-faced warrior did not answer with an immediate conversion. He asked a practical, rather rude question: if he abandoned human meat, was he supposed to live by drinking the wind? The rules of religious life sounded to him like a program for starving. Guanyin reminded him that the world contained grains and vegetables. He could feed himself without devouring people. More important, he had an opportunity to take service with a pilgrim and work toward the release from his old disgrace.

He considered this offer. There was food in the world besides human flesh; there was also a task, a destination, and a chance that Heaven would no longer regard him only as a failure. He surrendered and accepted a new name: Zhu Wuneng. Zhu was the pig surname, and Wuneng expressed awakened ability. He promised to keep to vegetarian food and wait for the scripture seeker.

The two future disciples did not meet one another that day. Sha Wujing remained at the dreadful river. Zhu Wuneng remained near his mountain cave. Each had fought Guanyin's escort before accepting her instructions. Each had a record of violence the pilgrimage would not erase, and each had been given an opportunity to do something else.

The future traveler still had no face, no name, and no knowledge of the promises being made on his behalf. Yet if he reached this river, someone who knew its dangers would be there. If he reached this mountain, a difficult companion with a rake would be waiting. Guanyin had begun to turn the landscape itself into a company of possible helpers.

There remained another prisoner to find, hanging where no road passed, and after him a voice beneath the mountain that had held a monkey king for centuries.

## Journey to the West — The Dragon Prisoner and the Monkey Beneath the Mountain

Guanyin and Hui'an were traveling east when they heard cries above them. A dragon was suspended in the air, bound and awaiting execution. He had the shape and voice of a powerful creature of the waters, yet he could do no more than call to someone passing by. Guanyin stopped and asked why he had been condemned.

He said he was the son of the Dragon King of the Western Sea. A fire had broken out in his father's palace, damaging a precious pearl. His father had complained of his conduct to the heavenly court, and the Jade Emperor had ordered him beaten and sentenced to death. He had already received the blows. Now he waited for the sentence to be completed.

Guanyin did not promise a rescue that she could not grant. She went to Heaven and petitioned the Jade Emperor himself. The scripture seeker, she explained, would have to travel a great distance through difficult country. A human monk would need a strong mount. If the condemned dragon were spared, he could serve as the pilgrim's horse, and by that service turn punishment into merit. The emperor consented. The dragon was released and sent to a deep ravine to wait for the traveler. Later, when the moment arrived, he would take the form of a white horse.

The detail is easy to miss among the bright battles of Wukong's rebellion. The pilgrim's future mount was a prisoner rescued from execution. So were several of his companions, in different ways. The great journey was being staffed out of Heaven's failures, people and creatures whose strength had become a danger without a new purpose.

The travelers went on until a distant mountain glowed with signs of supernatural power. Hui'an recognized it. This was the Mountain of Five Elements. At its summit the Buddha had placed the sacred written formula, and beneath it lay Sun Wukong. Guanyin paused there. She remembered the monkey who had caused the banquet scandal and the war in Heaven. She looked upon the great rock and spoke of the captive whose fame had once shaken the court.

A furious voice rose from below. Who, it demanded, was standing over the prison reciting its occupant's disgrace? Wukong had not lost the ability to hear an insult. He called up at the unseen visitor, and Guanyin descended to face him. The monkey recognized her at once. He greeted her with sudden enthusiasm and asked whether she had come to set him free.

He had been under the mountain for more than five hundred years. His strength remained, but he had no use for it. He could see a little of the world and hear the guardians, yet there was no way to reach the forests of his home or the monkeys who had once shouted his title. He begged Guanyin to speak on his behalf. He wanted to be released.

Guanyin knew better than to free him merely because he asked. This was the monkey who had wrecked a heavenly feast, stolen from the furnaces, and tried to seize the imperial throne. If he returned to his old habits, his freedom would become trouble for every place he passed. She told him that his former conduct had been serious, and that release without amendment would accomplish little.

Wukong answered that he had had time to regret his folly. He would be willing to cultivate himself properly if someone opened a path for him. These were the words Guanyin had been waiting to hear. She explained the mission. Somewhere in the Tang lands a pilgrim would be chosen to travel west and obtain the true scriptures. When that pilgrim reached the mountain, Wukong could become his disciple. He would protect the human monk and accompany him through the trials of the journey. His labor would make atonement possible.

Wukong accepted before she had finished. He wanted to go; he wanted a road, a purpose, and the chance to use his arms and legs. Guanyin asked his name and offered to give him a religious one. Wukong was pleased to explain that he already had a name from his former teacher: Sun Wukong. She was delighted that the name belonged to the same pattern as those of the other future disciples. She need not rename him.

She did not lift the mountain. That was not the arrangement. The pilgrim who would become his master was to come, meet him, and release him. The monkey would then owe his freedom to the relationship he was about to enter. Guanyin promised that the opportunity was on its way, but she could not tell him when. Wukong settled back under the stone, not comfortable, perhaps, but no longer without a reason to endure it.

The two travelers came at last to Chang'an. They did not enter as splendid supernatural visitors. Guanyin and Hui'an assumed the appearances of poor, afflicted monks, their clothes ragged and their bodies marked by disease. A city that admitted ministers in silk might turn aside a pair of shabby wanderers. That was useful to them. They were not there to announce themselves but to watch.

They found shelter at a small shrine of the local earth deity. The deity knew who they were and hurried to welcome them; neighboring protective spirits came as well. Guanyin told them to keep silent. She had come with a commission from the Buddha and needed somewhere to wait while she searched for a person of the right character. The local gods withdrew, leaving the travelers in their humble hiding place.

In Chang'an, other lives were already taking shape. One of them belonged to a young scholar whose hopes lay in a court examination. He could not know that decisions in a western paradise, a river full of bones, a monster's mountain cave, a dragon's prison, and a rock under which a monkey waited would eventually converge upon a child of his family.

The scripture mission had reached the east. The person who would carry it back west had not yet been named.

## Journey to the West — Chen Guangrui and the Crossing at Hong River

A young scholar named Chen E, known by his courtesy name Chen Guangrui, lived with his mother in the Tang lands. The emperor had announced an examination for men who hoped to serve in government, and Chen wanted to attempt it. His mother, Zhang, was proud of him. She urged him to travel carefully, sit the examination, and return as soon as the result was known. A scholar's household could rise or fall by the judgment of examiners, and the journey itself was not free of risk.

In Chang'an, Chen completed the examinations successfully and was awarded the highest distinction. For several days he rode through the city as the new champion scholar. He had scarcely begun to enjoy his success when the next event arrived. In the house of the minister Yin Kaishan, a young woman named Yin Wenjiao was choosing a husband according to a public custom: she cast an embroidered ball from a high decorated platform. The ball struck Chen's official hat. Her family recognized the newly successful scholar, and celebrations were arranged. Chen and Wenjiao married.

Soon the emperor appointed Chen to a post in Jiangzhou. The newly married couple were to travel away from the capital and take up the duties of a provincial official. Chen did not forget his mother. They went to collect her, intending that she should share their new home. But during the journey she fell ill at an inn, and the order requiring Chen to assume his post left him with little time. He could not remain indefinitely. After taking advice from his mother and wife, he arranged for Zhang to stay until she was strong enough to travel. He left money and promised to return for her when the weather improved.

At the inn he had performed a small act that would matter enormously. He found a fisherman offering a golden carp for sale. He bought it, thinking it might provide food for his sick mother. Then he noticed something striking about its eyes and began to suspect that this fish was no ordinary creature. Rather than have it cooked, he carried it to the river and released it into the water. His mother approved when he told her what he had done.

Chen and Wenjiao continued to the Hong River crossing. There they found a boat under the charge of two men, Liu Hong and Li Biao. The ferrymen helped carry the baggage. The river was a stage on a familiar administrative journey: the scholar had his appointment, his travel papers, and the assumption that an officer of the Tang state could safely reach his post. But in the boat Liu Hong saw Wenjiao and decided he wanted her for himself.

At a lonely part of the river, after dark, Liu Hong and his companion attacked. They killed Chen's servant and then Chen. They threw both bodies into the water. Wenjiao saw her husband murdered before her eyes and moved to throw herself in after him. Liu Hong seized her, threatened to kill her if she refused him, and made it clear that he intended to claim both her and her husband's position. She had no protectors aboard and no path to escape. She submitted to his demands under the threat of violence. It was not a new marriage of her choice; it was captivity imposed after murder.

Liu Hong dressed in Chen's clothes, took the official papers, and carried Wenjiao onward to Jiangzhou. There the local clerks and officials welcomed the new magistrate, believing the man before them was the examination champion appointed by the emperor. They congratulated him and gave him the courtesies due to an administrator. The killer was occupying his victim's office in public. Wenjiao had to appear beside him while knowing exactly how he had obtained it.

In the river below, the two bodies did not receive the same fate. The servant's remains drifted away. Chen's body stayed beneath the water and was discovered by a patrol from the underwater palace. The guards carried their report to the Dragon King. When the Dragon King examined the dead scholar, he recognized him. The golden carp that Chen had bought and released had been the Dragon King himself, traveling in another form. Chen had saved his life when he might have taken it, and now the man who had shown mercy was lying dead in the water.

The Dragon King summoned the scholar's spirit. Chen told him about the examination, the appointment, the ferry crossing, the murder, and the loss of his wife. The Dragon King would not let the debt go unpaid. He ordered Chen's body preserved with a pearl that kept it from decaying, and he offered the scholar's spirit a temporary place in his underwater court. When the time came, Chen might return to life and meet his family again.

For Wenjiao in Jiangzhou, none of this was visible. The man who had killed her husband wore the clothing of an official, sat in the public hall, and received the respect that ought to have belonged to Chen. She could only maintain the appearances demanded of her while holding fast to the knowledge of what had happened. Worse was to come. She was pregnant with Chen's child, a fact that made her life and the child's life even more dangerous.

The world's justice seemed to have failed at a river crossing. Its remedy was beginning in a hidden palace beneath the water. But it would have to reach back into the human world through the one person Liu Hong had not yet learned to fear: an infant who had not been born.

## Journey to the West — The River Child and the Blood Letter

In Jiangzhou, Yin Wenjiao lived in a house where everyone addressed the man who had murdered her husband as the rightful official. Liu Hong had taken Chen Guangrui's clothes, papers, and place at the government's table. He had also forced Wenjiao to remain with him. She could scarcely reveal what had happened without risking her life. The pregnancy she carried offered hope that Chen's family had not been entirely destroyed, but it also placed a child in the path of the murderer.

One day while Liu Hong was away on official business, Wenjiao gave birth to a boy. In her distress she heard a supernatural warning. The messenger told her that the child had a great future and that Liu Hong would kill him if she failed to protect him. Her true husband had not been forgotten: the Dragon King had preserved Chen, and the family would one day have a chance to recover its scattered members. But the immediate danger was real. She must act while she could.

Liu Hong returned and saw the infant. He wanted the child drowned. Wenjiao pleaded for a delay until the following day, and the danger passed for a few hours. In the morning, official duties drew Liu Hong away again. She had a narrow opening. There was no family member she could trust in the house, no safe path to the capital, and no way to defend a newborn against an armed man. She decided on a terrible form of rescue: she would send her son away and surrender his fate to the river.

She bit her finger and used the blood to write the story of his origins, his father's name, and the violence that had brought them to this place. She placed the written account with a garment of her own. In the version of the tale preserved in this chapter, she also marks the child by biting away the small toe on his left foot, so that she could recognize him if they ever met again. It is a harsh and distressing detail, and the narrative offers it as a desperate means of identification in a world where papers could be stolen and faces changed with age.

She found a wooden board at the riverbank, fastened the baby to it as securely as she could, and placed the written message with him. Then she pushed the little raft onto the water. She could not know whether it would overturn, strike a rock, or drift toward a stranger who might show mercy. She watched the current draw the child away. Then she returned to the house where Liu Hong would soon expect to find her.

Downriver, a Buddhist monk named Faming was living at Gold Mountain Monastery. While sitting in meditation he heard the cry of an infant. He went to the river and found the child upon the board. The message told him that this was no abandoned child without a family but an infant deliberately placed beyond a killer's reach. Faming took him into his care. He named the boy Jiangliu, the River Current, and arranged for him to be nursed and raised. He kept the blood letter safe.

Years passed in the monastery. The boy grew among monks, familiar with the chanting of scriptures, the sound of bells, and the daily work of religious life. When he was old enough, Faming gave him the religious name Xuanzang. The child who had been sent away on a wooden board became a disciplined young monk, intelligent and eager in his study. He knew the men who had cared for him, but he did not know his parents.

At eighteen, he was drawn into an argument with another monk. A disagreement about religious matters grew sharp, and the other man attacked him where he was most vulnerable: he mocked Xuanzang for not knowing who his father and mother were. Xuanzang could not answer with a name. He went to Faming and begged to know the truth. He had grown up believing that the monastery was his entire world. Yet the accusation had exposed a silence around his beginnings.

Faming had waited for this question. He took out the box in which he had preserved the garment and letter. Xuanzang read the account his mother had written. He learned of his father's success and marriage, of the river crossing, the murder, Liu Hong's seizure of the official position, and the danger that had required Wenjiao to place her baby upon the water. The details that had seemed to belong to no one now belonged to him.

Xuanzang wept. He wanted to find his mother and see that she was safe. He also wanted to honor the father whose face he had never seen and the teacher who had saved him. Faming advised caution. A young monk could not simply walk into Jiangzhou and accuse the powerful official who governed it. He must go as a mendicant collecting offerings, enter the household without announcing his purpose, and use the letter when he found a private moment.

So Xuanzang left the monastery, not yet as the pilgrim bound for the western scriptures, but as a son searching for the woman who had set him on the river. He carried the garment and the account of his origins. In Jiangzhou, Liu Hong's authority still appeared unshaken. Yet one of his intended victims was approaching the gate in a monk's robe, carrying no weapon beyond a piece of writing his crime had failed to erase.

## Journey to the West — The Missing Toe and the Family Reunited

Xuanzang arrived at the Jiangzhou government residence dressed as a monk seeking alms. By good fortune, Liu Hong was away. Yin Wenjiao had dreamed of a broken moon becoming round again, and when she heard a monk calling at the gate, she came to see who had arrived. He said he was a disciple of Faming at Gold Mountain Monastery. She offered him food and watched his face and manner. There was something in the young man that reminded her of the husband she had lost.

They spoke carefully. At last he showed the garment and the blood-written letter, and she recognized her own account. The river had carried her child to safety. Eighteen years had passed without certainty that he had survived. Now he stood before her. They wept together, but they could not remain together openly. If Liu Hong discovered the meeting, he might kill them both.

Wenjiao devised a reason to visit Gold Mountain Monastery. She told Liu Hong that an old vow required her to provide shoes for monks. He ordered the people of the district to produce them; in the novel, even her attempt to obtain a safe meeting place passes through his control of the community's labor. Wenjiao traveled to the monastery with the offerings. There she and Xuanzang could meet without his household watching. She asked to see his left foot, and the missing toe confirmed his identity as the child she had sent away. The wound that had marked their separation became evidence of their reunion.

Yet their private recognition was not enough. She told him about his father's mother, Zhang, whom Chen had left at the inn on the way to Jiangzhou. She also told him where to find his maternal grandfather, Yin Kaishan, a high minister at the Tang court. He must go to them and show the letters. Only someone with the authority of the capital could confront the thief who sat in Chen's official chair.

Xuanzang began his long work of reconnecting the family. He went first to the inn and found his paternal grandmother, now poor and nearly blind, still waiting in the place where her son had once promised to return. She had been left behind by an illness, not abandoned by choice, but the years had turned that distinction into a lonely fact. Xuanzang spoke to her, told her who he was, and learned what the loss had cost her. He arranged help and continued to Chang'an.

At the house of Yin Kaishan, the young monk presented his mother's message. The minister had lost a daughter to distance and silence; now he learned that she had been held captive by the killer of her husband. The family account fitted a grave political offense: a man had murdered an appointed official and occupied his post under false papers. Yin Kaishan brought the matter before the emperor and received authority to arrest the criminal.

An armed party went toward Jiangzhou. Liu Hong, who had for so long appeared untouchable to those around him, was seized. The account does not allow him to negotiate away what he had done. The officials investigated, and his crimes were exposed. He was executed, and the family went to the river to perform rites for Chen Guangrui. In the old tale, the vengeance is brutal; the villain's body is made part of the sacrifice at the water. This is the story's justice in its own terms, not an act the retelling needs to celebrate.

Beneath the same water, the Dragon King had kept his promise. Chen's spirit had served in the underwater palace, and his body had been preserved. When the family came to the river, the Dragon King sent him back. A body floated to the surface, and before their eyes the dead man moved. He stood up. He spoke. Chen Guangrui had returned from the kingdom of water to meet a wife who had survived by silence and a son who had grown to adulthood without him.

For a moment it seemed that every division could be healed. The scholar had his name again; Liu Hong could no longer steal his place; the rescued child stood beside his parents. They went to find Zhang at the inn and then returned to Chang'an. The emperor restored Chen to office. The reunion feast was celebrated with gratitude and amazement, a gathering made possible by an act of mercy toward a golden carp long before anyone knew what it would cost to lose that mercy.

But there is a final sentence in this chapter that prevents the reunion from being simple. After the restoration of Chen and his family, Wenjiao later takes her own life. The narrative does not give her the untroubled future it gives her husband. It had already forced her to endure murder, captivity, sexual coercion, separation from her baby, and long years of concealment. The joy of the public reunion cannot undo those events. The woman's end remains part of the tale even after the reunion feast.

Xuanzang does not take his restored family as a reason to abandon religious life. He chooses to remain a monk. The imperial court honors his father's learning, and Xuanzang continues his practice under the name he received at Gold Mountain. He has learned what happened to the River Child. He has answered the questions of kinship and origins. His life now appears settled in a monastery, with his future defined by the Buddhist texts he studies.

Far away, Guanyin is still waiting to find a scripture seeker. Xuanzang has not yet met her in that role. A dragon's mercy, a blood letter, a wounded toe, and a monastery beside the river have brought him into the world and carried him to adulthood. The next events belong to the emperor, whose own encounter with death will call the young monk to a task greater than any he has imagined.
