# The Decameron — Teodoro and Violante

In Trapani, a gentleman named Amerigo buys several boys from sea raiders who have taken captives in the eastern Mediterranean. One boy seems more accomplished than the others. His name is Teodoro, but because his new owner believes him to be Turkish, he is baptized and given the name Pietro. Teodoro grows up alongside the master's children, wins his confidence, is eventually freed, and becomes a trusted manager of the household. But the freedom granted to a formerly enslaved servant is not the same as social equality.

Amerigo's daughter Violante and Teodoro fall in love. They hesitate to speak; both understand what it would mean for the daughter of the house to be found in a relationship with the man once owned by her father. A storm gives them privacy. They take shelter in a damaged building, confess their affection, and become lovers. Later Violante realizes that she is pregnant. Teodoro fears that discovery will bring his death. She persuades him to stay, promising to conceal his part in the affair.

The secret fails. Violante gives birth, and her father unexpectedly hears the newborn's cries. He demands to know whose child it is. Under threat of death she names Teodoro. Amerigo has the young man arrested and tortured; a magistrate orders him scourged through the city and hanged. The father also sends a servant to Violante with a choice between poison and a sword and commands that her infant be killed. These are not exaggerated predictions by frightened lovers. They are orders within the narrated action.

As Teodoro is being marched toward execution, three Armenian ambassadors look down from an inn. An older man notices a distinctive mark on the condemned youth's chest. He calls out the name Teodoro in Armenian. The prisoner recognizes the language and tells what little he remembers of his childhood. The older man, Fineo, realizes that his son, abducted years earlier, stands before him. He intervenes to stop the execution.

The revelation transforms Amerigo's reasoning. The man he believed an inferior dependent is the child of an honored foreign nobleman. A marriage that would have disgraced the house becomes acceptable. Teodoro chooses Violante; she declares her wish to marry him. The family recovers the infant and celebrates the match. Lauretta carries them eventually to the home of Teodoro's recovered family, where they live in safety.

This is one of the day's sharpest exposures of rank. Nothing about Teodoro's conduct changes when Fineo recognizes him. He has the same knowledge, affection, and courage before the intervention. What changes is the information the authorities possess about his birth. At one moment they are prepared to torture and kill him. At the next they regard him as a fitting husband. The story's machinery of recognition makes the social contradiction visible.

There is also a difference between escape from punishment and justice. Amerigo's willingness to destroy his daughter and grandchild is not vindicated by the wedding. The reader can welcome their survival without calling the threatened deaths reasonable. The romance resolves its crisis by discovering an equality of status that was hidden, not by abolishing the rule that made the crisis possible.

Teodoro's two names tell the whole story in miniature. Pietro is the name bestowed by the household that purchased him. Teodoro is the name called by the father who thought him lost. The second name rescues him. It is a powerful conclusion, and an unsettling measure of the world he has survived.
