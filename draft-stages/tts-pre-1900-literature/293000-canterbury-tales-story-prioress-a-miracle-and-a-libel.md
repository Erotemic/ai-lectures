# The Canterbury Tales — The Prioress’s Tale, a Miracle and a Libel

The Prioress begins with an invocation to the Virgin Mary and the hope that a simple, humble voice may praise her worthily. Her tale takes place in an unnamed Asian city where a small Christian schoolboy learns to sing a Marian hymn. He does not fully understand the Latin words, but loves the sound and the devotion they represent. He repeats the hymn while walking through a Jewish neighborhood. The tale then makes a false and dangerous collective accusation: it depicts Jews as conspiring to kill the child because he sings a Christian song.

The child is murdered and his body hidden. His mother searches desperately. A miracle permits the dead child to continue singing, leading people to discover him. A religious procession gathers around the body, and the child explains that the Virgin Mary placed something upon his tongue so that he could continue her hymn after death. The story ends in the language of wonder and innocent devotion, but also depicts the punishment of Jews as if a community could justly be held responsible for a fabricated religious crime.

That accusation is not a report of something Jews did. It belongs to the medieval Christian tradition of the blood libel, the false allegation that Jews murdered Christian children for ritual or religious purposes. Such stories contributed to persecution and violence. Jews had been expelled from England in 1290, long before Chaucer wrote. The tale's imagined Jewish community is a hostile stereotype inherited from European Christian culture, not a representation of actual Jewish life. These facts are necessary for understanding the tale rather than modern qualifications appended to it from outside.

The Prioress's voice makes the material more troubling. In the General Prologue she seemed exquisitely sensitive to suffering animals and concerned with elegant manners. Her tale lavishes tenderness upon a child but withholds humanity from the people it names as his enemies. A reader can examine how the style of gentle devotion is used to make exclusion feel pious. Some critics see Chaucer as exposing the Prioress's narrow sympathy; others argue that the story participates directly in the prejudice it depicts. The presence of a fictional narrator does not automatically absolve its author or erase the harm of the inherited genre.

The narrative is also built as a Marian miracle story. The miraculous singing of a murdered child transforms grief into proof of heavenly favor. To a medieval Christian audience such a story might have been understood as praise of Mary's intervention. But we can distinguish the devotional literary mechanism from the false charge on which the plot depends. Recognizing how the story is constructed helps us account for its cultural force without pretending that its accusation is credible.

We have now heard a sequence in which moral language has been turned toward incompatible purposes. The Pardoner used a true warning against greed to steal. The Prioress uses the language of innocence and compassion to tell a story with violent anti-Jewish content. Chaucer's collection gives us no simple guarantee that a moving style serves a just cause. The next speaker is Chaucer as a character, whose attempt at a harmless rhyming romance is so bad that the Host refuses to let him finish. The collision of stories will continue, but laughter at bad verse must not make us forget what the Prioress's verse asked its audience to believe.
