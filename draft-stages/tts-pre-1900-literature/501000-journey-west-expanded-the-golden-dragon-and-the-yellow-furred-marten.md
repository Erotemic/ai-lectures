# The Golden Dragon and the Yellow-Furred Marten

Yellow Wind King came out armed and angry. Wukong's staff had damaged his gate, and the monkey stood in the open with no visible allies. The king assumed that his earlier storm had failed only because he had not used enough force. This time he would blow the challenger from the mountain entirely.

He lunged with the three-pronged fork. Wukong turned aside, drove the iron staff toward his shoulder, and forced him to step back. For a few exchanges they fought exactly as they had before. The king still had the strength and skill to meet his enemy's blows. Wukong did not call out to the sky or reveal that Lingji was present.

The demon drew away from the staff and turned his head toward the direction from which he summoned his wind. His mouth opened. He was about to release the gust that had darkened the heavens, sent Wukong's doubles spinning, and wounded the Great Sage's eyes.

From above came the bodhisattva's staff.

As it fell, the weapon changed. The shape of polished wood became a golden dragon with eight claws. The dragon plunged through the air and caught the demon before the storm could form. Its claws closed around him. It lifted him, struck him against the rocks, and held him there until the commanding figure in armor was gone.

In his place lay a small yellow-furred marten, shuddering in the dragon's grasp.

Wukong advanced with the iron staff. He had crossed the mountain twice, watched his master vanish, fought the king and his officer, and suffered the wind that threatened to put out his sight. He was ready to finish the matter in a single blow.

Lingji stopped him. The creature would not be killed on the ridge. It was to be taken before the Buddha, who would judge the violation of the terms under which it had once been spared.

The bodhisattva explained the history. The marten had lived near the sacred mountain of Vulture Peak, where the Buddha taught. It had stolen clear oil from a glass lamp, dimming the light. Fearing punishment from the guardian warriors, it fled and acquired the powers of a demon. The Buddha had ordered Lingji to restrain it. The first capture had ended in mercy on condition that it cause no further harm. Now the animal had returned to predation and taken the scripture pilgrim prisoner.

The king had appeared before Wukong as a richly armored sovereign. He had commanded troops, accepted a captured human as food, and used a wind that made the whole world tremble. The small body now exposed by the dragon did not make his crimes smaller. It showed how much power a creature of modest origin had gathered, and how readily that power had been used against the weak.

Wukong lowered his staff. He thanked Lingji for the help. The bodhisattva departed with the captive, taking him westward to the place where judgment would be made. The great wind was gone. The cave remained, but its ruler would no longer answer from behind the gate.

Wukong went up the mountain to find Bajie. The younger disciple was still guarding the horse and baggage where he had been told to wait. He had seen the clouds move and heard the distant sound of combat, but he did not know its outcome. When he recognized his senior brother's voice, he hurried out to ask what had happened.

The Great Sage reported the capture. Lingji had used the flying-dragon staff, the feared king had been revealed as a yellow-furred marten, and the bodhisattva had carried him away. Bajie was delighted. For once the news was wholly good. Their enemy's power had been broken, and the way to their master stood open.

The two entered the cave together. They found its remaining inhabitants: rabbits, foxes, deer, and other creatures who had served the king's predatory household. Wukong and Bajie attacked them with staff and rake. The novel does not turn this into a quiet liberation of prisoners. The disciples kill the creatures within, then pass through the chambers toward the rear garden. Their rescue involves real violence, even after the principal demon has been taken away.

Xuanzang was still tied to the wind-stilling post. When he heard their voices, he raised his head. Bajie hurried to help free him while Wukong assured him that the king would not return. The monk wanted to know how they had succeeded when the power of the Yellow Wind had been so great. Wukong recounted the fight, the injury to his eyes, the unexpected medicine, the overheard name, and the visit to Lingji. His master listened with relief. He thanked the disciples whose persistence had brought him out alive.

The cave held food suitable for the monks. They ate before leaving, not because the danger had been forgotten but because Xuanzang needed strength after his confinement and the road offered no guarantee of a meal. Bajie, who had been thinking about supper when they first approached Yellow Wind Ridge, could finally eat without being accused of longing for Gao Village.

The horse was brought from its hiding place. The bags were repacked. Xuanzang mounted, and the brothers took their places, one scouting ahead and the other shouldering the burden. They left the broken entrance of Yellow Wind Cave behind and returned to the western road.

The three travelers had faced their first great danger together since Bajie's recruitment. The new disciple had killed the tiger who took their master and had guarded the horse while Wukong sought help. Wukong had fought, been injured, accepted medicine, gathered intelligence, and called upon an authority whose power matched the enemy's. Xuanzang had endured captivity without being able to free himself.

They were still far from the scriptures. But the road was open again. Ahead lay other mountains and, beyond them, the river monster whose coming had been foretold at Pagoda Mountain.

## Non-spoken source and editorial notes

Source: [*Journey to the West*, chapter 21](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E8%A5%BF%E9%81%8A%E8%A8%98/%E7%AC%AC021%E5%9B%9E) (`cl-jtw-ch21`). Lingji staff becomes an eight-clawed gold dragon and reveals a yellow-furred marten. Lingji prevents Wukong from killing it and takes it to Buddha for judgment. The disciples kill the cave’s remaining demons and free Xuanzang. Independent English narration from the selected Chinese source; the magical events and characters are fictional, not historical reports.
