# The Princess’s Borrowed Body and the Debt of Silver

Liu Quan returned to the Tang court alive. The emperor had sent him to the underworld with southern melons, and now the messenger stood before him giving a report. The Ten Kings had accepted the offering. They had recognized the emperor's good faith. And Liu Quan, who had gone down burdened by the death of his wife, had met her again and been told that both of them could return to the human world.

There was one part of the account the court could scarcely understand. The wife whom Liu Quan had met in the underworld had no body to return to. The judges had therefore assigned her spirit to the body of the emperor's sister, a princess named Li Yuying, whose life had reached its appointed end. The princess had fallen unconscious and then awakened speaking with the memories and manner of a different woman.

Taizong summoned the royal woman. She came before him with the conduct of someone who belonged to Liu Quan's household, not to the imperial family. Asked whether she knew her husband, she pointed to Liu Quan as naturally as if she had seen him that morning. Liu Quan recognized her words, her memories, and her account of their marriage. But the face before him was not the face of Li Cuilian. He hesitated. It was his wife, and yet it was the princess.

The emperor heard them both and accepted the underworld's arrangement. He sent the pair away with gifts from the princess's possessions, together with a decree relieving them of certain burdens. Husband and wife returned to their children. In the telling, the household is reunited. The miracle is not smooth if one thinks about the body whose owner has been displaced. The novel, however, moves its characters onward: what matters within the episode is that the underworld's promise has been executed and the messenger has returned with evidence of it.

The other debt now came due. Taizong had borrowed money in the city of wrongful death from an account held by a man named Xiang Liang. The emperor ordered a minister to find him and pay the obligation. In Kaifeng the official discovered not a wealthy creditor but a poor man who made a living selling water and pottery with his wife. He had no great stores of silver in his house. When the emperor's representative presented a large payment, Xiang Liang was terrified.

He insisted that he had never loaned the monarch money. To accept wealth that he had not earned would be a dangerous act. The official explained that the loan had been made in the realm of the dead, where Xiang Liang's small offerings to monks and spirits had accumulated as treasure. The poor man's generosity in the living world had made him rich in the underworld. The emperor had borrowed one treasury of that invisible wealth and wished to repay it in silver.

Xiang Liang and his wife refused. They considered the proposed gift far beyond anything they had given. No argument about an invisible ledger could make them comfortable taking the money. The minister reported their refusal, and Taizong found another way to fulfill his obligation: the funds could support religious construction and charitable purposes in the man's name. In this way the emperor honored the debt without forcing the reluctant couple to become rich by command.

Taizong had now heard two extraordinary reports. A spirit could return in another person's body, and offerings that appeared insignificant in the world above could possess value in the world below. More important, he remembered the crowd of spirits who had stopped his own passage near the city of wrongful death. He had promised them a great rite of deliverance. Unlike the melons, that promise could not be carried by one messenger. It needed the religious institutions of the capital, monks, temples, texts, ceremonies, and the participation of the government itself.

He ordered the work begun. Officials would choose monks with learning and discipline. The court would provide support. A great gathering would be organized to bring prayers and teaching to the souls of those who had died without care. The emperor who had previously feared a dragon at his door could now act without turning the next fear into another execution. He would call upon religious knowledge, not merely the force of his soldiers.

Among the monks of the Tang lands was Xuanzang, the River Child who had grown to manhood at Gold Mountain Monastery. The officials had not chosen him yet. He had his own history of loss, a rescue by the river, and a decision to remain in religious life after finding his family. None of that was known to the emperor as the shape of a future pilgrimage. The decree for the ceremony would bring him to the palace, and the search Guanyin had been conducting in secret was about to come to its end.
