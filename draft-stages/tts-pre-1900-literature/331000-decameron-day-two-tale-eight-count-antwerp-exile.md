# The Decameron — The Count of Antwerp in Exile

A trusted nobleman serves the royal household in France. In the tale he is the Count of Antwerp, a man whose office and reputation depend on the belief that he can be entrusted with matters of great importance. The wife of the king's heir desires him. When he refuses her advances, she turns disappointment into accusation. She claims that the count behaved improperly toward her. Because her place in the household gives her words immediate force, his defense is not enough to protect him.

The count must flee. He takes his children away and journeys to England, where he cannot keep them all beside him. He arranges care for them in different places and assumes a lower station. Eventually he finds service in Ireland. The man who once moved among rulers now lives by disguising his rank and working under another name. He can still exercise judgment, loyalty, and skill, but he has lost the public recognition that formerly gave those qualities their reward.

Years pass. His children grow older, separated from the story of their own origins. By a chain of encounters and changes of fortune, they find security and promising futures. Their father returns to England without revealing himself, observing how much has happened while he was absent. His long exile has made him a stranger to his former life. At the same time, the woman's false allegation has never stopped being the cause of his disgrace. The accusation has endured longer than the desire that produced it.

At last the queen who made the charge becomes seriously ill. Confronting death, she confesses that she lied. The truth reaches the king, and measures are taken to find and restore the injured man. The count's loyalty and innocence are publicly acknowledged, his standing returns, and the separated family is reunited. The outcome is not achieved by a brilliant escape from one single danger. It takes many years and several lives moving through different countries before the false story loses its power.

Elissa's plot is built on a sharp injustice: the word of someone close to the throne can destroy the life of a person who has committed no offense. It borrows an old narrative pattern of rejected desire turned into false accusation. That pattern should not be confused with a general truth about women's testimony. In this particular fiction, a powerful accuser abuses a position inside the ruling family. The reader has been shown her motives, so the reader can recognize the gap between what the court believes and what happened.

The children and the father's disguises extend the same question into a second generation. Who are people when titles, names, and histories have been stripped away? What makes recognition possible again? Fortune places the count among strangers, but character is the thread by which the tale guides him back. The long-awaited confession repairs his public reputation. It cannot give him back the time that accusation and exile consumed. The happy ending names a restoration; the story has made us feel how precarious the conditions of that restoration were.
