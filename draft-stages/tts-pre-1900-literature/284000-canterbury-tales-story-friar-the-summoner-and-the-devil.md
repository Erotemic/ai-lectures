# The Canterbury Tales — The Friar’s Tale, a Summoner and the Devil

The Wife of Bath has scarcely finished when the Friar promises to entertain the company with a story about a summoner. The real Summoner in the group protests. Officials of the church courts have the authority to summon people accused of religious or sexual offences, and their power can be a profitable opportunity for extortion. The Friar knows this, and he knows the Summoner cannot stop him from speaking. Their quarrel gives the story the feel of an attack made before the victim's eyes.

A summoner sets out on the road looking for a person from whom he can collect money. He encounters a young yeoman dressed in green. The stranger seems friendly and familiar with the pleasures of cheating the public. The two talk as professional colleagues. The summoner frankly confesses that he threatens people with court action and accepts payment for overlooking their offences. What matters to him is not whether someone has sinned but what can be extracted. He is delighted to discover that his new companion appears to follow the same trade.

The yeoman eventually admits that he is a devil. The summoner is startled, but not enough to break off their acquaintance. He asks how devils seize their victims and whether human curses have real power. The devil explains that words of anger are not always genuine gifts to Hell; intention matters. The point is illustrated when a carter curses his horses as they struggle to pull a wagon. The devil refuses to claim them, because the man does not sincerely wish their ruin. The summoner's attention does not improve. He sees a possible partner in professional exploitation, not a warning about his own soul.

They reach the house of a poor widow. The summoner demands payment and threatens to carry off her household possessions if she refuses. She protests that he has no rightful claim, that he has already taken money from her and that she has little left with which to live. He continues to press. Finally she curses him to Hell, with the money and goods he would steal. The devil asks whether she means it. She answers that she does, unless he will repent. The summoner refuses. The devil takes him away.

The tale is satisfying as a fantasy of justice: a corrupt official who uses religious authority to injure the vulnerable is delivered to the punishment he has effectively chosen. Its humor comes from the gap between the summoner's professional self-confidence and the audience's foreknowledge that his companion knows more about spiritual prosecution than he does. The widow's words are not powerful because she has occult knowledge. They become effective when a victim speaks truthfully against a man who has lost the capacity to repent.

But this is also an attack made by a Friar, not a neutral reformer. The real Summoner has every reason to believe that religious professionals can be just as greedy as secular officials. He is now entitled by the rules of the contest to answer. The road creates a rough kind of debate: no one speaker can establish the entire truth about church corruption, but each can make the concealed practices of the others audible. The Summoner's response will be more bodily and more insulting than the Friar's devil story.
