# Middlemarch — The Widow and the Wife

The widow and the wife of Book Six are Dorothea Brooke and Rosamond Lydgate, though other marriages cast their shadows over the title. Dorothea has recovered the right to shape her future, but everyone around her offers advice about what a woman of means ought to do. Rosamond has the marriage she desired, yet the imagined comfort of that marriage is breaking against accounts and obligations.

Dorothea remains at Lowick, sensitive to the contempt implied by Casaubon's codicil. She has money, opportunities for charitable action, and a family eager to keep her from scandal. None of those resources answers her deeper question: what kind of life will make her capacity to help other people real? Her attachment to Will Ladislaw becomes increasingly difficult to ignore. Will wants a vocation, not simply the financial shelter of a wealthy woman. The codicil turns his wish to be independent into a problem of public honor.

Fred Vincy begins to change by doing the thing he has resisted: regular work. Caleb Garth gives him an opportunity in land management and expects him to learn from practical tasks. The novel treats these tasks seriously—surveying, negotiating, dealing with tenants, and understanding a landscape beyond the drawing room. Fred's willingness to work for Mary's respect begins to make him worthy of it. Mary is not a magical reward for reform; she continues to make decisions and to insist that Fred's choices are his own.

One episode involving the proposed railway brings the landowning and working worlds into direct contact. Surveyors are threatened by local people suspicious of the line. Fred's intervention helps prevent a confrontation. The railroad represents change, but it is not presented as a simple blessing from intelligent elites. The people whose fields and roads may be altered have reasons to fear it. Fred is learning to hear those reasons even when they complicate an engineer's plan.

In the Lydgate household, debt mounts. Rosamond maintains the habits of status and refuses to recognize the scale of the problem. When she rides despite medical advice during pregnancy, she loses the child. The event is devastating; it does not prove that Lydgate's authority over her was complete or that her choice can be reduced to a single character flaw. Their marriage now contains grief, fear, anger, and a widening inability to speak honestly about money.

Lydgate makes compromises that would once have seemed impossible to him. He dreams of medical discoveries, but the household requires money before any discovery earns a reputation. His professional judgment is also increasingly exposed to local factions. He can diagnose illness without being able to diagnose the social machinery that will decide whether he succeeds.

Will Ladislaw learns more of his own family's history and the dishonest fortune behind Bulstrode's present power. Bulstrode tries to use money to make Will accept a morally compromised settlement. Will refuses. The gesture matters because he is not wealthy, and the refusal may cost him an easier future. It also brings into view a woman whose past exclusion from a family inheritance helped make Bulstrode rich. The consequences of financial deception reach descendants, not merely the people present when it occurred.

Dorothea and Rosamond are sometimes set against each other by readers as the morally generous woman and the vain one. Eliot's method is more demanding. Rosamond's choices are often selfish, but her training has taught her that a wife should obtain status through refinement. Dorothea has a capacity for self-questioning that her surroundings frequently interpret as eccentric. Both women act within an economy of marriage, inheritance, and reputation. The material differences between their situations are as important as their temperaments.

By the end of this movement, Fred has begun earning his future. Lydgate is in danger of losing his. Dorothea has recovered a legal independence she never sought through widowhood, and Will has rejected a tainted offer. These trajectories will intersect when Bulstrode's concealed past returns with immediate consequences. The next book will make the costs of silence public.
