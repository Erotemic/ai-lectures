# Moby-Dick — Story: Ishmael and Queequeg Find a Ship

Ishmael begins with a decision that is both practical and difficult to explain. When ordinary life feels intolerable, when a dark mood makes city streets dangerous to his peace of mind, he chooses the sea. He does not announce a plan to become a hero. He wants a change of scene and a way to put distance between himself and his thoughts. He has sailed on merchant ships before, but now he intends to go whaling. He will receive a share of the voyage's profits instead of the ordinary wages of a merchant sailor. This financial arrangement matters: the coming enterprise will risk human bodies in pursuit of oil, bone, and money.

His journey brings him to New Bedford, where the whaling economy is visible in houses, shops, churches, and the men who pass through the streets. At the Spouter-Inn, the landlord offers him a shared bed. Ishmael's roommate is absent and, according to the landlord, has been trying to sell a shrunken head. When the stranger returns, his tattoos, his harpoon, and his unfamiliar religious practices frighten Ishmael. The man's name is Queequeg. He is a harpooneer from a fictional Pacific island kingdom, trained as a future ruler before leaving home to see the Christian world. Ishmael initially sees him through the prejudices of his own society. Within a day, the supposed savage proves a more generous companion than many people calling themselves civilized.

Their friendship becomes the first decisive relationship of the novel. They share food, tobacco, and a bed; they converse despite the difficulty of understanding each other's languages and customs. The intimacy is sometimes comic and sometimes moving. Queequeg demonstrates genuine courage when he saves a man who has mocked him and then falls into the water. Ishmael learns that a person's character cannot be determined by clothing, origin, or the familiar labels of the shore. The lesson will matter when we meet a ship commanded by a celebrated Christian captain whose moral judgments are much harder to defend.

In a chapel devoted to seamen, Ishmael sees memorial tablets for people lost at sea. The families who know their dead will not return live with a form of grief that offers no grave and no recovered body. The preacher, Father Mapple, arrives dramatically, climbs to his pulpit, and delivers a sermon on Jonah. Jonah is ordered to speak an uncomfortable truth, attempts to flee by ship, and is swallowed by a great fish. The sermon asks what it means to obey a calling when obedience is terrifying. This is not simply advance warning that Ahab will hunt a whale. It raises a question that will follow several characters: how should a person tell the difference between duty, fear, and the desire to make his own will seem divine?

Ishmael and Queequeg travel to Nantucket to find a whaling ship. They examine several vessels and choose the Pequod, named for a Native American people. The ship's owners include Captain Peleg, an old seaman who speaks bluntly, and Bildad, a Quaker whose religious language does not prevent stern bargaining. They negotiate the fractions of the future catch that will be allotted to Ishmael and Queequeg. The unequal shares reveal how experience, status, and control of property shape life aboard ship before anyone raises a sail.

The captain is absent. Ishmael is told that Ahab commands the vessel, that a great whale has torn away part of his leg, and that he is recovering from a dark state of mind. His absence is oddly theatrical: he becomes a presence through what other people say. A wandering stranger named Elijah issues warnings that neither Ishmael nor Queequeg can make sense of. His insinuations suggest something wrong with the voyage, but they do not amount to evidence that an actual supernatural curse has been proven. Ishmael hears enough to feel troubled and not enough to turn back.

Queequeg practices his private religion and observes a period of ritual seclusion. Ishmael does not always understand it; at one point the attempt to help his friend produces a comic alarm. Yet the story does not ask us to accept either man's religious judgments without examination. Ishmael has begun by fearing Queequeg and ends by treating him as a brother. On a ship assembled from many peoples and faiths, that change is already significant.

The Pequod sails on Christmas Day. The owners remain ashore, and the crew heads into a world where the economy of whaling, the rituals of command, and one captain's secret purpose will meet. For the moment, Ishmael's hope of escape has become a concrete occupation. He has signed for a share, gained a friend, and boarded a ship. The captain he has not yet met will decide what the voyage is really for.
