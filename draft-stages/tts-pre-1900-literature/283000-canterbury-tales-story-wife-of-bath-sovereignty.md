# The Canterbury Tales — The Wife of Bath’s Tale, Sovereignty

The Wife of Bath carries her listeners back to King Arthur's time, when Britain is imagined as a place of fairies, wonders and knights. One knight violates a young woman by rape. The law says he should die. Arthur's queen and her ladies are given authority to decide his fate and offer a condition: he has a year and a day to discover what women most desire. If he returns with the right answer, he may live. The story begins with coercive violence, not with a harmless romantic misunderstanding. That beginning remains part of the meaning of the eventual marriage.

The knight rides everywhere and hears contradictory answers. Some women wish for wealth, others pleasure, fine clothing, praise, freedom, security or a reputation for virtue. A male listener might prefer the neat comfort of one answer. The knight's travel reveals that women are not interchangeable objects from which a general rule can be extracted. As his deadline approaches he meets an old woman in a clearing, where a group of dancing women seems to vanish before his eyes. The old woman promises the answer if he will grant her a future request. He agrees because his life is at stake.

She whispers that women desire sovereignty: the power to govern their own lives and their relations with men. At court the assembled women accept the answer, and the knight is spared. But the old woman now claims the reward. She demands marriage. The knight protests that she is old, poor and unattractive. She insists on his promise and the wedding takes place. The man who was ordered to learn about women's will is horrified when a woman uses that will to bind him.

On their wedding night the old woman challenges his contempt. She argues that true nobility comes from virtue rather than ancestry. Wealth is not proof of goodness, and age may possess experience and fidelity that youth cannot promise. Then she offers him a choice. She can be old and faithful, or young and beautiful with no assurance that he can control her conduct. He cannot decide. At last he yields the decision to her, asking that she choose what seems best. She rewards the surrender by becoming both beautiful and faithful. The story ends with harmony and the Wife's provocative prayer against husbands who refuse to be governed.

There are at least two important ways to hear the ending. Within the fairy-tale economy, the knight learns to abandon the right to command another person, and a marriage becomes possible only when he grants his wife choice. But the transformation also leaves the raped woman from the opening behind. She receives neither voice nor visible justice beyond the threat initially made against the knight. The new wife is rendered desirable according to conventional standards of beauty, although the tale has just challenged those standards. A promise of freedom has been bound to a fantasy of a perfectly accommodating woman.

The Wife of Bath is not presenting a courtroom argument free from contradiction. She is arguing from inside a world where men take women's bodies and voices for granted. Her tale makes power visible by giving it to the queen and an old woman, then makes the result attractive through magic. After it ends, the Friar and Summoner are quick to revive their own quarrel, turning the road from the question of marriage to the business of church officials who profit from other people's fears.
