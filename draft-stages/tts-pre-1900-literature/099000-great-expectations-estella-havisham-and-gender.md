# Great Expectations — Estella, Miss Havisham, and the Uses of Power

Miss Havisham and Estella are sometimes described as if they were one wicked woman and one heartless girl. That judgment follows the young Pip too closely. To understand their relationship, begin with what each can do to the other. Miss Havisham controls a great house, money, and the child's upbringing. Estella receives comfort and status, but not a free emotional education. The woman who was betrayed before marriage tries to prevent future betrayal by turning a child into an instrument of revenge. The child is taught to resist affection so thoroughly that she is then blamed for lacking it. Love and power have been made into competing demands before Pip enters the room.

Satis House keeps the hour of an interrupted wedding visible. Its stale meal and hidden clocks are not merely Gothic decorations. They stage Miss Havisham's refusal to allow time to change the meaning of one injury. Guests, servants, Pip, and Estella enter a drama already written by its owner. Yet control is less complete than it appears. Miss Havisham cannot restore the wedding, cannot create genuine love by command, and cannot guarantee that the pain she directs outward will leave her untouched. Her scenes with Pip are uncomfortable partly because she understands the mechanisms she is using better than he does.

Estella's warnings are the clearest evidence against the story Pip tells himself. She tells him that she cannot offer the affection he seeks. Rather than accept that refusal, he construes it as another step in Miss Havisham's plan. He takes her attention, her beauty, and her occasional kindness as signs of a hidden destiny. His emotional mistake mirrors his financial one: he imagines a plan whose existence would make his wishes inevitable. Estella has been shaped by coercion, but Pip still owes her the courtesy of believing what she says about herself.

Estella marries Drummle, whose cruelty Pip has reason to fear. The marriage is not a proof that Pip was entitled to rescue her by marrying her instead. It reveals the terrible range of choices available to a woman whose guardian has trained her for display and whose social world evaluates marriage through property and prestige. In retrospect, readers can recognize in Estella's refusal of Pip an act of self-knowledge amid severe limitations. The final meeting does not erase the years of damage or announce an effortless cure of mistrust.

Biddy offers a counterexample without becoming a moral measuring device. She helps Pip learn and tends Mrs. Joe after the attack. She sees through some of Pip's excuses and has the courage to ask difficult questions. When Pip returns to propose, she has chosen Joe. The novel grants her a life not held in reserve for the protagonist's eventual awakening. Mrs. Joe herself is harder to read sympathetically: she disciplines the household with violence, and her own labor and insecurity do not excuse it. After her assault, however, her dependency forces others to reveal whether care can persist when gratitude and cooperation are not available.

Molly, Jaggers's housekeeper, is another figure whose history reaches us through the power of men. Her scarred wrists and hands become an intimidating spectacle, while her past legal case and the fate of her child emerge through partial accounts. She was defended by Jaggers and later entered his service. Pip reconstructs the connection between her, Magwitch, and Estella, but the recovery of that genealogy is not the recovery of Molly's whole perspective. The scene raises questions about how a woman's body can be treated as evidence, labor, and property while her own words remain scarce.

Compare the women in the novel by asking not which one is virtuous, but which resources each controls. Miss Havisham has wealth and authority but organizes her life around injury. Estella has social desirability but is bound by a training she did not choose. Biddy has knowledge, work, and judgment but less wealth. Mrs. Joe exercises domestic power in a constrained household. Molly's place is defined by legal and household dependence. They occupy different positions, and Pip's gaze does not reveal them all equally.

A good interpretation must also account for Pip's agency. He is neither the cause of Miss Havisham's revenge nor a helpless recipient of Estella's warnings. He chooses to cherish a fantasy that damages his judgment of Joe and Biddy. When he finally sees the harm of Miss Havisham's training, he begins to recognize that another person's suffering is not proof of his own romantic destiny. The women of Great Expectations reveal the danger of treating affection as an object to be manufactured, won, or withheld in order to control someone else. The novel's ethical hope lies in learning to recognize a person as more than a role in one's private story.
