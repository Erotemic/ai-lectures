# The Decameron — The Lady, the Friar, and the Message

A married woman in Florence longs to meet a young man who lives nearby. She cannot simply invite him into her house without endangering her reputation. The person who can carry information between two people without causing suspicion is a friar, and the friar she chooses takes pride in his spiritual seriousness. Her plan depends on that pride.

She goes to confession, but instead of admitting her desire, she complains that the young man has been pursuing her. She presents herself as a woman distressed by unwanted attention and begs the friar to admonish him. The friar believes her immediately. He seeks out the young man and delivers a severe warning, describing in detail the woman who says she is troubled by him. For the young man, who had not understood the situation, the warning becomes a revelation that she is interested in him.

The woman soon returns to confession with fresh distress. The young man, she says, has dared to send presents or attempt new approaches. Again the friar passes her complaints along. She is using the very language of refusal to transmit an invitation. Because the friar is certain he knows the moral positions of the people in the story, he cannot recognize that he is the one being led.

At last she tells him that the young man has supposedly discovered how to enter her garden at night and may climb into her house. She makes sure the warning contains a workable route. The friar hastens to reprimand the young man again, and this time he unknowingly supplies the practical directions the lovers need. When the husband is away, the visitor arrives by the appointed way. The woman welcomes him. The sequence of confessions has produced exactly the encounter she wanted without any direct written declaration from her.

Filomena's pleasure in the trick comes from its repeated reversal. Every time the friar tries to prevent an illicit relationship, he strengthens it. He thinks he is stopping a rumor; he is confirming interest. He thinks he is guarding a household; he is explaining how its defenses may be crossed. The religious office gives his words weight, and that same weight makes his mistaken intervention useful to someone else.

Yet the woman is the most skilled interpreter in the story. She understands the friar's habits, the young man's likely reactions, and the conditions of her own confinement. Her speech is carefully directed at two audiences: the confessor who hears an accusation and the neighbor who will hear an invitation. The audience knows both meanings at once. This is dramatic irony built into everyday conversation, with no magical device and almost no physical adventure.

We should be precise about what is being mocked. The teller does not show that confession is inherently foolish. She shows a man whose certainty about a penitent's sincerity prevents him from asking how his own intervention may affect the world. The story also refuses to idealize domestic respectability: the husband's absence is the condition of the successful encounter, and reputation matters more than any discussion of the marriage itself. The lady gains what she desired by finding a way to make an institution speak on her behalf without the institution realizing what it has said.
