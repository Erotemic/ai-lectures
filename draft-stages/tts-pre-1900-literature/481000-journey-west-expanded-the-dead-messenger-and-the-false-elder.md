# The Dead Messenger and the False Elder

Wukong ate a little of the food offered to him at Guanyin Monastery and went back toward Black Wind Mountain. A direct assault had failed: the Black King could match him in combat and then simply close his doors. Wukong had to find another way inside.

He had not traveled far when he spotted a small demon hurrying along the road with a decorated wooden box under one arm. There was no need to guess what such a courier was carrying. The Black King had talked of a birthday feast, and this creature was plainly going somewhere on its master's business.

Wukong struck before asking a question. The messenger fell dead. The box broke open, and inside lay an invitation, folded with all the formal care of a letter exchanged by polite religious friends.

Its address made Wukong laugh. The Black King was writing to Elder Jinchi of Guanyin Monastery, thanking him for previous kindness and inviting him to celebrate the splendid Buddhist robe recently acquired. The invitation expressed regret at having been unable to help with the monastery fire. A person reading only the letter might have imagined two refined practitioners arranging a tasteful visit.

Wukong had seen the charred monastery and the terrified surviving monks. He knew how the robe had been taken, and he knew Jinchi was dead. The invitation turned that disaster into a reason for further hospitality. But it also gave him a plan. If the Black King expected the old monk to visit, why not let him receive that very guest?

The monkey tucked away his staff, altered his face, stooped his back, and became a likeness of Elder Jinchi: old, bowed, and dressed for a visit between religious companions. He walked to the cave and asked the guard to admit him.

A servant announced that Jinchi had arrived. The Black King was surprised. His invitation had only just been sent. How could his guest already be at the door? He suspected a trick, and he ordered the precious robe hidden before admitting the caller.

Wukong entered a cave built with the care of an elegant retreat. There were cultivated spaces, decorated rooms, and screens separating one court from another. Even the inscription at the gate praised quiet seclusion and freedom from worldly concerns. He had expected a den. Instead he saw a place where a creature of the mountains could play at being a gentleman scholar.

The Black King received him politely. They exchanged greetings, offered compliments, and sat down to drink tea. The host asked why Jinchi had come so early. Wukong explained that he had encountered the messenger and hurried over when he learned of the robe festival. Might he see the new treasure? The monastery fire had thrown everything into confusion, he said, and he had not had time to admire the garment properly when it was in his own possession.

The Black King smiled. The robe, he observed, had belonged to the Tang monk who had been staying at Jinchi's monastery. Surely Jinchi himself had seen it. Why make such an elaborate visit to another man's cave merely to look at something the old monk had already held?

Wukong improvised. The robe had been lent for the night, but there had hardly been a moment to unfold it before the fire. Its disappearance had come with the destruction of many of Jinchi's possessions. What good fortune that such a worthy friend had carried the robe safely away. A single look at the recovered treasure would comfort the old man.

The conversation continued, smooth on the surface and dangerous underneath. Wukong was trying to move his host toward the hiding place. The Black King was trying not to reveal it. Neither believed the other's explanation.

Then another guard rushed in. He had found the dead messenger on the road. The invitation had been taken. Worse, there was news of a hairy monk who could change his shape. The supposed elder must be Sun Wukong himself.

The Black King snatched up his spear. The visitor abandoned the bent posture of old age and resumed his own form, pulling the iron staff from its hiding place. Their courteous visit ended in a battle that broke through the rooms of the cave, out into the courtyard, and up the mountain.

This time the contest lasted into evening. Wukong tried to force an advantage while there was still light. The Black King fought him to a standstill, then declared that he had no wish to continue after dark. He broke away as wind and locked the stone doors once more.

Wukong struck at the barrier, shouted accusations, and finally had to admit that the robe was still inside. He returned to Xuanzang, carrying only the stolen invitation and the story of a failed impersonation.

Xuanzang was not encouraged. Wukong had killed a messenger, assumed the form of a dead monk, fought the thief twice, and still brought back nothing. The monastery people, hearing that the Black King had really been Jinchi's friend, explained that the relationship was longstanding. Their elder had not himself been a demon; he had merely practiced religious arts with one who was. The Black King had taught him methods of breathing and bodily cultivation, which helped explain the extreme age for which the monks had revered him.

The invitation was signed with a name belonging to a bear. Wukong told Xuanzang that the adversary was almost certainly a black bear spirit. Xuanzang wondered how an animal could cultivate powers so great. The monkey had an obvious answer: he too was an animal, and he had once been the Great Sage Equal to Heaven. In a world where living beings could practice and transform themselves, why should a bear be denied that possibility?

Xuanzang returned to his first concern. If the bear could fight Wukong to a draw, how could his disciple get the robe back? Wukong had a remedy in mind, but for once he did not boast about it. He would leave at dawn and seek help from someone the creature could not so easily refuse.

## Non-spoken source and editorial notes

Source: [*Journey to the West*, chapter 17](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/西遊記/第017回) (`cl-jtw-ch17`), Wukong kills the courier, reads the invitation to Jinchi, impersonates the elder, is exposed by a guard, and fights the Black King to evening. The letter names 熊羆 and identifies the old monk as a friend of the demon; Wukong's inference that the creature is a bear follows. This is original prose depicting fictional events; it does not present killing as the ethically correct response.

Orientation: [Black Bear Demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Bear_Demon), [*Journey to the West*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_to_the_West).
