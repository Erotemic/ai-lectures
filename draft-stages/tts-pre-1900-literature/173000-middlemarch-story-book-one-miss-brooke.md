# Middlemarch — Miss Brooke

Before Dorothea Brooke appears, George Eliot asks us to think about Saint Teresa, a woman whose enormous religious energy found a recognizable vocation. What happens, the novelist asks, when a person with comparable hunger for useful action is born into a society that offers her no matching institution or public role? That question brings us to a provincial English community in the years before the Reform Act of 1832. We will be following several lives, not just Dorothea's. The connections between them are the substance of the novel.

Dorothea and her younger sister Celia are living under the protection of their uncle, Mr. Brooke, at Tipton Grange. They are comfortably placed, though not extravagantly so. Dorothea dresses plainly, is drawn to earnest arguments, and designs better cottages for estate laborers. Celia has a shrewd eye for her sister's impracticality. When they divide their deceased mother's jewelry, Dorothea initially condemns ornament but is still drawn to the beauty of some stones. Eliot does not introduce her as a perfect ascetic; aspiration and appetite inhabit the same person.

Sir James Chettam admires Dorothea and would like to marry her. He is kind, conventional, and able to help build the cottages she imagines. Dorothea, however, mistakes his attentions for a sympathetic interest in her projects. She is drawn instead to Edward Casaubon, an older clergyman and scholar preparing a vast work called the Key to All Mythologies. She thinks his learning will give her access to a lifetime of serious intellectual service. Casaubon speaks respectfully, though stiffly, and recognizes that such admiration may suit him. The marriage proposal pleases Dorothea and alarms Celia. Their judgments are different, and neither sister has access to the other's future.

The decision illustrates a recurring danger. Dorothea has made a real moral commitment to understanding and doing good; she has also imagined a mentor and partner with qualities that Casaubon has not yet shown. She interprets his reserve as intellectual depth. Her uncle raises objections, but he is too inattentive and inconsistent to argue them convincingly. Sir James, disappointed, does not turn vindictive. He begins to care for Celia, whose commonsense companionship may suit him much better.

Elsewhere, a young doctor named Tertius Lydgate arrives with professional ambitions. He has studied new approaches to anatomy and disease and believes he can improve medical practice. He expects his work to be evaluated by its results, while Middlemarch evaluates an outsider through family background, manners, patronage, and the possibility of marriage. Rosamond Vincy, accomplished and elegant, notices him. She has been trained to imagine a refined life, preferably one that lifts her above the limitations of her birthplace. Lydgate, for his part, expects women to be charming domestic companions rather than serious participants in his vocation. Their early attraction already contains conflicting expectations.

Rosamond's brother Fred has a very different problem. He is personable, educated for a profession he does not want, and unwilling to settle into steady employment. He hopes to inherit the property of old Peter Featherstone, a wealthy, unpleasant relative who enjoys keeping prospective heirs uncertain. Fred loves Mary Garth, who works in Featherstone's household as a companion. Mary cares for Fred but refuses to organize her future around money he may never receive. Their relation introduces a practical test of character. It is easier to profess devotion than to become dependable.

We also meet the religious banker Nicholas Bulstrode, whose money and influence shape the town's institutions. He speaks in the language of conscience and moral responsibility, but the reader has not yet been given the full history behind his wealth. When Lydgate looks for opportunities to practice medicine, Bulstrode's projects and alliances will matter. The new hospital is not a neutral room where science can simply replace custom; it is financed, staffed, defended, and disputed by people with conflicting interests.

At the close of this opening movement, Dorothea has accepted Casaubon, Lydgate has entered the town's world, and Fred is still waiting for a fortune rather than earning a future. These lines may look like separate novels. Eliot's point is that they occupy a single place. A marriage changes property; a debt reaches someone else's household; a professional appointment depends on a banker; and a political argument enters an ordinary friendship. Middlemarch is the name of that mesh of consequences.
