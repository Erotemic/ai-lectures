# Journey to the West: The bells of Qilin Mountain

## The visitor in the wind

High over the palace of Zhuzi, Sun Wukong planted his feet upon a cloud and faced the creature who had come through the dust. Below him the king, only just recovered from his long illness, had retreated into his underground shelter. Xuanzang was with him. Bajie and Sha Wujing stood guard over the stone covering the entrance. The whole court knew that the stranger belonged somehow to the terror that had taken their queen three years before. No one yet knew whether the monster himself had arrived.

Wukong demanded the creature's name. The answer was proud but revealing. He was not Sai Taisui, the lord of Qilin Mountain. He was Sai Taisui's advance guard, sent to bring back two more palace women to serve the Golden Sage. A request had become a threat, and the threat had been repeated so many times that the mountain court treated it as routine government business.

The monkey answered with his own name. If the messenger had heard of the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, here was the chance to make proper acquaintance. He was the first disciple of the Tang monk on pilgrimage to India, and he had come to end the intimidation of Zhuzi. The guard lowered his spear instead of listening. Wukong raised his iron staff. There was a short, hard clash in the air, after which the guard's spear broke in two. The creature fled toward the western horizon with the remains of the weapon.

Wukong did not follow. He descended to the palace, called the king and his master from below ground, and found a full cup being held out to him. The king, still trembling, wished to thank him with wine. Before Wukong could drink, an official rushed through the gate: fire had sprung up at the western side of the city.

The monkey tossed the cup into the air. It fell with a clang. The king was hurt and bewildered. Had the Great Sage rejected his thanks? Was the court guilty of some breach of manners? Wukong laughed, for a second messenger had arrived with news that a sudden rain had put the fire out. Water ran through the streets, the messenger said, carrying a smell of wine. That was the cup's work. The defeated advance guard had set the fire from afar, and Wukong had sent its answer by the same distant road. A simple act at a banquet had become a defense of an entire quarter of the capital.

The king offered greater thanks. Wukong would take neither the throne nor a heap of silver. He wanted the whereabouts of the captive queen. The guard would report his defeat, he said. Sai Taisui would prepare another attack. Better to meet the demon at his own mountain than allow the city to be the battlefield.

The courtiers described Qilin Mountain as terribly far away. Soldiers who had gone to inquire into it had spent weeks on the journey. The king asked for time to arrange a fast horse, provisions, and spending money. Wukong listened politely to this entirely human plan for crossing three thousand li. Then he explained what a somersault cloud could do. A poured cup would scarcely have time to grow cool while he went and returned. He had practiced with masters, passed through the disciplines of immortality, and learned to travel where ordinary legs could not take him.

Xuanzang remained behind. Bajie and Sha were ordered to stay and protect him. Wukong rose into the air before anyone could object that the king had not yet furnished the travel allowance.

## The smoke that came before its master

The mountain rose out of the landscape like a wall thrown up to stop the sky. Pine branches covered the ridges. In the hollows were animal cries, hidden ravines, and narrow passages beneath stone cliffs. Wukong landed where he could look around without announcing himself. He knew from the guard's burning attack that Sai Taisui possessed something more dangerous than a spear.

As he watched, fire shot upward from a valley. Smoke thick with colors followed. Then came sand, the fine grains driven hard enough to blind the unwary. It filled Wukong's nose and made him sneeze. He stuffed two pebbles into his nostrils and became a bird, beating across the smoke to see what lay beneath it. The blazing stream dwindled; the sand fell away. Farther down the slope, he heard a gong.

He had expected an army, but the gong sounded like a courier's signal on a public road. A small demon came hurrying past, carrying a yellow flag, a document, and the instrument he struck at every few paces. Wukong turned into an insect and settled on the courier's pack. The creature muttered as he walked, for he was troubled by the mission given to him.

Sai Taisui had taken the Golden Sage three years before, he complained, but still had not succeeded in touching her. The ruler's repeated demands for attendants had destroyed other women. Now the advance guard had been beaten, and the mountain lord intended to send a declaration of war to Zhuzi. Once the king was destroyed, the demon would take the kingdom and appoint his followers to offices of state. The courier could see the attraction of promotion. He could also see that burning a city full of people was not a deed Heaven would approve.

Wukong was struck by this last thought. Even among the demons, here was someone who could recognize the wickedness of the order. He wanted to know more. He flew ahead, resumed the shape of a youthful Daoist wandering by the road, and greeted the messenger in friendly fashion.

The courier needed only a little encouragement. He explained how the Golden Sage had survived her captivity. Soon after Sai Taisui carried her away, an immortal had presented her with a marvelous garment of many colors. Whenever the demon reached for her, the beautiful robe raised sharp points against his skin. His hands hurt too badly to lay hold of her. Sai Taisui, furious and thwarted, had taken out his desire upon the women brought from Zhuzi to wait upon her. His cruelty had not ceased because he could not reach the one person he wanted.

The news was important. Wukong had known the king's despair, but not whether the queen was still alive or whether the demon had harmed her in the manner he intended. Now he knew she remained beyond his reach, protected by a gift whose maker had not yet been identified.

He let the courier continue a few steps. Then the iron staff fell upon the back of the creature's head. The document slipped from his clothes. Wukong killed him, took the flag and gong, and found a metal pass at his waist bearing the name Youlaiyouqu: Come-and-Go. The monkey made a pun at the dead messenger's expense. Today he had gone, but would never come back. He also noticed, rather late, that he had killed the fellow before asking his name. The pass supplied what the interrogation had not.

A person who had expressed misgivings about a wicked order had nevertheless been killed for carrying it. Wukong did not debate the matter aloud. He had a court to protect and a disguise to obtain. The journey's hero could make an opportunity out of any encounter, and sometimes his quickness left no room for mercy.

## The court receives a headless report

Instead of pressing straight to the cave, Wukong carried the body back to Zhuzi on his staff. He dropped it near the palace steps to demonstrate that he had already struck at the enemy's household. Bajie rushed forward with his rake and made a great show of delivering a blow to the corpse. That, he announced, was his own share of the victory. His evidence was plain: nine holes made by his rake.

Wukong told him to look more closely. The corpse had already lost its head. Bajie, refusing to surrender a useful claim, laughed that this explained why the victim had not moved when struck. He was prepared to take credit for anything left standing long enough to receive a blow.

Wukong found Xuanzang and quietly pushed the demon's letter into his sleeve. Do not show the king yet, he warned. A formal declaration of war would frighten the court without helping the rescue. The king came out and recognized at once that the dead courier was not Sai Taisui himself. The demon lord he had seen was enormous and terrible. Wukong agreed. The corpse was a servant, caught in the course of reconnaissance. If the court wanted a first achievement, it could mark that one down.

Then he asked for an object the Golden Sage would recognize. A stranger could enter the mountain and claim to have come from Zhuzi, but how could she know that he spoke truly? Did she have a bracelet, a jewel, some small possession left behind on the day of the festival?

The king's grief returned with the question. No farewell token had been exchanged. The queen had been taken without warning. Yet in her dressing room was a pair of golden wrist ornaments that she had removed while the women prepared the festival threads. The ornaments had stayed where she left them. The king could scarcely bear to look at them, and looking reminded him of the illness he had just survived. Still, he gave them to Wukong. The monkey looped them about his arm and promised to bring back not a story about the queen, but the queen herself.

He set out again before the wine he had refused could be poured afresh.

## Come-and-Go comes back

This time he found the approach to Xiezhi Cave, the mountain lord's stronghold. Hundreds of armed creatures guarded it. Wukong did not want to fight all of them before he knew the whereabouts of the Golden Sage. He returned to the place where the courier had fallen, took the hidden yellow flag and gong, and became Youlaiyouqu. At the cave entrance a guard called him by name. Wukong answered as if nothing were more natural than a messenger returning from a dangerous errand.

The false courier came before Sai Taisui, who sat in a pavilion furnished like a ruler's audience hall. Wukong deliberately kept striking his gong. The demon called his name. He pretended not to hear. The demon called again. Still the gong sounded. Finally Sai Taisui seized him and asked what manner of reception this was.

The little messenger's account was a masterpiece of injured pride. He had warned that a mission to Zhuzi might end badly, he said, yet had been sent regardless. At the city he was surrounded by soldiers, dragged before the king, threatened with execution, and saved only because ministers reminded the ruler that envoys were not to be slain. Then the guards had beaten him with sticks and sent him away. What decent courier could be expected to come home cheerful after that? His back still hurt too much to answer respectfully.

Sai Taisui accepted the story. He asked about Zhuzi's defenses. Wukong described a forest of weapons and fighting men, far more than he had actually seen. The demon laughed. Swords and spears were nothing before his magical smoke and fire. Let Zhuzi gather the armies of the world; all of them would burn.

Then he gave the courier an order Wukong could scarcely have hoped for. Go to the Golden Sage, he said, and tell her that the people of Zhuzi intend to fight. The news should soothe her: she may imagine that her old kingdom can defeat me. Wukong bowed and hurried through the inner rooms.

The Golden Sage sat among attendants with tears on her cheeks, her rich ornaments neglected. She barely looked up when the returning servant greeted her. The manner was too familiar, and she rebuked him. Wukong maintained the servant's character until the other women had been sent away. Then he revealed himself.

She did not trust him immediately. A demon's house was a place of false promises. He told her of the king's cure, of the Tang monk and his mission, of the little physician who had made Zhuzi's palace laugh again. Finally he held out the golden wrist ornaments. At the sight of them her suspicion gave way. She knew these were from her own chamber. The rescuer had brought proof that someone beyond the mountain remembered her and meant to act.

Wukong asked about the fire and sand that had hidden the cave. She told him of three purple-gold bells carried against Sai Taisui's body. One released flame, another smoke, and the third deadly yellow sand. They were rarely out of the demon's reach. Wukong proposed a risky plan. She must behave for an evening as though she had become reconciled to her captor; persuade him that a wife should be trusted with a husband's treasures; accept the bells into her keeping. The monkey would find a moment to remove them.

The Golden Sage understood the danger. She had survived three years by resisting every approach. Now she was being asked to invite the same creature near, talk to him gently, and play the part he wished her to play. She agreed because the alternative was continued captivity, not because she had changed her mind about him.

## A theft interrupted by the treasure itself

Wukong returned to the pavilion wearing the courier's face and announced that the queen wished to see her lord. Sai Taisui was delighted. She had usually met him with anger. Why should she call him now? Wukong improvised that Zhuzi's king had taken another wife and no longer cared to recover her. The demon believed it. In the queen's chambers she pressed the invented reconciliation further. She spoke of the humiliation of never being entrusted with the household's most precious things. In Zhuzi she had kept her husband's jewels. Here Sai Taisui would not even let her hold his three little bells.

The demon, eager to be trusted, removed the bells from beneath several layers of clothes. He wrapped them carefully, stopping their openings with cotton so they would not ring by accident. The queen set the bundle on her dressing table, and ordered food and drink. She smiled and offered him wine. It was as convincing a performance as she could make.

Wukong edged toward the dressing table, took the bundle, and slipped out to the pavilion. Alone, he began to examine his prize. There were three bells, one larger than the others. Why were their mouths stuffed with cotton? He pulled a plug free.

A bell rang.

Fire, smoke, and yellow sand erupted at once. The flames lit the pavilion. The guards rushed inward, shouting that the treasure had been stolen. Sai Taisui came out and caught his supposed courier fumbling with the bells. Wukong could no longer keep the disguise. He threw down the weapons, raised his staff, and fought his way among the guards. Sai Taisui seized the bells again and ordered the gates shut.

With hundreds of demons hunting him and no safe path to the entrance, Wukong turned into a tiny fly and clung to a bare stone where the fire had not reached. The guards looked through the corridors and found nothing. Their master suspected at last that the clever servant had been Sun Wukong all along. He would not open the gates. The Golden Sage remained within the palace, and the real bells were once more in the hands of her captor.

The Great Sage had reached the queen, given her hope, and lost the decisive advantage by pulling out a piece of cotton. His next task was not simply to defeat Sai Taisui. He had to leave a locked mountain stronghold in which every guard now knew that a master of disguise had entered it.
