# The River Child and the Blood Letter

In Jiangzhou, Yin Wenjiao lived in a house where everyone addressed the man who had murdered her husband as the rightful official. Liu Hong had taken Chen Guangrui's clothes, papers, and place at the government's table. He had also forced Wenjiao to remain with him. She could scarcely reveal what had happened without risking her life. The pregnancy she carried offered hope that Chen's family had not been entirely destroyed, but it also placed a child in the path of the murderer.

One day while Liu Hong was away on official business, Wenjiao gave birth to a boy. In her distress she heard a supernatural warning. The messenger told her that the child had a great future and that Liu Hong would kill him if she failed to protect him. Her true husband had not been forgotten: the Dragon King had preserved Chen, and the family would one day have a chance to recover its scattered members. But the immediate danger was real. She must act while she could.

Liu Hong returned and saw the infant. He wanted the child drowned. Wenjiao pleaded for a delay until the following day, and the danger passed for a few hours. In the morning, official duties drew Liu Hong away again. She had a narrow opening. There was no family member she could trust in the house, no safe path to the capital, and no way to defend a newborn against an armed man. She decided on a terrible form of rescue: she would send her son away and surrender his fate to the river.

She bit her finger and used the blood to write the story of his origins, his father's name, and the violence that had brought them to this place. She placed the written account with a garment of her own. In the version of the tale preserved in this chapter, she also marks the child by biting away the small toe on his left foot, so that she could recognize him if they ever met again. It is a harsh and distressing detail, and the narrative offers it as a desperate means of identification in a world where papers could be stolen and faces changed with age.

She found a wooden board at the riverbank, fastened the baby to it as securely as she could, and placed the written message with him. Then she pushed the little raft onto the water. She could not know whether it would overturn, strike a rock, or drift toward a stranger who might show mercy. She watched the current draw the child away. Then she returned to the house where Liu Hong would soon expect to find her.

Downriver, a Buddhist monk named Faming was living at Gold Mountain Monastery. While sitting in meditation he heard the cry of an infant. He went to the river and found the child upon the board. The message told him that this was no abandoned child without a family but an infant deliberately placed beyond a killer's reach. Faming took him into his care. He named the boy Jiangliu, the River Current, and arranged for him to be nursed and raised. He kept the blood letter safe.

Years passed in the monastery. The boy grew among monks, familiar with the chanting of scriptures, the sound of bells, and the daily work of religious life. When he was old enough, Faming gave him the religious name Xuanzang. The child who had been sent away on a wooden board became a disciplined young monk, intelligent and eager in his study. He knew the men who had cared for him, but he did not know his parents.

At eighteen, he was drawn into an argument with another monk. A disagreement about religious matters grew sharp, and the other man attacked him where he was most vulnerable: he mocked Xuanzang for not knowing who his father and mother were. Xuanzang could not answer with a name. He went to Faming and begged to know the truth. He had grown up believing that the monastery was his entire world. Yet the accusation had exposed a silence around his beginnings.

Faming had waited for this question. He took out the box in which he had preserved the garment and letter. Xuanzang read the account his mother had written. He learned of his father's success and marriage, of the river crossing, the murder, Liu Hong's seizure of the official position, and the danger that had required Wenjiao to place her baby upon the water. The details that had seemed to belong to no one now belonged to him.

Xuanzang wept. He wanted to find his mother and see that she was safe. He also wanted to honor the father whose face he had never seen and the teacher who had saved him. Faming advised caution. A young monk could not simply walk into Jiangzhou and accuse the powerful official who governed it. He must go as a mendicant collecting offerings, enter the household without announcing his purpose, and use the letter when he found a private moment.

So Xuanzang left the monastery, not yet as the pilgrim bound for the western scriptures, but as a son searching for the woman who had set him on the river. He carried the garment and the account of his origins. In Jiangzhou, Liu Hong's authority still appeared unshaken. Yet one of his intended victims was approaching the gate in a monk's robe, carrying no weapon beyond a piece of writing his crime had failed to erase.
