# Crime and Punishment — Confession and Loss

At the opening of Part Five, Luzhin has lost Dunya's consent to marry him. Instead of questioning his demand to control her, he looks for a way to disgrace the family and reassert his superiority. He turns to Sonya, the least powerful person among the people gathered around Raskolnikov. His plan will demonstrate how easily public judgment can be manipulated when a victim has already been marked as disreputable.

At a gathering connected with Marmeladov's funeral, Luzhin accuses Sonya of stealing a banknote. He has placed the money where it can be discovered and attempts to convert the discovery into proof. Sonya is frightened and cannot defend herself through social standing. But Andrei Lebezyatnikov has seen Luzhin's action and exposes the trick. The accusation fails because a witness supplies the missing information. We are asked to distinguish proof from a display designed to look like proof.

The incident also demonstrates the cost of poverty and sexual stigma. Luzhin chooses Sonya because the listeners may find a charge against her plausible before hearing it. Her position as a woman supporting a family through prostitution makes her vulnerable to moral contempt from people who have benefited from or ignored the conditions that produced it. The novel condemns Luzhin's deliberate falsification without asking the reader to imagine that Sonya's social difficulties vanish once he is exposed.

Raskolnikov goes to Sonya and tells her what happened in Alyona Ivanovna's apartment. He confesses that he killed the pawnbroker and Lizaveta. The second name matters profoundly: Lizaveta was Sonya's friend. The confession is therefore made not to a neutral vessel of forgiveness but to someone who has a personal relation to one of the victims. Sonya is shocked and grieves. She does not accept his theory as permission. She urges him to acknowledge the crime publicly and to submit to punishment rather than continue using others as shelter for his secret.

Raskolnikov tries to explain himself. He speaks of money and good deeds, but these explanations break down under questioning. He speaks of Napoleon and of wanting to know whether he could step across an accepted boundary. At other moments he despises the pride that drove him. His words shift because he has not settled on a truthful account of himself. Confession of the act does not produce a stable confession of motive.

Sonya's response is central. She does not say that the killings are harmless because Raskolnikov suffers. She asks him to live in truth with what he has done. Her willingness to remain near him is a form of care that does not depend on falsifying the crime. That combination is difficult for him to accept. If he could believe the act was justified, he would not need forgiveness. If he could believe himself beyond repair, he would not need to change.

The Marmeladov household meanwhile reaches catastrophe. Katerina Ivanovna, ill and humiliated, tries to make her children perform in the streets for money. Her desperation becomes public spectacle. She collapses and dies, leaving the children without the parent who struggled to care for them. Svidrigailov uses his wealth to arrange material support for the orphaned children and help with funeral expenses. The help is real. It does not erase his predatory conduct toward Dunya, any more than Raskolnikov's earlier gift erased the murders.

Svidrigailov has overheard enough through the wall of Sonya's room to know Raskolnikov's secret. This creates a new danger. A confession intended for moral truth can be converted into leverage by someone who has no intention of using it responsibly. Dunya's future and Raskolnikov's legal exposure are now linked through a man whose motives remain hard to trust.

At the end of Part Five, the family arguments, the legal investigation, and the poor household's suffering no longer look like parallel episodes. Luzhin's false accusation rehearses what unreliable proof can do. Sonya's knowledge makes the murder personal rather than theoretical. Svidrigailov possesses the secret for his own purposes. Raskolnikov has spoken the truth to one person. He has not yet taken responsibility before the society whose law he broke.
