# Literary Classics Before 1900

A distinct audio-first literature pack for the period between the ancient narrative
traditions already covered in **World History** and the twentieth-century works
covered in **Modern Literature**. The title identifies a chronological scope, not
an assertion that any particular date is the starting point of literary modernity.

## Current listening sections

**Early modern drama — Shakespeare, *Hamlet***: 001000–007000. Three consecutive,
spoiler-complete story lectures (Acts I–V) precede four critical lectures on
revenge and knowledge, theatre and surveillance, Ophelia and Gertrude, and the
surviving texts and history of performance. The retelling uses an ordinary modern
composite reading text for orientation; no unique Elizabethan performance text
is claimed. The editorial dossier identifies textual alternatives and places
where motive is not settled by the play.

**Early modern prose — Cervantes, *Don Quixote***: 008000–015000. Four continuous
narrative lectures cover all of Part I and Part II, including the inserted
stories, the transformation in Part II when characters have read Part I, the
ducal pranks, Sancho's government of Barataria, the response to Avellaneda, and
the final illness. Four critical lectures study narration, the evolving Sancho–
Quixote friendship, power and moral cost, and print/translation history. The
lectures treat *Don Quixote* as a great innovation in an existing field of
prose fiction, not as the literal invention of all novels.

**Medieval poetry — Dante, *Commedia* (*The Divine Comedy*)**: 016000–037000.
Twelve consecutive story lectures follow all 100 cantos: four *Inferno*, four
*Purgatorio*, and four *Paradiso*. Ten critical lectures then examine narrative
architecture and retrospective time, contrapasso and sympathy, free choice,
knowledge and love, Florentine politics and exile, Virgil and Beatrice, terza
rima and translation, medieval cosmology, close reading, and textual history.
See `notes/dante-listening-guide.md` and
`notes/dante-textual-history-and-interpretation.md` for canto coverage and
source/evidence boundaries. Modern moral assumptions and present-day physical
science are not attributed to Dante; allegorical controversies remain noted.

**Early modern tragedy — Shakespeare, *King Lear***: 038000–047000.
Four continuous story lectures (division, storm, Dover, final battle) precede
six analyses of sovereignty and need, the Gloucester subplot and documentary
deception, the three daughters, the Fool and performed madness, stagecraft,
and the Quarto/Folio texts and reception history. The retelling does not
pretend that the 1608 Quarto and 1623 Folio are interchangeable. See
`notes/king-lear-versions-and-stage-history.md` for scene coverage, contested
readings, and the history of Tate's adaptation.

**Victorian mathematical fiction — Edwin Abbott Abbott, *Flatland***:
048000–049000. A shorter two-lecture unit: first the complete A Square
story, then geometry, satire, and the limits of analogy. The second lecture
distinguishes geometric higher dimensions from claims about physical space
and the narrator's rigid social attitudes from Abbott's historical advocacy
of educational reform. See `notes/flatland-geometry-and-satire.md`.

**Jacobean tragedy — Shakespeare, *Macbeth***: 050000–058000.
Four story lectures carry the prophecy, Duncan's murder, Banquo and the apparitions,
and the Scottish resistance through the final battle. Five analyses examine the
limits of prophecy, the Macbeths' changing complicity, kingship, blood/sleep/time
as theatrical language, and First Folio/chronicle/transmission history. The
retelling distinguishes the witches' words from Macbeth's independent decisions,
and does not pass off contested Middleton attributions as settled. See
`notes/macbeth-sources-and-performance.md`.

**Jacobean tragedy — Shakespeare, *Othello***: 059000–068000.
Four continuous story lectures from the Venice hearing to the bedroom murder
precede six analyses of Iago's manufactured evidence, race and institutional
service, Desdemona/Emilia/Bianca, jealousy and responsibility, stage language,
and Quarto/Folio sources. The handkerchief's full route remains explicit, and
neither Iago's accusations nor Othello's final self-account are treated as
an independent finding of fact. See
`notes/othello-sources-texts-and-performance.md`.

**Regency social fiction — Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice***: 069000–078000.
Four story lectures follow the full narrative, from the first Netherfield assembly
through Darcy's letter, Lydia's crisis, and the final marriages. Six analyses
examine narrative irony and free indirect discourse; money, property and the
specific entail of Longbourn; letters and the revision of judgment; women's
unequal choices; landscape and class; and the novel's 1813 publication and
literary influence. See `notes/pride-prejudice-plot-forms-and-1813-edition.md`.

**Romantic Gothic fiction — Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein***: 079000–090000.
Four complete story lectures follow Walton, Victor, and the creature through the
Arctic conclusion. Eight analyses study nested testimony, abandoned dependents,
scientific ambition, language and prejudice, the underheard women, Miltonic and
Promethean references, 1818 versus 1831 texts, and scene-based close reading.
The narrative uses the **1818 first edition** and explicitly marks the changed
Elizabeth backstory in 1831. See
`notes/frankenstein-1818-1831-versions-and-story-map.md`.

**Victorian development novel — Charles Dickens, *Great Expectations***:
091000–102000. Four continuous narrative lectures cover the marshes, Satis
House, London, Magwitch's return, the failed escape, and the ending. Eight
analyses address retrospective narration and shame, labor and class,
criminal law and transportation, money and debt, Estella/Miss Havisham,
place and serialization, close reading, and the **two distinct endings**.
The spoken story follows the first-published revised conclusion without
inventing a marriage for Pip and Estella. See
`notes/great-expectations-serial-endings-and-story-map.md`.

**French realism — Gustave Flaubert, *Madame Bovary***: 103000–113000.
Four narrative lectures cover all three parts, continuing beyond Emma's death
to Charles, Berthe, and Homais. Seven analyses study free indirect style,
romantic reading, marriage, consumer credit, the Hippolyte operation,
close reading, and publication, censorship and translation. The retelling
separates Emma's desires from established events and does not treat the
English translations as identical to the 1857 French book. See
`notes/madame-bovary-1857-text-and-story-map.md`.

**Russian historical fiction — Leo Tolstoy, *War and Peace***: 114000–129000.
Nine continuous story lectures cover the society and families of 1805, the first
campaigns and Austerlitz, Pierre and Andrei's search for meaning, Natasha's
engagement and crisis, the 1812 invasion and Borodino, Moscow's abandonment,
captivity and retreat, the postwar households and both epilogues. Seven analyses
examine panoramic narration, historical agency and the *great man* thesis,
moral education, women and domestic ideals, serfdom and class, war scenes, and
publication/translation. Tolstoy's historical theses are presented as arguments,
not military facts. See `notes/war-peace-story-map-history-and-versions.md`.

**Russian philosophical fiction — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *The Brothers Karamazov***:
130000–146000. Seven consecutive story lectures cover the family and monastery,
three-thousand-ruble conflicts, Ivan's *Rebellion* and *Grand Inquisitor*,
Zosima's death, Dmitri's desperate night, Ivan's interviews with Smerdyakov,
the trial, and Ilyusha's funeral. Ten analyses distinguish the sons and their
shared inheritance, theodicy and active love, distributed moral responsibility
from legal guilt, women's lives and children's lives, narrated voices and
rhetoric, translation, and close reading. The attempted murder case and
three monetary paths are distinguished. See
`notes/brothers-karamazov-plot-evidence-and-versions.md`.

These are original lectures, not extracts or dramatic recordings of the primary
texts. Source citations, critical questions, and edition notes are outside the
spoken regions; bibliographic records and anchored claims appear in `metadata/`.
Spoken lengths are measurements only, not writing or validation targets. No audio
or binary assets are part of this pack.

## Editorial and sourcing boundaries

- Distinguish a dramatic character's beliefs from the public facts of the play,
  and a fictional narrator's claim from the surrounding literary history.
- Do not give Ophelia's death an unambiguous intention or assume Gertrude knew
  Claudius's crime without a stated interpretive argument.
- Do not treat the Q1, Q2 and Folio *Hamlet* as one identical witness. A composite
  reading text is a pedagogical convenience, not a resolved archetype.
- Do not treat Cide Hamete Benengeli as a historical author, or confuse the
  1614 Avellaneda sequel with Cervantes's 1615 second part.
- The available full-text pages and corroborating critical/bibliographic
  materials were consulted; the complete originals and every textual variant
  were **not** exhaustively line-by-line collated in this authoring pass.
- No fixed word count or runtime minimum/maximum is used to accept a lecture.


**American whaling novel — Herman Melville, *Moby-Dick***: 147000–159000.
Six story lectures follow Ishmael and Queequeg, the Pequod and Ahab, shipboard
labor, Pip and other whalers, the Rachel's request, the three-day chase and
Ishmael's survival. Seven analyses address narrative forms, the uncertain
symbolism of whiteness, authority and refusal, race and maritime labor,
whaling science, scripture and stage form, the 1851 British/American editions,
and close reading. The **American epilogue** is used explicitly; the British
edition's omission is documented in
`notes/moby-dick-story-map-text-and-history.md`.

**Russian domestic and social novel — Leo Tolstoy, *Anna Karenina***:
160000–172000. Seven story lectures follow all eight parts, including Levin
and Kitty's parallel life, Anna's son and daughter, her death, and the final
part's political and ethical debate. Six analyses examine parallel form,
interior consciousness, unequal marital constraints, land and work, family
and faith, editions, translation, and close reading. The separately published
Part Eight is **part of the novel**, not a detachable postscript. See
`notes/anna-karenina-eight-parts-and-edition-history.md`.
