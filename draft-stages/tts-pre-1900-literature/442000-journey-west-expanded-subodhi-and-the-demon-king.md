# The Midnight Lesson and the Demon King of Havoc

The master gave his new student a name, but a name was not a secret formula. Sun Wukong had arrived at Patriarch Subodhi's dwelling after years of searching for immortality. He wanted an answer quickly. What he received instead was a place among other disciples and the ordinary work of a household.

He learned where to stand when the teacher spoke. He learned how to greet other students, how to carry wood, tend a garden, sweep the rooms, and fetch water. He listened to religious teaching and practiced the customs of the community. One year passed, and then another. The novel measures the time partly through ripening fruit: Wukong notices that he has eaten the peaches from a neighboring mountain seven times. By the time the master publicly questions him, he has been serving for about seven years.

Imagine how different this is from his life in Water-Curtain Cave. There the other monkeys had hailed him as king after one spectacular act. They had not expected him to clean their rooms. Here, having left home to learn the highest teaching, he is doing chores among students whose abilities may seem entirely ordinary. The tale does not say that he has ceased to care about eternal life. He waits because this is the only place he has found where the answer might be taught.

At last the Patriarch addressed the students in an assembly. His teaching ranged across religious traditions and methods of cultivation. Wukong listened with such excitement that he could not remain still. He smiled, moved his hands, danced, and expressed the delight that he had been holding in. The teacher stopped and reprimanded him for his commotion. But he also asked how long the monkey had been there and what he wished to study.

The question released the ambition that had carried Wukong across the seas. He said he would learn whatever teaching the master chose, provided it could give him lasting life. Subodhi described one branch of instruction concerned with techniques: foretelling events, dealing with spirits, and learning how to avoid danger. Wukong asked whether any of it would allow him to live forever. The answer was no. He declined.

The master offered another kind of learning, involving religious schools, study, observance, and discipline. Wukong again asked the question that mattered to him. Could he escape death through these teachings? The answer, expressed through an image of a supporting pillar in a wall, was that these methods did not provide the certainty he sought. The monkey rejected them too.

Subodhi proposed quiet practices such as meditation, fasting, and withdrawal. Wukong was not persuaded by an assurance that sounded no stronger than an unfired clay brick facing rain. Finally the teacher spoke of active procedures, exercises, alchemical preparations, and other demanding arts. The monkey asked whether they would achieve his one aim. Their promise proved as elusive as a reflected moon lifted from water.

To the other students it looked as though a monkey was disputing with a great teacher. The pupil who had been accepted as a stranger now refused every offer made to him. Subodhi rose from his seat in anger, took a ruler, struck Wukong three times on the head, turned his hands behind his back, and retired through the center doorway, closing it behind him.

The other disciples were appalled. They rebuked Wukong for offending the master. Their lessons had been interrupted because this impatient newcomer would not stop arguing. But Wukong's reaction was strange: he did not appear devastated. He seemed to have understood something they had missed.

The three strokes, he reasoned, indicated the third watch of the night. The gesture behind the back and the closing of the main entrance directed him toward the rear door. What looked like a punishment could also be a hidden appointment. A teacher who would not explain an advanced lesson before the whole assembly had given his most alert student a way to hear it privately.

Wukong waited through the evening. He lay still while the other students went to sleep. When the time seemed right, he rose and made his way quietly through the building. At the rear entrance he found the door partly open. He slipped inside. The Patriarch was resting, apparently asleep. Instead of making a grand entrance, Wukong knelt and waited for him to wake.

The teacher knew why he had come. He had understood the signal, kept the appointment, and avoided boasting to the others. Subodhi was willing to teach him more. The knowledge concerned the cultivation of life, the handling of vital energies, and a way of transformation that the story treats with the vocabulary of spiritual practice. We need not pretend that the novel has supplied a modern scientific method for making a body immortal. It has described a secret teaching in a world where bodies, spirits, and divine offices follow rules different from those of ordinary experience.

Wukong learned. For a time he worked at the practice with the concentration he had once brought to the waterfall. Then the master issued a warning. Long life, if achieved, would not mean freedom from every ordeal. A cultivated being might still face three great calamities across vast spans of time: heavenly lightning, a strange inward fire, and a destructive wind. Each required protection and understanding. Simply declaring oneself immortal would not make such dangers disappear.

Subodhi taught him transformations, traditionally numbered as seventy-two. They allowed him to assume other forms and to escape threats in ways that a strong body alone could not. The monkey was delighted. His mind immediately turned to what he could do with such powers. Yet a creature capable of becoming many things could still be recognizable by his habits. Changing a shape did not necessarily change the pride that lived within it.

The teacher also explained a way of traveling through the clouds. One day, with the students gathered to see, Wukong tried to demonstrate his ability. His first method of leaping was energetic but unsatisfactory. Subodhi noticed that a monkey naturally began a leap with a somersault. He adapted the lesson accordingly. Wukong learned the somersault cloud, a way of crossing an enormous distance in a single turn through the air.

A journey that had taken him years on foot and raft could now be covered almost in a moment. This is one of the novel's great reversals: the wandering seeker had spent so much of his life reaching the teacher, and the teacher had now given him a method that made distance seem trivial. But the years of wandering had been the means by which he found the person willing to instruct him. Being able to arrive instantly somewhere is not the same as knowing where it is worth going.

With each new success the monkey grew happier and less careful. One day other students pressed him to show what he could do. He chose the form of a pine tree. A moment earlier they had been looking at Wukong; now they looked at a tree. The performance astonished them, and Wukong enjoyed their praise.

The master heard the commotion and arrived. The transformation itself was not the problem. It was the display. Such a power, shown carelessly in public, would attract people who wanted to learn it or possess it, and people who feared or envied the person able to use it. Those people might give Wukong no peace. The Patriarch declared that the monkey's time at the mountain was over.

Wukong begged to remain. He had found a teacher after a long search, and leaving felt like losing the greatest good he possessed. Subodhi refused. He ordered the monkey to return to the place from which he had come, and placed one emphatic condition on his departure: whatever troubles Wukong caused, he must not identify Subodhi as the man who taught him.

The teacher did not tell him that his powers were bad. He foresaw that his student would use them badly. Nor did he claim the right to control everything the monkey might do thereafter. He severed the public connection between master and pupil. Wukong left the cave with extraordinary abilities and an instruction that could not be misunderstood.

He made a somersault into the air. The familiar seas and lands passed beneath him, and Flower-Fruit Mountain came into view. His home stood where he had left it, and Water-Curtain Cave still lay behind the waterfall. Yet the welcome was not the one he imagined. There was distress among the monkeys, and the king learned why.

A creature called the Demon King of Havoc had attacked the mountain. The intruder came from another region, established himself in a northern cave, and terrorized the troop. He had carried away monkeys and possessions. The king had departed to seek a way beyond death, but those he left behind had been living with a more immediate danger.

Wukong could not repair years of absence simply by declaring himself returned. His companions wanted protection. He told them to wait and went to confront the raider. The journey to the demon's stronghold was no longer a difficult expedition. With his new cloud travel he reached the place quickly and announced himself.

The demon was enormous, armed, and confident. He saw a small monkey who had come to challenge him and regarded the encounter as an insult. Wukong did not yet possess the famous iron staff with which later audiences associate him. He relied on his body, his training, his transformations, and his own willingness to fight.

The battle showed how much had changed. When enemies crowded him, Wukong took hairs from his own body, used a spell, and produced little copies able to join the struggle. The creature who had once faced the waterfall alone could now turn himself into a company. He defeated the Demon King of Havoc, seized his weapon, and killed him. The demon's stronghold was destroyed, and the captive monkeys were brought back to the mountain.

The rescued troop celebrated, and their king rejoiced with them. For the first time his new powers had been used to answer a wrong done to someone else. In this episode he is not only showing off or demanding a personal privilege. He restores a home that had been violated. But the method is force, and the result confirms that force works. A monkey already delighted by his transformations will remember that lesson as readily as any warning he received from Subodhi.

The cave was full again. The stone-born king had become Sun Wukong, a pupil of an extraordinary teacher and the victor over a predatory demon. He could leap across seas, multiply his body, change his shape, and resist kinds of death that had terrified him at the feast. Yet he still ruled a mountain, and a powerful king needed subjects, defenses, and weapons. The more successful he became, the greater the world he believed he could command.
