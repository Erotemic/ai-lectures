# The Decameron — Girolamo and Salvestra

In Florence a young man named Girolamo, heir to a prosperous household, grows up alongside Salvestra, the daughter of a tailor. Their affection deepens into love. Girolamo's widowed mother considers the attachment unsuitable. Wealth and family reputation suggest to her a more advantageous marriage, and she resolves to separate the young people until the feeling fades.

Her strategy is patient. She arranges for Girolamo to travel to Paris on business for several years. Distance, she believes, will do the work she cannot accomplish through direct persuasion. The scheme succeeds in one narrow sense: while he is away, Salvestra marries another man. But the mother has mistaken absence for forgetfulness. Girolamo returns still attached to the life he imagined with her.

He finds that the woman he loves has a husband and a home of her own. Rather than accept the new situation, he searches for an opportunity to speak with her privately. He gains access to her house and pleads for a revival of their relationship. Salvestra is distressed and refuses. She tells him that their old love cannot now be acted upon without injuring the commitments she has made. Her refusal is not a theatrical device intended to make him try harder. It is a boundary that the story makes clear.

Girolamo insists that she owes him one last proof of kindness. He asks to lie beside her for a moment, promising not to touch her. She reluctantly permits him to rest near her. What follows is not the reconciliation he hoped for. He dies there, without violence and without any sign that she has changed her mind. Salvestra first fails to recognize the death, then confronts the terrifying discovery that a man has died in her home under circumstances nobody will easily explain.

She seeks help from her husband, who is startled but responds with practical care. They carry Girolamo's body back toward the house where he belongs. His family and neighbors gather for the funeral. Salvestra watches the mourning from within the crowd and is overcome. She goes to his body, and there she too dies. The lovers are reunited only in death, and the arrangements made to prevent a socially inconvenient marriage have helped lead to the loss of both lives.

Neifile's narrative is about the limits of the schemes families devise for their children. Girolamo's mother understands commerce, reputation, and the usefulness of travel. She believes the heart can be managed as a household account: send the boy away, wait, and return him to a more profitable arrangement. The plan leaves out the persistent inner experience of the person being moved. The tale asks readers to recognize the destructive difference between a prudent calculation and a just one.

Yet the story also gives Salvestra a position that should not disappear into the lovers' final tableau. She has married someone else; she declines Girolamo's proposal to resume their affair; she is frightened by what happens when he will not leave. The tragic force of his death does not convert her earlier refusal into a mistake. Her later death is represented as overwhelming grief, not as evidence that she secretly agreed to abandon her marriage.

The ending turns a private attachment into a public spectacle. In the bedroom, the lovers cannot resolve their past. At the funeral, family and community must confront what the separation has produced. There is no clever speech to reverse the damage, no sudden inheritance to make the union possible, and no successful escape to another city. The story's final image is one of shared burial, an intimacy that can be acknowledged only when it no longer threatens the family's plans.

Among the fourth day's tragedies, Girolamo and Salvestra show how a decision can cause harm without being designed as a murder. The mother believes she is protecting a household. The lover believes he is asking for one final kindness. Each acts from an intelligible desire, and yet the outcome is irreversible. The gap between intentions and consequences has become the day’s central subject.
