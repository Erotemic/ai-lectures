# Journey to the West: The queen of Western Liang

## A city with no men

Beyond the Mother-and-Child River lay the royal city of Western Liang. The crossing had nearly ended the pilgrimage. Xuanzang and Bajie had drunk the river water and begun to develop the magical pregnancies for which the kingdom was known. Only when Wukong and Sha Wujing fetched water from the guarded Abortion Spring could the two travelers continue. Now they approached a capital unlike any they had visited.

Xuanzang warned his disciples to behave. The country was governed by women and inhabited, so the people said, by women alone. It was not a demon's cave, but unfamiliar customs could still produce danger. He did not want the others gawking at strangers, making crude jokes, or forgetting the dignity of their work.

The market was orderly and prosperous. Women sold rice, salt, tea, wine, cloth, and the ordinary goods of city life. Women managed the shops and moved between the houses. The streets had the busy self-confidence of a place which did not lack for trade merely because its inhabitants were all of one sex.

Then the travelers entered, and the market stopped.

Women came from their doorways and stalls, astonished by the sight of male visitors. Some clapped their hands and called out that men had arrived—human seed, the remarkable beings who might change the manner in which their country made families. The pilgrims had survived demons, but they were not prepared for the power of a crowd's curiosity. Xuanzang was embarrassed. The white horse could barely move among the people.

Bajie, who often coveted attention, suddenly found that being the object of universal attention was not so pleasant. Wukong told him to show his real face. Bajie lifted his enormous ears, bared his strange mouth, and shouted. The crowd recoiled. Wukong and Sha also showed the alarming features which had so often frightened villagers elsewhere. A pathway opened, not through reverence for a visiting holy man, but because everyone was afraid of the company he kept.

It was a poor introduction to a kingdom; still, it got them to the official building. A woman officer ordered the visitors to register at the Welcome-the-Sun Posthouse before entering the royal city. The pilgrims accepted the instruction. They had been passing through kingdoms by means of an imperial travel document, and this, at last, was a difficulty they knew how to manage.

## The report brought to the throne

The official received them, asked their business, and heard Wukong identify the party. Xuanzang was the sworn brother of the Tang emperor, traveling west for the Buddhist scriptures. His attendants were Wukong, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. They required the sovereign's approval and seal upon the travel pass, after which they wished to continue.

The official copied the names. She apologized for not recognizing an embassy from a distant and powerful realm. The party could remain at the posthouse while she reported to the queen and requested permission for them to pass. The pilgrims settled to a vegetarian meal, relieved that they had found a government office rather than another armed demon.

At court the report produced an effect the officer had not anticipated. The queen had dreamed of good fortune. Now she was told that a man of noble birth, beautiful in appearance, had come out of the East, with servants who looked more like monsters than courtiers. She saw a chance not simply to welcome a guest but to alter her dynasty.

The queen offered a plan. Xuanzang should become her husband and royal consort, even sharing the throne; she would give him the wealth and authority of her kingdom. Their children could inherit the rule. His three disciples, she suggested, could have their documents stamped and continue toward the West. The queen was not pretending to be a demon. In her own court, before her ministers, she proposed a political marriage that made sense to her as a ruler.

The ministers approved. A union of a queen and a royal brother from the great Tang would be a magnificent alliance. They appointed a senior court figure to serve as matchmaker and sent the posthouse officer back with her. Courtship was conducted through agents, titles, and promises of succession, as though the traveler had arrived as a prince seeking a crown.

Xuanzang listened to the offer in growing distress. He had left his own emperor with a vow to obtain scriptures. He could not abandon the road because someone promised him another kingdom. But he also knew that simply refusing a queen who controlled the gates, the guards, and the official seal could leave the whole company imprisoned.

He turned to Wukong. "If she will not let us go unless I agree, what are we to do?"

"Agree," said Wukong. "We shall find a way to leave."

Xuanzang stared at him. To the monk, consent to marriage was not a game. To Wukong, it was the opening move in a negotiation whose final action had not yet been announced.

## A marriage accepted in words

Wukong spoke for the party before the court's representatives. He praised the queen's generosity and allowed them to believe the offer had been accepted. But he put forward conditions. The traveling document must be examined and sealed; the three disciples should have permission to continue; the arrangements should be performed with the proper public dignity. Without those steps, the supposed bridegroom could hardly take his new position.

The representatives were delighted. They hurried back to announce that the match was settled. Xuanzang was less delighted. He was not sure where his disciple intended to draw the line between a harmless performance and a lie made before rulers. Wukong assured him that they would not leave him to become the queen's husband.

The queen ordered preparations. Ministers arranged a feast. Attendants made ready a splendid royal procession, with banners, musicians, carriages, and the ceremonial grandeur of a court accustomed to being seen by its citizens. When the queen appeared at the posthouse, she looked upon Xuanzang with unmistakable admiration. He was the young and handsome stranger the officer had described, dressed as a monk and burdened by a vow that meant everything to him and very little to the woman offering him a realm.

She came forward and called him her royal brother from Tang. She invited him to enter her carriage and proceed to the palace as her intended husband.

Xuanzang blushed and hesitated. Wukong urged him to be polite and not offend his new mother-in-law. He was teasing, but he was also performing for the surrounding officials. If the queen suspected the plot, she might refuse to seal the document. Xuanzang stood close enough for the monkey to hear his whispered fears. Wukong kept smiling. He wanted the carriage to move.

The queen took Xuanzang by the hand. They rode together through the city while crowds watched. She was happy; he was weeping behind an effort to appear composed. This was not a courtship between two people who had discovered that they wanted the same future. It was a royal woman sincerely planning a marriage and a pilgrim trapped in a fiction his disciple hoped to end before it became binding.

Bajie could barely contain himself at the prospect of a wedding feast. He rushed ahead demanding that the cooks make ready food and drink. The queen asked who the great-eared figure was and learned that he was the second disciple, famously ruled by his appetite. She ordered that meat and vegetarian food both be prepared, taking care to accommodate the visitors' religious habits.

The feast was magnificent. Bajie treated the wedding arrangements as an excuse to eat. The queen attended to Xuanzang, asking whether he ate meat, what he drank, and whether he was pleased. Xuanzang answered in the proper forms, always trying to bring the talk back to his wish that the travel pass be stamped promptly. His disciple had taught him to keep the exchange polite, but each new expression of the queen's affection made the deception more painful.

## Names written on the pass

At last Xuanzang asked that the official work be completed before the wedding. The queen called for the travel document, examined the imperial seals it had already acquired, and questioned him about the names of his companions.

She noticed that the disciples were not listed. Xuanzang explained that the three had not come with him from the Tang court. Wukong came from the eastern continent, Bajie from a western district, and Sha Wujing from the Flowing-Sand River. All had lived with grave offenses behind them; Guanyin had persuaded them to accompany the pilgrimage as a way of seeking redemption. They had joined him on the road, after his commission had already been written.

The queen offered to add their names. Xuanzang agreed. She carefully wrote Sun Wukong, Zhu Wuneng, and Sha Wujing on the document, and affixed her seal and mark. In that moment the three disciples gained something more durable than a feast. Their service was publicly recorded in the passport by a sovereign whom they had helped deceive.

The queen then tried to give them gold and silver for the road. Wukong refused: wandering monks should live by begging and proper gifts, not carry treasure. She offered silk, and he refused that as well. At last she offered a small amount of rice for a meal. Bajie accepted immediately. Rice, he explained, was not something that made the luggage heavy for long. He intended to eat it.

With the document properly sealed, Xuanzang made another request. The three disciples should be escorted out of the west gate. He wished to give them his last instructions, he said, before returning to the palace and entering the married life the queen expected. The queen, pleased by his apparent willingness, ordered the procession to go with him.

Wukong had secured the important object. Now he had to secure the man.

## The west gate

Beyond the gate the procession halted. The road curved away from the city. Wukong, Bajie, and Sha Wujing had the horse, the bundles, and the duly sealed travel pass. Xuanzang stepped down and thanked the queen for her hospitality. Then he announced that he himself was going west to obtain scriptures.

At first the queen did not understand. She had offered him the kingdom, prepared the feast, summoned the ministers, sat beside him in the royal carriage. Their wedding had been spoken of openly. Why would he abandon her at the very moment when the disciples were meant to depart?

Xuanzang maintained that he must continue his vow. The queen pleaded with him, caught between private humiliation and a ruler's shock that a formal arrangement should be reversed before the eyes of her court. Bajie stepped forward and began to make frightening faces. He accused the queen of trying to make a married man out of a monk. The court women recoiled; Sha lifted Xuanzang out of the confusion and helped him onto his horse.

The escape was not entirely admirable. The queen had not been feeding men to a fire or hiding a poisoned weapon. The pilgrims had made a promise in order to extract a document, then broken it. Their duty to the scriptures was real, but so was the hurt of the person deceived. The novel allows the reader to see both things at once, even while its comic energy favors Wukong's ingenuity.

Just when the companions seemed to have won, a woman stepped from the roadside. She was neither a court minister nor an embarrassed spectator. She called to the Tang royal brother and demanded that he come with her for pleasure.

Sha Wujing raised his staff. Before he could strike, she summoned a whirlwind. The air filled with dust; Xuanzang vanished from his saddle. The woman had taken him away.

The queen's rejected invitation had been a human danger, governed by ceremonies and words. This new invitation came from something that did not mean to ask permission at all. Wukong looked toward the wind and called the others to pursue. They had escaped the palace, only to lose the monk at the city gate.
