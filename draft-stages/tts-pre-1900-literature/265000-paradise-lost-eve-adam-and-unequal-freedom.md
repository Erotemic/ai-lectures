# Paradise Lost — Eve, Adam, Marriage, and Unequal Freedom

Eve is the first human being to eat the forbidden fruit, but it does not follow that Milton presents her as the only source of human wrongdoing. The poem draws different choices for Eve and Adam, in a world that has already established unequal expectations between them. To understand their decisions, we need to hear what each wants and what each knows. It is not enough to repeat the old formula that a woman was tempted and a man followed.

In Eden, the narrator describes Adam as superior in certain forms of judgment and depicts Eve in terms of beauty, grace, and relation to him. That hierarchy is explicit; it should not be edited away for a modern audience. Yet the poem also gives Eve curiosity, practical intelligence, strong affection, and a capacity to challenge her husband's arguments. Her proposal that they work separately in Book Nine has a rational premise: dividing tasks could make the work more effective. Adam's objection has a rational premise too: Raphael warned them that their enemy would use deception. The disagreement is not between a thinking man and a mindless woman. It is between people who understand freedom and danger differently.

When Eve asks whether a virtue that cannot meet trial is truly virtue, she reaches a philosophical question at the heart of the epic. A creature protected from every temptation might remain unfallen, but would that creature be exercising the liberty God says he has granted? Adam does not compel her to stay. His decision may be read as respect for her agency or as a failure to press a warning he believes important. Critics have argued both possibilities. The narrator's lament over her departure complicates but does not settle the question of how much one partner should control the other.

Satan targets Eve's aspirations. He praises her as a queen, suggests that beauty deserves recognition, and offers the forbidden fruit as a passage toward greater understanding and power. Her temptation is not reducible to vanity; she wants knowledge and considers her place in a relationship that ranks her below Adam. Her language about equality is especially pointed. A reader who believes that equality is a good will feel an uncomfortable pressure: an apparent desire for justice is being enlisted by a deceiver for a destructive aim. Recognizing the coercive structure of the relationship need not require agreeing with the devil's solution.

Adam's transgression is different. He understands that the command has been broken. He eats because he cannot imagine living apart from Eve. The narrator treats this knowingly chosen disobedience as a serious moral failure. It is therefore misleading to treat Adam as a passive victim of her deception. The poem gives both characters responsibility, but not the same motives or the same access to authority.

After the Fall, they experience shame, accusation, and fear. Their reconciliation requires more than deciding who began the trouble. Eve approaches Adam in grief and eventually offers to bear punishment for him. Adam responds not by declaring her incapable of moral action but by recognizing the generosity and pain of her offer. Their mutual prayer is an achievement in a relationship whose earlier innocence made forgiveness unnecessary.

Later readers have disagreed deeply over what this means for Milton's treatment of women. Susan Wiseman places Eve within debates in seventeenth-century religious communities about women's voices and interpretation. Other scholars emphasize the poem's persistent hierarchy, while still others examine how Eve's self-sacrificial gesture anticipates a central Christian virtue. None of these perspectives makes the text simple. A poem can give a woman persuasive inner life and still organize her world through rules a contemporary reader finds unjust.

The final picture is of two people walking together. They are still unequally represented in many respects: Michael instructs Adam at length while Eve sleeps through much of the prophecy. But the last human action is shared. Milton does not give Eve the sole burden of the Fall or Adam the sole possession of hope. An adequate reading has to keep the contradiction between hierarchy and mutuality visible, rather than solving it by denying that either exists.
