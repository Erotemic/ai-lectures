# The Decameron · Day 6: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Day Six: Elissa, the Kitchen Argument, and the Power of a Reply

On the sixth storytelling day the company is governed by Elissa. She has chosen a subject apparently much smaller than Fiammetta's long romances: people who answer a provocation with a skillful remark, or escape humiliation, loss, or danger through a quick word or well-judged action. A phrase can turn the direction of a conversation as a ship's rudder turns a voyage. The stories tend to be short because their force depends on a single moment of understanding.

Before the first tale, however, voices erupt in the servants' quarters. The narrator takes us into a social space that has mostly remained outside the cultivated discussions at the fountain. Licisca, a maid, and Tindaro, a young servant, have been arguing fiercely. Elissa calls them before the company. Their dispute concerns a young woman's sexual history before marriage. Tindaro says she was innocent; Licisca laughs at the assumption that women arrive at marriage without sexual knowledge. She argues from what she claims to know about the behavior of ordinary couples, and speaks with an assurance that disturbs the formal decorum of the assembled gentlemen and ladies.

The audience laughs. Dioneo is asked to settle the dispute, and sides with Licisca. But Elissa sends the maid back to the kitchen and threatens punishment if she does not obey. The group has welcomed candid speech as entertainment while reminding the person who spoke that she is a servant. This is the day's first lesson in verbal power. A witty remark may win applause; it does not necessarily change the speaker's social position. Boccaccio gives Licisca a name, a point of view, and a voice, then shows the boundary placed upon that voice.

The stories follow. Madonna Oretta saves herself from a bad storyteller by extending his own comparison. Cisti, a baker, corrects a nobleman's servant without presuming to scold the nobleman openly. Nonna de' Pulci returns an indecent joke to the men who make it. Chichibio's desperate invention converts an angry master's threat into laughter. Giotto and his traveling companion exchange insults about appearance, while Michele Scalza wins a wager by making a ludicrous argument sound formally correct.

The second half brings higher stakes. Madonna Filippa answers an adultery charge in court and persuades listeners to alter a harsh statute—an imagined reform within the tale, not proof of an actual Prato ordinance. A young woman's uncle tries, without success, to make her see her own unpleasantness. The poet Guido Cavalcanti outwits men who mock his intellectual life. Fra Cipolla, the final speaker's subject, survives a prank by inventing an explanation for a religious relic that is no longer where he expected it to be.

Notice that these are not ten versions of the same moral. A clever phrase can correct arrogance, save someone from a threat, conceal error, or preserve a false claim. We may admire the skill without accepting every purpose to which it is put. The gift of language is neither virtuous nor vicious by itself.

Because the tales are short, the afternoon leaves time for an excursion. The women visit a secluded valley with water and greenery, enjoying a swim away from the men. The place is later brought into the plans for the next day's gathering. Dioneo is crowned ruler and chooses a theme prompted by Licisca's earlier words: the tricks wives play on their husbands. The brief exchange with a servant has reached farther than her mistress intended.

The contrast is subtle but important. At the fountain, the companions can make speech into art. In the kitchen, speech threatens to break rank and custom. Day Six listens to both, and asks what happens when a quick answer crosses the border between laughter and authority.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 1: Madonna Oretta and the Bad Storyteller

A group of ladies and gentlemen is walking between country houses. One gentleman, wishing to entertain Madonna Oretta, offers to carry her part of the way with a story. The riding is imaginary. He means that a fine tale will make a tiresome journey feel short and easy. Oretta accepts. It sounds like a courteous offer, and the promised story is, by its nature, a good one.

The difficulty lies in its telling. The gentleman repeats words over and over, loses the thread, calls characters by the wrong names, corrects himself, backs up, and destroys the effect of scenes that ought to have been moving or amusing. He has a story but cannot make it move. As Oretta listens, what should have been an easy ride becomes a series of jolts. The account is so uncomfortable that she can scarcely endure another moment.

A direct complaint would embarrass the speaker in front of the company. Silence would leave her trapped in a bad performance. Oretta chooses a third way. She accepts his own metaphor and tells him, in effect, that his horse is too rough and she would rather get down. The gentleman immediately understands. He stops the botched story and turns to other conversation. His readiness to take the hint restores the courtesy he had failed to show through narration.

Filomena has given us an image of eloquence without a learned treatise on rhetoric. An excellent tale can fail through poor pacing, repetition, confused names, and a voice unsuited to the people being described. The listener feels this failure bodily, as if jolted by a horse. Oretta's answer is itself a miniature performance of the skill the man lacks. It is brief, figurative, perfectly timed, and clear to its intended hearer.

The joke also depends on a reciprocal agreement. The gentleman proposed a ride by story; Oretta replies as a passenger. She does not seize control of the whole conversation or call him an incompetent fool. She makes her discomfort legible while leaving him a graceful way to stop. In a collection built from the pleasure and difficulty of storytelling, this is an apt opening for a day about speech.

The scene invites a question worth carrying into the longer tales: who has the right to say that a performance has failed? The teller controls the words, but the listener has an experience that those words create. Oretta's power is to report that experience in a form the teller can hear. In a few lines Boccaccio places a practical criticism of narrative craft at the very center of his own hundred-story design.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 2: Cisti the Baker

Cisti earns his living as a baker in Florence. His trade is humble by the social standards of the men who pass his shop, but his business has prospered, and he owns exceptional wine. When Messer Geri Spina and visiting papal envoys begin walking past his doorway on their way to discussions, Cisti wants to offer them something worthy of their company. He fears that an open invitation from a working man might seem presumptuous to distinguished guests.

So he arranges a small performance. He takes a seat outside in spotless clothing, sets out shining cups and a fine flagon, and drinks a little wine with visible appreciation. The sight and his evident pleasure attract the attention of the passersby. Eventually they ask to taste it and discover that his wine is excellent. For several days he offers them refreshment, and the difference between baker and gentleman seems briefly suspended by shared appreciation.

The exchange becomes more delicate when Messer Geri wants to serve some of Cisti's wine to guests at his own house. He sends a servant with a large container. Cisti refuses to fill it and sends the man back. When Geri asks what happened, the baker makes clear, by implication rather than an insult, that such wine is not meant for an indiscriminate serving as though it were ordinary table drink. The nobleman understands the correction. He sends a more fitting vessel and receives the wine. Cisti later offers the remainder of his supply as a gift, and Geri treats him with enduring respect.

Pampinea asks us to see a noble soul concealed by a supposedly low occupation. That is the narrator's social vocabulary, not proof that baking is inherently base. In the action itself, Cisti's accomplishment lies in knowing the quality of what he makes and what he owns. He is willing to share, but he will not allow generosity to be confused with an absence of judgment.

Cisti's reply is effective because he makes Geri reconsider the request without forcing him to lose face in public. The exchange moves from hierarchy toward recognition without pretending the hierarchy disappears. Geri can issue instructions through a servant; Cisti can decline and reveal why the instructions were ill-conceived. The men meet at the level of discernment.

The wine is more than an expensive object. Its fragrance, small cups, and careful serving furnish the language in which Cisti can speak to people who would not ordinarily consult a baker about dignity. A lesser teller might finish with the servant being humiliated. Pampinea ends instead with the gentleman capable of recognizing a rebuke and the craftsman capable of making it.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 3: Nonna de’ Pulci

Lauretta begins by offering a rule for verbal wit. A clever remark should usually bite gently; if it is intended merely to wound, it has become discourtesy. But what if the speaker is answering someone who has already inflicted a public insult? Her example concerns a Florentine bishop, a visiting marshal, and a woman named Monna Nonna de' Pulci.

The marshal, Dego della Ratta, has become notorious through a shameful transaction. He arranged to spend a night with a married woman by making a bargain with her husband; the woman's will was not respected. The payment, moreover, was made in gilded coins of small value rather than genuine gold. The ugly story has circulated. The bishop is related to the woman wronged and publicly acts as though nothing occurred.

One day the bishop and the marshal ride through Florence and encounter Nonna, a respected young married woman. The bishop gestures toward the marshal and makes a suggestive comment about whether the man might conquer her. It places Nonna in a position where silence risks leaving an indecent insinuation unanswered, while anger might be used against her as evidence that she cannot take a joke.

Nonna replies at once that, if such a conquest were attempted, she would want to be paid in sound money. She has not simply returned the man's sexual innuendo. She has recalled the episode of false payment, exposed the cruelty behind the supposedly playful question, and forced the bishop to hear what he was helping to excuse. Both men fall silent.

The remark's force depends on knowledge shared by the street audience. Nonna does not recite an accusation or ask for legal redress. A few words restore the omitted history that makes the bishop's jest disgraceful. An observer who did not know of the earlier transaction would miss the point entirely. Wit here is a way of recalling what power expects everyone to overlook.

Lauretta's rule about the gentle bite now has a qualification. Nonna answers sharply because she was attacked first. The unequal relation between a high-ranking churchman and a woman in the street helps explain why a direct rebuke would be difficult. She speaks inside his chosen register and returns the moral embarrassment to its source.

The tale is not evidence that the earlier abused woman was compensated or that the marshal's conduct had no consequences. In fact the joke depends on the wrong remaining publicly known. Day Six rewards Nonna for maintaining her dignity with language; it does not tell us that language always supplies justice. The two men ride away, temporarily silenced. That is the modest but real victory the story records.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 4: Chichibio and the Crane

Currado Gianfigliazzi has hunted a fine crane and sends it to his cook, a Venetian named Chichibio, with orders that it be roasted for supper. The cook prepares the bird carefully. Then Brunetta, a young woman he loves, arrives in the kitchen, drawn by the smell, and asks for a leg. Chichibio refuses at first. She persists and threatens to withhold her affection. He gives in, cuts off one leg, and serves the rest of the crane to his master.

Currado notices that the bird is missing a leg. Chichibio, unable to admit what he has done, declares that cranes have only one leg. Currado knows this is false and angrily demands proof. Chichibio, trapped by his own invention, promises to demonstrate the fact on living cranes the next morning. The master warns that failure will bring punishment.

At dawn they ride to a place where cranes stand in water. Some birds are resting on one leg, with the other drawn up beneath their feathers. Chichibio points to them in triumph. Currado shouts, and the cranes lower their hidden legs and hurry away. The cook's proof has dissolved as quickly as it appeared. He is left facing the angry man who controls his livelihood and can threaten his body.

Then Chichibio improvises. Had his master shouted at the roast crane the night before, he says, the missing leg would have appeared there too. The answer is absurd, but its timing makes Currado laugh. Anger breaks, and Chichibio escapes punishment.

No one in the audience needs to believe that shouting can restore a roasted bird's leg. The joke works because Chichibio transfers the master's successful demonstration to the wrong situation at the precise moment when the master wants an admission of defeat. The lie is transparent, and that transparency becomes its saving quality. He converts an argument about zoology into an admission that they are now playing with words.

Neifile introduces the story with the thought that chance can supply a timid person with a remark he could never invent in calmer circumstances. Chichibio is not an orator planning a triumph. He is an anxious servant who has made an error for affection and now faces a man with greater power. The joke permits the angry superior to withdraw his threat without feeling that he has lost all control.

Day Six is full of people who know exactly what to say. Chichibio is memorable because he does not know until the decisive instant. The bird's second leg reappears; the cook's life is saved by a sentence that could only have been uttered after everyone had seen it.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 5: Giotto and Forese

Two men ride together from Mugello toward Florence through heavy rain. One is Giotto, the painter, known for extraordinary skill in representing the visible world. The other is Messer Forese da Rabatta, learned in law and respected for his intellect. Neither has the appearance that his reputation might suggest. They have borrowed worn coverings to keep off the rain; their clothes are wet and shabby, and both are rather plain to look at.

Forese glances at his companion and begins to laugh. Would a stranger meeting such a figure, he asks, believe that Giotto was a great painter? The point is easy to understand. The famous artist's outward appearance gives no hint of the beauty he can create. But Forese has neglected one detail: he himself looks equally disreputable.

Giotto replies that a stranger might indeed believe it, if the stranger first took Forese for someone who knew his alphabet. The lawyer's insult has returned to him. He has suggested that appearance proves the absence of talent; Giotto extends the same standard to Forese's learning. The scholar recognizes that he has invited the comparison.

Panfilo's brief story depends on the difference between a person's visible surface and the powers that cannot be inspected by looking at a rain-soaked coat. The two travelers are not nobodies revealed as geniuses by a miraculous trick. Their accomplishments are already public. The comedy is that each now appears so unlike the polished idea of his profession that the next passerby could not guess his name or work.

There is another subtlety. Forese initiates the joke, but his choice of target undermines the claim on which it rests. He wants to use his companion's poor appearance as evidence against him without applying the same reasoning to himself. Giotto does not need a lengthy defense of painting or an exhibition of his art. He simply demands consistency.

The story calls the painter a brilliant talker as well as a master of images. His reply proves that observation need not stop at surfaces. To notice how shabby Forese looks is one thing; to understand that his question contains a self-defeating premise is another. The painter's true skill in this scene is not the ability to draw a convincing likeness, but to reveal the likeness his companion has failed to see.

Giotto was a historical artist, and later biographies have their own anecdotes about his personality. This is a literary story in Boccaccio, not a transcript of a verifiable conversation on a particular wet journey. It makes a compact argument that intellect and craftsmanship are not visible in clothing, and that a person who judges too quickly may find his own judgment turned back upon him.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 6: Michele Scalza and the Baronci

Some young Florentines are arguing about nobility. Which families, they ask, can claim the most ancient and distinguished ancestry? The question matters in a city where family standing shapes opportunities and where prominent names are repeated in public affairs. Each young man proposes a lineage familiar to him. Michele Scalza, a spirited storyteller, declares that they are all wrong. The most ancient noble family, he insists, is the Baronci.

His companions laugh because the Baronci are not famous for elegant looks. Michele offers a wager: let them take the question to a judge, and the loser will pay for supper. He does not intend to prove his claim by genealogy, a chronicle, or records of public office. Instead he exploits the very premise of the debate. If ancestry becomes more noble the farther back it goes, then the oldest family should win.

Michele asks the others to consider the great variety of faces in the Baronci family. They look, he says, as though the maker of humankind had not yet mastered his craft. One has a long face, another a broad one, another a peculiar nose or chin, and the proportions seem uncertain. God must have created them early, before gaining the skill evident in later and better-proportioned people. Therefore their descent reaches further back than anyone else's. The judge and audience laugh and award Michele the supper.

The argument is impossible to take as a factual genealogy, and Boccaccio does not ask us to do so. Its joke consists in applying an apparently serious criterion so mechanically that it produces an absurd conclusion. The audience must know what respectable evidence for family antiquity would look like in order to enjoy the substitute Michele provides.

One may also notice the expense at which the laugh is purchased. The Baronci are made the object of public mockery because of bodily differences. The tale treats the group as comic material without requiring that anyone present answer for the insult. That distribution of power is unlike Nonna de' Pulci's earlier reply, where the verbal target has provoked the speaker. Wit can punish arrogance; it can also recruit an absent person into a joke without consent.

Fiammetta's own contribution is formally playful. Michele has not proved that the family is older than any other; he has proved that, given a premise accepted for fun, clever language can win a prize. A supper is the material reward for a verbal performance. The pleasure lies in watching an argument turn on itself until the room decides to accept what nobody believes.

The central difference is between validity and truth. Michele constructs a chain whose joints sound persuasive only while we agree to ignore its initial assumption. The gentleman who has memorized names of noble houses loses to someone who knows how to make the rules of the debate ridiculous. It is a small satire of prestige resting on ancestry rather than merit, even as it uses physical mockery to deliver the blow.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 7: Madonna Filippa Before the Court

In Prato, according to Filostrato's tale, an ordinance prescribes death by burning for a wife caught in adultery, treating such a woman the same as someone who sells sexual services. Madonna Filippa is discovered with her lover by her husband, Rinaldo. He is enraged and considers killing them, but decides instead to use the ordinance against his wife. The accusation seems to have every fact needed for conviction. Friends urge Filippa not to appear in court, or at least to deny what happened.

She appears. She will not purchase safety with a falsehood. The magistrate is moved by her bearing and wishes she would avoid the admission that the law requires him to punish. Filippa acknowledges the relationship. Then she turns from the fact of her conduct to the reason for the punishment. Who, she asks in substance, made this law? Did the women it condemns agree to it? How can a statute justly treat a woman's desire as a thing that only her husband has the authority to measure?

Filippa asks Rinaldo a question the court cannot evade. Has she ever refused him the affection and intimacy he sought from her? He agrees that she has not. She then frames her conduct as the use of something her husband has not needed. The metaphor of surplus turns a grave accusation into a witty problem of distribution. The crowd laughs and moves toward sympathy with her. The magistrate and townspeople, the story says, alter the law so that the severe penalty no longer applies to the adultery she has admitted, though it remains for commercial sex.

Filippa survives. The courtroom has become an arena in which the accused woman speaks more effectively than the man who brought the charge. She does not win by proving that the accusation was factually mistaken. She wins by denying that the fact should have the consequence the statute assigns to it.

This is a particularly useful test case for Day Six. Elsewhere the quick reply saves a servant from an angry master or a lady from a social slight. Here the threat is a death sentence. Wit is therefore not simply elegance of speech. It makes a legal rule newly visible to those who have been enforcing it as though it were self-evident.

Yet the reform has limits. Filippa does not establish equal freedom for all women. The narrator's conclusion preserves a severe distinction between married women whose conduct can be presented as pleasure and women who have sex for payment. The logic also makes something of the husband's satisfaction a precondition for the defense. Modern readers can recognize the force of Filippa's argument without imagining a general victory for sexual equality.

The ordinance and reform belong to the fictional Prato of the novella. They should not be repeated as verified events in the legislative history of the town simply because Boccaccio gives them a civic setting. His achievement here is dramatic: a woman tells the truth in front of the people who could kill her, and a few sentences reorganize what that truth is taken to mean.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 8: Fresco and the Mirror

Fresco da Celatico has a niece called Ciesca. She believes herself superior to nearly everyone she meets and is continually disgusted by the appearance and manners of ordinary people. Walking through the city seems a trial because she finds no face or manner worthy of her approval. Her uncle has heard enough of these complaints and would like her to consider that a constant habit of contempt may say something about the person expressing it.

One day Ciesca returns home irritated by the people in the streets. She tells Fresco that she cannot bear the sight of so many tiresome, offensive faces. He advises a remedy of startling simplicity. If the sight of objectionable people distresses her, she should avoid looking into a mirror.

The answer is pointed, but it fails to change its hearer. Ciesca does not understand that her uncle is naming her as one of the people she detests. She simply insists that she intends to use a mirror like everyone else. Fresco's joke has reached the audience, but not the woman for whom it was designed. Emilia closes with her pride still intact.

This makes an interesting contrast with Madonna Oretta's opening story. Oretta finds a gentle phrase that the bad storyteller can understand and accept. Fresco finds a sharper phrase, but the intended target is not capable of hearing its implication. A witty sentence can be perfect as a sentence and ineffective as an attempt at persuasion. That difference matters in a day devoted to verbal victories.

There is also a social dimension. Fresco has no court, magistrate, or employer to appeal to. He is a relative who can admonish without issuing commands that Ciesca must obey. Her self-regard is therefore safe from correction unless she chooses to reconsider it. The narrative measures her deficiency partly by the refusal to imagine that another person might perceive her differently from how she perceives herself.

The mirror becomes an economical symbol. It makes a person's face visible, but it cannot guarantee self-knowledge. Someone who approaches it only to admire herself can remain blind to the character she displays in speech. In a book full of disguises and mistakes of identity, this little scene turns the problem inward. The person least able to recognize herself has no costume to remove.

The story is brief because anything longer would blunt its effect. Fresco's entire correction fits into one instruction. That Ciesca misses it allows the listener, rather than the character, to understand the joke. We end with a form of wit that succeeds as comedy and fails as moral education.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 9: Guido Cavalcanti Among the Tombs

Guido Cavalcanti was a Florentine poet and intellectual. In Boccaccio's tale he is also a man who prefers serious thought to the easy games of the young gentlemen who ride together through the city. Messer Betto and his companions would like Guido to join their company. He declines. They interpret this reluctance as arrogance or as a sign that his philosophical speculation has carried him too far from ordinary life. They repeat the gossip that he thinks too much about whether God exists.

One day they find him walking near the baptistery of San Giovanni, among monumental stone tombs. The gentlemen ride toward him as though to capture him in a social trap. If he has been investigating the nonexistence of God, they jeer, what will he have gained by proving such a thing? The question is not a sincere inquiry about philosophy. It is an attempt to embarrass a man who has refused their company.

Guido answers that they may say what they please in their own house. With that he places a hand on a tomb, vaults across it, and goes away. The riders are confused. They are standing in a public space, not inside a private house. Why would Guido refer to a house belonging to them? Some take his answer as nonsense.

Betto understands. A tomb is a house for the dead. Guido's phrase has placed the riders among the dead, suggesting that those who refuse to learn and think are, by comparison, scarcely alive in the life of the mind. The insult is quick, but it takes time to decipher. Betto explains it to the others, and they cease mocking the poet.

The story is about more than a neat insult. Guido's physical movement makes his words visible. He answers from a place of burial and immediately leaps beyond it. In a few moments he distinguishes living thought from social immobility. The gentlemen are mounted on horses but remain standing in bewilderment; the solitary pedestrian moves away freely.

There is a historical Guido Cavalcanti, a poet associated with the late thirteenth-century intellectual and literary life of Florence. The scene in the cemetery is nevertheless Boccaccio's narrated anecdote, not a stenographic record of Guido confronting a documented group on a specified day. Nor does it establish what Guido himself believed about God. The gossip within the tale is part of the provocation, and the retort works independently of proving the gossip true.

Elissa has chosen an apt concluding tale for the speakers who remain under her rule. It lets wit depend on intellectual distance. The audience must first fail to understand the sentence, then have another person interpret it. The joke rewards the listening community for seeing a meaning inaccessible to the men who began it. Words have power here, but understanding the words is itself an accomplishment.

## The Decameron — Day Six, Tale 10: Fra Cipolla and the Coals

In Certaldo, a friar nicknamed Cipolla, or Onion, comes to collect offerings on behalf of Saint Anthony. He is small, red-haired, sociable, and an extraordinarily fluent speaker. He may lack formal learning, but he knows how to hold a crowd. Announcing that he has returned from a sacred journey, he promises to display a feather left by the angel Gabriel at the Annunciation. The villagers prepare to listen and contribute.

Two local pranksters, Giovanni and Biagio, decide to test the friar's powers. They discover that his servant Guccio is easier to distract than to supervise. While Guccio is occupied, one of the men searches Cipolla's belongings, finds the box containing the supposed feather, and removes it. He replaces the contents with a handful of charcoal. The plan is simple: when the friar opens the box in public, the marvelous relic will have disappeared, exposing him before everyone.

The appointed moment comes. Cipolla addresses the gathering and opens the casket. He sees the coals. He does not confess fraud, accuse his servant, or stop speaking. Instead he announces that two precious objects from the Holy Land were kept in similar boxes. One contained Gabriel's feather; the other held coals from the fire on which Saint Lawrence was roasted. A providential mistake, he says, has brought out the second relic precisely when the people should honor it. He gives a long, elaborately inventive account of his travels and discoveries, drawing the listeners along with the confidence of someone who controls his own performance.

The people accept the substitution. Cipolla shows the charcoal and uses it to mark those who come forward. Offerings continue. The pranksters, surprised and delighted by the escape, return the feather to its box and enjoy the friar's verbal agility. He has not proved anything about the objects. He has prevented the interruption from destroying his authority.

Dioneo has chosen a larger story than the brief replies of the preceding speakers. It shows improvisation operating not as a single sentence but as an entire replacement narrative. Cipolla's power comes from anticipating what the crowd will treat as evidence. The sight of charcoal, which could expose the promised feather as fraudulent, becomes evidence for another marvel the audience did not know it was about to encounter.

A listener should distinguish the saint named in Cipolla's story from the actual history of Christian relics. The tale satirizes a persuasive collector who offers unverifiable wonders in exchange for alms. It does not show that every medieval religious object was fabricated, nor does it independently verify the friar's own account of Gabriel or Lawrence. The interesting fact inside the fiction is that a skillful explanation can absorb apparently contradictory evidence without ever submitting the original claim to a test.

The story also returns to the servants' world that opened the day. The trick succeeds because Guccio fails to guard the box. The larger social drama revolves around who manages the labor and attention required to stage an authoritative public performance. Cipolla's fluent voice can conceal a neglected practical task. Yet he depends on Guccio just as the company at the country house depends on its cooks and servants.

After Dioneo's story, the ten friends have time left in the afternoon. The women explore the nearby Valley of the Ladies, with its clear water and secluded beauties, and arrange to share its pleasures with the group. Dioneo takes the crown for the next day and announces a theme born from Licisca's kitchen argument: tricks played by wives upon husbands. A servant's outspoken opinion, dismissed by Elissa at the beginning of the session, now shapes the next gathering.

The final tale of Day Six tests the difference between mastering language and speaking truth. Fra Cipolla is brilliantly successful at maintaining his story. What he says has become no more credible. Boccaccio has led us through a day when words protect, criticize, rescue, humiliate, deceive, and delight. The measure of a reply is not simply whether the audience applauds it, but what that reply enables its speaker to do.
