# The Canterbury Tales — The Second Nun’s Tale, Cecilia and the Martyrs

The Second Nun begins with an appeal to diligence against idleness. She then tells the life of Saint Cecilia, a Christian martyr from the legendary past of Rome. Cecilia is promised in marriage to Valerian, but has privately vowed to remain chaste and devout. On their wedding night she tells her husband that an angel guards her body and that he must honor her commitment. Valerian asks to see the angel. She sends him to Pope Urban, who instructs and baptizes him. Returning, he sees Cecilia with the angel and understands the claim that had seemed impossible.

Valerian's brother Tiburce joins them in Christian faith. The brothers give help to the poor and bury martyrs, defying Roman authorities who demand obedience to other gods. Their courage leads to arrest and execution. The Roman officer Almachius then summons Cecilia. He assumes that a young woman, isolated from her husband and brother-in-law, can be frightened into compliance. She answers him with confidence, refusing to treat earthly office as the ultimate source of truth. The exchange becomes a contest over whose authority lasts.

Almachius orders her put to death. First he attempts to kill her by heat in her bath, but she survives. An executioner strikes her neck three times with a sword yet fails to sever her head. Law or custom forbids a fourth blow, and Cecilia remains alive for several days, speaking with her followers and arranging for her possessions to be used for the Christian community. She finally dies and is buried by Urban. The story presents her suffering as testimony rather than defeat.

The Nun's account is a saint's life, not a modern reconstruction of third-century Rome from independent documentary evidence. Its legendary miracles matter to the genre because they display divine power against the emperor's officers. The historical cult of Cecilia is real, but the details of her martyrdom were shaped in later devotional traditions. Treating this narrative as a literary account of sanctity allows listeners to understand its purpose without having to claim that every scene can be verified as history.

The story has a particular force in the Canterbury company because Cecilia is a female speaker who resists a man's political and marital claims. She does not approach marriage as the Wife of Bath did, but her insistence upon a commitment made to God supplies another answer to the question of authority over a woman's life. Her husband accepts rather than destroys that commitment. The contrast with Walter's testing of Griselda or January's wish to possess May is significant, even though each story belongs to a different literary tradition.

The Second Nun's devotional seriousness also follows the Monk's tragic catalogue and the Nun's Priest's comic rescue. These pilgrims have not agreed about the proper subject of a tale. A tragic fall, an animal fable and a saint's triumph can all occupy the same road. Their proximity exposes what changes when suffering is interpreted through Fortune, comic intelligence or miraculous faith. The next story will return to the moral danger of claiming knowledge, this time through a servant who describes the exhausting failures and frauds of his master's alchemical laboratory.
