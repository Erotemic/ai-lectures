# The Decameron — Peronella and the Barrel

Peronella lives in Naples with a husband who leaves early each morning to earn a living. Their household is modest: a single useful room, small goods to sell, little time for privacy. When he has gone, she sometimes receives a man named Giannello. One morning, before her husband is due home, the two are together. Then the door sounds. Her husband has returned. Peronella thrusts Giannello into an empty barrel, the great household vessel that ordinarily takes up space rather than concealing danger.

The husband arrives not empty-handed but excited. He has met a buyer for that very barrel and expects to receive five gold florins for it. He thinks this is a fortunate business opportunity. Peronella, with no time to devise a second hiding place, reverses the situation. Five florins? She has already sold the barrel for seven. The buyer, she says, is inside it at this moment, examining it before taking it away. What appeared to her husband as evidence of her infidelity suddenly becomes evidence of her financial cleverness.

Giannello hears his cue. He comes out, plays the demanding customer and complains that the inside is dirty. He will pay the higher price only if the vessel is cleaned. The husband, now afraid of losing a better bargain than the one he brought home, climbs into the barrel to scrub its interior. Peronella bends over the opening and gives brisk instructions about where to rub and how thoroughly to wash. At the same time Giannello takes sexual advantage of the concealment created by the husband's labor. The narrator makes the physical arrangement unmistakable, even as the dialogue presents a brisk scene of commercial negotiation.

At last the cleaning is finished. Giannello pays, takes the barrel and departs. Peronella's husband believes he has helped her complete a successful sale. He has been made to do the work that allows his wife's lover to escape, and he congratulates the household on its fortunate transaction. Money changes hands, but in the accounting of the tale the more important transaction is control of the scene.

Filostrato tells the story quickly. There is no supernatural claim and no elaborate invented biography. Peronella merely makes her first statement fit the visible evidence so well that everyone must act as though her account is true. The husband sees a stranger emerging from a barrel; the narrator has supplied a reason for that stranger to be there. Once the man accepts the role of vendor's assistant, every new action reinforces the false story.

The comedy has a class dimension. These are working people whose small property and earnings matter. The barrel is not a decorative prop. Selling it can noticeably improve the household's means, and that possibility makes the husband cooperate. The wife's command of the situation is brilliant, but the erotic joke depends on turning the husband's trusting labor into his humiliation. Boccaccio asks us to watch a household object become a stage, where the same gestures can mean honest work to one person and deception to another.
