# Paradise Lost — Book Six: The War in Heaven

Raphael continues his warning to Adam by describing the war that followed Satan's revolt. Abdiel, the angel who left the rebel camp alone, returns to the faithful host and is welcomed for resisting the pressure of numbers. He has not defeated Satan by speech, but he has refused to allow a false claim to stand unchallenged. His arrival marks a striking contrast between the rebel army's devotion to a charismatic commander and a loyalty that can exist without guaranteed success.

Heaven's forces march under Michael and Gabriel. Satan's host comes to meet them. The violence is immense but not yet like earthly warfare. Angels do not suffer ordinary human wounds, and the poem imagines their bodies as capable of strange transformations and recoveries. Michael strikes Satan and wounds him. The spectacle humiliates the leader who promised himself invulnerable. Yet neither side produces a final settlement on the first day. The rebellious forces retreat, still determined to discover some means of turning the balance.

Through the night Satan and his officers plan an innovation. They produce artillery filled with explosive powder and iron shot, a technology more familiar to the seventeenth-century reader than to the biblical story. On the second day the guns roar. Heavenly soldiers who expected a contest of strength and courage are staggered by machines designed to strike from a distance. The rebels laugh at those they have surprised. Then the faithful host tears great hills from their foundations and hurls the mountains at the batteries. Milton turns the whole landscape into ammunition.

It would be a mistake to read these scenes as straightforward admiration for superior weaponry. The war escalates into absurdity. Each side can produce a larger act of force; the scale threatens the world that contains it. Satan treats ingenuity as evidence that his cause is just, when it proves only that he can devise new means of injury. The poem's most crowded military scenes make an old question newly pointed: how much does a heroic triumph mean when courage and invention are detached from goodness?

On the third day the Father sends the Son. The purpose of the prolonged struggle becomes clearer: the Son will reveal a kind of authority that does not merely exceed Satan's power by a measurable amount. He rides in a divine chariot and drives the rebels before him. Rather than celebrating an endless massacre, the poem describes the host losing the capacity to stand. At the edge of Heaven they are cast out, falling through the enormous gulf toward Hell. The narrative we heard in Book One has now been given its earlier cause.

The war is difficult to picture chronologically if one imagines Earth's ordinary calendar already in place. Raphael is recounting an angelic history before the created world has settled into human time. Milton supplies days and sequences to make the account tellable, not a precise timetable for an astronomer to verify. The angel reminds Adam that divine action has to be translated into forms a human mind can hear.

Raphael ends by relating this distant conflict to a danger close at hand. Satan has not been cured of hatred by defeat. He now seeks to hurt the beings who received a world he cannot enjoy. Adam and Eve are not to treat the warning as proof that resistance is hopeless. They are being told that obedience remains theirs to choose. Heaven's victory in a great battle does not substitute for the smaller decision each human will make in a garden.

And this is where the story's notion of heroism begins to shift. Satan gathered armies and improvised weapons; Abdiel stood alone; the Son's power ended the conflict. The reader is asked to distinguish bravery, technical ingenuity, and righteousness rather than calling them all the same thing. A battlefield can show what someone dares. It cannot, by that fact alone, tell us what ought to have been dared.
