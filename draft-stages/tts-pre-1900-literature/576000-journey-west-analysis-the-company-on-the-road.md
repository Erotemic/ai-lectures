# Journey to the West — The Company on the Road: Appetite, Work, and Companionship

## Why four companions and a horse?

When people remember *Journey to the West*, they often see a monkey in motion: a somersault across the clouds, a staff expanding to impossible size, a monster realizing too late who its opponent is. Yet a journey made only by Sun Wukong would not be the same novel. If speed were all that mattered, Wukong could cross great distances in moments. If fighting prowess were all that mattered, he might travel alone. The scripture mission instead gives him a slow human master, two other former celestial offenders, and a dragon sentenced to be a horse. They must move at the pace of the body that cannot fly.

That arrangement is the source of much of the comedy, but it is also a formal invention. It turns a quest into a social world. Food must be found. Lodging must be negotiated. A horse must be led and burdens carried. Travel papers must be endorsed. Someone must stay with Xuanzang when Wukong scouts ahead. Someone must tell Wukong when he has behaved intolerably. Someone must be willing to go looking for him when pride or pain drives him away.

The five travelers are not five examples of the same kind of courage. Their abilities are uneven, their origins are disgraceful in different ways, and their hopes at the end of the road are not identical. The novel's portrait of cultivation is therefore inseparable from the practical problem of living together.

## Bajie and the honesty of appetite

Zhu Bajie enters the story as a source of trouble for a family in Gao Village. A creature who once served in Heaven has fallen through misconduct and been reborn with a pig-like form. The narrative does not excuse his harassment of the household by making him funny. He is dangerous, possessive, and capable of imposing himself on people with less power. But when he joins the pilgrimage, the story gives this same figure a long comic life: greedy at meals, interested in comfort, susceptible to flattering offers, quick to suggest division of the luggage when disaster seems inevitable.

It is tempting to turn Bajie into a symbol of appetite and leave him there. He certainly gives that reading material. In the test of the false widow and her daughters, his eagerness for marriage is exposed by divine visitors. The joke works because Bajie has converted an explicitly religious journey into an opportunity for a private household. Throughout the book he dreams aloud of food, rest, respectable domestic life, and escapes from suffering.

Yet what is wrong with wanting a home? Xuanzang has renounced the ordinary bonds of marriage and family in service of a vow. Bajie has not attained such renunciation. He is subject to the claims of a body that gets hungry and tired. The novel mocks his evasions, but it also permits a perspective from which the pilgrim ideal looks demanding, perhaps even impossible for most creatures. A group that has to cross a real road cannot dispense with those bodily facts by speaking in lofty phrases.

Bajie also supplies practical labor. He digs, clears obstacles, uses his rake, fights in conditions where Wukong cannot watch every side, and helps move the group onward. In the episode of the giant python and the filthy, blocked road, his body and his tool become an answer to problems that cannot be wished away by a single heroic leap. His tasks may be less glorious than his brother's aerial combat, but the pilgrimage would not be an earthly pilgrimage without them.

The small reversals of Bajie's character matter more than one spectacular conversion. He complains, but he returns. He is frightened, but he sometimes acts. He is jealous of Wukong's prestige, but after Xuanzang's transformation into a tiger he travels to Flower-Fruit Mountain to beg the monkey to come back. This is not a moment when Bajie suddenly becomes a new person. It is a moment when affection and obligation prove stronger than the easy solution of abandoning a difficult companion. He can be cowardly and useful in the same episode, even the same conversation.

His final appointment preserves the comedy. The others receive exalted names, while Bajie is rewarded in a form still linked with offerings and food. He is dissatisfied, and that dissatisfaction sounds exactly like him. A critical reader can see the outcome as evidence that his appetite remains incompletely transformed. Another can see that the novel refuses the implausible ending in which every lively imperfection is erased. The joke is not simply punishment. It is continuity of character under a new religious title.

## Sha Wujing and the visibility of work

Sha Wujing is less noisy. Before joining the pilgrims he inhabits Flowing-Sand River, a former celestial attendant cast down into a terrifying condition. He has harmed travelers. The party cannot simply ask him politely to become good and proceed. His enlistment comes through recognition, intervention, and the possibility of atonement.

Once he joins, his steadiness is easily mistaken for an absence of character. Wukong's speeches seize attention. Bajie's jokes provoke replies. Xuanzang's distress sets events moving. Sha often carries the baggage, holds the line, looks after the master, or provides a measured warning. Yet if we ask what a long journey requires, much of its answer resembles Sha's work. He endures the time between spectacular incidents. He does not receive the story's best jokes because he is not principally organized around dramatic self-advertisement.

In the two-Monkey-Kings episode, Sha must confront a world in which even a famous protector can be counterfeited. He is not simply background luggage. His choices and reporting help move the question of identity through a succession of celestial authorities. The ordinary labor of accompanying, identifying, retrieving, and accounting becomes crucial when even superior magical perception fails.

We might call Sha a character of maintenance. That is an interpretive description, not a claim that the novel gives him a job title. Maintenance lacks the glamour of rebellion and victory, but it is indispensable to a partnership. Someone must continue when another is offended. Someone must keep watch while the heroic figure pursues a clue. Someone must carry what everyone hopes will eventually be found at the end.

There is a risk in praising his quietness too easily. The comparative lack of speech can also expose a limitation of the narrative's attention. A reader may want to know more about what Sha thinks or feels. Rather than inventing an interior life and claiming the novel supplies it, we can use the silence as a question about which kinds of work heroic storytelling chooses to make audible.

## The dragon horse and the burden of being overlooked

The white horse begins as a dragon prince under punishment. His transformation turns a celestial being into transport. For long stretches, he can appear to be an animal in the background of his master's spiritual journey. The reader who looks only for spoken character development may almost forget him.

But the horse provides the clearest measure of the journey's bodily duration. Wukong can scout a mountain in a moment; the horse has to climb it. A river must be crossed with weight, papers, and baggage, not just with a victorious leap. The horse is the difference between knowing where the Buddha is and taking a human body there.

The episode in which Xuanzang has been transformed into a tiger offers an important interruption of this apparent passivity. The dragon resumes an active form to challenge the yellow-robed enemy and is wounded. He then helps direct attention toward the recovery of Wukong. The action reveals a capacity that was always present beneath the humble role. In the final chapter, his dragon form is restored and he receives an appointment of his own. The novel makes us look again at the being whose contribution was largely to carry someone else.

The symbolic phrase 'horse of the will' offers another way to read the animal. A bridled horse might figure the direction of desire or intention. But symbolic language has to live alongside the literal journey. The horse does not become less important because he can stand for a mental faculty. Quite the reverse: one reason the symbol works is that managing a horse is a continuous, material practice of moving toward a destination.

## Fellowship as repeated disagreement

Many adventure stories form a team and then allow its members to become seamlessly cooperative. This novel refuses that convenience. Bajie repeatedly urges retreat or the partition of baggage. Wukong repeatedly insults him. Xuanzang misunderstands warnings. Sha repeatedly has to steady situations that more vocal characters worsen. The conflicts are not accidental interruptions to a story of spiritual progress; they are one of the means by which progress is tested.

Take the protective circle Wukong draws in the episode of the golden cave. He warns the other pilgrims not to leave its safety. Hunger, comfort, and curiosity help draw them out. They find an enchanted dwelling, take things that do not belong to them, and fall into a trap. We can laugh at the folly, but the scene presents several versions of responsibility. Wukong issues a rule that would have worked if obeyed. He must also leave companions who cannot understand the risks as he does. The others exercise freedom badly, but their mistake does not relieve their protector of the task of returning. Fellowship persists after the rule has failed.

At Heaven-Reaching River, the spirit demands children as offerings. Wukong and Bajie assume their places so the children may live. In that episode physical courage and theatrical deception serve an act of rescue. The same pair whose quarrels fill the road can coordinate to protect strangers. Their mischievous acting is not separate from their ethical purpose; it makes the purpose possible.

The old turtle later carries the entire group across the water. That rescue creates a promise to ask the Buddha when the turtle may shed his shell and become human. Xuanzang forgets. On the return journey the turtle throws them into the river. This failure belongs neither to a monstrous villain nor to the usual suspects in the team. The pious master has neglected an obligation to someone who helped them. The companions cannot avoid accountability by saying the scriptures were finally obtained. Group life includes debts that remain after the official mission is complete.

## Four ways of failing

We can distinguish the companions by their characteristic temptations without imprisoning them in allegorical slots. Wukong is drawn to pride in his ability and his perception. Bajie is drawn to bodily gratification, retreat, and self-excusing complaint. Sha can be too easily overlooked, even by the narrative that depends on him. Xuanzang is drawn to righteous certainty about things he has not understood. The horse endures an imposed role whose meaningfulness is visible to others only intermittently.

But the most revealing moments occur when someone exceeds that expectation. Bajie goes back to retrieve Wukong. The horse takes up a fight. Xuanzang persists when every companion doubts the route. Wukong must admit that his weapons and transformations are not enough and seek help from a being who knows the demon's origin. Sha steps forward when identification and loyalty, not combat, are the decisive tasks.

If the novel presented a procession of perfect saints, every episode would amount to an external test of a group already completed. Instead it presents companions who continually need to be companions again. Their mistakes are repeated because character is not refashioned by one vow. Virtue is something they have to do across days of fatigue, anger, danger, and dependence.

This reading also complicates the familiar phrase 'master and disciples.' Xuanzang holds religious authority, but without his disciples he cannot survive. Wukong seems the most capable, but cannot make the scripture mission meaningful by himself. Bajie may be comic, but he has friends to persuade and burdens to carry. Sha may lack prestige, but the group rests on his reliability. The horse is a prince reduced to service, yet every mile he carries is part of his own transformation. Hierarchy does not erase dependence; it organizes dependence and sometimes conceals it.

## The group at the end

The last chapter might have collapsed these personalities into a single triumphant collective. It does not. They receive different appointments. Bajie still protests. The horse changes form. Wukong reaches for his circlet. The solemn ceremony has room for jokes, surprise, and self-recognition.

The mission was collective, but it has not made the travelers interchangeable. That is a stronger conclusion than a claim that they have finally learned to agree on everything. They have learned, with considerable difficulty, to complete something together while remaining recognizable as themselves.

One way to describe *Journey to the West* is an epic about a dangerous mind learning its limits. Another is a religious quest across an elaborate cosmos. Both are valuable. But in the daily life of its road, it is also a story about work: the work of carrying, feeding, waiting, retrieving, forgiving, and turning back for the person who has left. Those labors do not merely support the pilgrimage. They are part of what the pilgrimage teaches.
