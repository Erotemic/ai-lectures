# Paradise Lost — Book Two: The Council, Sin, Death, and Chaos

The devils have built a palace, but their next action requires a decision. Satan presides from a throne like a monarch while inviting discussion like the leader of a council. Four proposals expose four ways to live after a failed revolt. Moloch wants open warfare. Better to hurl themselves again at Heaven, he argues, than endure a humiliating peace. His courage has ceased to measure probable results. Destruction itself has become a form of defiance.

Belial speaks with an elegance that conceals fear. Another assault may bring consequences more terrible than their present sufferings. Perhaps the punishment can grow no worse if they remain still. Mammon recommends a different kind of pride. They can turn Hell into their own kingdom, mine its resources, practice their arts, and learn to prefer self-sufficiency to obedience. Neither peace nor war need be discussed in relation to the welfare of those beyond the assembly. The devils argue about freedom while taking their own injured interests as the whole universe.

Beelzebub then presents a plan far more attractive to the room. He has heard of a new world and a race of human creatures. Instead of assaulting the inaccessible divine throne, the rebels might corrupt these creatures and draw them away from the Creator. We learn that the proposal originated with Satan. The debate has not produced an independent conclusion so much as prepared the audience to welcome what its chief already wished. The assembly applauds. Yet when someone must cross the immeasurable abyss to discover the world, the room falls silent. Satan offers to go alone, winning the glory of risking himself for a mission designed to ruin the innocent.

He flies toward the gates of Hell. There he encounters two figures whose bodies are themselves stories: Sin, a woman above the waist whose lower form is monstrous, and Death, a terrible shadow with a weapon. Death threatens the stranger, and Satan prepares to fight. Sin interrupts. She recognizes Satan as her father. When he first conceived rebellion in Heaven, she sprang from his head, an embodiment of the sinful thought that had taken shape. Satan desired her and fathered Death. Death in turn violated Sin, and the creatures born of this violation continually torment their mother. This is a horrifying allegory of an evil that breeds further injury within its own family.

Sin has been entrusted with the keys of Hell. She is not persuaded by a moral argument. Satan promises that if he succeeds, she and Death may roam the new world and feed there. The opening of the gates will not injure only the rebels; it will release an expanding power of destruction into the space where human life is to unfold. Sin gives way. Behind Satan the gates swing open, and their immense doors cannot easily be closed again.

Beyond them lies Chaos, not empty space in a modern astronomical sense but a turbulent realm of matter and possibility, where up and down lose their reliability. Elements struggle before they have been ordered into a world. Satan must struggle through shifting winds, abysses, and regions without stable orientation. He encounters Chaos and Night, ancient powers who resent the ordered universe emerging within their domain. They have reason to welcome trouble for the world Heaven has made, and Satan obtains directions.

Finally he glimpses the newly created universe suspended in the vastness, with Heaven above and Hell behind. His crossing has made him look, for a while, like the lone traveler of a heroic epic. But we should remember the purpose the poem keeps before us. He has endured danger to reach a place whose inhabitants have not harmed him. His intended triumph is to produce in them the captivity he claims to resist. The book ends with a traveler nearing the world, not with a victory for the principles he uses to describe himself.
