# Moby-Dick — Scripture, Theatre, and the Language of Prophecy

Moby-Dick repeatedly sounds like a religious book, a sermon, and a Shakespearean tragedy. This does not mean that it can be reduced to one religious allegory. Melville borrows the languages of authority and places them in competition. The preacher at New Bedford, the captain on the quarterdeck, the sailor telling a story, and the narrator lecturing about whales all ask listeners to trust different kinds of words. The novel is interested in what happens when language does more than describe: when it binds people, interprets disaster, or claims permission to command.

Father Mapple's sermon tells the biblical story of Jonah. Jonah's duty is frightening because he is commanded to confront the powerful with truths they do not want to hear. He tries to escape the command by sailing away, and the sea becomes the setting of his punishment and return. The sermon gives the voyage a language of calling and refusal before Ahab appears. Readers should resist turning the passage into a one-to-one allegory. Ahab can resemble both the fugitive and the false prophet, and Starbuck also has a difficult duty to speak against authority. The story supplies questions more fruitfully than it supplies a fixed set of character labels.

The quarterdeck is a ceremony of persuasion. Ahab displays the doubloon, raises his voice, presses a shared oath upon his men, and transforms a private injury into an apparent sacred cause. These are dramatic actions with real consequences. The crew's cries resemble a chorus; Ahab is a tragic speaker whose eloquence makes dangerous ideas emotionally attractive. Melville gives him a power that the reader can feel. To recognize the grandeur of the speech is not to accept its reasoning. The same scene contains Starbuck's objection, a countervoice speaking for ordinary work and human limits.

Ahab often speaks as though he can read the universe through signs. Fedallah's predictions appear to promise that the captain's death requires impossible conditions. Ahab hears them as assurance of near invulnerability. Yet the wording is equivocal, and the final chase produces physical interpretations he did not imagine. The whaling lines and the bodies of ships are not exempt from ordinary causation. Prophecy becomes dangerous when a commander mistakes his favorite interpretation for a guarantee and compels others to live according to it.

The novel's theatrical form makes these interpretive contests visible. Ahab has soliloquies; Starbuck speaks inwardly; sailors enter and exit scenes as though on a stage. These passages do not obey the limits of a shipmate's eyewitness report. Their purpose is to expose competing inner lives. Ahab wants the world to contain one antagonist. Starbuck wishes to keep a promise to employers and family. Pip's speech comes from trauma that cannot be turned into the captain's heroic quest. The theatrical arrangement allows the voices to confront one another without supplying an authoritative judge at every moment.

The story also borrows names and imagery from religious and historical sources: Ahab, Elijah, and other biblical resonances complicate the sailor's world. Names create expectations, but resemblance is not proof that any event has one predetermined meaning. The risk of treating every biblical echo as a code is that we begin to read the book with the same compulsive certainty that destroys its captain. Attention to form requires more patience: where does the narrator encourage a parallel, and where does the scene resist it?

One useful exercise is to compare Father Mapple's sermon with Ahab's oath. Both involve speech before an audience, the sea, an extraordinary demand, and fear. But who benefits from the demand? Who may refuse? What evidence exists that the speaker has authority to impose it? Then compare Ahab's late conversation about home with his next decision to continue. In that gap between eloquence and action, a tragic character's insight fails to become moral change.

Melville's religious and theatrical languages give the book its scale, but they also make it self-critical. A person can speak magnificently and still act unjustly. A prophecy can carry aesthetic force and remain a poor guide to survival. A sermon can open questions that one captain's performance attempts to close. The novel places all these voices at sea and lets the final disaster test what their words have done.
