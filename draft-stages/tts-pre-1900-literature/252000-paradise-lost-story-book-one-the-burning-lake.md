# Paradise Lost — Book One: The Burning Lake and Pandemonium

Milton begins with a catastrophe already accomplished. Before the first human being has entered the story, an angelic rebellion has failed. The rebels have been thrown from Heaven, and their leader lies stretched upon a burning lake in Hell. There is no gradual introduction of Satan as a child who becomes evil. The poem opens after a choice has been made and a battle lost. We must learn the cause of that defeat through the voices of people who have good reasons to explain it to their own advantage.

The poet begins by asking a heavenly Muse to tell of humanity's first disobedience, the fruit of a forbidden tree, and the loss of Paradise. This is an epic invocation like those in Homer and Virgil, but its source of authority is not the pagan pantheon. Milton names biblical places associated with revelation and asks the Spirit that witnessed creation to teach a poet attempting a theme beyond ordinary human memory. He announces a difficult ambition: to defend the justice of God's dealings with humankind. We have heard the result of the human story before its beginning. Suspense will lie less in whether Eden is lost than in the process by which creatures capable of love and reason become capable of betraying them.

The fallen commander is still imposing. He turns to Beelzebub, his closest companion, and speaks of a struggle that must not end merely because the army has been beaten. His voice makes catastrophe sound like an opportunity. Since they cannot conquer by force, perhaps they can strike by cunning. He sees the awful immensity of Hell, its darkness visible, its fire that illuminates nothing comforting, and refuses to call his defeat final. This is courage in the grammatical shape of pride. The narration asks us to hear both.

Satan and Beelzebub rise from the lake. The great fallen leader summons his scattered legions from the flames. They have lain stunned, but now they assemble with military discipline. Milton gives their commanders a procession of names familiar from ancient religions: Moloch, Chemos, and other deities whom later human societies will worship. The catalog moves backward and forward in history at once. These spirits have fallen before humanity's creation, yet the narrator already knows the corruptions through which their names will recur in human worship.

Satan surveys a vast host, shaken by its losses but responding to him. He does not say openly that his leadership has brought them here. He tells them that the battle has revealed what they must try next. There is talk of a new world and a new species of creatures whom Heaven intends to place there. If angels cannot overthrow their Creator, perhaps they can damage the happiness of this more vulnerable creation. A plan to injure people who have not attacked the rebels is thus announced as a continuation of a war for freedom.

Meanwhile, other spirits turn to construction. From the infernal ground they extract precious metal, and an immense hall rises. Its architect is Mulciber, associated with the divine craftsman later called Vulcan. Hell can imitate the splendid accomplishments of civilization: architecture, technical skill, ceremony, ornament, and bureaucracy. The building is called Pandemonium, a place for all the demons. The host, immense in the open field, contracts itself to fit within its walls. Even spirits capable of changing size submit to the organization of a political court.

We finish the book not with a charge into battle but with an assembly. The military hero has called a legislature, and the court will debate how to proceed. Nothing in the scene proves that reasoned speech is always virtuous. This parliament has assembled to decide how best to injure innocent beings. Milton's Hell is frightening partly because its residents can think, build, admire speeches, and create institutions. Their reason has not disappeared. It has been recruited to make rebellion permanent.
