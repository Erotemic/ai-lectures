# The Canterbury Tales — The Physician’s Tale, Virginia and the Judge

A father named Virginius has a daughter, Virginia, praised for beauty and chastity. The physician who tells the story presents her almost as an example in a moral treatise: she is careful, virtuous and protected from the temptations that ruin other young people. Her beauty attracts a powerful judge named Appius. He wants her, and because he holds public office he has the means to disguise private desire as a legal decision.

Appius recruits a corrupt man named Claudius. Claudius brings a fraudulent claim before the court: Virginia, he says, is not Virginius's daughter but an enslaved girl who belongs to him and was unlawfully taken from his household. Appius plays the part of an impartial judge while already knowing the accusation is invented. He orders the girl handed over. The court, which should distinguish a lawful claim from a criminal scheme, becomes the mechanism by which the girl is to be stolen.

Virginius returns home and tells Virginia what has happened. He frames her choice as dishonor at the judge's hands or death by her father's. Virginia is terrified; she asks whether there is no way to save her life and chastity together. He kills her and takes her head to court. The tale treats his action as an appalling response to threatened violation, and its moral language praises the preservation of honor. Yet the story gives Virginia no adequate escape from the men who claim to protect or possess her. A father has killed his own daughter rather than allowing the state to injure her.

The public sees the consequence of Appius's decision and rises against him. His scheme is exposed. He is imprisoned and takes his own life. Claudius is condemned but spared death through Virginius's intercession and sent into exile. The ending supplies the shape of a moral example: a wicked judge falls and a girl's virtue is vindicated. But the girl is dead. Political justice comes only after a killing that the narrative's honor code has made thinkable.

The tale is particularly difficult beside the Wife of Bath's story, where a knight's sexual violence is met by a judgment giving women the right to name what they desire. Virginia is not asked what she would choose from real possibilities. She is given a cruel question after her options have already been destroyed. The Physician's praise of virtue cannot restore her voice. Chaucer's company includes a physician, a man whose occupation should preserve bodies, telling a story in which the body of a young woman is destroyed to protect an abstract code of honor.

The Host reacts with anger and sorrow and calls for a lighter story. The Pardoner offers to speak. That transition is sharp: after the judge who turned law into predation comes a religious salesman who openly confesses that he turns moral warnings into money. In neither case can a public role be trusted to guarantee private goodness. The tale's lasting power lies not in its claim that Virginius found a correct solution, but in the discomfort created when an official's crime and a father's supposed virtue lead to the same loss of life.
