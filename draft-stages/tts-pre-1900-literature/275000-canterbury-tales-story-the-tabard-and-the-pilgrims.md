# The Canterbury Tales — The Company at the Tabard

April has returned. Rain has loosened the winter's hold, and the earth begins to grow again. Birds wake early. People who have spent the colder months indoors begin to think of roads, inns, distant shrines. In Southwark, just across the Thames from London, a traveller has stopped at the Tabard Inn. He is bound for Canterbury, where pilgrims visit the shrine of Thomas Becket, archbishop and martyr. By chance a company of other pilgrims arrives, and the journey that follows will become one of the great collections of stories in English.

We meet this company not as a finished list of social categories but through someone's eyes. The narrator says he will describe the people as they appeared to him, their clothing, their occupations, their gestures and ambitions. A Knight comes first. He has fought in many campaigns abroad and is presented as experienced, modest and courteous. His son, the Squire, cares about love, music and dress as much as arms. Their Yeoman carries a bow, arrows and the equipment of a professional attendant. Even in this apparently honorable group, generations and kinds of service look different.

A Prioress named Madame Eglentyne speaks French learned in England rather than Paris. She has elaborate table manners, an elegant cloak and a brooch bearing a declaration about love. The narrator admires her tenderness toward little animals. Whether such refinement is evidence of religious holiness is another question. Nearby is a Monk who prefers hunting to the strict discipline of his order. His horses and fur-lined sleeves make the financial possibilities of religious office visible. A Friar knows taverns, innkeepers and profitable acquaintances better than the poor people whom he is supposed to help. He can make himself welcome almost anywhere.

A Merchant displays his prosperous appearance and talks of profitable trade; the narrator adds that no one knows he is in debt. An Oxford Clerk spends what he has on books and learning. A lawyer has achieved the reputation of ceaseless work, though the narrator quietly suggests that he may not be quite so busy as he seems. A Franklin is a wealthy landholder who enjoys open hospitality and a table supplied with fine food. A Haberdasher, Carpenter, Weaver, Dyer and Tapestry Maker represent prosperous urban trades, accompanied by a Cook whose skill does not prevent the narrator from noticing an infected sore on his leg.

A Shipman brings knowledge of ports and tides, along with habits that would look less admirable ashore. A Physician can diagnose disease through the language of medieval medicine, but loves money too. The Wife of Bath wears fine red stockings, travels widely, and has had five husbands, not counting other companions of her youth. A good Parson and his brother, a Plowman, stand apart: one teaches by living as he preaches, the other works hard and gives generously. The contrast is not between church and lay society but between service and exploitation.

Finally come the Miller, strong and noisy, fond of obscenity and willing to steal from grain; the Manciple, skilled at buying provisions for lawyers; the Reeve, a narrow and watchful estate manager; the Summoner, who trades upon people's fear of church courts; and a Pardoner with supposed relics, singing voice and a talent for persuading people to pay. Chaucer the pilgrim says he is reporting their appearances in good faith. Readers learn to notice the distance between what he notices and what he understands.

Then the innkeeper, Harry Bailey, makes a proposal. Travel can be tedious, he says, so why not hold a storytelling contest? Each pilgrim should tell tales going to Canterbury and returning. The best, judged for entertainment and instruction, will receive a supper paid for by the others. Harry offers to travel with the company and serve as judge. Everyone agrees, or at least is drawn into agreement. The Knight is selected to begin.

There is no finished account of this competition. We will not hear every promised tale or return to the Tabard for the prize. What survives is more interesting than a tidy sequence would have been: people argue, interrupt, answer insults, take over genres belonging to other speakers and sometimes refuse to tell the kind of story demanded of them. Chaucer gives the road a company of witnesses, but no single witness the authority to explain everyone else. The first speaker, the Knight, will offer a polished romance. It will be answered by a drunken miller who thinks refinement has had the floor long enough.
