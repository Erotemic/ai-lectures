# The Canterbury Tales — The Reeve’s Tale, the Mill and Revenge

The Reeve has reason to dislike the Miller's story. He is an older man who once worked as a carpenter, and the Miller has just made a carpenter the butt of a public joke. The Reeve responds with a tale about an arrogant miller, Simkin, who boasts of his wife, daughter and ability to cheat his customers. If the Miller thinks he can laugh at another craft, the Reeve will show him what happens when the laughter turns back upon its teller.

The story takes place near Cambridge. Two students, John and Aleyn, come from a college with grain to be ground. They have been warned that Simkin steals flour, so they plan to watch the work closely. Simkin is not alarmed. While their attention is diverted, his wife releases their horse, which runs free among the mares. The students spend the day trying to retrieve it. During their absence the miller steals their grain. By nightfall they are hungry, tired and stranded, too late to go back to the college. Simkin agrees to lodge them, enjoying his advantage over young men who imagined that education would protect them from a working tradesman's tricks.

The miller's house is small. In the darkness everyone must sleep close together: Simkin and his wife in one bed, their daughter Malyne nearby, and the students within reach. Aleyn decides to retaliate for the theft by sleeping with Malyne. John, unwilling to be left without a share in the night's revenge, moves the infant's cradle to confuse the positions of the beds and sleeps with the miller's wife. In the morning darkness Aleyn, returning from Malyne's bed, follows the misplaced cradle and climbs beside Simkin by mistake. He boasts of what he has done with the daughter, thinking he is speaking to John. The miller erupts in anger; a violent fight breaks out. His wife strikes with a staff, intending to hit a student but hitting her husband instead. The students escape, having recovered flour and taken other advantages from the household.

This is not a gentle victory of clever youth over corrupt power. The story treats sex as a means of revenge between men, and the consent and vulnerability of the women are not given equal attention. Its comic machinery depends on confusion of persons in a dark room, but the social violence does not disappear because the teller regards it as funny. Simkin steals; the students answer by treating his household as property that can be taken in payment. Replacing a stolen bag of grain with sexual humiliation reveals an economy of grievance in which other people bear the cost.

The Reeve has gained his revenge on the Miller inside the contest: his story is set in a mill, and a boastful miller is beaten and mocked. But he has also made the atmosphere of the road harsher. The Knight's Tale turned conflict into public ceremony; the Miller turned it into practical jokes; the Reeve turns it into vengeance against a family. There is no reason to assume that every pilgrim shares the same moral judgment, or that Chaucer wants us to make the victims vanish behind the punch line. The Cook is delighted by the rough entertainment. He begins a story about an apprentice, but the manuscript will not preserve a finished conclusion.
