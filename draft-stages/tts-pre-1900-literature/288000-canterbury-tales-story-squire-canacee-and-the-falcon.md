# The Canterbury Tales — The Squire’s Tale, Canacee and the Falcon

The young Squire, son of the Knight, chooses a romance of wonders set in a distant court. A king named Cambyuskan rules in Tartary and celebrates his birthday with a magnificent feast. Into the hall rides a knight bearing gifts from a foreign ruler: a horse of brass that can carry its rider through the air at extraordinary speed, a mirror that reveals danger and deception, a sword whose wounds can be healed by touching them with the same blade, and a ring that permits its wearer to understand the speech of birds. The king and his court marvel at the gifts. Their meaning has not yet been disclosed by a complete adventure.

The brass horse stands so convincingly alive that people speculate about its making. Some appeal to mysterious sciences, others to engineering, and still others to sorcery. The messenger explains how to mount and direct it. The tale takes visible delight in the idea that nature might be imitated or extended by art. The gifts resemble objects from the romances the young Squire loves, but also raise questions about technology and knowledge: a machine can be understood as magic when no one in the audience can explain how it works.

Among the court is the princess Canacee. She puts on the ring, walks into the garden and suddenly hears birds speak. One falcon is bleeding and grieving, having wounded herself in distress. Canacee shelters the bird and asks for her story. The falcon speaks of a male falcon who vowed faithful love but then abandoned her for a kite. As she recounts the deception, the marvel of a talking bird turns into a familiar account of betrayed trust. A court can receive wonders from abroad and yet still fail in the ordinary duty to keep a promise.

Canacee treats the suffering falcon with care and arranges a chamber for her recovery. The Squire announces that other adventures lie ahead: those of the brass horse, the princess, her brothers and romantic obstacles. But the surviving poem stops before these promises are fulfilled. We do not learn the complete story of the falcon's healing, the horse's exploits or the remaining family adventures. A continuation here would be the work of a later writer, not the end Chaucer supplied.

The Franklin interrupts the Squire with lavish praise, saying that a young man with such eloquence would make a father proud. The compliment also cuts the narration short as the text comes down to us. This provides one of the great reversals of expectation in the collection. We are led to anticipate a sweeping romance of exotic technology and dynastic adventure, but the only emotionally completed movement is the grief of a wounded bird and a young woman's response. The promise of marvel has been redirected toward a small act of sympathy.

We should also recognize the conventions through which medieval English writers imagined Asian courts. 'Tartary' here is a literary setting, not an ethnographic account of a real population. The young Squire's distances are part of the romance's attraction. The next teller, the Franklin, will remain in a romantic mode, but he will make courtesy and freedom depend less upon miraculous objects than upon the decisions people make when their promises conflict.
