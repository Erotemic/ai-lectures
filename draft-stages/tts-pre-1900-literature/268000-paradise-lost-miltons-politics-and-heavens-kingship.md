# Paradise Lost — Milton's Republican Politics and Heaven's Kingship

Paradise Lost was written by a man who had argued forcefully against human monarchy, defended the overthrow of Charles the First, and served the English republic. Yet his poem appears to place the whole universe under a supreme ruler whose legitimacy no creature may challenge. The contrast is not a small biographical curiosity. It is one of the reasons the speeches of Satan and the politics of Heaven have generated such difficult readings.

John Milton lived through the English Civil Wars and the experiment in government without a king. He wrote works defending the freedom to argue and publish, criticized arrangements of church authority, and supported the political cause that helped bring Charles the First to execution in 1649. During the Commonwealth and Protectorate he served in government as a Latin secretary, preparing communications and defenses of the regime. When the monarchy returned in 1660, his earlier advocacy placed him in danger. He was briefly held and lost the position he had occupied. A religious epic published in 1667 was therefore produced in the shadow of real political defeat.

The infernal council in Book Two speaks the language of debate, freedom, servitude, and resistance to tyranny. Its members have different programs and are willing to argue them in public. Satan's speeches sometimes sound like those of a republican who will not submit to arbitrary power. Readers soon discover that the deliberation is compromised: Beelzebub offers the policy Satan has already selected, and the entire host is invited to applaud what amounts to a plan for harming uninvolved human beings.

If Milton believed political debate could be valuable, why give a debate to devils? Precisely because the outward appearance of liberty does not guarantee its existence. A court can announce open deliberation while organizing the result in advance. A speaker can pronounce the word freedom while demanding loyalty to himself. The poem tests political language by what the speakers do with it, not by the frequency with which they use admired terms.

Heaven is more difficult. Satan says the Father's rule is tyranny and the Son's promotion an arbitrary humiliation. Raphael's account rejects that description. The Son's authority is not another ambitious angel's successful coup; in the poem's ontology it belongs to the one through whom creation is ordered. To treat God's kingship as identical to Charles Stuart's throne would be to collapse the distinction Milton needs between an authority that makes the world possible and a merely human government accountable to its people.

But this distinction does not remove every discomfort. The language of hierarchy is everywhere in Heaven, as it is in Eden's marriage. The poem has to make obedience meaningful rather than merely habitual. Abdiel offers one model: he follows what he recognizes as true without waiting for the approval of a majority. The Son offers another: power is expressed through the willingness to suffer for others. Neither gives us a simple parliamentary blueprint.

The final historical prophecy is politically sharp. Michael warns that religious teachers will exploit institutions, accumulate worldly power, and obscure the message they claim to guard. Milton's distrust of priests and imposed religious authority is audible even in the story that announces the triumph of Christian redemption. Heaven's sovereignty does not authorize every earthly person who claims to act on Heaven's behalf.

It is too simple to say that Satan represents a particular king, a particular revolutionary, or Milton himself. Dryden and other early readers made politically charged interpretations, sometimes reversing one another's identifications. The poem uses contemporary political language for a theological conflict that cannot be mapped cleanly onto one set of seventeenth-century parties.

The political question to take away is therefore not merely which side Milton favored. It is how to distinguish genuine freedom from prideful self-rule, legitimate authority from coercion, and shared counsel from managed consent. These were problems in England's streets and assemblies, not only in Heaven and Hell. In Paradise Lost the vocabulary of the republic survives inside a poem that asks its readers to measure political claims against moral action.
