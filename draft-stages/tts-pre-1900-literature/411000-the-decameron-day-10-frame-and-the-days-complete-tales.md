# The Decameron · Day 10: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Day Ten: Panfilo and Magnificence

Panfilo, the final ruler of the ten storytellers, has assigned a subject that sounds like the culmination of everything they have heard: magnificence, or great-hearted actions. The word does not mean only splendor, expensive clothing, enormous buildings or lavish hospitality. It means a readiness to act generously where something of real value is at stake. A ruler can give money or land, but a person without royal wealth can also make a costly gift. By choosing this subject for the final storytelling day, Panfilo asks his companions to measure how people use power rather than simply how much power they possess.

At daybreak, clouds are still colored by the rising sun. Panfilo wakes the company and leads a walk through the grounds. The friends talk about the future as they go, for the retreat cannot continue forever. They have escaped the plague-stricken city and have managed their days through elected leadership, meals, work performed by servants, music and stories. Now their carefully organized interlude is coming to its natural conclusion. They return to the palace, drink by the fountain, take breakfast, rest and assemble again in the shade.

Neifile begins. Her tale concerns a knight who believes a king has rewarded him unjustly. Other stories will test the meaning of giving: an outlaw unexpectedly honors a captured abbot; a man offers his own life to someone who comes to murder him; a rescuer surrenders the woman he has saved; a suitor relinquishes a promise that he has secured at immense cost. Kings, lovers, friends and powerful men will all face situations in which refusing to take something may count for more than acquiring it.

There is an argument hidden inside this apparently noble theme. A gift made from abundant wealth may cost its giver little. An act by a poor man or a vulnerable woman may cost nearly everything. A gesture praised by other men as generous may involve the disposition of a woman who was never asked to agree. The last day's stories therefore require a distinction between magnificence as a reputation and generosity as something actually experienced by the recipient.

The sequence has a deliberate escalation. After the royal stories, Filomena tells of friendship carried to the point of surrendering a marriage. Panfilo offers an expansive tale of a host who unknowingly shelters a foreign sultan and is later rescued by him. Then Dioneo, who enjoys the final speaking turn, tells the story of Griselda and the Marquis of Saluzzo. It will look at first like an extreme example of patience. Yet Dioneo himself calls the marquis's conduct a kind of mad folly and does not offer it for imitation.

That is an important distinction as we enter the ending. The ten stories do not demonstrate that every praised sacrifice is good. They invite us to ask who chooses the sacrifice, who bears its cost, and whether a powerful person can honestly call himself generous while demanding submission from another. The frame around these tales retains the brightness of a leisurely afternoon, but the idea of magnificence will not remain simple for long.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 1: Ruggieri, Alfonso, and Fortune

Ruggieri de' Figiovanni is a knight from Florence who goes to the court of Alfonso, king of Spain, hoping to display his courage and worth. He acquits himself well, keeps a splendid household and performs deeds admired by those who see them. The king is known for generosity, but the rewards Ruggieri expects never come. Other courtiers are granted castles and estates, sometimes, in Ruggieri's judgment, despite having done far less to deserve them.

At last the knight asks permission to go home. Alfonso grants it and gives him a fine mule for the journey. The gift is useful, yet it cannot remove Ruggieri's hurt. The king, aware that there is more to the departure than simple homesickness, arranges for a trusted attendant to travel with the knight and report what he says. Along the road Ruggieri observes the mule with the same critical eye he has turned on its owner. The animal relieves itself at the wrong places, and the knight compares that poor timing to the king's habit of making gifts where they are least deserved.

The attendant reports the complaint. Alfonso recalls Ruggieri and asks him to explain. The knight speaks plainly. He is not needy, he says, and his disappointment is not chiefly that he has received no additional wealth. He wanted visible recognition of his worth. A king who bestows gifts on poorer candidates for merit while overlooking those who serve him well seems to undermine the very standard that makes a generous court admirable.

Alfonso replies that he does recognize Ruggieri's abilities. He attributes the mismatch between merit and reward to the knight's ill fortune, then proposes to demonstrate the point. In the test that follows, the knight chooses between apparently promising containers and receives the one lacking the more valuable treasure. The king uses that result to argue that chance, not a failure of judgment, has obstructed earlier gifts. He then makes good the practical absence of reward through an ample donation.

Neifile's tale moves between a rational complaint and an account of fortune. Ruggieri measures the distribution of gifts by service. Alfonso measures generosity by a willingness to give, then invokes a power outside human control to explain why reward has not matched deserving. The tale invites listeners to admire a ruler who takes offense without resorting to punishment and allows a disappointed servant to speak frankly.

It also invites a more skeptical question. If the king possesses the power to choose the objects of his largesse, why must fortune bear the blame for his earlier omissions? The final gift resolves the knight's practical grievance, but it does not entirely settle the philosophy of reward. The story opens the last day by proposing that magnificence is more than a treasury: it is a relation between judgment, obligation and recognition.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 2: Ghino di Tacco and the Abbot

Ghino di Tacco lives outside the law. Banished and dispossessed, he has made a stronghold in the Tuscan countryside and extracts wealth from people passing through. The next traveler of consequence is the Abbot of Cluny, who is going toward the Roman court while suffering from a troublesome stomach. Ghino and his men seize the abbot and his considerable traveling company. The religious leader expects ransom, rough treatment or confinement of the most humiliating kind.

Instead Ghino has another plan. He sees that his prisoner has been made ill by rich living, and confines him comfortably but with a restricted diet. For several days the abbot receives little more than toasted bread and a measured amount of wine. He is angry and hungry, especially because those attending him cannot make him understand why the outlaw is acting as though he were a physician. Ghino sends the food in his own name while withholding his identity from direct conversation. The abbot has no freedom to refuse the treatment, but gradually his stomach improves.

When the abbot is better, Ghino arranges an excellent meal, reunites the traveler with his attendants and shows him the horses and valuables taken at the capture. He makes it clear that he could retain far more than he has chosen to take. He then identifies himself and tells the abbot that poverty and exile, not a natural delight in cruelty, have driven him to this life. The abbot is free to depart.

The encounter transforms an enemy into an advocate. At the papal court the abbot speaks on Ghino's behalf. Ghino is reconciled with Pope Boniface, raised into a respectable office and restored to public standing. In the terms of Elissa's tale, the gift is not just the food the robber relinquishes or the property he returns. It is the abbot's willingness to use ecclesiastical influence for a man the Church has reason to regard as an enemy.

Elissa introduces her story by mocking the clergy's reputation for grasping wealth and seeking revenge. Against that background, the abbot's choice is especially pointed. He receives courtesy and restoration from a bandit, then responds with generosity rather than with the full weight of the authorities. The supposed criminal and the supposed defender of Christian virtue exchange expected moral roles.

The story is fiction even though it invokes historically recognizable people and institutions. Its geography and names should not be mistaken for a documentary record of this medical encounter. And the abbot's recovery does not validate forcible detention as a treatment. The praise within the tale is directed to a sequence of choices: Ghino's refusal to treat a captive as prey alone, and the abbot's refusal to reduce a former captor to a permanent enemy. Magnificence begins to look like the capacity to interrupt an expected cycle of retaliation.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 3: Nathan and Mitridanes

On a road in the distant East stands a great house where any passing traveler may receive welcome. It belongs to Nathan, whose fame rests not simply on wealth but on the constancy with which he uses it. A stranger may eat, rest, and find shelter without having to prove his importance. The house has become so well known that another wealthy man, Mitridanes, feels injured by its reputation. He too has built a name for generosity, yet Nathan's name is the one people repeat.

Mitridanes decides to remove his rival. Rather than give more freely, he resolves to kill Nathan and thus end the comparison. He rides toward Nathan's home, and along the way he meets an old man dressed modestly. He does not recognize him as Nathan. The stranger hears his purpose and, rather than flee or condemn him, gives practical directions for where and how Nathan may be found alone. He also offers him the hospitality for which the household is famous.

Mitridanes takes the advice. But as he approaches the place indicated, he realizes the man who helped him is the person he means to murder. Nathan has not been fooled. He understood the request and chose to cooperate with a guest who asked for his very life. The would-be killer is overcome with shame. Instead of drawing his weapon he confesses his envy and begs for forgiveness.

Nathan does more than forgive. He explains that a life, too, can be treated as a gift. He is old, and he has devoted his wealth to answering the needs of others. If a guest demands the remaining years he possesses, Nathan says, he would rather give them than allow that guest to leave unsatisfied. Mitridanes is moved from murderous rivalry into friendship.

Filostrato describes an action so extreme that it strains an ordinary definition of magnificence. A generous person might give shelter or a fortune, but can it be right to offer someone permission to commit murder? Nathan's willingness dismantles Mitridanes' desire for fame by revealing that the comparison is futile. He cannot exceed a man who places even survival outside the calculations of reputation.

Yet the story's admiration creates its own philosophical difficulty. Nathan's consent to die would not absolve an assassin of wrongdoing. He has found a way to expose Mitridanes' envy without drawing blood, but the strategy depends on a degree of self-surrender few listeners would accept as a general ethical rule. The story deliberately takes the day's theme to an extreme. Generosity can cease to be comfortable admiration and become a question about whether anything should be withheld, even when giving it would permit another person's crime.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 4: Gentile and the Woman Buried Alive

Messer Gentile of Bologna loves a woman who has married Niccoluccio Caccianimico. She does not return his love. Gentile leaves for a time to govern elsewhere, but he hears that the woman has died suddenly while pregnant and has been buried. Grief draws him back. He goes to the tomb, longing even to see the woman once more. When he opens the burial place, he discovers a faint sign of life. She has not died after all.

He carries her away and brings her to his mother's care. Together they restore her health. She has been placed in a grave after a condition mistaken for death, not brought back from a literal death. As she recovers, Gentile assures her that she will not be compelled to accept his love. He asks only that she stay with his household long enough for him to arrange a public restoration to her family. She agrees, though she longs to tell her relatives that she is alive. Before the arrangement is finished, she gives birth to the child she was carrying.

Gentile invites leading citizens to a banquet, including the husband who believes himself bereaved. He proposes a question: if one person abandons a servant believed beyond recovery, and another rescues and cares for that servant, who has the better claim afterward? The assembled guests agree that the rescuer has earned a claim. The husband himself endorses the conclusion. Gentile then brings in the restored woman and her child and reveals the analogy. The guests recognize her as someone they believed dead.

The dramatic unveiling temporarily gives Gentile enormous power. He can point to the care he provided and say that by the table's own judgment the rescued woman belongs to him. Instead he publicly renounces that claim and returns her to her husband. The family is reunited, and Gentile is honored for generosity. A situation that could have been described as a lover's victory ends with a lover's refusal to possess the person he rescued.

The praise is not difficult to understand. Gentile saves a life, nurses a mother and child, and resists using their vulnerability to press a forbidden desire. Yet the language of the banquet is unsettling. The woman's fate is argued through the example of a servant transferred between masters. The audience of men debates competing claims before she is allowed to be publicly recognized as a person in her own right.

Lauretta's story therefore makes an instructive case for the last day's theme. Gentile's magnanimity is a genuine relinquishment within the fiction: he gives up an opportunity for personal gratification, and the woman returns to the life she wants. At the same time, the theatrical form reveals how readily her body becomes the apparent object of male ownership. Reading the story well means recognizing both features, without forcing the tale either into unqualified moral perfection or simple cynicism.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 5: Dianora's Garden in January

Madonna Dianora is married to Giliberto in Udine. A nobleman named Ansaldo is passionately attached to her, but she does not wish to accept him. His repeated messages become an intolerable nuisance. Hoping to end them, she sets a condition no sensible person could expect him to fulfill: if he can create a garden near the city, blooming in January as though it were May, she will grant what he asks. Otherwise he must stop pursuing her.

Ansaldo does not give up. He seeks the help of a practitioner of necromancy who promises the impossible for a very large payment. When January comes, snow and ice cover the countryside, but the magician produces a garden with grass, flowers and fruit in apparent defiance of the season. Within the story the marvel is accepted as real, not explained away as a hidden greenhouse or a clever illusion. Dianora is horrified. The condition she believed safe from fulfillment has been met.

She tells her husband what has happened. Giliberto responds with an act intended to preserve her honor as he understands it: he urges her to keep her word, even if doing so means going to Ansaldo. Dianora obeys, though she is distressed. When Ansaldo learns that she has come not from love but because her husband has instructed her to fulfill a terrible bargain, he releases her from the promise. She may return home untouched by him.

The renunciation passes to another person. Ansaldo has incurred a vast debt to the magician and would normally have to pay it regardless of whether he enjoys the prize for which he contracted. The magician, learning that Ansaldo has yielded his apparent claim, likewise waives his fee. Three men have given up something: the husband his interest in keeping the promise from being carried out, the suitor the promised encounter, and the magician the payment for his labor. Emilia's listeners debate which has acted most generously.

This chain is the tale's formal accomplishment. An action freely made by one person prompts another to act at least as freely, until the material costs disappear. The winter garden is the visible marvel, but the greater marvel may be that the characters repeatedly refuse to demand everything to which a bargain appears to entitle them.

The questions do not end there. Dianora's supposed promise is extracted by persistent pursuit, not by a welcome courtship. The central debate among the men can almost obscure that she does not want the encounter in the first place. Giliberto's decision to permit it is presented as great-hearted, but his permission does not substitute for her choice. Ansaldo's decision to release her is the moment in which her refusal becomes effective again. The tale invites admiration for generosity while leaving the listener to notice whose freedom the three men's celebrated acts determine.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 6: King Charles and the Two Sisters

King Charles, conqueror in southern Italy, visits the estate of Messer Neri degli Uberti near Castellammare. Neri's family comes from the politically divided world of Florence, where victory for one faction can mean exile for another. He has built a garden full of water, trees and carefully ordered pleasures. A king willing to dine at the home of a man associated with an opposing party demonstrates a measure of political ease before the meal has even begun.

During the evening two young women enter the garden to serve and entertain the guests. They are Neri's daughters and are described with the deliberate brightness of courtly romance. The girls are very young, approximately fifteen in the tale. Charles becomes powerfully attracted to them. He is a king, accustomed to the possibility of satisfying desires that lesser people must conceal. But the attraction also presents a conflict between personal appetite and the duties of a ruler.

The desire troubles him after he leaves. A trusted counselor, Count Guy de Montfort, brings him back to the responsibilities that his rank ought to impose. A king's conduct will be imitated and will shape the position of those under his authority. He has already received magnificent hospitality. To take advantage of that hospitality by seeking the girls would make his power a threat to the household that welcomed him.

Charles chooses restraint. Rather than pursue the sisters, he arranges honorable marriages for them and provides the wealth necessary to establish them in their new positions. The narrative treats these acts as an expression of magnificence because the gift is accompanied by a refusal. The king can make demands; his moral achievement is that he does not make the demands he could enforce.

Fiammetta's story matters in a sequence otherwise crowded with extravagant gifts and elaborate sacrifices. Here the crucial decision is internal, though visible in its consequences. The king does not prove greatness by taking what fascinates him, nor by forcing the recipients into a spectacle of gratitude. He acts on an acknowledgment that another household's safety is a limit on his own desires.

The tale nevertheless uses the language of its social world. Young women are rewarded through marriage arrangements, and a king's self-command is narrated from the standpoint of his honor. Readers may ask how much choice the girls are afforded, not merely whether Charles behaves better than a predatory ruler. The distinction matters because a story can praise a powerful man's restraint while taking for granted the power structures within which he chooses. The last day will repeatedly ask whether magnanimity changes those structures or only makes their possessors more admirable.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 7: Lisa and King Pedro

Lisa is the daughter of Bernardo Puccini, a wealthy Florentine apothecary living in Palermo. She sees King Pedro of Aragon at a public celebration and is seized by love. She knows that her feelings cannot easily find a path to fulfillment. The king belongs to an incomparably higher rank, and she is not part of his court. She becomes so despondent that her family fears she will die. Neither money nor physicians' remedies bring her comfort.

Lisa asks for Minuccio d'Arezzo, a singer who is known to the king. Her father thinks she wants the consolation of music, but she has a specific purpose. In private she tells Minuccio her story and asks him to carry it to Pedro without exposing her to public shame. A song is composed to express what she cannot speak to the king herself. Minuccio performs it at court, and the sovereign understands that an unnamed young woman has put her life and hope into his hands.

Pedro does not respond by using the opportunity for an affair. He visits Lisa, comforts her and allows her to speak with dignity. The encounter helps her recover. He later returns with his queen and courtiers, honors Lisa publicly and announces that he will help settle her future. He chooses a young gentleman named Perdicone as her husband, provides the means for a distinguished marriage and offers his own continuing, ceremonial devotion as her knight. Lisa accepts the arrangement and obtains the queen's permission for the kiss the king offers as the only tribute he will ask of her affection.

The tale transforms private longing into a public relationship that avoids scandal. Lisa does not receive what her first passion imagined, but neither is she merely dismissed for having loved beyond her station. Her account is heard. Her feelings, carried through art, have become a reason for the king to honor rather than exploit a vulnerable subject.

The use of a song is essential. The court musician's performance makes an inward experience legible to someone whom Lisa cannot address directly. Boccaccio is writing a story about the power of crafted language within a larger work whose organizing premise is that stories can preserve human beings in a time of suffering. The song does not cure disease in a medical sense. It creates a social encounter, and the encounter alters the situation that had made her despair seem absolute.

Pampinea praises King Pedro's courtesy, but a reader can distinguish courtesy from perfect equality. Pedro remains the person who chooses Lisa's marriage, and the woman's words show how readily she submits her own will to his. The happy ending depends on his being humane and on others allowing her private feeling to become public without punishment. The story imagines generosity at the top of a hierarchy, and it asks what might happen when that hierarchy is governed by compassion rather than appetite.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 8: Titus, Gisippus, and Sophronia

Titus and Gisippus grow up together in Athens and become devoted friends. Titus is a Roman, Gisippus an Athenian; their families and cities give them different obligations, but their affection is presented as nearly complete. When Gisippus is betrothed to Sophronia, Titus sees her and is overwhelmed by love. The young Roman wastes away with the conflict between his desire and the loyalty he owes his closest friend.

Gisippus learns the cause. Rather than reproach him, he offers to give up his bride. He argues that he can find another wife more easily than another friend. This is the moment Filomena expects her audience to read as extraordinary liberality. The gift, however, is not an object Gisippus owns. Sophronia is a person whose family has already negotiated her marriage and who has not agreed to the exchange. To prevent the families from interrupting the plan, the two men proceed deceptively.

Gisippus completes the wedding ceremonies, but on the wedding night Titus takes his place in the dark. Sophronia consents to the man she believes she has married. She discovers later that the partner was someone else. The friends appeal to marriage law and the fact of consummation to defend the arrangement. Her relatives are furious and threaten consequences. Titus uses the authority of a Roman citizen and the prospect of political retaliation to force acceptance. Sophronia goes with him to Rome; Gisippus remains behind in Athens.

The fortunes of the friends reverse. Gisippus loses his standing, is exiled and arrives in Rome in destitution. He sees Titus pass and believes he has been ignored. In despair he allows himself to be accused of a murder he did not commit, hoping to die. Titus recognizes him and declares himself the murderer to save him. The real killer, hearing the two friends claim guilt, comes forward. All three are spared. Titus then welcomes Gisippus into his household, shares his wealth and arranges for him to marry his sister.

The second half supplies the repayment for the first. Titus risks his own life for the man who relinquished a marriage for him. Filomena's audience is encouraged to see a chain of friendship stronger than self-interest, poverty or death. Yet the first half remains difficult to assimilate to generosity. The friends consider the loss and rescue of one another but make Sophronia the medium of the transaction. Her mistaken identity of the bridegroom means that the first sexual encounter occurs without informed consent.

This tale therefore puts the day's problem in its starkest form. The men can be genuinely devoted friends and still wrong a person who has no comparable voice in their compact. Ancient settings, Roman legal claims and the elevated language of loyalty may make the action sound noble to the narrator. A listener can admire the later willingness to face execution and still ask whose desires the original gift required the story to disregard.

Titus and Gisippus are not a simple pair of exemplary men. They are figures in an argument about what loyalty means. Their willingness to sacrifice for each other is real within the fiction. So is their willingness to arrange another person's life. Filomena's long narrative makes those two judgments occupy the same space, which is why it remains more interesting than a mere illustration of friendship.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 9: Torello and Saladin

Saladin travels through Europe disguised as a merchant, accompanied by trusted companions. Near Pavia he encounters Messer Torello, a gentleman whose hospitality is as instinctive as it is generous. Torello does not know that his guest is a powerful ruler from the eastern Mediterranean. He knows only that travelers may need shelter and honor. He draws the strangers into his home, entertains them splendidly and treats them as friends. Saladin is struck by a courtesy that has been offered without calculation of a royal reward.

The visit ends with mutual respect. Later, when conflict carries Christian knights eastward, Torello joins a crusade. Before he leaves, he and his wife Adalieta agree upon a limit to her waiting: if he does not return or send reliable news by an appointed time, she may remarry. The agreement is meant to spare her indefinite uncertainty. It will become the clock upon which the rest of the story turns.

Torello is captured far from home. He survives in captivity by tending hawks, a skill that brings him to the attention of Saladin. The sultan recognizes the gentleman who welcomed him so generously; Torello recognizes the disguised guest from Pavia. The relationship formed when neither was a visible sovereign now becomes an alliance across religion, distance and war. Saladin honors Torello with gifts and freedom. But even that welcome cannot dissolve Torello's anxiety: he learns that his wife is about to be given to another man because the agreed time has passed and his family believes him dead.

The ordinary journey home cannot be completed quickly enough. Saladin therefore calls on extraordinary art. Torello is put into an enchanted sleep and transported, richly dressed and surrounded by gifts, to Pavia in a single night. He wakes in a church, alarming the monks, who take the splendid bed and unfamiliar figure for something supernatural. Gradually he is recognized. His relatives and friends discover that the man they mourned has returned at the exact moment before the new marriage can be completed.

Torello does not simply burst into the wedding and claim ownership. He goes in disguise to observe what is happening and reveals himself through a token and his knowledge of Adalieta. She recognizes him, and the interrupted wedding gives way to the joy of reunion. The story ends with renewed marriage and the material riches given by Saladin. More important than the riches is the restoration of time, a resource that could not otherwise have been bought.

Panfilo places this expansive romance near the end of the collection because it makes magnificence reciprocal. A private gentleman welcomes apparent merchants. A ruler later recognizes that gift and returns it on a scale only his power makes possible. Neither needs to share the other's religion to recognize courtesy. At the same time, the marvelous journey belongs to fiction; it cannot be cited as evidence for historical magical transportation or as a literal account of Saladin's itinerary.

There is a fragile moral premise beneath the fairy-tale structure. Torello's first gift did not depend on knowing who his guests were. If he had recognized Saladin as a ruler, hospitality might have become self-interested diplomacy. By disguising him, the narrative tests whether generosity is offered because someone needs it or because someone can repay it. The answer, in Torello's case, is the first; only afterward does reciprocity become possible. And after this great act of rescue, the company is ready for its last tale, where wealth, authority and the supposed virtue of patience collide.

## The Decameron — Day Ten, Tale 10: Griselda and Gualtieri

Gualtieri, Marquis of Saluzzo, has no wish to marry. His vassals fear that without an heir his estate and their future will become unstable. They press him to take a wife. Eventually he agrees, but insists on choosing without their interference. His chosen bride is Griselda, the daughter of a poor peasant named Giannucolo. She has worked in humble circumstances and possesses none of the wealth or rank a great lord would normally seek. Her poverty makes the marquis's choice seem both surprising and, to his subjects, potentially admirable.

Before the wedding, Gualtieri asks whether she will obey him in all things, without contest or complaint. She agrees. He dresses her in costly garments, marries her and brings her into his household. Griselda quickly earns the affection of the people. She governs herself with courtesy and intelligence, and her birth ceases to be a reason for them to despise her. A daughter is born, and later a son. The political demand for an heir has been fulfilled.

But Gualtieri decides to test the patience he has required. He tells Griselda that their daughter's low maternal birth troubles his vassals. He sends an agent to take the girl away and lets Griselda believe the child is to be killed. She submits to the demand, grieving inwardly but refusing to reproach the man who has ordered it. In truth Gualtieri has the child sent to be raised secretly by relatives. The cruelty lies in what he makes the mother believe, and in the willingness to use her love for her child as the instrument of his experiment.

When a son is born, he repeats the ordeal. Again the child disappears under the appearance of an order for death. Again Griselda is told that obedience is the only acceptable response. The marquis's attendants and subjects condemn his conduct; they pity her. The reader knows the children are alive but has watched the mother endure the reality of their supposed deaths. It is one of the sharpest differences between what a story's audience knows and what its central sufferer is allowed to know.

Years pass. Gualtieri announces that he has secured permission to dismiss Griselda and marry a woman of more suitable rank. The permission is another fiction. He orders Griselda back to her father's house and speaks of returning her dowry. She answers that she brought no wealth to the marriage and asks only to be permitted to wear a simple shift when she leaves. She has borne his children, yet she must appeal for the smallest material recognition of a shared life. She returns to poverty without turning her suffering into a public accusation.

The marquis is still not satisfied. He summons Griselda again and commands her to prepare the house for the arrival of his new bride. She obeys and receives the young woman with courtesy, even advising Gualtieri to be gentler to this bride than he was to the first. The supposed bride is actually their daughter, now grown up. The younger boy is their son. The father has constructed the final trial so that Griselda must greet the living children without knowing they are hers.

At last Gualtieri declares the truth. The children are alive; there is no replacement marriage; every ordeal has been an invented test. He restores Griselda to her position, gives her the children back and expects the story to conclude with celebration. The household and the vassals rejoice. The marquis's purpose, as he claims it, was to establish beyond doubt the constancy of his wife's devotion.

Dioneo does not begin this story by calling it an ideal pattern of magnanimity. He introduces the marquis's behavior as mad folly and says he would not advise anyone to imitate it. This matters enormously for the ending of the entire hundred-tale cycle. Griselda's patience may be represented as extraordinary; Gualtieri's decision to demand ever greater proof is not represented as an ordinary or defensible requirement of marriage. He has the power to inflict suffering and repeatedly chooses to exercise it.

What does Griselda's triumph amount to? She survives a system in which she has promised obedience so complete that resistance seems impossible. She demonstrates composure when the people around her believe her children have been murdered. She behaves with dignity when stripped of rank, then treats a supposed successor with care. These are capacities the narrative gives her. But one can acknowledge the strength of the character without celebrating the institutions and the husband that require her to display it.

The tale becomes still more complicated when later writers retell it. Petrarch produced a Latin version; Chaucer adapted the story into the Clerk's Tale. Different contexts and narrators alter how patience, obedience and endurance are praised or questioned. The story's later fame is not evidence that all of its readers approved Gualtieri. Indeed the power of the plot lies in an almost intolerable disjunction: the man who designs the test claims to reward virtue, but he has manufactured most of the misery that virtue must endure.

The last tale ends with a family reassembled, as earlier stories have ended with lost children returned and lovers reunited. But this reunion has a different price. Nothing has required Gualtieri to surrender power. Griselda has been made to surrender certainty, motherhood, status and even the right to express protest. Dioneo offers one final opportunity to distinguish an admirable response to suffering from an admirable cause of it. That distinction is among the most important things the *Decameron* asks its audience to carry home.

## The Decameron — After the Last Tale: The Return to Florence

Dioneo finishes the tale of Griselda. The listeners do not respond as a single chorus. Some find the narrative admirable; others criticize it. Their disagreement is one of the final acts of the storytelling company, and it is a fitting response to a tale that presents patience under treatment its narrator has already called folly. No single speaker receives authority to close the debate. Instead Panfilo, the king for this tenth and final day, looks at the hour and begins another kind of conclusion.

The company has been away from Florence for nearly fifteen days. Its members fled a city struck by pestilence, funerals and a collapse in the ordinary habits that had made urban life intelligible. In the country they have arranged a different rhythm. They have elected rulers for each storytelling day, obeyed conventions, eaten together, sung, danced and invented a space for judging stories without the immediate fear that dominates the city. Their refuge has had some success. Yet even a pleasant form of isolation cannot be made permanent without changing its character.

Panfilo argues that they should return. Their retreat has preserved their health and maintained their decorum. But if they stay indefinitely, the pleasure may become stale and their company may attract so many outsiders that it ceases to provide shelter. The group discusses the proposal and agrees. There will be no eleventh storytelling day. Panfilo retains the crown only until they leave the next morning.

That evening they dine, dance and sing. Fiammetta gives a song concerned with love and jealousy. Dioneo jokes about the lover who has inspired it. This is not a solemn farewell that turns the whole collection into a morality play. Ordinary playfulness survives almost until midnight. The music has accompanied joy, despair, tenderness and the comic fictions told through ten afternoons; now it fills the final hours of their temporary society.

At dawn the servants have prepared the goods. The company goes back to Florence and returns to Santa Maria Novella, the church where the young women first gathered amid the plague and where their journey began. The three young men take leave of the seven young women. The women return to their homes. There is no report that the plague has vanished, that the city has recovered or that all ten people are now safe forever. The narrative simply places them back in the world from which they withdrew.

This restrained ending is vital. The stories did not cure the Black Death. They did not establish a new political order, nor did the group agree on a single ethical explanation for everything it had heard. What they accomplished was more limited and more human: a period in which speech, hospitality, order and attention sustained a community under threat. Their creation was temporary; the act of making it mattered all the same.

The structure has also allowed contradictions to remain visible. The company has praised wit and exposed its cruelty; celebrated love and shown its deceptions; admired generosity and permitted a final story to question what admiration ought to mean. Its departure asks the reader to leave the protected frame without expecting those questions to be resolved. The book's last movement returns from stories to ordinary lives. Beyond the church door, history continues.

## The Decameron — Author’s Epilogue: What Stories Are For

After the ten friends have returned to Florence, Boccaccio speaks again in his own authorial voice. The hundred tales are finished. He thanks God and the women to whom he has addressed the book for helping him carry the work through. But he is not quite ready to lay down his pen. He anticipates complaints about what he has written, and he answers them before the reader can close the book.

One objection concerns sexual material. How could decent women tell, or listen to, stories of such behavior? Boccaccio replies that his language has generally suited the subjects and that a story sometimes cannot be told honestly without describing what happens within it. A different reader may object to the earthy words. He argues that ordinary speech and even the vocabulary of trades and kitchens offer legitimate material. The painter, after all, is allowed to depict bodies and their different parts without every act of representation becoming an offense.

Another objection concerns where the stories are told. They do not unfold in a church service or philosophical classroom. They are exchanged in gardens and other places of recreation among young people trying to preserve their lives during a terrible crisis. Context matters. The same story may cause harm to one audience and benefit another; the fault cannot always be assigned to the words alone. Boccaccio compares this to tools and substances that can be useful in one setting and destructive in another. His argument makes no claim that every reader will find every tale admirable.

He also reminds his audience that each story has a short statement of its contents. Readers are not helpless. They can pass over what they dislike and return to what pleases or instructs them. This is more than a defense of indecency. It presents reading as a practice of selection, judgment and responsibility. The audience has been given enough information to make decisions about the kind of material it wants.

Boccaccio anticipates people who find some stories too long. His answer is refreshingly practical. Readers who have other things to do are free not to read them. The collection is offered to an audience with leisure, and the length of a tale is to be understood in relation to the pleasure and attention it affords. He does not announce a universal rule that every story must be of one size. He treats length as part of the range of human purposes a story can serve.

He anticipates criticism for jokes and for irreverence toward friars. He refuses the premise that a serious writer must never make anyone laugh. He is capable of joking about himself as well as about his targets. His final defense is therefore performed in the same spirit as the collection: agile, argumentative, mischievous, and acutely aware that different people will hear different things in the same words.

There are limits to this defense. An author's insistence that a reader may choose what to read does not remove the harms stories can reinforce. Nor do the historical settings of bawdy jokes turn all their characters into models for action. The hundred tales include tenderness, cruelty, ingenuity, credulity, good fortune and coercion. Attending to those differences is part of reading well. Boccaccio's epilogue asks for an active audience, not an audience that automatically approves.

The final book thus returns to the problem with which its frame began. People who are vulnerable need ways to live through fear, loss and uncertainty. For the ten companions, storytelling organized temporary safety and delight. For the women imagined as readers, Boccaccio offers a book that may be taken up, put down, judged, rejected in part, and enjoyed in part. After the plague, after one hundred stories, the possibility of making sense together remains both necessary and unfinished.
