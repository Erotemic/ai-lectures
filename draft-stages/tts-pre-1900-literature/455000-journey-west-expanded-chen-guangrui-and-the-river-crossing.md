# Chen Guangrui and the Crossing at Hong River

A young scholar named Chen E, known by his courtesy name Chen Guangrui, lived with his mother in the Tang lands. The emperor had announced an examination for men who hoped to serve in government, and Chen wanted to attempt it. His mother, Zhang, was proud of him. She urged him to travel carefully, sit the examination, and return as soon as the result was known. A scholar's household could rise or fall by the judgment of examiners, and the journey itself was not free of risk.

In Chang'an, Chen completed the examinations successfully and was awarded the highest distinction. For several days he rode through the city as the new champion scholar. He had scarcely begun to enjoy his success when the next event arrived. In the house of the minister Yin Kaishan, a young woman named Yin Wenjiao was choosing a husband according to a public custom: she cast an embroidered ball from a high decorated platform. The ball struck Chen's official hat. Her family recognized the newly successful scholar, and celebrations were arranged. Chen and Wenjiao married.

Soon the emperor appointed Chen to a post in Jiangzhou. The newly married couple were to travel away from the capital and take up the duties of a provincial official. Chen did not forget his mother. They went to collect her, intending that she should share their new home. But during the journey she fell ill at an inn, and the order requiring Chen to assume his post left him with little time. He could not remain indefinitely. After taking advice from his mother and wife, he arranged for Zhang to stay until she was strong enough to travel. He left money and promised to return for her when the weather improved.

At the inn he had performed a small act that would matter enormously. He found a fisherman offering a golden carp for sale. He bought it, thinking it might provide food for his sick mother. Then he noticed something striking about its eyes and began to suspect that this fish was no ordinary creature. Rather than have it cooked, he carried it to the river and released it into the water. His mother approved when he told her what he had done.

Chen and Wenjiao continued to the Hong River crossing. There they found a boat under the charge of two men, Liu Hong and Li Biao. The ferrymen helped carry the baggage. The river was a stage on a familiar administrative journey: the scholar had his appointment, his travel papers, and the assumption that an officer of the Tang state could safely reach his post. But in the boat Liu Hong saw Wenjiao and decided he wanted her for himself.

At a lonely part of the river, after dark, Liu Hong and his companion attacked. They killed Chen's servant and then Chen. They threw both bodies into the water. Wenjiao saw her husband murdered before her eyes and moved to throw herself in after him. Liu Hong seized her, threatened to kill her if she refused him, and made it clear that he intended to claim both her and her husband's position. She had no protectors aboard and no path to escape. She submitted to his demands under the threat of violence. It was not a new marriage of her choice; it was captivity imposed after murder.

Liu Hong dressed in Chen's clothes, took the official papers, and carried Wenjiao onward to Jiangzhou. There the local clerks and officials welcomed the new magistrate, believing the man before them was the examination champion appointed by the emperor. They congratulated him and gave him the courtesies due to an administrator. The killer was occupying his victim's office in public. Wenjiao had to appear beside him while knowing exactly how he had obtained it.

In the river below, the two bodies did not receive the same fate. The servant's remains drifted away. Chen's body stayed beneath the water and was discovered by a patrol from the underwater palace. The guards carried their report to the Dragon King. When the Dragon King examined the dead scholar, he recognized him. The golden carp that Chen had bought and released had been the Dragon King himself, traveling in another form. Chen had saved his life when he might have taken it, and now the man who had shown mercy was lying dead in the water.

The Dragon King summoned the scholar's spirit. Chen told him about the examination, the appointment, the ferry crossing, the murder, and the loss of his wife. The Dragon King would not let the debt go unpaid. He ordered Chen's body preserved with a pearl that kept it from decaying, and he offered the scholar's spirit a temporary place in his underwater court. When the time came, Chen might return to life and meet his family again.

For Wenjiao in Jiangzhou, none of this was visible. The man who had killed her husband wore the clothing of an official, sat in the public hall, and received the respect that ought to have belonged to Chen. She could only maintain the appearances demanded of her while holding fast to the knowledge of what had happened. Worse was to come. She was pregnant with Chen's child, a fact that made her life and the child's life even more dangerous.

The world's justice seemed to have failed at a river crossing. Its remedy was beginning in a hidden palace beneath the water. But it would have to reach back into the human world through the one person Liu Hong had not yet learned to fear: an infant who had not been born.
