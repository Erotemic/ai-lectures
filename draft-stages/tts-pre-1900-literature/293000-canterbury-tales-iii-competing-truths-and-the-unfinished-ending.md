# Canterbury Tales III: competing truths and the unfinished ending

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Prioress’s Tale, a Miracle and a Libel

The Prioress begins with an invocation to the Virgin Mary and the hope that a simple, humble voice may praise her worthily. Her tale takes place in an unnamed Asian city where a small Christian schoolboy learns to sing a Marian hymn. He does not fully understand the Latin words, but loves the sound and the devotion they represent. He repeats the hymn while walking through a Jewish neighborhood. The tale then makes a false and dangerous collective accusation: it depicts Jews as conspiring to kill the child because he sings a Christian song.

The child is murdered and his body hidden. His mother searches desperately. A miracle permits the dead child to continue singing, leading people to discover him. A religious procession gathers around the body, and the child explains that the Virgin Mary placed something upon his tongue so that he could continue her hymn after death. The story ends in the language of wonder and innocent devotion, but also depicts the punishment of Jews as if a community could justly be held responsible for a fabricated religious crime.

That accusation is not a report of something Jews did. It belongs to the medieval Christian tradition of the blood libel, the false allegation that Jews murdered Christian children for ritual or religious purposes. Such stories contributed to persecution and violence. Jews had been expelled from England in 1290, long before Chaucer wrote. The tale's imagined Jewish community is a hostile stereotype inherited from European Christian culture, not a representation of actual Jewish life. These facts are necessary for understanding the tale rather than modern qualifications appended to it from outside.

The Prioress's voice makes the material more troubling. In the General Prologue she seemed exquisitely sensitive to suffering animals and concerned with elegant manners. Her tale lavishes tenderness upon a child but withholds humanity from the people it names as his enemies. A reader can examine how the style of gentle devotion is used to make exclusion feel pious. Some critics see Chaucer as exposing the Prioress's narrow sympathy; others argue that the story participates directly in the prejudice it depicts. The presence of a fictional narrator does not automatically absolve its author or erase the harm of the inherited genre.

The narrative is also built as a Marian miracle story. The miraculous singing of a murdered child transforms grief into proof of heavenly favor. To a medieval Christian audience such a story might have been understood as praise of Mary's intervention. But we can distinguish the devotional literary mechanism from the false charge on which the plot depends. Recognizing how the story is constructed helps us account for its cultural force without pretending that its accusation is credible.

We have now heard a sequence in which moral language has been turned toward incompatible purposes. The Pardoner used a true warning against greed to steal. The Prioress uses the language of innocence and compassion to tell a story with violent anti-Jewish content. Chaucer's collection gives us no simple guarantee that a moving style serves a just cause. The next speaker is Chaucer as a character, whose attempt at a harmless rhyming romance is so bad that the Host refuses to let him finish. The collision of stories will continue, but laughter at bad verse must not make us forget what the Prioress's verse asked its audience to believe.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: Sir Thopas, the Host Interrupts

After the Prioress has finished, the Host notices a quiet, rather withdrawn member of the company: Chaucer the pilgrim, who has been riding among them without making a performance of himself. Harry Bailey tells him to stop staring at the ground and give the company a tale of mirth. This moment turns the whole collection inside out. The poet who has invented the pilgrims now becomes a fictional character judged by them. His literary prestige will give him no protection from the Host's boredom.

Chaucer the pilgrim says that he knows a little rhyme about Sir Thopas, a young knight of remarkable beauty who lives in Flanders. His face is white and red, his clothing impressive, his appetite small, his accomplishments various. He rides across a landscape of birdsong, flowers and well-known romance decorations. The verse keeps announcing that he is a worthy knight. Yet hardly anything happens. Sir Thopas decides to seek an elf queen as his beloved, encounters a giant named Olifaunt, and flees long enough to prepare himself for a suitably splendid encounter.

The narrator recounts the knight's preparations with loving attention to his equipment, horse and provisions. His rhymes are deliberate, easy, repetitive and absurdly pleased with themselves. Where the Squire's unfinished romance promised a complicated world of wonders, this one seems satisfied with a catalog of romance clichés. The hero's grand quest moves forward with all the speed of a parade in which every participant stops to describe his costume.

The Host finally interrupts. He cannot bear to hear any more of the miserable rhymes, he says. They are worth nothing and are making him sick. If Chaucer has anything else to offer, it should be in prose. The fictional author's attempt at an entertaining romance has been rejected within his own book. The storyteller does not protest that he is famous or that the audience ought to appreciate his art. He submits and offers another tale, longer and more serious.

The interrupted poem is not merely bad writing accidentally placed inside a masterpiece. It is a parody of popular tail-rhyme romances, with their predictable formulas, heroic declarations and habit of substituting lavish description for meaningful action. Its badness is an artistic achievement because the Host recognizes and responds to it. It also supplies a test of the storytelling contest itself. If a knight's romance can be rejected for form, who decides what counts as valuable literature? Harry Bailey has taste, but his taste is not the final court of appeal; the audience outside the fiction may enjoy the joke precisely because he objects.

Sir Thopas ends without meeting the elf queen or defeating Olifaunt. The interruption is deliberate within the frame, unlike the textual incompletion of the Cook's and Squire's tales. Both kinds of incompletion occur in this manuscript, and they should not be confused. The distinction is important: in one case an author stops writing, in another a character stops another character from speaking. Chaucer the pilgrim now proposes the Tale of Melibee, a long prose argument about injury, vengeance and patience. After the lightweight rhymes, the Host may find himself wishing he had been less demanding.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: Melibee, Prudence and Counsel

A man named Melibee has enemies. While he is away, three intruders enter his home, beat his wife Prudence and wound their daughter Sophie in five places. Melibee returns, sees the injury and is overcome by grief and anger. He wants revenge. The story Chaucer the pilgrim has promised is not an adventure in which a hero rides out to defeat the criminals. It is a long, careful argument over what a person ought to do after terrible harm.

Prudence urges her husband to master his anger long enough to seek sound counsel. She is not asking him to forget the attack or deny its seriousness. She knows that grief can make an injured person choose an action that produces further injuries. She asks who can be trusted to advise him, how advisers may be corrupted by interest, and what difference there is between justice and private retaliation. Melibee wants action; Prudence wants to know what action would accomplish. Their exchanges fill page after page with proverbs, authorities and examples.

The household begins by gathering advisers. Some encourage a forceful response, while others recommend caution. Prudence asks Melibee to weigh their motives. People who enjoy war may find war attractive even when someone else pays its cost. Flatterers tell a powerful person what he already desires. A sound adviser must be willing to state what is difficult to hear. This is a story of political judgment on a domestic scale: Melibee has power to command followers, but authority does not guarantee that the anger guiding his command is wise.

Prudence is especially careful about time. An action done in anger may be impossible to reverse after the feeling passes. A settlement made under pressure may invite another wrong; revenge may set a chain of retaliation moving. Melibee gradually learns to deliberate. He hears from the offenders and eventually considers mercy. The attackers acknowledge fault and submit to his judgment. With Prudence's counsel he chooses reconciliation rather than violent revenge. His choice is presented as a victory of wisdom over pride, not as evidence that the wounds did not matter.

Modern readers often find this tale exhausting. Its characters spend more time quoting moral authorities than doing things. It can be read as a medieval conduct manual set inside a frame narrative. Yet its very form is part of the collection's argument. The Knight's Tale made conflict beautiful through tournament and ceremony; the Miller's and Reeve's tales answered humiliation with new humiliation; the Physician's Tale let a father's commitment to honor lead to murder. Here a woman's counsel interrupts the expected cycle. The cure for injured pride is not another spectacular punishment, but the discipline of listening.

There are limits to the achievement. Prudence has to persuade a husband who holds the formal power to decide. Her authority is intellectual and moral rather than guaranteed by the institutions of marriage. Sophie, the wounded daughter, does not receive a comparable speaking role. The dialogue about forgiveness never becomes her story. The distinction recalls the Wife of Bath and Griselda: one woman demanded sovereignty, another endured coercion, and Prudence finds practical authority by showing that her husband's power is insufficient without her judgment.

The Host praises the tale after it ends and complains that his own wife would react very differently if anyone struck a servant or challenged their household. His domestic remarks expose another gap between literature and daily life. The patient solution of a prose exemplum is easier to admire than to practice. The Monk now takes the road in a darker direction, offering a succession of people raised to power and hurled down by Fortune. Where Prudence sought to slow disaster, the Monk seems determined to show that disaster is unavoidable.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Monk’s Tale, the Falls of Great People

The Monk chooses tragedy, by which he means accounts of people who once possessed greatness and then fell into misery. He is not telling one extended story. He tells a series of brief examples, each demonstrating how unstable worldly power can be. A king may stand in splendor at one moment and be reduced to nothing at another. Human reputation does not constrain Fortune, and royal birth offers no special exemption from change.

He begins on the largest scale, with Lucifer's fall from heavenly glory and Adam's loss of Eden, then moves through legendary and historical figures. Samson's strength cannot save him from betrayal. Hercules, the great hero, is brought down through deception and the fatal garment of Nessus. A powerful ruler like Nebuchadnezzar is humbled. Belshazzar's court is interrupted by a sign of judgment, and his reign ends. The stories bring biblical exempla, classical legends and accounts of kings into one pattern, so that the particular circumstances begin to matter less than the repeated movement from height to ruin.

The Monk also turns to women and rulers from other histories, including Zenobia, whose command and courage do not protect her from defeat. Later examples include Julius Caesar, whose military and political accomplishments end in assassination, and Croesus, associated with wealth and a fortune that proves unstable. The Monk's voice is steady and learned. Every apparent exception to misfortune becomes one more item in the collection of fallen lives.

After a while the repetition becomes oppressive. The Knight asks him to stop. His own story contained death, but also moments of mercy, grief and marriage that allowed the living to continue. Here each life seems designed to end at the same point. The Host agrees that the pilgrims need something less depressing. He calls upon the Nun's Priest to tell a tale capable of restoring cheer. The Monk's authority as a learned and apparently imposing religious man cannot make his hearers endure an endless sequence of sad endings.

The interruption is part of Chaucer's art. The Monk offers a view of history organized almost completely around reversal. It is not wrong that kingdoms fall or reputations vanish. Yet a collection in which every example proves the same proposition ceases to let its subjects act as particular people. The audience's resistance is not simply a failure of moral seriousness. It is a demand for narrative variety and for a relation between suffering and the lives in which suffering occurs.

The Monk's tragedy also invites comparison with the social portraits in the General Prologue. There he was the well-fed hunter who rejected the strict disciplines of his order. Here he can command moral learning and examples of worldly vanity. His learning does not erase his own appetite for comfort. Chaucer refuses to sort people into those who understand moral truths and those who fail them. A person may know, repeat and teach a lesson whose practical claims he does not fully accept. The next speaker will put this problem into a barnyard comedy, where a rooster who can reason at length about the meaning of dreams is nearly caught by a fox because he enjoys a flattering voice.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Nun’s Priest’s Tale, Chanticleer and the Fox

In a small cottage a poor widow keeps a few animals, including a brilliant rooster named Chanticleer and a group of hens. Chanticleer is magnificent by the standards of the farmyard: his comb is like a castle wall, his voice is finer than a trumpet and he knows the hours with almost scholarly precision. His favorite hen is Pertelote, with whom he shares affectionate and occasionally argumentative company. Into this ordinary yard Chaucer's Nun's Priest brings the language of heroic epic.

Chanticleer wakes frightened from a dream. He has seen a beast that looks like a fox and fears that it means danger. Pertelote dismisses the dream as a disturbance caused by bodily imbalance. She recommends medicinal herbs and urges him to be brave. Chanticleer answers with an impressive parade of authorities and examples showing that dreams can foretell events. The quarrel about a rooster's nightmare becomes a parody of learned debate, with medical theories, ancient stories and affectionate insults occupying more room than a prudent bird might spare.

When morning arrives the widow's yard seems peaceful. A fox has entered and waits among the greenery. Chanticleer notices him, but the fox flatters him. He praises the rooster's father and the astonishing musical gift of his family, then invites Chanticleer to demonstrate his singing. The rooster closes his eyes and stretches his neck to sing at his finest. The fox seizes him in his jaws and runs.

The barnyard erupts. The hens scream, the widow calls for help, and villagers and dogs give chase. Chanticleer, still held in the fox's mouth, finds an opportunity. He advises the fox to turn and shout a triumphant insult at his pursuers. The fox opens his mouth to speak. Chanticleer escapes into a tree. The fox now tries another speech, urging the rooster to come down. Chanticleer refuses. Both agree that speaking carelessly can destroy the speaker's interests, but the fox has learned the lesson after losing dinner and the rooster after almost losing his life.

The story gives animals human motives without losing their animal bodies. A rooster can debate fate, but he remains edible. A fox can compose a persuasive speech, but must open his jaws to utter it. The comedy's sudden physical reversal works because the narrative has spent so much time elevating an ordinary domestic danger into philosophical and military grandeur. The chase briefly seems as consequential as the fall of Troy, which makes its ridiculous cause even sharper.

The Nun's Priest is alert to the power of language. Chanticleer's erudition cannot protect him from a compliment that answers his vanity. The fox's rhetorical talent nearly wins a meal, then costs him the meal when his desire to boast outruns his practical need to keep his mouth closed. The teller offers multiple possible morals rather than one that tidies away the story's pleasures. We can apply it to the pardoner who preaches against greed while taking money, the Monk who knows the fragility of glory while enjoying status, and the pilgrims who insist they can identify the best tale. Chaucer's animals are not merely examples of human weakness. Their quick intelligence also gives the poem permission to laugh at the imposing languages in which people describe themselves.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Second Nun’s Tale, Cecilia and the Martyrs

The Second Nun begins with an appeal to diligence against idleness. She then tells the life of Saint Cecilia, a Christian martyr from the legendary past of Rome. Cecilia is promised in marriage to Valerian, but has privately vowed to remain chaste and devout. On their wedding night she tells her husband that an angel guards her body and that he must honor her commitment. Valerian asks to see the angel. She sends him to Pope Urban, who instructs and baptizes him. Returning, he sees Cecilia with the angel and understands the claim that had seemed impossible.

Valerian's brother Tiburce joins them in Christian faith. The brothers give help to the poor and bury martyrs, defying Roman authorities who demand obedience to other gods. Their courage leads to arrest and execution. The Roman officer Almachius then summons Cecilia. He assumes that a young woman, isolated from her husband and brother-in-law, can be frightened into compliance. She answers him with confidence, refusing to treat earthly office as the ultimate source of truth. The exchange becomes a contest over whose authority lasts.

Almachius orders her put to death. First he attempts to kill her by heat in her bath, but she survives. An executioner strikes her neck three times with a sword yet fails to sever her head. Law or custom forbids a fourth blow, and Cecilia remains alive for several days, speaking with her followers and arranging for her possessions to be used for the Christian community. She finally dies and is buried by Urban. The story presents her suffering as testimony rather than defeat.

The Nun's account is a saint's life, not a modern reconstruction of third-century Rome from independent documentary evidence. Its legendary miracles matter to the genre because they display divine power against the emperor's officers. The historical cult of Cecilia is real, but the details of her martyrdom were shaped in later devotional traditions. Treating this narrative as a literary account of sanctity allows listeners to understand its purpose without having to claim that every scene can be verified as history.

The story has a particular force in the Canterbury company because Cecilia is a female speaker who resists a man's political and marital claims. She does not approach marriage as the Wife of Bath did, but her insistence upon a commitment made to God supplies another answer to the question of authority over a woman's life. Her husband accepts rather than destroys that commitment. The contrast with Walter's testing of Griselda or January's wish to possess May is significant, even though each story belongs to a different literary tradition.

The Second Nun's devotional seriousness also follows the Monk's tragic catalogue and the Nun's Priest's comic rescue. These pilgrims have not agreed about the proper subject of a tale. A tragic fall, an animal fable and a saint's triumph can all occupy the same road. Their proximity exposes what changes when suffering is interpreted through Fortune, comic intelligence or miraculous faith. The next story will return to the moral danger of claiming knowledge, this time through a servant who describes the exhausting failures and frauds of his master's alchemical laboratory.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Canon’s Yeoman’s Tale, the Alchemists

Late in the journey two riders gallop up behind the pilgrims. One is a canon, a religious official, and the other his yeoman. Their horses are exhausted; the men have hurried to join the company. The Host welcomes them and asks what kind of knowledge the canon possesses. The yeoman boasts that his master can perform extraordinary transformations by alchemy. The canon grows alarmed as the servant speaks too freely. He tries to silence him, then rides away rather than endure exposure. Freed from his employer's presence, the yeoman begins a bitter confession.

He describes years spent in a laboratory chasing the philosopher's stone, the supposed means of transforming base metals into precious ones. Furnaces burn day and night. Crucibles, powders and substances are bought, melted, heated and combined. Every failure prompts another expenditure, another piece of secret terminology, another hope that the correct mixture has nearly been found. The yeoman's clothing and appearance show the cost of the work. Wealth has been consumed in attempting to manufacture wealth. He warns the company that the craft has left him poor and exhausted.

Then he tells the story of a canon who persuades a London priest that he can turn common metal into silver. The canon performs carefully staged demonstrations. He hides genuine silver in places where it will emerge from an apparent transformation, using sleight of hand and prepared materials to make ordinary operations look miraculous. The priest sees silver at the end and mistakes a trick for a discovery about matter. His desire to possess the secret makes him eager to pay. He gives the canon a large sum for the technique, and the fraud departs before any independent trial can expose it.

The teller insists that this particular swindler is not necessarily his own master. The distinction matters, though his anger toward his master has already made the broader world of alchemy suspect. Chaucer's poem is unusually attentive to apparatus, costs, procedural claims and the difficulty of assessing results. The problem is not that the priest lacks faith in learning. It is that a demonstration can be designed to prove something it does not prove. An operator who controls all the conditions can hide an ordinary cause behind a dramatic result.

It would be a mistake to dismiss every historical alchemist as a mere fool. Medieval and early modern practices mixed practical chemical techniques with ambitious theories, religious symbolism and commercial fraud. What this tale exposes most clearly is the asymmetry between someone who knows the trick and someone who believes he is buying reproducible knowledge. The canon has an incentive to sell the promise of success; the priest cannot inspect the actual process by which the observed result was achieved.

The tale belongs alongside the Pardoner's confession of religious fraud. Both speakers know that the audience's desire can be turned into payment. The Pardoner sells spiritual reassurance; the dishonest canon sells an apparently material miracle. Neither needs the promised effect to be real in order to profit. The yeoman's own speech also contains ambivalence: he is ashamed of his wasted years yet remains fascinated by the vocabulary of the craft. His warning is a record of dependence as well as fraud. After this laboratory of false transformations, the Manciple tells of a bird whose speech exposes a secret and destroys the household that hears it.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Manciple’s Tale, Phoebus and the Crow

The Manciple buys supplies for a company of lawyers and has learned how to make skilled purchases in a world of educated clients. He has little respect for the Cook, who is so drunk that he cannot ride or answer a question properly. The Host and others intervene before their quarrel grows worse. The Manciple then tells a story about a man whose desire to hear the truth produces a violent result.

Phoebus Apollo is imagined as a magnificent archer and musician, proud of his skill and of his beautiful wife. He keeps a white crow that can speak as well as sing. Although Phoebus possesses extraordinary gifts, he cannot command his wife's desire. While he is away she takes a lover. The crow witnesses the affair and, when Phoebus returns, tells him what happened. The revelation is made with the bird's apparent confidence in reporting what it saw.

Phoebus reacts with immediate rage and kills his wife. His grief follows the act too late to save her. He turns against the crow that brought the news. He curses its speech, plucks out its white feathers and condemns its descendants to have black feathers and harsh voices. The tale offers this as an origin story for the crow's present appearance, but its moral weight lies elsewhere: an accurate report has not prevented catastrophe. Phoebus's violence, not the bird's speech, kills the woman; then he punishes the witness for making his ignorance impossible.

The Manciple draws a lesson about restraint in speech. It may be dangerous to tell a powerful person an unpleasant truth, especially about a matter in which anger can overcome judgment. He urges caution, repeating the advice that one should keep one's tongue under control. But readers need not confuse this practical wisdom with a moral defense of Phoebus. If those who report harm are punished, silence becomes a means of protecting the violent. The storyteller's advice may help the vulnerable survive, while also revealing a world in which power demands careful management by those who do not possess it.

Other tales on this road have used revelation in different ways. The Man of Law's forged letters deceived a king; the Pardoner exposed his own tricks yet still tried to sell them; the Canon's Yeoman accused an employer who then fled. The crow's fate sharpens the question of what speech can accomplish when listeners choose anger over understanding. The tale's final turn toward silence is troubling precisely because a book made of competing voices cannot exist on such terms.

The pilgrims are approaching Canterbury. The Manciple has described the destructive power of an uncontrolled response to truth; the Parson, whose life was praised in the General Prologue, will now offer a long prose discourse on repentance. The competition began with a proposal to entertain. It ends, in the surviving arrangement, with a speaker who refuses to invent a fable at all.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Parson’s Tale, the Road to Penance

It is late in the day. The travellers have heard stories of love, theft, saints, violence, deceptive speech and marvelous transformations. Harry Bailey asks the Parson for a tale to complete the entertainment. The Parson refuses to tell a fable. He will speak in prose, he says, about the journey that matters beyond every earthly road: the way toward repentance and salvation. His contribution is not a plot in which an individual hero acts through a sequence of events. It is an extended account of how a person recognizes wrongdoing and attempts to change.

The Parson begins with penitence. A person must feel genuine contrition, confess the truth and make satisfaction as far as possible. Such acts cannot be reduced to embarrassment or fear of being caught. Contrition requires seeing sin as a corruption of the relationship with God and with one's neighbors. A catalogue of religious categories follows, including the seven deadly sins and the virtues that counter them. Pride is answered by humility, envy by love of neighbor, anger by patience and meekness, sloth by spiritual effort, greed by generosity, gluttony by temperance, and lust by chastity.

Each category expands into an account of ordinary life. Pride can attach to clothing, rank or the desire to dominate another person. Envy can thrive in conversation that delights in another's misfortune. Greed can become the wish to hold more wealth than one needs, or the exploitation of someone who cannot defend himself. These are not abstractions detached from the pilgrimage. Readers who have heard the Reeve's revenge, the Merchant's possessive husband, the Pardoner's sales techniques and the Friar's corrupt visitor can recognize patterns of wrongdoing without being given a list of which pilgrim must be condemned.

The Parson also reflects upon confession. Speaking truth is difficult because the sinner must surrender the self-image that made wrongdoing tolerable. Confession should not become another performance for advantage; the Pardoner's admission of fraud was brilliantly clear and yet did not lead him to stop selling. Nor is penitence merely a private feeling. Satisfaction includes trying to repair the harms one has caused. That claim connects the treatise with Prudence's advice to Melibee and with the losses that cannot be repaired, such as Virginia's death.

The discourse is long and demanding, and it lacks the quick reversals of comedy or romance. Its placement can therefore feel like a deliberate challenge to the Host's original contest. The pilgrimage to Becket's shrine has always had a religious destination, though many stories seemed to forget it. The Parson brings spiritual pilgrimage into the language of explicit instruction. He insists that the company has been moving through questions of conscience whether or not the tellers intended to do so.

But an edition's decision to place this treatise near the end is not evidence that Chaucer finalized the order of every fragment. The poem survives as an unfinished collection whose larger architecture remains uncertain. We can appreciate the resonance between a literal road and a moral path without assigning an authorially guaranteed conclusion to all preceding tales. This is a final voice in many manuscript arrangements, not a verdict that cancels earlier voices. After the Parson's prose comes a retraction in which the author asks forgiveness for writings that may have encouraged worldly vanity. That closing gesture will raise one final question: can the creator of such lively fiction also ask his readers to renounce it?

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: Retraction and the Unfinished Journey

At the end of many editions of The Canterbury Tales stands a brief prose text known as Chaucer's Retraction. The author thanks Christ, Mary and the saints for whatever in his writing has led people toward devotion and asks forgiveness for works of worldly vanity. He names several of his own poems and stories. The piece seems, at first, to contradict the noisy book that precedes it: after hundreds of pages of quarrels, erotic jokes, romances, sermons and false claims, a writer asks that anything spiritually harmful be forgiven.

How should a listener receive this? One possibility is to hear the Retraction as a genuine devotional statement by an aging author, concerned about the moral implications of his career. Another is to notice that literature in the collection has repeatedly tested the relation between a speaker and a statement. The Pardoner condemns greed without reforming; the Wife of Bath uses scriptural learning while mocking those who use it to silence her; Chaucer the pilgrim is himself interrupted for telling bad poetry. The Retraction may be sincere and still exist within a literary structure that prevents us from reducing the preceding voices to one lesson.

The actual pilgrimage is not completed in the surviving collection. We do not watch the full company arrive at Becket's shrine, receive the promised supper after the return journey, or determine a winning tale. The host planned a large competition with outward and homeward storytelling. What survives is twenty-four tales, some unfinished, grouped in ten fragments that contain internal connections but no certain final arrangement of every group. Chaucer died without delivering the neatly completed book that Harry Bailey's rules seemed to anticipate.

The absence of an ending has shaped every generation of readers. Copyists and editors arranged fragments, chose where to place links, and sometimes supplied additional works in the hope of filling gaps. A modern edition has to make practical decisions about sequence, but those decisions are not identical with proof of Chaucer's final intentions. The distinction makes the book more revealing rather than less. It has become an object that readers, scribes and scholars must continually assemble, and therefore continually interpret.

The journey's central idea remains powerful. People from different walks of life agree to travel together and, while sharing a road, tell stories. They do not become morally identical through the act of pilgrimage. They compete for attention, expose one another's hypocrisy, delight in incompatible genres and recognize different forms of authority. Some use stories to injure, others to advise, and many do both at once. The text offers no single voice capable of explaining all the others away.

If we return to the opening spring morning, the movement is clear even without a final supper. The road made strangers into a public audience. At the inn they could have exchanged names and occupations; as tellers and listeners they reveal grudges, fantasies, fears and methods of judgment. Some stories teach readers how to recognize patterns of harm. Others force readers to ask why a joke once considered harmless can wound people excluded from its audience. Every teller is situated within a social world, and every listener has a stake in what is said.

The last surviving movement, then, is neither a completed travel itinerary nor a straightforward triumph of one literary form. It is an invitation to continue thinking after the texts stop. The pilgrims' proposed contest raises the possibility of a best tale; the unfinished collection leaves us with the more demanding task of deciding what kinds of excellence, truth and responsibility can coexist in stories told by people who do not agree. That is a more lasting achievement than a prize dinner would have been.
