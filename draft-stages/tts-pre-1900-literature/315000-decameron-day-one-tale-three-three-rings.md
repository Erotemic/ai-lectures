# The Decameron — Melchisedech and the Three Rings

Saladin, a ruler with pressing financial needs, hears that a Jewish moneylender named Melchisedech possesses great wealth. He could ask for a loan directly; instead he considers a more profitable trap. Summon the man, demand an answer on a question that will offend someone powerful whatever he says, then use the offense to seize his money. The question is which of three faiths—Judaism, Christianity, or Islam—is true. Saladin counts on having the authority to punish an answer he dislikes.

Melchisedech recognizes the danger. No speech that simply praises his own faith, or dismisses the ruler's, will be safe. Rather than claim to settle the question, he offers a story. A rich man has a beautiful and precious ring which he intends to pass to the son he favors most. For generations this ring has served as the token by which a father identifies his chosen heir. At last the ring comes to a father who loves three sons equally. Each son has reason to hope he will inherit. Each has received assurances he believes. The father secretly has two convincing copies made, and at his death each son receives a ring.

Now all three possess what appears to be the family treasure. Each can tell a plausible story about his right to it. But the rings are so alike that no observer can reliably say which is the original. So it is, Melchisedech says, with the religions whose followers each believe that the true inheritance has descended to them. He has given Saladin an answer without choosing a target for punishment. A demand for certainty has been met by a parable about the limits of available evidence.

Saladin understands that his planned test has failed. Instead of taking offense, he admires the speaker's intelligence and abandons the trick. He explains that he needs money. Melchisedech lends it, and the ruler repays him honorably, rewarding the prudence that saved him. The danger to the Jewish merchant's wealth has been turned into a relationship grounded, at least by the end, in open negotiation rather than intimidation.

The rings do not establish that the three religions have identical teachings. That is not how a parable works. They expose the difficulty of settling a question by asking a vulnerable person to make a declaration under threat. The most important change in the story is not that every disagreement disappears, but that Saladin stops using unequal power to force an answer. His capacity to recognize wit becomes an element of his greatness. Melchisedech's eloquence protects his life and fortune.

This story has a long afterlife, including later versions that give the ring parable a more explicit argument for religious toleration. Here it remains shaped by danger, wealth, and the uneven position of the speakers. The first two stories of the day put Jewish and Christian figures into tense conversation; the third brings a Muslim ruler into the scene. All three ask whether a person's official standing reveals the truth about that person's judgment. Melchisedech survives because he can tell a story that Saladin would rather admire than punish.
