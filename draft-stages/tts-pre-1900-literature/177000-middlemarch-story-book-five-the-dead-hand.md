# Middlemarch — The Dead Hand

The title The Dead Hand points first to control exercised after death. George Eliot is interested in the ways property, memory, professional ambition, and resentment can continue acting when the person who made a decision is no longer present to explain it or reconsider it. The most intimate example takes place between Dorothea and Casaubon, but the book extends that question across Middlemarch.

Casaubon worries that his work will never reach completion. Dorothea has already promised to help him; he wants something more binding. He asks her to pledge obedience to a request he may make concerning his interests after his death, without yet knowing exactly what he will ask. The moral pressure on her is immense. To refuse could seem cruel to a sick husband. To promise blindly would yield her future judgment to a man who has not shown trust in it.

Dorothea does not immediately give the answer he wants. She tries to think responsibly about the implications. Before the request can be resolved, Casaubon dies. The death ends their unhappy marriage but does not end his efforts to shape her life. A clause added to his will provides that Dorothea loses the property he has left her if she marries Will Ladislaw. The codicil is both material and accusatory. It assumes a relation that Dorothea has not entered and makes that suspicion public.

Her relatives can now interpret even her grief through the supposed meaning of the codicil. Dorothea is outraged not just by the property condition but by the falseness of its premise. She has tried faithfully to support Casaubon. His final arrangement carries the humiliation of being judged disloyal by the man who should have known her best. Yet she also understands that he was vulnerable and fearful. Eliot asks us to recognize the origin of a wrong without denying that it remains a wrong.

Mr. Brooke's political career offers a public counterpart. He declares himself interested in parliamentary reform, but his record as a landlord is vulnerable to criticism. At his election speech, the crowd mocks his rambling pronouncements. He cannot make his general opinions stand up to concrete questions about what he has done. Will Ladislaw, working in journalism, discovers that advocacy requires more clarity than admiration for a cause. The atmosphere of reform before 1832 exposes both genuine possibilities and easy self-deception.

Lydgate and Rosamond are now married. He continues working at the hospital and imagines a medical career built on patient study and improved treatment. Their finances, however, tighten under the costs of a large household. Rosamond's approach is to preserve the appearance of ease; Lydgate increasingly resents the conversations he has avoided. Their marriage becomes another case in which a decision made for an ideal future acquires a claim on the real present.

Bulstrode acquires Stone Court after Joshua Rigg's brief tenure, bringing another property into his expanding circle of influence. Raffles, Rigg's stepfather, recognizes him and knows compromising facts about his earlier financial life. Their encounters threaten to expose the distance between Bulstrode's public religious respectability and his conduct before he established himself in Middlemarch. His anxious response will make past actions more, not less, powerful over him.

The town does not divide neatly into reformers and reactionaries. Brooke likes reform but governs poorly. Lydgate advances scientific medicine yet misjudges personal dependence. Casaubon is erudite yet terrified of examination. Bulstrode speaks the language of spiritual obligation while hiding the foundations of his fortune. Eliot's narrator is interested in the discrepancy between what an institution says it values and what its daily habits reward.

Casaubon's dead hand is visible in a will. Other dead hands are harder to identify. Lydgate is bound by expectations set when he bought a house. Fred has lived under the shadow of a hoped-for legacy. Bulstrode is threatened by transactions from years before. Whether the hand is literally dead matters less than whether someone else has surrendered the power to choose again.

Dorothea can no longer repair her marriage. She can decide what to do with her freedom, although money and reputation still constrain it. The novel is not promising an easy new beginning. It has shown how difficult new beginnings become when the past has acquired legal and social force.
