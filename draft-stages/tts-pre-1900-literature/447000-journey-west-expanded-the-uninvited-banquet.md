# The Uninvited Banquet and the Army of Heaven

The seven peach-gathering maidens remained still beneath the trees. Sun Wukong was already on his way to the Queen Mother's feast. He had not been invited, and he had not settled what he meant to do about it. Anger carried him rapidly enough. The great houses of Heaven stood about him, splendid, orderly, and apparently secure. Somewhere among them tables were being laid, jars of wine were waiting, and the guests for whom all this effort had been made were still on their way.

As he approached the banquet, he met the Barefoot Immortal, who was going to the celebration by the usual route. Wukong decided to make use of the meeting. He presented himself as an official with urgent instructions and claimed that a new rule had been issued. Before going to the feast, guests were to visit another hall and perform a preliminary ceremony. The Barefoot Immortal was puzzled. The arrangement was not what he remembered. But the Great Sage spoke as though he knew the emperor's wishes, and an honorable guest who had received an invitation was not eager to disobey a last-minute command. He turned away to do as he was told.

Wukong took the immortal's appearance and went on in his place. He reached the hall before the guests. The elaborate tables and vessels were already arranged. Fine foods waited in the open, and the smells of the feast rose through the rooms. The Monkey King gazed at them with an appreciation entirely absent from the solemn arrangements that had placed them there. He might be uninvited, but that did not prevent him from thinking the preparations excellent.

He found the workers finishing the wine. They stood beside jars, bottles, and vessels containing drinks made for gods. Simply ordering them away would invite questions. So he took hairs from his body, used his magic to turn them into tiny creatures that brought sleep, and sent those creatures among the attendants. Eyes closed, hands dropped from work, and the staff sank into stupor. Wukong looked about the hall. No one was likely to disturb him now.

He began with the food. He passed among dishes meant for the great guests, sampled them, and returned to what he preferred. He drank from the celestial wine jars, drank again, and kept drinking until he could scarcely remember that his original purpose had been to investigate the guest list. He did not seize a throne or proclaim a new calendar. He ate and drank until the feast that was meant to display Heaven's harmony had become the disorderly remains of a private meal.

At last the thought of discovery alarmed him. What if the guests arrived and found him here? They could report everything, and the emperor would be furious. Better to return to his own house and sleep until he had recovered. He left the banquet hall and went in what he believed was the correct direction. But the heavenly wine had confused his sense of the roads. Instead of reaching his residence he wandered into the palace of Laozi, the great Daoist sage, high in the celestial order.

He recognized the place and decided that, since he was here, he might as well pay a visit. The rooms were deserted. Laozi was elsewhere discussing religious matters, and his attendants had accompanied him. Wukong entered the chamber where the medicines and elixirs were made. A furnace stood among the instruments of cultivation. Nearby were five gourds containing pills whose preparation represented an immense labor of skill and time. They were not to be eaten like ordinary sweets. The Great Sage looked at them, understood that they were treasures, and decided to taste them.

He opened the gourds and poured the elixir pills into his mouth as though they were beans. By the time he finished, there were none left. The excitement of eating and the effects of the miraculous medicine cleared his drunkenness. The result was an unwelcome moment of understanding. He had eaten the Queen Mother's precious peaches, ruined a feast attended by the greatest beings in Heaven, deceived an immortal with a false instruction, and now swallowed Laozi's carefully prepared pills. This was not the kind of offense that could be smoothed over by an apology or a new title.

There were, as far as Wukong could see, two choices. He could remain in Heaven and be found, or he could leave. He chose the course most familiar to him. He slipped away by another gate, used magic to conceal himself, and returned to Flower-Fruit Mountain.

His followers came forward with the joy and complaints of creatures who had been left behind. They believed he had spent many years away. For him, the time had seemed shorter. He told them that the emperor had accepted his chosen title and given him a splendid house. But he also told them of the Peach Garden and of the banquet to which he had not been asked. The monkeys listened eagerly as he described the wine, dishes, and elixirs he had found in the celestial halls.

They offered him their own drink. It was coconut wine, familiar to the mountain and ordinarily welcome at the king's feasts. He tasted it and declared that it was not nearly as good as what he had drunk above. His officers reminded him that home had its own value, but Wukong's response was to make that value material. If the celestial wine was so excellent, why should his people never drink it? He leaped back toward Heaven, made himself invisible, stole jars from the banquet, and brought them down to the cave. There, the Great Sage and his monkeys held the feast from which he had been excluded, with the best drink Heaven had to offer.

In the celestial garden, the spell on the seven maidens finally wore off. They returned to the Queen Mother and reported both their delay and the strange state of the peach harvest. Other reports came quickly. The workers responsible for the feast had awakened to find food and wine missing. Laozi discovered that the medicines he had prepared for a great occasion were gone. The Barefoot Immortal described the false instructions that had sent him on an unnecessary errand. The officials assigned to attend the Great Sage could not find him. Piece by piece, the court assembled a complete account of the monkey's movements.

There was no longer any plausible way to treat the affair as a misunderstanding. The emperor ordered the heavenly army to bring Wukong in by force. Li Jing and Nezha were again in command, joined by the Four Heavenly Kings, the celestial constellations, star gods, and many other officers. The army was said to number a hundred thousand divine soldiers. Around Flower-Fruit Mountain they spread eighteen layers of celestial nets, surrounding the place by land and sky so that an ordinary fugitive would have found no path out.

At first Wukong continued drinking. Reports of officers demanding his surrender came into the cave, and he shrugged them off. He had good wine, his companions were merry, and he preferred to enjoy the present hour. But the officers would not stand at the entrance indefinitely. The nine fierce star gods pressed forward. The lesser mountain creatures rushed back in alarm, and Wukong at last took up his staff. He sent his one-horned commander and the rulers of the surrounding caves into the field, then followed with the four marshals of his own monkey troop.

The battle was immense. The nine star gods could not hold against the Monkey King and withdrew. Then the Four Heavenly Kings and their allies joined the fighting. Divine banners and mountain standards filled the air. Spears, axes, swords, and the iron staff crossed in quick succession. Through the day the armies struggled, and by evening the mountain was scarred by battle. Wukong's allied animal kings and the one-horned commander had been captured; the four monkey marshals and their troop had retreated to the cave. The losses were real, even though the king himself remained unharmed.

As the light failed, Wukong plucked a handful of hairs, changed them into a host of fighting copies of himself, and sent them across the field. A hundred thousand soldiers could surround one monkey, but the sight of countless monkeys, each appearing to wield the same tremendous staff, was another matter. The divine forces fell back. Wukong gathered his hairs again and returned to the mountain, a victor in the immediate contest.

The four marshals were waiting at the bridge. They wept and laughed as they greeted him. They wept because the mountain's allies had been taken, and laughed because their own king was alive and had driven back the army. Wukong dismissed the losses of the other animal rulers more lightly than his followers did. None of his own monkeys had been captured, he said, and the enemy had been beaten off. The army remained below the mountain, but everyone was tired. They should eat, rest, and fight again in the morning.

For Heaven, the campaign had revealed a serious problem: a powerful army could seize many lesser creatures without subduing the individual who had provoked the war. For Wukong, the evening could still end with wine, confident threats, and sleep. Around him the celestial nets remained in place. At dawn the soldiers would be there, and the monkey's next opponent would not be another ordinary commander of the court.
