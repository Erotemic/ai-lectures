# Journey to the West: The princess behind the wall

## A garden where gold had been spread

The monks of Compassionate Cloud woke to find their honored guests gone. They were distressed at having failed to give a proper farewell. Word spread through Jinping that the Great Tang travelers had departed secretly, and households that had prepared another day of vegetarian feasts were left with their tables set. Some said the holy men must have flown away. Others bowed toward the west and wished them success. The pilgrims themselves were already far along the road, relieved to have escaped the difficulty of saying goodbye to two hundred generous families.

They traveled for half a month through country where the hills rose again. Xuanzang saw another high ridge and warned the disciples to be watchful. Wukong said they were close to the Buddha's land now and that the great dangers of the road were nearly behind them. Xuanzang answered that nearness did not remove mountains or transform frightening things into harmless ones. He still recited the Heart Sutra that had been taught to him by the monk at Crow-Nest Mountain. He knew its words well.

"The words are not the same as understanding," Wukong said.

Xuanzang challenged him to explain his understanding. Wukong declared that he understood, but he offered no discourse. Bajie began to laugh at the monkey who had spent years wielding a staff and now claimed the wisdom of scholars. Sha joined the teasing. Xuanzang stopped them. Silence itself could carry understanding, he said; it need not be displayed in a contest of speeches. Wukong said no more.

Beyond the ridge they reached a monastery known as the Gold-Spreading Temple, a place whose name recalled the ancient patron of the Buddha, the elder who purchased a garden by laying gold upon its ground. At least that was the history preserved by the monks living there. They told the visitors that their monastery stood on land associated with Jetavana, the garden once given for the Buddha's teaching. In heavy rains, the monks said, old fragments and small precious objects sometimes appeared in the earth. Whatever the site's exact past, the residents treated the story as the reason for its name and its long reverence.

Xuanzang was deeply moved. Places that had been names in scripture were becoming places on the road. The distance he had traveled no longer sounded like an abstract number. He had reached a district where the history of Buddhist generosity shaped the name of the building that offered him shelter.

In the courtyard stood merchants with loaded animals. The monks explained that a nearby road through Hundred-Leg Mountain had been troubled by centipede spirits. Caravans waited here until the morning cry of the rooster, the hour believed safe for travel past the dangerous pass. Xuanzang agreed to wait as well. His impatience to reach the end of the journey was strong, but not so strong that he wished to take his company into a known hazard merely to leave a day earlier.

## The voice behind the enclosure

That night the moon rose over the monastery's rear garden. Xuanzang and Wukong went to see the remains of the old place remembered as Jetavana. An aged monk joined them, walking carefully with a staff. He was more than a hundred years old and delighted to speak with travelers from the Chinese lands. He pointed out stones that he believed belonged to the ancient foundations and told stories passed down among the guardians of the site. Xuanzang bowed and reflected on the men who had given their wealth so that a place for teaching might exist.

They heard a woman's crying behind a wall.

The sound changed the evening. It was not the laughter of the merchants or the chant of the monks. It was a young voice calling for parents who did not hear her. Xuanzang stopped and listened. He had heard lamentation in prisons and caves on the pilgrimage, but this was inside a Buddhist monastery, where hospitality and refuge were being offered to strangers. He asked who was hidden there.

The old monk hesitated, dismissed the others, and asked Wukong to hear him privately. Nearly a year earlier, he said, a wind had passed through the temple grounds at night. When he went out to investigate, he found a finely dressed girl near the old garden. She claimed to be the daughter of the king of Tianzhu, carried away by a sudden storm while viewing flowers with the royal family.

The monk had not believed her. A real princess would be sought by the court, and no royal messengers came. He went to the capital and heard that the princess was alive and well. He feared that the girl at his gate might be a demon posing as a human victim, or that letting her among the monks would bring scandal or trouble on the temple. Yet he would not injure her. Instead he confined her in a small room behind a wall, leaving a narrow opening through which food and water could be given.

The young woman had learned to behave as though she were mad whenever visitors came near. She lay in filth, spoke nonsense, and tried to frighten away anyone who might force his way into her confinement. The old monk thought that this strange conduct might support his suspicions. At night, when the monastery became quiet, she called for her mother and father in a voice that moved him despite himself.

He had made inquiries and found the court still reporting that the princess was present. He had never learned what that meant for the girl behind the wall. His solution had been to leave her imprisoned and alive. Now that Wukong had come, he hoped a man skilled in discerning supernatural deception could decide whether the prisoner was an innocent person or a threat.

Wukong listened without boasting. This was a different kind of problem from a cave guarded by a demon with a sword. There was no visible monster to challenge. The story of the captive had one witness, the monk, while the royal court seemed to provide another, equally convincing claim. Xuanzang asked that the young woman not be harmed while they investigated. The old monk promised to keep feeding her and begged them to carry the question to the king.

## The city of Tianzhu

At dawn the travelers crossed the mountain pass with the merchants. The feared centipede spirits did not attack. Soon the road opened into the countryside near the capital of Tianzhu itself. From a distance the walls and towers were immense, and Xuanzang felt the nearness of the goal he had named so often to his frightened disciples. He had not yet reached the Thunderclap Monastery, but he had come to the court of the country within which the sacred mountain was said to stand.

The party entered a lodging house for official visitors. Xuanzang asked about the king and the procedure for having his imperial travel document certified. The host told him that the reigning monarch loved gardens, flowers, and courtly entertainments. He also had a daughter of marriageable age, and on this very day the princess was to select a husband by throwing an embroidered ball from a raised pavilion into the crowd.

The announcement should have been no concern of a monk who had renounced marriage. But Xuanzang remembered the voice behind the monastery wall. The old monk's prisoner claimed to be the princess, and here was the court making a public ceremony on behalf of someone with that title. Wukong suggested that they go and see. A crowd might offer an opportunity to examine the woman publicly celebrated as the king's daughter.

Xuanzang hesitated. Watching a marriage spectacle in his monk's robes invited misunderstanding. Wukong argued that the road to the palace passed through the same streets and that investigating the prisoner's claim mattered more than avoiding curious eyes. Sha and Bajie remained at the lodging house to guard the baggage and horse. Xuanzang went with Wukong, his saffron robe and broad hat distinguishing him from the festival crowd.

The streets were packed. People from every trade had gathered beneath a colorful pavilion built over the crossroads. Musicians played. Courtiers looked down from balconies. Young men pushed forward and adjusted their clothing, each imagining that the embroidered ball might make him a member of the royal family. No one had to be invited twice to watch a royal marriage determined in public.

## The ball and the monk

The woman in the pavilion was not the girl imprisoned behind the monastery wall. The tale lets its listeners know what the court did not. A supernatural being had taken the true princess away during an evening of flowers and changed into her likeness. The impostor knew that the Tang pilgrim would pass this very street. Her public ceremony had a private aim: to bring Xuanzang into marriage and steal the spiritual essence she believed he carried.

At the appointed moment she offered incense and looked through the crowded streets. Her eyes settled not on the men who had dressed themselves to be chosen, but on the traveler wearing a monk's cap beside the small sharp-faced attendant. She raised the embroidered ball and cast it so that it struck Xuanzang's head. His hat tipped sideways. The ball dropped into the fold of his sleeve.

A shout traveled down from the pavilion. The princess had struck a monk. The courtiers proclaimed that the matter was settled. Men who had crowded near the pavilion hurried toward Xuanzang to see the unlikely winner and perhaps take the ball for themselves. He tried to move away, but the crowd hemmed him in.

Wukong enlarged his body and showed his teeth. The men who had pushed forward scattered at once. The monkey then returned to his usual size and looked at the bewildered monk, who was still holding his hat as though that might undo what had happened.

"You planned this," Xuanzang accused him.

"I did not throw the ball," said Wukong. "It came from above and landed on you. If I could make a princess marry anyone I wished, do you think I would choose a monk who has been terrified of marriage since the first day of his vows?"

The laughter did not settle the danger. Court attendants came down and bowed to the man now named the prospective royal son-in-law. They asked him to come before the king. Xuanzang told them of his pilgrimage and his vows, but a royal ritual performed in front of the whole capital could not be dismissed as an accident by one embarrassed traveler.

Wukong whispered a plan. Xuanzang should go to court and ask for an opportunity to speak with his disciples before any marriage was agreed. They had a genuine claim to investigate. If the princess was an impostor, the best chance of exposing her might come from entering the ceremony she herself had arranged. The monkey would return to the lodging house and bring Bajie and Sha when summoned.

Xuanzang disliked every part of the proposal except the promise that his disciple would not abandon him. He was escorted toward the palace. Wukong slipped back to the lodging house, where Bajie had already noticed that the city was in a state of excitement. A messenger arrived with the king's invitation for the three strange companions of the monk chosen by the embroidered ball. The official trembled at the sight of Bajie's face and could scarcely deliver the message in the right order.

Bajie assured him that no one was about to beat him. Wukong told Sha to gather the baggage, bring the horse, and follow. The disciples had been summoned to court as the attendants of a monk who was now being treated as a royal bridegroom. The true princess remained behind a locked door at the monastery, still calling for her parents. And the false princess had succeeded in drawing the pilgrim into her father's palace, where Wukong would have to discover how to turn a marriage ceremony into a rescue.
