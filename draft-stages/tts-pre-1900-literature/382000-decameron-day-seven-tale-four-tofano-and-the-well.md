# The Decameron — Tofano and the Well

Tofano of Arezzo is deeply jealous of his wife, Monna Ghita. Whatever affection may once have existed between them has become a struggle over freedom and suspicion. The more closely he watches her, the more determined she becomes to escape his control. He drinks heavily. One of Ghita's ways of arranging an evening with her lover is to encourage that habit until Tofano sleeps. The marriage has made deception a routine rather than an exception.

One night Tofano decides to investigate. He pretends to be drunk, then waits until Ghita has gone out and locks the house against her return. When she comes back, he stands behind the secured door and refuses to let her in. This is the moment in which he believes he has finally won. He has physical command of the threshold and intends to turn her absence into public disgrace. Ghita begs him to open. He will not.

She then changes the stakes. If he persists, she says, she will throw herself down the well. The well is nearby and dark; a splash would be heard from the house. Ghita takes a heavy stone, throws it into the water and hides. Tofano hears the crash and believes she has carried out her threat. His suspicion gives way to panic. He leaves the house and rushes toward the well to help her.

Ghita has been waiting for exactly this. She slips back into the house, bolts the door and makes herself the person who controls admission. Tofano is left outside, as she was. He demands that she let him in; she loudly accuses him of drinking and behaving disgracefully. Neighbors are now likely to hear her version rather than his. The same public reputation he meant to use as a weapon against her can be turned against him. He is no longer the husband gathering witnesses to an adulterous wife's conduct, but a drunken man causing a scene at the doorstep.

The reversal ends in a negotiated accommodation. Ghita retains far more freedom than Tofano intended to give her, and the husband discovers that the power to lock a door does not necessarily mean the power to control the story told about it. Lauretta treats the wife's resourcefulness as the satisfying achievement. The trick works because it creates a sudden demand on Tofano's genuine concern: he may be jealous and coercive, but he does not want her dead.

That is a disturbing basis for comedy. A false suggestion of suicide makes Tofano vulnerable to fear; the threat of public shame makes Ghita vulnerable to him. Neither spouse can speak honestly without first considering how the other might use the disclosure. The well is an ingenious stage prop, but it is also a measure of the distance between them. Their final agreement ends the immediate conflict, not necessarily the distrust on which it grew.
