# The Decameron — Day Three: A New Garden and the Things People Want

After two days of stories, the ten members of Boccaccio's company do not simply proceed to a third evening. Their ruler, Neifile, has set aside Friday and Saturday for religious observance, personal care, and rest. She has also arranged a move. This matters because the book's famous ten days of storytelling are not ten consecutive days, and the guests' retreat is a succession of carefully staged spaces, not a timeless room filled with speakers.

On Sunday morning the company walks through countryside alive with birdsong to another residence. The new house stands on a modest rise. Its rooms are richly furnished; its courtyard holds fresh water and provisions. A garden stretches beyond it, with trellised walks, roses, jasmine, and fruit trees. At the center a fountain sends water into a marble basin; ingenious channels conduct the overflow through the grounds and eventually toward working mill wheels. Boccaccio describes beauty and practical management in the same breath. The garden is cultivated, watered, and economically productive, and the guests compare its delight to an earthly paradise.

The travelers sing, eat, rest in the shade, read, play games, and gather again around the fountain. Outside their refuge, Florence still suffers from plague. Inside, supplies are brought and meals prepared by servants. The company's pleasant order depends upon labor that its members do not all perform. That division should remain visible when we describe their freedom to spend the afternoon telling stories. Their life is not a record of the whole society; it is the imagined life of a privileged group attempting to defend a patch of civilization against a surrounding catastrophe.

Neifile's theme is now more deliberate than the first day's open invitation and different from the second day's reversals of Fortune. The speakers are to describe those who have obtained something they longed for by ingenuity, persistence, or exertion, or who have recovered something they lost. This gives us a series about intention. Someone wants an object, a lover, a change in circumstances, or the restoration of a relationship. We will learn what that person is willing to do and how other people's wishes come into conflict with the plan.

Filostrato, who begins, tells of Masetto, a young man who gains entry into a convent by pretending he cannot speak. The apparent difference between a sacred vocation and bodily appetite will become a recurring comic resource. But later tales will make the same machinery less playful. A woman manipulates a confessor to arrange her own affair; a jealous wife is deceived into approaching the wrong man; an abbot turns a husband's imagined journey through the afterlife into a means of control. In each, somebody has a plan, but success for one participant need not be good fortune for another.

The day also includes longer romance-like accounts of Tedaldo's return and Gillette's campaign to reclaim her marriage. Dioneo closes with a deliberately provocative tale about a young seeker of religious life and the hermit who teaches her an earthly meaning for spiritual language. The register ranges from resourceful comedy to conduct that deserves more than laughter.

A garden is an apt setting for this day. The company has transformed uncultivated movement into a sequence of shaded paths and fountains. Their characters likewise try to direct unruly desires into chosen ends. Yet gardens can be enclosed, and the person who controls a gate may not be the person who must live inside. Boccaccio asks us to listen to both the clever action and its distribution of power.
