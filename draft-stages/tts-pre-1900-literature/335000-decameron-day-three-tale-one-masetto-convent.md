# The Decameron — Masetto at the Convent

In a village near Florence, a young farm worker called Masetto hears news from a man named Nuto who has left his job at a convent. The garden is beautiful, the work plentiful, and the pay poor. Nuto speaks bitterly of the young nuns who continually find fault with how things are done. Masetto hears something different in this complaint. He imagines that living among the women may prove more rewarding than farming for the people he already knows.

The convent needs a gardener, but Masetto fears that the women will not hire a healthy, attractive young man. He travels there with his tools and pretends to be unable to speak. A silent laborer, the convent's steward reasons, will bring less risk of gossip and will be easier to manage. Masetto is employed. He digs, carries, and plants. The nuns assume that their new worker cannot tell anyone what occurs within their walls.

Two of the younger nuns begin to wonder whether a man who cannot speak might nevertheless offer them an experience forbidden by their vows. They experiment with the idea and draw Masetto into an intimacy concealed from the rest of the community. The knowledge spreads. Others also seek him out, and eventually the abbess herself participates. Masetto's disguise has achieved its intended end almost too effectively. The work of tending the garden becomes the lesser part of his work; secrecy and the competing demands made upon him leave him exhausted.

At last, when alone with the abbess, he begins to speak. Rather than expose the convent, he says he has recovered his speech and explains that he cannot continue under the conditions they have made for him. The abbess is shocked, then finds a way to preserve appearances. The sisters agree to tell their neighbors that the prayers of the convent have miraculously healed a mute gardener. Masetto is given a more secure position as steward, and the entire arrangement continues behind the institution's respected public face.

Years pass. Masetto eventually returns home with wealth and children, whom he has not had to support or raise in the usual way. Filostrato presents him as someone who got more than he sought by trusting his cleverness and energy. The last joke depends on the gap between the convent's public story and what actually occurred there. People celebrate a holy miracle of recovered speech while the audience knows that Masetto was never mute.

But notice how much depends on the storytellers' assumptions. Filostrato begins by mocking the belief that religious clothing can make human desire disappear. That is part of the story's argument: a nun has not ceased to be a human being because she has entered a convent. At the same time, the social arrangements that make everyone believe the gardener cannot speak also make it harder to recognize his ability to refuse. Masetto has engineered the deception, yet he must eventually declare a limit to the demands placed upon him. A comic fantasy of unlimited opportunity turns into a negotiation over labor and bodily capacity.

The joke also turns on class. The young man is valuable because he can do difficult outdoor work, but he is admitted only when those above him regard him as safely powerless. Silence makes him seem manageable. He uses that imposed expectation as cover. The story is not a report on convent life; it is an anticlerical novella that invites an audience to doubt the perfect correspondence between religious profession and human behavior. Within the day's theme, Masetto gains what he desires, but the way he acquires it leaves the convent's celebrated holiness standing on a fabricated account.
