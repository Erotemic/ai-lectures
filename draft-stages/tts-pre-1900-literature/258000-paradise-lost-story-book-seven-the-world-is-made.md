# Paradise Lost — Book Seven: The World Is Made

We return from the violence of angelic rebellion to the question of how the world began. Milton pauses for another invocation, this time addressing Urania, a name from classical tradition that he redirects toward a heavenly source of song. He asks to be kept from false ambition as he narrates events no human observer witnessed. The prayer quietly echoes a theme of the story. The poet wants knowledge and greatness, but asks not to be destroyed by the pride that made Satan turn desire into disobedience.

Adam asks Raphael how the universe came to be. He has heard of Heaven's ancient conflict and now wants to understand the world in which that conflict can reach him. Raphael agrees, while warning that knowledge can become a temptation when we seek it without regard for what we can use rightly. The account begins with God choosing to create a new order in the realm of Chaos. The Son carries out the creative command. Divine speech moves out into the formless region and gives shape to a world.

The first day brings light and the division of light from darkness. The second distinguishes the waters and forms the expanse of the heavens. The third brings dry land and the growth of vegetation. On the fourth, lights are established in the heavens to mark days and seasons. The fifth fills water and air with living creatures. The sixth brings animals of the land and, finally, human beings. Raphael's outline follows the structure of Genesis, but Milton gives the stages the scale and texture of epic description.

The process is not simply the manufacture of useful objects for human possession. There are expanses, seas, mountains, creatures that swim and fly, and a fertility that seems to exceed any immediate need. The poem finds meaning in order and variety together. The newly separated elements do not cease to be active, and living things do not become monotonous just because they share a Creator. If the rebel angels tried to convert the resources of Hell into a monument to their own power, creation is presented here as a gift that can be enjoyed without claiming authorship of it.

At the creation of humanity, Adam and Eve receive their special place within the world, made in the image of God and entrusted with life in the garden. They are given companionship and work, and also the single prohibition that will test their trust. The command is part of their freedom rather than a magical property of a fruit that could otherwise make obedience unnecessary. They know that they are not the ultimate source of the world's law.

The account does not settle every scientific question, nor does Raphael pretend that an ancient poem supplies a modern physics. Milton's cosmos speaks through a seventeenth-century imagination using biblical and classical vocabularies. Light, motion, matter, time, the Sun and stars occupy the poem, but their role is to ask what a created and intelligible order means for creatures able to question it.

The heavenly host celebrates the completion of creation. The contrast with Satan's host is again instructive. In Hell, an assembly applauded a plan to diminish the good that others enjoy. Here the angels rejoice because something other than themselves has been brought into being. Milton has described a cosmos whose beauty does not depend upon becoming the prize of a victor. Adam's question has been answered as far as Raphael judges helpful; the next subject is how human beings themselves first encountered that beautiful world.
