# Great Expectations — Law, Crime, and Transportation

Great Expectations opens with a criminal terrifying a child and closes a major part of its action with the same man dying under a sentence of the law. Between those events the book asks how society produces the figure it calls a criminal. Abel Magwitch is not declared innocent of every act in his life. He is capable of threats and participated in crimes. The harder question is whether the criminal identity assigned to him explains the person. Dickens answers by showing the difference between legal classification, the performance of respectability, and the daily exercise of loyalty.

On the marshes Pip sees leg irons, soldiers, and a hunt. Magwitch is hungry enough that food becomes an immediate preoccupation, and frightened enough that he threatens the person who can supply it. He helps conceal Pip's theft when they are caught. These scenes do not turn the convict into a gentle hero. They place contrary actions together. A man who has frightened a child can also try to protect him. A child can steal from a loving home because the official enemy looks even more dangerous. The relation resists a simple division between good citizens and bad criminals.

Magwitch's account of his prosecution introduces a second division: the courtroom can see a man's clothes and manner more readily than the whole history of his actions. Compeyson has the speech and dress of a respectable person. Magwitch looks and sounds poor. Their punishments are unequal. The novel stages this as a particular injustice, not as proof that every court always behaves in the same way. The point is that the adversarial appearance of evidence is not sealed off from class perception. People already carry expectations about who is capable of dishonesty. The formally innocent-looking man may be the more calculating offender.

Transportation is indispensable to the plot. Convicted people were sent from Britain to penal settlements overseas, including Australia. Magwitch serves his sentence and eventually acquires money there, but his status as a transported convict remains dangerous when he returns to England without permission. The fortune Pip spends in London is the outcome of Magwitch's labor outside Britain. That means Pip's cultivated domestic life is financed through a penal and imperial geography he barely understands. We should not reduce Australia to a metaphor for exile; it is a real location tied to the British state's movement of labor and prisoners.

Magwitch's wealth also changes what the law appears to do. Jaggers can manage money and keep secrets within the bounds of his profession. Pip can live comfortably while never asking what produced the allowance. But the person who earned the money is denied the right to stand beside the person for whom he earned it. When Magwitch returns, law and affection come into direct conflict. Helping him evade capture creates risk for Pip and Herbert. The novel does not make the decision easy by pretending the law is irrelevant. It asks whether an orderly society's prohibitions can become ethically inadequate in particular human circumstances.

Jaggers and Wemmick make the legal world more complex. Jaggers defends accused people and commands a respect that approaches fear. Wemmick's office version of himself insists on obtaining portable property, keeping personal feeling out of transactions, and securing practical advantage. Yet at home he takes care of his father and enjoys unprofitable tenderness. The division between public system and private life is both useful and suspect. It lets professionals function, but it can also help them forget how decisions at a desk enter someone else's body and future.

By the river, Magwitch's attempt to escape makes the abstract sentence concrete again. The men row, watch the banks, and plan around tides and schedules. A struggle with Compeyson leads to capture. Magwitch's property will be forfeited, and his health is already failing. Pip's final gift is not a legal argument that could reverse the verdict. It is sustained presence at the bedside. He recognizes that the person the court has condemned is also the person who loved him most faithfully from afar. That recognition does not restore the fortune or undo transportation. It marks a moral relation the law cannot fully describe.

If we use the novel to speak about Victorian justice, we should keep evidence and inference separate. A fictional trial does not document the frequency of sentencing disparities. Dickens's artistic arrangement, however, shows what sorts of questions readers were being invited to ask: who gets believed, what class markers disguise, how empire is used to remove unwanted people, and whether punishment measures a whole human life. The greatest expectation exposed here may be the expectation that a respectable appearance tells the truth about a person's worth. The legal plot gives that illusion its most devastating test.
