# The Decameron — King Agilulf and the Groom

King Agilulf of the Lombards rules from Pavia. One of the servants who tends the royal horses falls in love with the queen, Theodelinda. The difference in rank between them is enormous, and he cannot approach her openly. He watches instead. He notices how the king dresses and how he passes from his own room to hers at night, carrying a torch and a staff. The groom studies the little signals by which the royal household recognizes authority.

He arranges a similar cloak, staff, and light. When night comes, he imitates the king's silent journey to the queen's chamber. A sleepy attendant admits him. The queen believes her visitor is her husband; the groom takes advantage of that mistake and returns to his lodging before anyone raises an alarm. Shortly afterward the real king visits. The queen's surprise at his second appearance gives him reason to suspect that somebody came before him in his name.

Agilulf is angry, but he reasons that a public accusation would disgrace the queen and spread a story he cannot undo. He does not believe she knowingly betrayed him. Instead he investigates with extraordinary care. Entering the servants' sleeping quarters, he finds the man whose heart is beating faster than his fellows' and secretly cuts a patch of hair from his head. The mark will allow the king's officers to identify him later without a scene.

But the groom wakes enough to understand what has happened. He takes a pair of shears and cuts the hair of the other sleeping servants in the same way. When the king returns with his officers, he sees a room full of men bearing the same mark. To seize all of them would be to acknowledge that his private discovery has failed and invite the scandal he wished to prevent. He chooses a different remedy: a guarded rebuke to the entire group, making clear that the offense is known and must not be repeated.

Pampinea's tale is built from two almost wordless scenes. First the groom borrows the king's bodily gestures and gets through a door. Then the king tries to turn a bodily sign into proof of an individual crime. The groom defeats the sign by making it common. In neither scene does rank operate by abstract command. It works by repeated habits, recognizable clothing, servants who know when to admit a visitor, and the confidence with which a ruler expects a mark to identify his subject.

The result may be called a triumph of quick thinking, but we should not mistake its circumstances. The queen agreed to receive her husband; she did not knowingly agree to receive the groom. His impersonation violates that distinction. Boccaccio's teller emphasizes the groom's resourcefulness and Agilulf's restraint, not the queen's point of view. The story is therefore a useful case for asking who counts when we praise a clever plan. One person's successful disguise is another person's loss of knowledge about what is happening.

The king, for his part, makes an illuminating calculation. He might punish the whole household to maintain an appearance of absolute authority. He instead recognizes the limits of his evidence and the damage that publicity could cause. The tale ends with an uneasy balance: a servant has escaped detection, a king has avoided an open scandal, and the queen's mistaken encounter remains unaddressed by those who know what really occurred. Prudence and justice have not proved identical.
