# The Fillet That Would Not Come Off

Xuanzang sat beside the road with his horse and packs while he rehearsed Guanyin's words. The formula was not a prayer he had studied at his monastery. It had been delivered by a bodhisattva who knew precisely how violent his new companion could be. Every repetition carried the promise of restraint, and also the possibility of pain.

Soon Wukong returned. He had been gone only a short time by the measure of someone who could cross the sea in a leap, but for the monk the abandonment had been bitter. The monkey greeted him with cheerful familiarity. He had been to visit the Dragon King, he said, and he had come back to serve. Xuanzang was wary, but he could not conceal his relief.

The two prepared to eat. Wukong opened the bundles, searching for the rough bread given them earlier. Among the ordinary provisions he found a bright cotton robe and a cap embroidered with golden flowers. Such handsome garments seemed out of place beside the plain clothes of a mendicant. He asked where they had come from.

Xuanzang answered with an invention. They had belonged to him when he was young, he said. The cap had a special virtue: a person who wore it would learn the scriptures with remarkable ease. The robe would help its wearer perform religious observances. Wukong, who had spent years acquiring powers but had little patience for humble religious practice, was tempted. He asked to try them on.

The garment fitted. The cap fitted too. The monk looked at his disciple, then began reciting the formula he had learned by the roadside.

Wukong cried out. The pain was sudden and fierce, as though something were squeezing his head from every side. He pulled the cap apart in his struggle. Beneath the flowers he felt a narrow golden band against his skin, tighter than any ordinary circlet. The band would not move. He tugged and twisted. It seemed to have become part of him.

Xuanzang was alarmed by what he had caused, yet when Wukong reached for the staff in his ear and tried to prise the band loose, the monk recited the formula again. The pain forced the monkey to the ground. He rolled and twisted, begging the monk to stop. As soon as the words ceased the agony vanished, but the fillet remained.

Wukong asked whether his master was doing this to him. Xuanzang denied casting a curse; he was reciting a sacred text. If the text did nothing, Wukong replied, perhaps he should recite it again. Xuanzang obliged. The monkey instantly cried for mercy. He could now be made to suffer by words spoken softly from the back of a horse.

The master demanded obedience. Wukong said he would listen. Xuanzang asked whether he would again behave with such violence toward his teacher. Wukong promised he would not. Yet even while promising, the old anger returned. He guessed that the old woman had been Guanyin and declared that he would fly to the southern sea and demand to know why she had done such a thing to him. Xuanzang pointed out that the bodhisattva who supplied the formula would hardly be helpless against it. Wukong thought better of going.

For one dangerous moment he turned his fury toward the monk himself. His staff grew in his hand. Xuanzang began the words, and the monkey dropped it. He could not attack the person holding the spell without first enduring a pain that drove every other thought from him. Finally he knelt and asked Xuanzang not to use it constantly. He would remain with him and guard the journey; he would not abandon him again.

Xuanzang told him to help with the horse and the packs. Wukong got up, straightened the robe, adjusted the tiger skin around his waist, and returned to the work of a disciple. They moved west.

There was no simple reconciliation in this new arrangement. The monk had refused to accept the killing of the highwaymen as righteous merely because they were robbers. The monkey had refused to accept criticism from a weaker person. Guanyin's device settled the immediate question of who could compel whom. It did not settle whether violence could be transformed into protection, or whether restraint through suffering would teach wisdom rather than resentment. Those questions would accompany the pair far beyond this mountain road.

The once-unruly Great Sage now walked beside the horse with a ring fixed around his head. His master carried no sword and commanded no army, yet he held words that could stop Wukong where heavenly soldiers had failed. The relationship that would define much of the pilgrimage had begun with rescue, continued through terror, and become a bargain of service enforced by a spell.

They had to make the bargain work. The west lay ahead, and the road had scarcely begun.

## Non-spoken source and editorial notes

Primary passage: selected Chinese chapter 14, Wukong's return, the gifted clothing and cap, the tightening-fillet mantra and the final renewed commitment to protect the pilgrim. The story makes Xuanzang's deception and Wukong's suffering explicit; those should not be hidden behind a simple 'tamed monkey' summary. The concluding discussion is an interpretation rooted in the narrated conflict. Source: `cl-jtw-ch14`.
