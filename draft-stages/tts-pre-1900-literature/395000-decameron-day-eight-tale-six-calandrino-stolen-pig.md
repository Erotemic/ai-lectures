# The Decameron — Calandrino and the Stolen Pig

Calandrino, the painter who believed he had become invisible by collecting stones from the Mugnone, has saved a pig for his household. Bruno and Buffalmacco, his companions in the earlier joke, learn about it and decide they would prefer to possess the animal themselves. They steal and slaughter the pig. Then, instead of letting Calandrino endure an unexplained loss, they return to offer their expertise in discovering the thief. His trust in these two men has survived the affair of the heliotrope.

Calandrino is distraught. The loss has obvious practical cost: the meat of a pig is a household resource, not an ornamental trinket. Bruno and Buffalmacco advise a test they present as capable of identifying a thief. Each suspect will take a specially prepared morsel of sweet food, and the guilty person will be unable to swallow it. It sounds like a procedure in which truth reveals itself involuntarily through the body. The tricksters take responsibility for supplying and distributing the morsels.

They control the test completely. Calandrino's portion is made bitter and almost impossible to eat, while the other portions are harmless. When Calandrino struggles, the false experiment seems to have produced a decisive result. His own companions now accuse him of stealing his own pig, perhaps to conceal it from his wife and use it elsewhere. They threaten to expose him at home unless he pays for their silence. Calandrino gives them capons, suffering a second loss on top of the first.

The trick has a particularly cruel arrangement. The thieves are the investigators. They plant both the evidence and its interpretation, then charge the victim for relief from an accusation they know to be false. Calandrino is made a suspect in the loss he came to them to explain. As in the heliotrope story, the fraud succeeds because he wants to believe that a simple external test can solve a confusing problem. This time the imagined marvel is not invisibility but a machine for reading guilt.

Filomena's audience is invited to laugh, yet the plot raises a question that the laughter cannot settle: what could Calandrino have done differently? He has chosen unreliable friends, but the false proof is imposed on him by people who know the truth and control the procedure. He lacks the knowledge necessary to test the test. The reader can see the invention before the victim does; that is why the joke makes sense to us and not to him.

The repeated characters give the day continuity. Calandrino does not become wiser after the first humiliation, while Bruno and Buffalmacco refine their method. Their cruelty is inventive, and Boccaccio's fictional Florence offers no guaranteed correction for it. In this story, unlike a witty retort that resolves a misunderstanding, cleverness is the deliberate creation of an injustice. The pig is stolen twice in a sense: first as meat, then as the occasion for extracting still more food from the person who lost it.
