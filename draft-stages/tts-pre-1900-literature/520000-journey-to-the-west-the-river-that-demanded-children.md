# Journey to the West: The river that demanded children

## The king's second decision

In Chechi Kingdom the three royal teachers were dead, and their king was grieving. From the point of view of Sun Wukong, the matter was simple. The tiger, deer, and goat had disguised themselves as great religious teachers, taken advantage of a time of drought, and persuaded a ruler to make Buddhist monks into slaves. Their magic had been real; their counsel had been disastrous. The king had watched them lose each contest and reveal their animal forms.

But rulers do not rearrange their beliefs as quickly as a monkey rearranges the contents of a covered box. The king had trusted his masters for many years. Their deaths were more than a public embarrassment. They left him mourning the men he thought had saved his country, while the foreign monk before him asked for travel papers and freedom for those his government had persecuted.

Wukong spoke to him without pretending that one religious tradition should now take revenge upon another. Buddhist monks and Daoist priests, he said, should each be permitted to cultivate their teachings. The old learning of the sages, the scriptures of the Buddha, and the way of the Dao need not become instruments for hunting one another. A kingdom should not be ruined by a ruler's decision to honor one path by destroying all the others.

It was a striking request from the same monkey who had just brought three religious masters to violent deaths. He had learned to distinguish the professions of faith from the creatures who used them as a weapon. The king could not restore his lost teachers, but he could undo their decrees.

He ordered the enslaved monks released. Five hundred men who had been dragged through months and years of forced labor were once again permitted to live as monks. Their temples would no longer be outlawed. Officials would cease treating shaved heads as opportunities for promotion. The king received the travelers respectfully, arranged the necessary endorsements of Xuanzang's passport, and sent them onward with gifts and provisions.

The pilgrims had not become ordinary visitors. They remained a band of strangers whose arrival had overturned the kingdom's laws. Yet when they passed out of the city, the men who had hauled heavy carts under armed supervision could walk through the gates without fear of being seized.

Autumn lay ahead. The days grew cooler. The road turned from inhabited plains to wide reaches of water and low banks where reeds bent in the wind. The company traveled until a great river filled the world before them.

## Eight hundred li of water

The river was called the Heaven-Reaching River. It stretched so far from bank to bank that no opposite shore could easily be made out. In the old measures of the country, people called it eight hundred li wide. Whether a traveler could measure every one of those li was beside the point. Looking across, he saw water where a road ought to be, waves where distant hills ought to stand, and clouds above both.

Xuanzang stood at the edge with his horse and considered how they could cross. It was too wide to swim. Even Wukong's leaps could not simply be shared with three companions and a horse. Their best course was to find a village, a boat, or someone who understood the river.

Along the near bank they found a cluster of houses belonging to a large family of the surname Chen. The family received them with a warmth that made the travelers hopeful. Beds were arranged and a meal set out. The elders of the household were prosperous people, able to feed guests and maintain buildings of their own. But at the very moment when they should have been glad of the company, grief kept intruding upon their hospitality.

The elder Chen brothers spoke together in low voices. Women of the house came and went with wet eyes. Children were kept close. The halls were being prepared as if for a funeral, yet no one had died. At first Bajie thought there had been some private quarrel about inheritance. Wukong heard too many sighs for that. He asked directly what burden pressed upon their hosts.

The Chen brothers explained that the village lived under a yearly demand imposed by the spirit who ruled the river. The villagers called him the Great King of Numinous Response. His shrine stood near the water, and the people believed that he controlled the rains and the fertility of their fields. If they honored him, crops could grow and boats could travel. If they failed, the river or the weather would turn against them.

The cost of his blessing was terrible. Each year the spirit required a boy and a girl to be brought to his shrine and offered as food. Their families were chosen by a rule that spared no household forever. The rich could not merely pay with silver. The children had to come.

This year the demand had fallen upon the very family that was sheltering the pilgrims.

The boy was seven years old, a child called Chen Guanbao. His sister was eight, and her family had given her a cherished name, A Scale of Gold. They were still small enough to be frightened by the dark corners of a house, to ask for food and play with whatever toys were at hand. Yet the great hall was being dressed for their farewell. Their parents had already begun mourning them while they were alive.

Xuanzang was appalled. He had left China to obtain scriptures so that people might be freed from suffering. Now he had reached a river where every year's harvest seemed to demand the death of two children. He could hardly offer consolation to a mother whose children were to be carried away that night.

Bajie said what frightened him most. What if the villagers had reason to fear this spirit? A monster able to command a river might be too strong for a party that could not even find a boat across it. Wukong looked at him. A man with a rake who had helped defeat demons across half the world ought to be able to think of something more useful than surrender.

## The price no one could exchange

Wukong first asked whether the family might send other children as substitutes. It was a cruel suggestion, and the Chen elders answered it at once. They could not put another family's son or daughter in place of their own, even if one could be found. The spirit knew the children who were promised. He had demanded this particular pair. He knew the households, the names, and the dates. The villagers had no reason to suppose they could fool him with a stranger.

But Wukong did not mean to send an innocent stranger. He meant to send himself.

He proposed to take the boy's shape and go to the shrine in his place. Bajie would become the girl. They would wait on the sacrificial platters until the Great King came to eat. Then, at the nearest possible moment, they would deliver a meal the spirit would remember.

The Chen household was stunned. Their guests had offered to take the children's places, but the disguise sounded impossible. How could a monkey with a staff look like a seven-year-old boy? Wukong told them to bring the boy forward. He examined his face, his clothes, and the way he stood. Then he shook himself and became so like Guanbao that the parents could scarcely tell which child was theirs.

He spoke in the boy's voice. He moved his hands and head as the boy moved them. The resemblance was enough to make the elders step backward. They looked from their own son to the visitor who had become his twin and knew that for the first time in many years a way out of the tribute might exist.

Bajie was less enthusiastic. He was being asked not merely to face a monster but to impersonate a little girl. His broad belly, snout, and ears were troublesome even in an ordinary human disguise. How was he to arrange so much pig into so small a child?

Wukong reminded him that he too knew the art of transformation. Bajie tried. His body altered, his clothes became those of a girl, and his features grew smaller, but something in the shape did not obey. A great round belly remained. Wukong laughed at the sight. To the parents the supposed daughter looked as though she had swallowed the household's cooking pot.

Bajie snapped that the transformation was difficult. Wukong offered assistance, tugged and adjusted the shape by magic, and at length produced a second child sufficiently like the Chen daughter to be carried to the shrine. Bajie grumbled through the entire operation. He had expected the journey west to bring him a monastery meal and perhaps a little praise. Instead he was to be a sacrifice dressed in a child's clothes, while his elder brother enjoyed making jokes about his stomach.

The real children were removed to safety. Their parents held them close, still unable to believe that anything had changed. The villagers prepared the offerings according to custom. Food and ceremonial objects were arranged; the procession was gathered; the river shrine waited in the darkness.

## The feast carried to the altar

The hour of the tribute arrived. Men took up great red lacquer trays, the sort that might carry choice dishes to a banquet. Upon these trays sat the two apparent children, surrounded by the formal offerings the villagers had learned to present to their terrible patron. Wukong, disguised as Guanbao, waited with an expression of small-boy bewilderment. Bajie, in the form of the girl, tried not to spoil the illusion by moving as a soldier or muttering complaints in his own deep voice.

The village had repeated this procession year after year. People knew where to stand, when to bow, and what words to say. Such familiarity did not make the rite less dreadful. For the parents of the sacrificed children it meant that the community had learned to conduct a killing as orderly public business. For the rest of the villagers it meant that the fear of what the river might do had become stronger than the horror of what they themselves were doing.

Xuanzang stayed behind with Sha Wujing, unable to accompany the disguised children into the monster's hands. His disciples were undertaking the danger for a family they had only just met. The journey to the scriptures had brought them many battles, but this one had begun with an ordinary domestic grief: a house in which two children were to be taken from their parents while everyone else watched.

Wukong had accepted the father's gratitude but had not promised an easy victory. The spirit's power was still unknown, and the pilgrims did not yet know the shape of the creature they would meet. They knew only that the tribute would draw him out.

At the shrine the platters were placed before the sacred image. Incense burned. The villagers performed the motions they had performed so often before, then withdrew. They had left what the Great King demanded. This time, without knowing it, they had left someone who might challenge his demand.

Inside the shrine, under the dim light, the two small figures sat upon their lacquer trays. Bajie was hungry enough to resent the untouched offerings around him and frightened enough to listen for every sound outside. Wukong kept his attention on the door. Somewhere in the river a being had grown accustomed to receiving children without resistance. Tonight it would come to find a monkey and a pig waiting in their places.
