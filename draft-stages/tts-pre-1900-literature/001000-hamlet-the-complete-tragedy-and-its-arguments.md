# Hamlet: the complete tragedy and its arguments

## Hamlet — Story: The Ghost and the Court

A soldier on the walls of a Danish castle calls into the darkness. Another voice demands that he identify himself. Even before a ghost appears, no one can be certain who is approaching. The castle is Elsinore. The old king is dead, his brother Claudius wears the crown, and Denmark is preparing for possible conflict with Norway. But the guards' most alarming news is not military. On two nights they have seen a figure that resembles the dead king, still wearing the armor of a warrior.

Horatio, a scholar and friend of the dead king's son, comes to witness the apparition. He begins as a skeptic, demanding evidence, but the figure appears before him too. It will not answer his questions. Its silence is almost more disturbing than a speech would be. Horatio decides the prince must be told. Hamlet has returned to court after his father's death. His mother, Gertrude, has already married his uncle Claudius. The new king addresses the court with practiced balance: grief for his brother, celebration of his marriage, and business about the Norwegian prince Fortinbras, whose family has its own grievances over lands lost in an earlier contest.

Hamlet hears that performance of stability from inside a grief that has not stabilized at all. Claudius asks him to stop mourning so visibly. Gertrude asks him to remain at court rather than return to his studies in Wittenberg. He agrees, but privately he is sickened by the speed of her remarriage and by the way the new arrangement makes his private pain seem inconvenient. He longs to escape the world; his words are not simply a plan to take his own life. They express the pressure of a world in which life itself has lost coherence.

In another family, the counselor Polonius prepares his son Laertes to leave for France. He gives Laertes a long display of worldly advice. Then Laertes and Polonius both warn Ophelia, Laertes' sister, against believing Hamlet's declarations of affection. The prince's choice of wife is not merely personal, they say: his rank exposes any promise he makes to the interests of Denmark. Polonius commands Ophelia to stop seeing him. No one asks with comparable care what she wants. Even before she is used as bait to test Hamlet, her family has defined her desires as an administrative problem.

Hamlet meets Horatio on the ramparts and watches the ghost arrive. The spirit beckons him away from the others. Despite their warnings, he follows. Away from witnesses, the ghost identifies itself as his father's spirit and offers a terrifying account of the king's death. The public explanation was that a serpent bit him while he slept in the orchard. According to the ghost, Claudius poured poison into his ear. The brother then acquired a crown and a queen. The ghost orders Hamlet to remember and avenge him, while telling him not to harm Gertrude and to leave her judgment to heaven and her conscience.

The instruction provides a plot but not a method. The ghost has told Hamlet something that feels like revelation, yet a being from beyond death is also a being he cannot examine as an ordinary witness. Hamlet promises revenge and makes his companions swear secrecy. He warns them that he may behave strangely from this point onward, adopting an appearance of disorder. An audience may think the ghost truthful; the prince still lives in a world where truth must be inferred from appearances, accusations and reactions.

Time passes. Ophelia tells her father that Hamlet appeared before her with his clothing in disarray and stared at her without speaking. Polonius concludes that thwarted love has driven him mad. He hurries to report his diagnosis to the king. Claudius and Gertrude have another project already in motion. They have invited Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, friends from Hamlet's youth, to discover what troubles him. The apparent gesture of friendship comes with a political assignment. The court grows crowded with people who are not allowed to speak openly about why they have come.

Hamlet recognizes that his old friends have been sent for. His conversation moves through jokes, philosophical observations and sudden turns of suspicion. Polonius approaches him with the confidence of a man who has solved a problem and finds himself mocked in wordplay he cannot entirely follow. Hamlet's speech can be cruel, dazzling and evasive within a single exchange. We know he has decided to feign disorder, but the play does not give us a clean gauge for separating every performance from genuine distress.

A company of traveling actors arrives. Hamlet's attention sharpens when a player delivers a passionate speech about the fall of Troy and the grief of Hecuba. The actor can summon convincing tears for a fictional event. Hamlet, who believes his father has been murdered, has spent his time talking and testing. He scolds himself for inaction. But an actor also suggests a new form of investigation. Hamlet asks the players to stage a murder resembling the ghost's story and plans to watch Claudius closely. Perhaps the king's body will reveal what his public language conceals.

At the end of this movement the prince has not killed anyone. He has gathered friends, enemies, performers and courtiers into a network of uncertain knowledge. What he has learned about the king comes from a ghost; what he hopes to learn next comes from a theatrical imitation of a murder. The old king's command to remember has made Hamlet an investigator. The danger is that the court treats every act of investigation as another form of threat.

## Hamlet — Story: The Play and the Closet

In the Danish court, privacy is becoming almost impossible. The king wants to know why the prince behaves so strangely. Polonius believes that Ophelia's refusal of Hamlet's affection explains everything. Together they arrange an encounter between Hamlet and Ophelia while they listen out of sight. The scheme requires her to return gifts and letters to someone she may still care about, while her father and the king use her reaction as evidence for a political diagnosis.

Before she reaches him, Hamlet speaks alone. He asks whether endurance or resistance offers a better response to human misery, and considers how fear of an unknown afterlife restrains people who might otherwise choose death. The famous speech does not deliver a philosophical system. It proceeds by comparison, hesitation and recoil. The question of what lies beyond death is immediate in a drama where a ghost has demanded an action that may bring death to everyone involved.

Ophelia approaches. She tries to return remembrances. Hamlet's answers alternately deny that he ever gave them and assault the possibility of love and marriage. He sends her away to a convent, a word that may sound different to different hearers and productions. Does he know he is being watched? The play gives actors room to suggest degrees of recognition and cruelty. What is visible is that a woman has been put in a dangerous place by authorities who then watch her suffer. After Hamlet leaves, Ophelia grieves for the brilliant, gracious person she believes she has lost.

Claudius is dissatisfied with Polonius's diagnosis. Whatever the source of Hamlet's behavior, the king fears a danger that cannot safely remain at court. He begins planning to send him to England. The contrast matters: Polonius treats Hamlet as a romantic problem; Claudius treats him as a possible threat to power. Both respond by arranging other people's lives.

Hamlet now turns to the players. He gives them detailed advice about performance: do not shout or overact, but do not drain passion from speech. The instruction is not a neutral interlude. Hamlet wants a representation persuasive enough to trouble an observer while ordinary enough not to advertise the trap. At court, the players enact a murder in which a ruler is poisoned and the murderer pursues the widow. Hamlet watches the king as much as he watches the stage. Claudius breaks off the performance and leaves. The court's composure fractures.

To Hamlet, the reaction confirms the ghost's accusation. Horatio, his trusted observer, has watched too. Yet a stage audience can also notice the limits of the test. A man may react for more than one reason to a public performance that seems to accuse him. We do not have to rely on this inference alone for long: Shakespeare allows the audience to overhear Claudius speak privately. The king recognizes his crime and tries to pray. He cannot bring himself to surrender the crown, the queen, or the benefits of what he has done. His words expose the division between remorse and restitution.

Hamlet comes upon the kneeling king with an opportunity to kill him. He stops. He imagines that killing Claudius while he prays might send his soul to heaven, which Hamlet regards as too gentle a recompense for his father's death. His reasoning is morally chilling. To revenge a murder, he begins calculating not just the king's death but the condition of the king's soul. The audience knows something he does not: Claudius has said that he cannot truly pray. Hamlet's delay is therefore built on a mistaken reading of what is happening directly before him.

Meanwhile Gertrude is awaiting a conversation with her son. Polonius has hidden behind a hanging so he can overhear it. Hamlet confronts his mother over her marriage and its haste. She becomes frightened and calls for help. From behind the hanging comes another cry. Hamlet thrusts his weapon through the fabric and kills the hidden listener. The victim is Polonius, not Claudius. The prince who wanted perfect certainty about the ghost has killed a man without knowing who stood behind the screen.

The confrontation continues in the presence of Polonius's body. Hamlet sets before Gertrude contrasting images of the dead king and Claudius, urges her to examine her choices, and tries to shock her into moral recognition. At a crucial moment he sees the ghost again. Gertrude does not see it. The difference between their experiences revives the question of how a viewer decides what is real. The ghost reminds Hamlet of its purpose and tells him to attend to his mother's distress. Hamlet believes he is obeying a demand for justice; his mother sees a son speaking to empty space beside the body of a man he has killed.

The act ends with the court more dangerous than before. Hamlet has evidence that Claudius is guilty, but he has not exposed that guilt in a form that can establish public justice. He has killed a counselor and made his own judgment suspect. Claudius now has an additional reason to remove him. Ophelia has lost her father and has been injured by Hamlet's words. Each attempt to discover the truth has widened the distance between knowing and doing, and the cost is no longer paid only by the person who began the investigation.

## Hamlet — Story: The Return and the Deaths

Polonius has been killed behind a curtain, and Claudius suddenly has a practical as well as a political crisis. Hamlet is dangerous to the court, and the court must answer for what happened to one of its most prominent servants. Claudius orders the prince to England, accompanied by Rosencrantz and Guildenstern. The journey appears diplomatic. In a sealed commission carried by the escorts, however, the king instructs England to have Hamlet executed. The machinery of government has become an instrument of private survival.

Hamlet refuses to disclose where he has hidden Polonius's body until he has made a succession of bitter jokes about decay. The jokes are not merely irreverent. In the prince's imagination kings, counselors and servants ultimately become matter indistinguishable in the ground. Claudius can control court titles while people are alive; he cannot command what death does to the bodies beneath them. That thought, however, does not restore the man Hamlet has just killed.

On the way toward his ship, Hamlet hears of an army led by Fortinbras, the Norwegian prince, marching to contest a small piece of territory. The spectacle of soldiers risking their lives for a claim of honor provokes another wave of self-reproach. Hamlet has a murdered father to avenge, yet others can act over something that appears trivial. The comparison does not prove that war is noble or that revenge is right. It reveals how much Hamlet still measures himself against models of masculine decision.

At Elsinore, Ophelia's grief has become public. She sings fragments of songs about lost love, betrayal, death and burial. Flowers and broken speech carry meanings no one at court can fully contain. Unlike Hamlet's planned display of madness, her disturbed behavior is not presented as a successful strategy for gathering knowledge. Her father is dead, her brother is away, and the prince she loved has been sent abroad after killing him. Gertrude and Claudius listen, but they cannot simply translate her suffering into a manageable report.

Laertes returns from France determined to avenge Polonius. His entrance resembles a distorted answer to Hamlet's story. He is ready to act without much hesitation, even when a crowd urges him toward rebellion. Claudius diverts his anger away from the throne and toward Hamlet. Then Gertrude reports that Ophelia has drowned. She describes a broken branch, water, drifting garments and a young woman singing as she sinks. The death takes place offstage; we hear an account, not Ophelia's own final intention. Later scenes raise the question of suicide and burial, but the play does not grant us direct access to what she understood or chose.

Hamlet, meanwhile, survives the voyage. He discovers the commission for his execution, substitutes an order that his escorts be killed, and is returned toward Denmark after an encounter with pirates. He explains these events in a letter and later to Horatio. His extraordinary escape combines alertness, improvisation and lethal responsibility for two men he once knew as friends. Whatever verdict we reach on their complicity, the prince no longer resembles a person incapable of action.

Back in Denmark Hamlet and Horatio encounter grave diggers working beside an open pit. The conversation turns on law, class and what counts as a voluntary death. One gravedigger jokes about how a person of rank may receive burial honors that others would be denied. He tosses skulls out of the earth while Hamlet imagines the lives they once held. One belongs to Yorick, a jester Hamlet knew as a child. A familiar face becomes a set of bones. The prince's reflections move from intimate memory to the physical fate of conquerors and emperors. No rank escapes that destination.

A funeral approaches. Hamlet discovers the body is Ophelia's. Her brother Laertes leaps into the grave in grief, and Hamlet confronts him. Each claims a special love for the dead woman; neither can ask her to confirm the claim. The funeral itself becomes a struggle for expression and precedence. Behind that scene Claudius has already planned to turn Laertes's anger into a second murder plot.

They arrange a fencing contest between Laertes and Hamlet. One weapon will carry poison. Claudius also prepares poisoned wine in case the blade fails. Hamlet accepts the match despite a foreboding he shares with Horatio. In the contest the men exchange hits. Gertrude drinks from the poisoned cup despite Claudius's attempt to stop her. Laertes wounds Hamlet with the poisoned blade, and a struggle causes the weapons to be exchanged. Hamlet wounds Laertes in turn. As Gertrude collapses, Laertes reveals the conspiracy. Hamlet finally attacks and kills Claudius. The revenge demanded by the ghost arrives only when the plan to kill Hamlet has already destroyed Gertrude and Laertes too.

With the poison working, Hamlet knows he will die. He stops Horatio from following him in death and charges him to tell the story. Fortinbras enters to find the royal household ruined. He takes political control of a scene whose central families have destroyed one another. Hamlet gives Fortinbras his dying voice, but the future government remains more a claim than an accomplished peace. Horatio remains to speak. The play ends not with the restoration of the dead father or a satisfying victory over evil, but with the survivor's duty to narrate a catastrophe in which every explanation has carried a cost.

## Hamlet — Revenge, Conscience, and Uncertain Knowledge

Imagine that a trusted person tells you a ruler has committed murder and that you are obliged to punish him. Now remove the usual guarantees. The trusted person is dead. You cannot summon witnesses to the original crime. The ordinary court is controlled by the person accused. The accusation may be true, but the form in which it arrives makes verification difficult. This is not every problem in Hamlet, but it explains why the play is much more than a story about a man who cannot make up his mind.

Revenge tragedy was a familiar theatrical kind by Shakespeare's time. It could offer a ghost demanding vengeance, secret crimes, apparent madness, elaborate traps and a final display of bodies. A major predecessor was Thomas Kyd's Spanish Tragedy. Shakespeare takes these components and makes the avenger's mind itself a contested place. His protagonist is not merely waiting for an opportunity. He is trying to establish whether an act of revenge can be understood as justice when the institution that should judge the accusation is under the accused man's control.

The ghost's command creates two tasks which are not identical: remember the father and punish the murderer. Remembering can mean preserving a person's identity against a court eager to move on. Punishment requires a conclusion about guilt, an appropriate procedure and a decision about what violence might count as legitimate. Hamlet repeatedly collapses those tasks together. He measures how well he remembers by how quickly he acts, and then finds that acting without understanding produces further injury.

His response to the theatrical performance illustrates both intelligence and weakness. He devises an experiment: present Claudius with a representation of his alleged crime and observe his behavior. It is an inspired use of the resources available to him. But what exactly does it test? A startled interruption of a play is not a sworn confession. The audience later receives stronger evidence from Claudius himself, who acknowledges the murder while struggling to pray. Hamlet misses that confession. Shakespeare lets the spectator know a crucial fact while the hero remains dependent on inference.

The prayer scene turns the problem inside out. Hamlet sees a chance to kill Claudius and refuses, reasoning that a praying murderer might be saved spiritually. This is not simply prudent hesitation or pure moral restraint. The prince wants an outcome more terrible than death. He thinks he can choose the conditions of another person's afterlife. His religious calculation exposes the transformation of a son seeking justice into a man aspiring to administer cosmic punishment. In the same moment he fails to know whether the king has sincerely repented. Claudius has just told us he cannot. An apparently careful judgment rests on a false premise.

A few minutes later Hamlet kills Polonius through a curtain. Here he makes the opposite error. He acts before verifying the identity of the person behind the fabric. A reader who calls Hamlet merely indecisive must account for this impulsive murder and for the quick alteration of the death warrant on the journey to England. A better question is not whether he can act, but what conditions provoke action or restraint, and whether those conditions are ethically defensible.

The prince's language about thought can mislead us too. He repeatedly declares that thought has weakened his capacity to act. Yet he also speaks with the self-dramatizing energy of someone comparing himself to an ideal of decisive masculinity. Fortinbras's campaign becomes a reproach because soldiers risk their lives for a small territory. We need not accept that model. A war fought for honor may itself be a demonstration of destructive reasoning. Hamlet turns other men's willingness to die into evidence against himself while the play invites us to see the waste of that willingness.

Laertes is another mirror. On learning of his father's death he races home ready to overthrow the king. Claudius redirects him toward a conspiracy. Speed alone does not protect him against manipulation; his apparent decisiveness leads to the poisoned duel in which he dies. Fortinbras arrives alive because his military and diplomatic position is different, not necessarily because he embodies one moral answer to Hamlet's doubts. The play distributes different forms of action among three sons whose fathers have been violently wronged.

A close reader can test this interpretation by following what each character knows in each scene. Ask who has heard the ghost, who has seen the players, who has heard Claudius pray, who knows the contents of the letter to England, and who can explain the poisoned weapon. Then ask what Hamlet would do differently with access to another person's knowledge. The resulting pattern is not a moral diagram with a single lesson. It shows a political world in which observation is selective, secrecy is habitual, and righteous intention does not prevent harm.

The tragedy gives Hamlet no secure procedure for transforming private knowledge into public justice. But it does not excuse his choices as inevitable products of circumstance. He wounds Ophelia with speech, kills her father, redirects a death order toward former friends and helps bring about a lethal final contest. The pressure of uncertainty makes these acts understandable as drama without making them harmless as actions. One reason the play remains powerful is that it demands two kinds of attention at once: sympathy for a mind trapped inside doubt and responsibility for what that mind does to other people.

## Hamlet — Theatre, Acting, and Surveillance

In Hamlet, nearly everyone wants a performance from someone else. The new king wants the prince to behave like a loyal son and subject. Polonius wants Ophelia to enact a meeting that will disclose the cause of Hamlet's behavior. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern must play the part of friendly visitors while reporting to their hosts. Hamlet deliberately performs disorder, then enlists professional actors to stage an imitation of a murder. And an actual theatre audience watches all these staged encounters at once.

This layered structure is not an ornament added to a plot about vengeance. It is how the play thinks about knowledge. What does an expression prove? Can a person look grief-stricken without grieving? Can someone tell the truth while playing a role? And what happens when an observer decides that another person's behavior exists primarily to be interpreted?

The opening court scene gives us the first instance. Claudius describes a balance between mourning for his dead brother and celebrating his marriage to Gertrude. Politically, the speech is capable. Emotionally, it sounds hollow to Hamlet, who has no patience for courtly claims that grief has been properly managed. The prince insists that external signs cannot exhaust what he feels. Clothes, tears and gestures may accompany sorrow, but they can also be imitated. His distinction between inward feeling and outward show becomes the play's central problem, even as he soon chooses to make himself difficult to read by acting mad.

The supposed madness is never just a costume he can remove without consequences. Once his behavior is observed and described by others, they assign it causes. Polonius decides that Ophelia is the key. Claudius fears a political challenge. Gertrude sees something wrong that she hopes others can repair. Hamlet's private motive becomes a public object, and the prince begins to lose authority over what his own performance means. The gap between intention and interpretation belongs to theatre and ordinary life alike.

Consider the scene arranged for Ophelia. The king and Polonius give her a task, hide, and watch. That arrangement could be performed as a farce of eavesdropping, but the consequences are intimate and brutal. The investigation uses a person as an instrument. Hamlet may or may not detect the watchers; different productions can make his awareness more or less visible. The scene is therefore a test not only of a prince's sincerity, but also of the spectators' willingness to treat her humiliation as the price of obtaining information.

When the professional players arrive, Hamlet offers advice about acting. He warns against theatrical excess, wanting speech and gesture proportioned to the action. The passage is often quoted as Shakespeare's own theory of stage realism, though it belongs first to a character with immediate tactical needs. Hamlet wants his play to create the appearance of recognizable behavior without looking like an obvious accusation. His lesson about acting is also a lesson about the unreliable relationship between what an image resembles and what it establishes.

The performance within the play has several frames. We watch Shakespeare's actors portray Hamlet watching fictional actors portray a murder. Claudius watches too, but his act of leaving is itself watched and interpreted. The prince later treats this reaction as decisive. Yet the audience's position is not identical to his: we hear Claudius alone acknowledging guilt. Shakespeare distributes evidence unevenly, making the playgoer at once more knowledgeable and less powerful than Hamlet.

There is another kind of performance at the graveyard. The gravedigger speaks in riddles, songs and legal jokes while digging. Hamlet meets a person whose verbal agility is not attached to court status or scholarly training. The two use language to shift power back and forth. A skull, which cannot answer, interrupts all verbal performance with a physical fact. Yorick's identity once depended on a voice and gestures that made a child laugh. The prince now holds the remains of a performer who can no longer perform.

For a practical close reading, imagine directing the play-within-the-play twice. In one version Claudius leaves almost immediately after the staged poisoning, visibly shaken. In another he maintains composure until Hamlet's pointed commentary becomes intolerable. In the first, spectators may see an involuntary disclosure; in the second, they may see a response to a public attack. The written plot permits a framework, but performance supplies a hypothesis about why people behave as they do. A production is an interpretation made in bodies and timing.

This helps explain why Hamlet resists reduction to a single famous speech. The work is designed for characters who watch one another and for audiences who watch the watchers. The theatre does not merely represent uncertainty in words; it creates that uncertainty through perspective, concealment, entrance, silence, movement and gesture. We are forced to read behavior as evidence and then to ask whether that reading has become another trap.

## Hamlet — Ophelia, Gertrude, and the State

An account of Hamlet told only from the prince's thoughts can make the people around him look like symbols placed there to explain his pain. The play resists that convenience. Ophelia and Gertrude are both denied important information, both subjected to other people's judgments, and both participate in a court where private life is political. Their stories show what the avenger's perspective excludes.

Ophelia first appears in a family that wishes to instruct her. Laertes warns against Hamlet's courtship because the prince's rank limits the freedom of his choice. Polonius rejects her account of Hamlet's affection and orders her to cut contact. When she later returns letters and gifts, she is acting within a plan devised by two men watching in secret. Whatever the truth of Hamlet's feelings, she is not given a fair opportunity to negotiate them. Her father translates her emotional life into evidence for his theory of the prince.

Hamlet's language to Ophelia has competing possible motives. He might wish to protect her from marriage and from the corrupt court. He might suspect the observers and attack her as their agent. He might simply be cruel in anger and confusion. These possibilities need not be mutually exclusive. But it would be a mistake to describe the scene only in terms of his strategy. The words land on a woman who has already been made responsible for his behavior. She cannot control the experiment of which she is part.

After Polonius's death, Ophelia returns singing fragments that range across love, sexuality, betrayal and burial. The scene gives her speech a prominence she has rarely been allowed when others assigned her lines and duties. That does not mean her distress secretly frees her; she is suffering and vulnerable, and her songs are not a clear manifesto. Their fractured form nevertheless makes the court listen to material it has previously suppressed. She speaks about bodies, promises and loss in ways the official language of the palace cannot easily absorb.

Her drowning happens offstage. Gertrude describes a branch breaking and Ophelia entering the water. The narration is delicate and strange: she continues to sing until her clothes fill and pull her down. This is a reported event, and the later argument over the funeral shows the question of intention has not been settled. If we say simply that she chose suicide, we assert more than the visible action establishes. If we insist on an accident, we make the opposite leap. The play creates an ethical demand precisely because Ophelia's final perspective is unavailable.

Gertrude has a different kind of power and a different lack of it. She is queen, but her marriage is one of the foundations of Claudius's claim to continuity. Her desire and her public position cannot be separated. Hamlet often speaks as though her remarriage were evidence of a general failure in women. Yet the text does not give the audience direct certainty about what she knew of the old king's murder. Some productions imagine her as actively complicit, others as unaware. The printed scene in which Hamlet confronts her supplies strong evidence that she is disturbed by his accusations; it does not give a private confession of murder.

The bedroom confrontation also forces a question about moral coercion. Hamlet arrives to instruct his mother about the superiority of his dead father and the ugliness of her new marriage. His outrage is comprehensible as grief, but the rhetorical pressure is severe. When he kills Polonius behind the hanging, the scene changes from accusation into violence. Gertrude's fear is not an unreasonable misunderstanding of an entirely innocent son. She has just witnessed him stab a hidden man. When he sees the ghost and she does not, she has grounds to question whether his account of what is present can be trusted.

At the fencing match Gertrude drinks from the poisoned cup. Claudius knows its contents and tries to prevent her, but cannot publicly explain why. Whether a particular staging gives her an inkling of the conspiracy is a directorial question. Her death belongs to the logic of a court where a king's schemes are concealed even from those nearest him. The attempt to remove one danger destroys his wife as well.

Race, religion and nation are less prominent as explicit categories in this plot than the institutions of kinship, succession and marriage, but the state remains present throughout. Denmark's foreign policy runs alongside its domestic violence. The army of Fortinbras approaches while the royal family disintegrates. Claudius turns a diplomatic letter into a death warrant. Ophelia's grief helps provoke Laertes's revolt. A private injury repeatedly becomes a crisis of government.

An instructive way to study the play is to write a brief account of each scene without mentioning Hamlet by name. What does Ophelia do, and who prevents her from acting? What can Gertrude know? What does Laertes want before Claudius guides him? What does Horatio witness? Such a reading does not eliminate the protagonist. It restores the human costs that his story, told from inside his suffering, can make hard to see.

## Hamlet — Sources, Texts, and the Life of a Play

What is the text of Hamlet? The question sounds simple until we discover that the work reached print in notably different forms. A short quarto appeared in 1603. A much longer second quarto appeared with dates of 1604 or 1605 on surviving copies. The First Folio printed another version in 1623. These are not interchangeable copies of a single modern file. They differ in passages, placement and sometimes the words assigned to characters. Any confident sentence beginning with the claim that Shakespeare wrote one exact form of a disputed line needs to identify the evidence behind it.

The earliest quarto, often called Q1, is shorter and different enough to challenge the assumption that all versions represent simple corrections of one manuscript. The term bad quarto has traditionally been used for it, but that label embeds a judgment about the conditions of its production. The second quarto, Q2, supplies much of the wording familiar to modern readers. The First Folio, F1, contains passages absent from Q2 and excludes others found there. Modern editions often combine the two longer versions, and different editors make different choices. The Folger reading text provides a convenient integrated text while marking material unique to particular witnesses.

That complication is not merely a scholar's problem. Imagine an actor learning a soliloquy that differs between editions, or a director selecting a scene whose lines one printed witness does not include. The choice changes how an audience hears the character's hesitation or confidence. The text is a record of a theatrical work, and theatre is a practice of selection, pacing, voice and embodiment. A modern production cannot escape interpretation by simply claiming to present the original Hamlet exactly as it was.

Shakespeare did not invent the bare revenge story. A version of the Danish prince Amleth appears in Saxo Grammaticus's medieval historical narrative. François de Belleforest reshaped that material in French. An earlier English stage play, conventionally called the Ur-Hamlet, is documented but does not survive in a text that we can consult. Its precise authorship remains uncertain; association with Thomas Kyd is a scholarly hypothesis, not a recovered signature. The conventional outline already included a murdered father, a dangerous uncle and a son who masks his intentions. Shakespeare's achievement is not the invention of those events but the distinctive pressure he places on perception, conscience, language and performance.

The older revenge story needs a hero who will eventually kill his father's murderer. Shakespeare gives his hero reasons to doubt how knowledge comes to him and to question the meanings of action. The ghost demands recollection, but a ghost might be interpreted through competing religious and theatrical assumptions. The play within the play promises proof, but an observed reaction is not a simple transcript of inward guilt. The ending brings the desired death of Claudius, but only within an epidemic of killings that makes triumph an implausible response.

Consider just the first spoken question on the battlements: a guard challenges another voice to identify itself. A production can make that moment briskly functional or ominously sustained. In either case it establishes the act of recognition as a problem. The question repeats indirectly in every encounter with the ghost, in Hamlet's attempted reading of Claudius, in Polonius's misreading of Hamlet, and in the graveyard where a skull must be identified by someone who remembers its owner. One small stage action provides a model of how the play's larger questions emerge from the theatre itself.

Now consider the lines at the end. Hamlet dies; Horatio remains with a story to tell; Fortinbras arrives to view the catastrophe and command honors for the dead prince. An actor can place emphasis on Horatio's obligation to report, on Fortinbras's emerging authority, or on the appalling sight of the bodies. These choices alter whether the ending feels like a political handover, a funeral, a warning about succession, or all three. The script does not convert the ending into a single moral proposition.

The play's later influence is extensive, but influence is not a free-standing claim that Shakespeare invented inwardness or that modern psychology begins here. Earlier literature also explores complex minds, and European revenge drama had existing conventions. A more precise claim is that Hamlet made a particular configuration of self-questioning speech, theatrical spectatorship and delayed revenge available for repeated reinterpretation. It can be a political drama, a family tragedy, an inquiry into action, or a meditation on mortality without ceasing to be the same recognizable play.

To continue reading older literature with care, bring this practice to every famous work: identify the surviving text, the story traditions it revises, the conventions its first audiences might recognize, and the artistic decisions that make it something more than an inherited plot. Greatness is not established by treating a work as though it fell from the sky. Hamlet becomes richer when its history is allowed to remain visible.
