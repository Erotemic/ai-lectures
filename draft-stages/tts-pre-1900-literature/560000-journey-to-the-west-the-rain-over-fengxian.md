# Journey to the West: The rain over Fengxian

## The city with empty wells

After leaving the woodcutter and his mother, Xuanzang and his disciples followed the road west for several days. The country began to open. They were approaching the borders of Tianzhu, the wide realm in which the Buddha's mountain stood. Xuanzang saw a city ahead and asked whether they had reached their goal. Wukong told him not to confuse a market town with the Great Thunderclap Monastery. They still had to travel to the sacred mountain. The city, however, might offer food, water, and the proper record for passing through its jurisdiction.

They entered through the gates and found streets that should have been busy but looked almost deserted. Shops stood open without customers. The stones were dusty; the wells were low; the faces of the inhabitants were drawn. At the market square stood officials with a notice they were preparing to hang. Bajie called for people to make way, and those nearest turned in terror at the sight of his snout. The officials moved forward cautiously to ask who these frightening strangers were.

Xuanzang dismounted and introduced the company. They were travelers from the Great Tang, bound for the Buddha's scriptures. He apologized for the alarm caused by his disciples and asked the name of the place. The officials answered that this was Fengxian Prefecture, a district outside the heart of Tianzhu. For three years the country had suffered drought. Their lord had sent them to post an announcement seeking anyone able to bring rain.

Wukong asked to read it. The notice described the condition of the district more plainly than any courtly speech could have done. Wells and springs had dried. Fields gave no grain. The price of a little food had risen beyond the means of poor families. People sold possessions, then clothing, then things no household should have to sell. Children had been exchanged for food. Some fled to places where crops might still grow; others were too weak to leave. The prefect offered a great reward to whoever could save the people.

Xuanzang turned to his disciples. They had seen kings frightened, monsters defeated, and prison doors opened. Here there was no single captive to untie. The whole population needed water. If one of them could bring rain, he said, that would be an act of compassion as important as any battle. Wukong answered that calling wind and rain had been among his earliest tricks. He would not take the promised gold, but he would see what could be done for the people.

The officials hurried to their lord with the news. Prefect Shangguan came out in person rather than send a formal escort. He bowed to Xuanzang in the street, begged the visitors to help, and invited them into the official residence. There he provided food, which Bajie ate with the appetite of a man determined to recover every missed meal on the road. The prefect watched him with wonder. He had summoned holy men and received a monkey, a pig, and a sand-colored warrior; yet each showed more concern for his people than a hundred officials who had spent three years drafting petitions.

## The dragon who needed an order

After the meal the prefect spoke of the famine. Three years had passed without the rain necessary for planting. He could keep writing notices and appealing to travelers, but he could not command the sky. He offered gold and honor to any power that would send water to his fields. Wukong stopped him. Rain could not be purchased from the Great Sage, he said. If the request came from a wish to save lives and accumulate good deeds, it was worth answering. If it came from desire to display wealth, the offer was meaningless.

He asked Xuanzang to remain in the hall and recite prayers. Bajie and Sha Wujing came outside with him to assist. The prefect prepared incense. Wukong spoke a summoning charm, and a dark cloud rolled from the east. The East Sea Dragon King, Ao Guang, arrived in human form and bowed. He knew Wukong well, but he did not know why he had been summoned into a famine-stricken district.

"Look at this land," Wukong told him. "The people have had three years without rain. Can you not send your water over their fields?"

Ao Guang had the power to govern rain, but not the authority to dispense it wherever he pleased. The dragon could appear when called, yet the rain itself belonged to an ordered celestial administration. Without a decree from the Jade Emperor and the proper rain officers, he would be acting against command. He could return to the sea and assemble his attendants if Wukong obtained the order from Heaven. The explanation made the matter sound as though a river needed permission to flow.

Wukong did not like being told that a good deed required paperwork. But he had learned enough of celestial procedure to know the dragon was not inventing an excuse. He dismissed Ao Guang, told Xuanzang he must make a request in person, and leaped toward Heaven. The prefect watched him vanish and hurried to instruct the people to set out water vessels, willow branches, and incense for the promised rain. In the streets hope traveled faster than the monkey.

At the western gate of Heaven, the guards recognized Wukong. He explained that Fengxian was dying of thirst. One of them asked whether he knew why the sky had been closed to that particular district. There was a matter, the guard said, concerning the prefect and an offense committed against Heaven. Wukong pushed onward. If a thousand households were suffering, he wanted the reason stated plainly.

## Three impossible conditions

The Jade Emperor heard the petition but did not issue a rain order. Three years before, on the twenty-fifth day of the twelfth month, he had passed over Fengxian while observing the lower world. There he had seen Prefect Shangguan overturn offerings prepared for Heaven, throw the food to dogs, and speak insulting words. The emperor had responded by imposing a punishment that would persist until three conditions were fulfilled. Wukong could view them in the Hall of Fragrance.

He went with the celestial officials and saw a chamber arranged like a nightmare of slow arithmetic. There was a mountain of rice, immense enough to have fed a district. A tiny chicken pecked at it, taking one grain after another. Beside it stood an even greater mountain of flour, and a little golden-haired dog licked at it with a small tongue. On an iron stand hung a golden lock. Beneath the lock a lamp burned, its delicate flame touching the metal. The officials explained that rain would not fall on Fengxian until the chicken had eaten all the rice, the dog had licked up all the flour, and the flame had burned through the lock.

Wukong stared at the three tasks. A staff could crack a mountain, but these were not ordinary heaps of food and metal. They were emblems of an emperor's sentence. If he destroyed them by force, the decree would remain, and he would have made a new quarrel with Heaven while the drought continued below. He felt the weight of a punishment meant to outlast human lifetimes. The life of a child in Fengxian had become dependent upon a chicken finishing a mountain of rice.

The celestial officials saw his despair. One told him that the conditions were not as immovable as they seemed. A sincere change of heart among the people and their ruler could move Heaven more surely than a staff could. If the prefect returned to good conduct, honored what he had insulted, and the people joined him in a work of charity, the rice and flour might vanish and the lock break at once. Wukong had not come to win a battle with a chicken and a dog. He had come to find the terms on which an entire community could be relieved.

He returned to earth and called the prefect before him. Shangguan knelt as Wukong recounted the incident from three years earlier. The official's face changed. He admitted it. He and his wife had quarreled on a day when offerings had been arranged. In anger he overturned the table, threw the vegetarian food to dogs, and spoke as though the heavens themselves had offended him. He had regretted his conduct, he said, but had never imagined that the years of dry fields and starving children were bound to it.

Wukong described the rice mountain, the flour mountain, and the lock. Bajie immediately offered to go to Heaven and eat both mountains himself. That was a task, he said, at which he could guarantee progress. Wukong refused to take him. The obstacles were not merely edible objects. The prefect must change his conduct and inspire genuine good action. Xuanzang said that repentance required more than terror at punishment. A lord who wanted rain ought to attend to the living people who would receive it.

Shangguan bowed and promised to change. The residents were summoned, the ways of prayer and charity explained, and households began taking part in acts of reverence and mutual aid. The prefect no longer spoke of buying rain with gold. He asked how the suffering could be brought to an end. In a district where people had nearly stopped hoping, the promise of a common effort was itself a change.

## Thunder before rain

Wukong knew that a promise made in a court might vanish when the court's guests departed. He asked that messages of the new intention be carried to Heaven. Then he went to the residence of the thunder officials and sought their help. They were as exacting about orders as the dragon had been. Had the prefect's sentence been lifted? Could the clouds act yet? Wukong told them what he had seen in Fengxian and what he had urged the people to do. He did not ask the thunder officers to break the decree. He asked them to appear so that the people, who had not heard thunder for three years, would know that the sky had not forgotten them.

The thunder officials consented. They came with the spirits of lightning, and the air over Fengxian shook. Bolts illuminated the roofs; the sound rolled from hill to hill. People left their houses and fell to their knees. Some held willow branches; others raised incense; many whispered prayers they had feared would never again be heard. Children looked up at the flash of light and clung to their parents. There was still no rain, but there was the first sign of a change in the sky.

In Heaven the petitions arrived. Reports came that the prefect and the people had turned sincerely toward better conduct. The Jade Emperor ordered the three symbols inspected. The mountains of rice and flour had collapsed and vanished. The golden lock had broken. The conditions were fulfilled without a chicken eating more than a handful of grains or a flame waiting through endless years. The emperor authorized the rain officials to proceed to Fengxian at the appointed time with a measured downpour.

Wukong was still directing the thunder when the other departments arrived. Wind came through the streets. Clouds thickened, and the first drops struck the dust. Then rain fell heavily. Roof tiles rang with it. Dry channels filled. Courtyards darkened from gray to deep brown; the smell of wet earth rose between the houses. The storm spread across the fields and into the valleys. A community that had counted each cup of water now watched abundance run from its eaves. Children reached into it with both hands.

The rain came in the exact quantity commanded by Heaven. Wukong, never reluctant to make a public lesson from a successful enterprise, asked the spirits to remain visible for a time after the storm. He called the prefect and the townspeople together to thank them. The clouds parted enough for the divine officers of wind, cloud, thunder, and rain to be seen above the streets. The people bowed. Wukong urged them to remember that the life of the district depended not only on an occasional miraculous rescue but on the conduct of those who governed and lived within it.

## A temple called Universal Rain

The prefect wanted to reward the travelers. He offered gifts, a banquet, and land for a new monastery. Xuanzang told him that the company could not stay for many days. They had a journey to finish. But the people were eager to give thanks, and the officials insisted on marking the return of rain in a lasting way. There were meals and prayers, public ceremonies, and a building raised in memory of what had happened.

The new monastery was named Sweet Rain, Universal Relief Temple. Images of the rain and thunder officers were installed, and a shrine honored the four travelers who had brought help. Wukong admired the skill with which the prefect had organized its construction. Bajie enjoyed the food offered at each gathering. Sha quietly tended the horse and the loads, aware that the farther west they came the more every delay seemed to matter. Xuanzang looked over the new grounds and hoped the district would remember the famine as clearly as it celebrated the rain.

For about half a month the hosts kept the party among them, thanking them with an enthusiasm the monks could hardly refuse. At last the road prevailed. Xuanzang declined the farewell gifts. The prefect and his officers accompanied the pilgrims out of the city, and the townspeople followed for miles, waving until the horse and the three disciples dwindled along the western road.

The drought had ended. The rain had not proved that every punishment of Heaven was just, or that the hungry had deserved the years they endured. It had proved, within the story's world, that a ruler's wrongdoing could fall upon those who had no voice in it, and that repentance was meaningful only when life changed for the people whose suffering made it urgent. Wukong had gone to Heaven seeking a simple command to rain. He came back having made a district look toward the sky and toward its neighbors at the same time.

The travelers moved west again. The scriptures still waited beyond the cities and mountains of Tianzhu. There would be other kings and other creatures on the road. But for this chapter the parting image was not a prison or a battlefield. It was the sound of water running where the ground had been dry.
