# Journey to the West: The false Buddhas of the Lantern Festival

## A city lit for spring

Several days after leaving Jade Flower, the pilgrims came to another city, lively enough to make their recent battles seem far away. Markets filled the approaches to the gates. There were tea houses, oil shops, voices calling prices, and families carrying lanterns through streets that had been swept for the spring festival. Children ran to look at Bajie's long snout, and the adults followed at a safer distance. Xuanzang worried that his disciples would cause a disturbance, but for once the people seemed more curious than hostile.

They stopped at a monastery called Compassionate Cloud Temple, Ciyun Si. The monks welcomed the traveler from the Great Tang and provided vegetarian meals. The city was Jinping Prefecture, and the annual Lantern Festival was at hand. At the first full moon of the year the streets would blaze with lamps, colored paper, moving lantern wheels, embroidered curtains, and images made to shine like living creatures. The monks urged their visitors not to rush away before they saw it.

Xuanzang had spent years resisting entertainments that delayed the pilgrimage. Yet he was a monk who could find holiness in a lamp before a shrine as well as in a scripture. The hosts spoke of the festival as an act of devotion, not merely an excuse to stay awake in the streets. He agreed to remain for the full-moon celebrations.

During the days before the festival the company wandered through monastery grounds filled with trees and towers. Xuanzang made a vow to clean one of the temple towers himself. He climbed its levels with Sha Wujing, swept each floor, bowed before the images, and looked out from each window over the expanding city. The work was humble and repeated. He could not make rain with a command or split himself into a thousand fighters, but he could carry a broom through an old tower and leave each level cleaner than he found it.

Bajie preferred the guest meals and the prospect of festival food. Wukong enjoyed the crowds but watched them carefully, hearing in every extravagant promise of a blessing an opportunity for someone to be deceived. He did not yet know how much reason he had to be suspicious.

## Three lamps of precious oil

On the night of the fifteenth day, the monks went into the city together. There were bridge lanterns, animal-shaped lanterns, flower-shaped lamps, and tall frames that made the streets look like gardens of light. Wealthy families had hung embroidered screens from their balconies. Musicians played under the moon; people in fine clothes jostled beside laborers who had saved their coins for a festival treat. Children peered between grown legs to see everything they had been told was worth seeing. The night had the relaxed disorder of a celebration in which nobody wanted to be the first to go home.

A special attraction stood at the Golden Lamp Bridge. Three enormous lamps burned there, their basins filled with a costly fragrant oil. Local custom claimed that three Buddhas descended to receive the offering, and that their appearance brought blessings on the city. Xuanzang wanted to bow. Such an event, so near the lands associated with the Buddha's teaching, sounded like an honor he ought not to miss.

Wukong asked the monks where the oil came from. The cost was staggering. The prefecture had to raise an enormous quantity of silver each year to fill the lamps with fragrant oil that the visitors would take away. The burden passed from officials to wealthy households and from them, by rents and prices and taxes, to people who had not asked to pay for a vision of holiness. The festival was joyful; the hidden account behind it was not.

"It is remarkable," Wukong said, studying the lamps, "how much money a Buddha is supposed to need for oil."

The monks were offended by his tone. The three figures had appeared before; many people had seen them. Why would the city doubt what the witnesses agreed upon? Wukong answered that crowds could be deceived as easily as a single traveler. The greater the display, the more people were prepared to believe it must be true.

Midnight approached. The lamps burned steadily and a hush moved along the bridge. Above them the air shone. Three august shapes appeared as though seated among the clouds, their outlines carrying the promise of a sacred visitation. People cried out, knelt, and pressed their hands together. Xuanzang fell to his knees with them.

Wukong looked once and saw through the disguise. These were not Buddhas. They were beings using Buddhist appearances to steal the oil and whatever else could be taken from a credulous town. He called to Xuanzang not to bow, but his master was already absorbed in reverence. As the Great Sage rose to confront the apparitions, the three figures darkened the lights, raised a wind, and swept the monk from the bridge with the oil. The crowd, still kneeling, had barely understood what had happened.

## The mountain behind the miracle

Wukong flew after them. He could follow a trail of magical wind across miles of darkness, but the thieves had a head start and their mountain country was full of ravines. By dawn he had not found Xuanzang. The local celestial messengers told him the name of the place: Green Dragon Mountain, and on it Mysterious Flower Cave, Xuanying Dong. Three kings lived there. They had adopted the names Cold-Averting, Heat-Averting, and Dust-Averting, titles that suggested protection rather than predation.

Wukong challenged the cave. Its outer guards were bull-headed spirits, rough creatures carrying crude weapons. He called through the entrance for the stolen Tang monk. The three kings had already made plans to prepare Xuanzang for a meal cooked with the fragrant oil. When the report of Wukong's arrival reached them, they delayed the cooking and had the prisoner brought out for questioning.

Xuanzang was frightened and still bewildered. He explained why he had bowed beneath the lights. He had traveled from the Great Tang to seek scriptures, he said, and had mistaken their visible shapes for genuine Buddhas. Asked who traveled with him, he named Sun Wukong, once the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, Zhu Bajie, once Marshal Tianpeng, and Sha Wujing, once the Curtain-Raising General. The demons were not ignorant of those names. They looked uneasily toward the gate. A pilgrim accompanied by the rebel who had once defeated Heaven's armies was less convenient prey than a solitary priest.

Outside, Wukong fought against their troops, but the three kings were skilled. As the day wore on he learned that they were not merely actors in a trick with lamps. They could command spells, take to the air, and oppose his cudgel. Their bodies carried traces of something larger and stranger than the bull-headed servants that ran through the cave. He could not break their defense without risking the life of the captive within.

When the hours of fighting stretched toward evening, the three kings signaled their bull-headed followers to join the struggle. Wukong had held his own against the three masters for well over a hundred exchanges, but he could not fight through a ring of armed minions while keeping the cave entrance in sight. He sprang into the air on his somersault cloud and left them without their prize. The demons called back their troops and ordered the captive kept securely bound. They had heard enough about the Great Sage to know that a prisoner guarded carelessly might not remain a prisoner for long.

Xuanzang was chained in an inner room. The demons offered him food, but he could hardly swallow. The sound of distant fighting had been the only reason to hope. Now the sounds were gone, and the question of whether Wukong had survived hung over him. He thought of the monks of Compassionate Cloud and the kindly streets that had turned into the place of his capture. If he had not insisted upon bowing before a radiant image, he might now be traveling west.

## The cost of three false images

When Wukong returned to the monastery, Bajie and Sha were waiting beside the white horse. They had heard the story from the terrified festival-goers. The Great Sage told them what he had found: their master was alive in the cave, the apparitions were demons, and the local population had been paying a great annual tribute in the belief that the oil was consumed by sacred beings.

Bajie wanted to know how much oil had been taken. He understood food better than taxes and thought of all the things that such a sum might buy. Sha was more concerned with the timing. The demons had already prepared to eat Xuanzang. Every hour they remained in the monastery gave the enemy another chance.

Wukong considered resting until morning. He had been fighting and searching through the entire day, and had confirmed that the three kings were large horned beasts skilled in magical combat. But Sha pressed the point: if their master were killed in the night, tomorrow's strength would be useless. Bajie agreed, gathering his rake. They would go under the moon and break the cave's confidence before its keepers expected another attack.

The monks of Compassionate Cloud promised to watch the luggage and the horse. Wukong asked them to prepare an account for the prefect after the rescue. When the impostors were proved false, the city should be freed from the expense of the three lamps. To save a single priest while leaving the whole prefecture to pay for more counterfeit miracles would be a poor conclusion to the work.

So the three disciples rose again into the moonlight. They had left Jade Flower wearing new robes given in gratitude, after defeating a lion whose stolen treasures had shone over a forge. Now, where a thousand festival lanterns had shone, they were following three pretended Buddhas who used the light of devotion as cover for theft. Their master waited in the dark rooms of Green Dragon Mountain, and the work of recovering him had only begun.
