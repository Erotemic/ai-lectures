# Boccaccio Before and After the Plague

In the summer of 1348, Florence was a place where familiar obligations could disappear in a few days. A father might cease to care for his child, a neighbor might close a door to a neighbor, and a funeral might take place without the customary mourners. That is the city Giovanni Boccaccio places at the opening of his *Decameron*. The writer who chose that opening had already traveled between very different worlds: merchant households, royal courts, universities, and the sometimes precarious life of a professional writer. His book draws its energy from the friction among them.

Boccaccio was born in 1313. Historians do not agree whether his birthplace was Florence or nearby Certaldo, and the identity of his mother is not securely known. His father, Boccaccino di Chellino, belonged to the Florentine world of finance and commerce. This matters more than a neat birthplace story would. A son growing up around merchants could see money as an instrument for moving goods, making agreements, measuring trust, and changing a family's position. In the *Decameron*, an account of love or religion can suddenly turn upon a debt, a contract, a merchant's goods, or a servant's information. Such details are not merely scenery.

In his adolescence Boccaccio went to Naples, where his father was associated with the Bardi banking company. He encountered the household and intellectual environment of Robert of Anjou's court. The years in Naples joined two educations that a later reader might expect to be separate. There was the commercial world of exchange and financial risk; there were also classical authors, French and Italian romances, scholarly conversations, and the pleasures of imaginative writing. Boccaccio did not become the banker his father might have wanted, nor did his study of canon law define his career. He became a writer for whom a merchant's calculation and a lover's lament could inhabit the same story.

His earlier works show that the *Decameron* did not emerge from nowhere. *Filocolo* developed a long romance narrative. *Filostrato* and *Teseida* reworked classical and chivalric matter in Italian verse. The *Elegia di Madonna Fiammetta* gave sustained attention to a woman's own account of deserted love. These are not all the same literary experiment. They demonstrate an author trying different ways of placing desire, speech, and memory inside inherited forms. When the seven women and three men later take turns telling short narratives, Boccaccio already knows how many voices an author can make available.

He returned to Florence around 1340 or 1341. The city was rich in craftsmanship, banking, and civic institutions, but its political life had been unsettled by faction, financial failure, and changes in government. Naples was not simply an idyllic memory and Florence not simply a grim alternative. They offered different kinds of social knowledge. Courtly largesse, urban bargaining, the authority of a priest, and the vulnerability of someone without money could all become subjects for fiction. The *Decameron* moves among them without consistently privileging one class as morally superior.

Then came the Black Death. Boccaccio witnessed the outbreak of 1348 in Florence. Among its dead were his father and his father's wife. Modern historians reconstruct the epidemic from many kinds of evidence; Boccaccio's opening is a literary portrayal by someone who had lived through calamity, not a hospital ledger. The book is generally dated to the years after the outbreak, around 1349 to the early 1350s. It presents itself as consolation for women suffering the constraints of love and domestic enclosure. That ambition may sound small beside the catastrophe. In practice it became a way to imagine a society's many competing accounts of what people owe one another.

In the decades after the *Decameron*, Boccaccio continued writing, studying ancient literature, and participating in Florence's intellectual and civic life. His friendship with Petrarch helped sustain a lifelong conversation between the study of classical Latin and the possibilities of the Italian vernacular. He wrote about famous women, the fates of great figures, and classical mythology. In 1373 Florence commissioned him to lecture publicly on Dante, an author he had worked to celebrate. Illness interrupted those readings. Boccaccio died in Certaldo in 1375.

It is tempting to tell his career as the inevitable arrival of Renaissance modernity: a merchant's son escapes medieval restraints and invents realistic fiction. The temptation is misleading. Boccaccio writes from within medieval literary, theological, and moral traditions even as he tests them. His prose is neither a diary of what he saw nor an announcement that the old world has ended. The book's most distinctive achievement is to make different moral languages speak in the same room: clerical authority, commercial prudence, courtly generosity, household discipline, erotic desire, and everyday wit.

The *Decameron* can now be heard as a work made by someone with more than one home and more than one education. From Naples he knew the court and its imagination of honor. From Florence he knew the republic of households and trades. From books he knew the ancient and medieval past. From the plague he knew how quickly arrangements can fail. His answer was not to invent a flawless society, but to let ten characters imagine and contest what living together might mean.
