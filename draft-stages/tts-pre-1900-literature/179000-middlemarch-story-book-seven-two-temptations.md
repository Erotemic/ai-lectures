# Middlemarch — Two Temptations

Book Seven is called Two Temptations. Its most dangerous decisions are not sudden crimes committed by visibly monstrous people. They involve respectable men who wish, above all, to keep a good account of themselves while escaping the consequences of what they have done. George Eliot shows the exact point where the wish not to do wrong becomes insufficient, because another person's life is at stake.

Lydgate is heavily in debt. He knows his household cannot continue spending as it does, but Rosamond quietly interferes with attempts to retrench. She pursues her own plans and makes changes he has not approved. Their arguments are painful because the marriage has been founded on the assumption that its difficulties would resolve themselves. Lydgate is humiliated by the prospect of asking for money in a town where he hoped to become a professional authority.

At moments of desperation he considers solutions that would compromise his self-respect. He has treated himself as a man of science above the petty bargains of provincial life. Now a loan appears capable of deciding whether he can continue working at all. The problem is not that a doctor should never borrow. It is that in Middlemarch loans can join private necessity to public influence, and the town is quick to infer an exchange even when none has been agreed.

Bulstrode's older secret is pressing in on him through Raffles, a man with knowledge of his former dishonest business and inheritance arrangements. Bulstrode has cultivated the language and public practices of evangelical piety. He cannot simply erase the harm of his earlier conduct by believing that subsequent charity has purified it. Raffles makes that inconsistency visible and threatens exposure.

Raffles becomes seriously ill while staying at Stone Court. Lydgate treats him and gives instructions, including a prohibition on alcohol and a prescribed regime of care. Bulstrode is left in a position where he can choose whether to carry out those instructions. Exhausted and terrified of disclosure, he fails in that duty. He permits the housekeeper to give Raffles brandy, contrary to the doctor's instructions. Raffles dies. Eliot gives us enough access to Bulstrode's thought to see the temptation to let death solve his problem, but not a neat courtroom demonstration of a deliberate murder plot.

At nearly the same time, Bulstrode offers Lydgate the large loan he urgently needs. The timing is disastrous. There is no evidence that Lydgate has agreed to participate in wrongdoing against Raffles; he does not know the private calculation behind Bulstrode's conduct. Nevertheless, from the outside the loan can look like payment for professional silence. When the news of Raffles's history becomes public, people interpret the doctor's financial connection through the scandal. Lydgate's reputation, essential to his work, collapses rapidly.

At a public meeting Bulstrode is called to answer accusations concerning his past. He is overwhelmed. Lydgate, who is also under suspicion, helps him away. The gesture can be read as humane concern for a stricken man; hostile observers can turn it into another sign of an alliance. The same action acquires different meanings because people already believe they know the story. The town demonstrates a collective power that can punish wrongdoing but can also destroy the innocent through untested inference.

Bulstrode has the clearest opportunity to choose openly and fails. Lydgate has several opportunities to confront his domestic and financial reality and repeatedly delays. Their situations are not morally equivalent. The banker knows facts the doctor does not. The novel's distinction between culpability and appearance is critical. Guilt cannot be assigned by the social position a person occupies or by the shame of being connected to someone corrupt.

We have arrived at the point where several plot lines merge. Dorothea, who knows something about being condemned by a false inference, will have the chance to act in Lydgate's defense. Rosamond's private distress and Will's loyalty will soon be involved. Fred's work has begun to repair the damage his debts once caused. What remains to be seen is whether the people of Middlemarch can make good judgments when rumor offers them such easy answers.
