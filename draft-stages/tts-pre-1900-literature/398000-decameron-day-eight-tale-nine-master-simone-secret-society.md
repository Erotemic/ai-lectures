# The Decameron — Master Simone and the Secret Society

Master Simone has returned from Bologna with the costume and title of a learned physician. His dress makes a claim about his competence, but he is more eager for admiration than capable of careful judgment. In Florence he makes the acquaintance of Bruno and Buffalmacco, the two painters who have already deceived Calandrino. They quickly recognize a different weakness. Simone wants not an invisible stone or a test for thieves, but admission to a world in which important men enjoy secret feasts and erotic pleasures beyond the reach of ordinary neighbors.

Bruno and Buffalmacco speak of an exclusive company that gathers at night for fantastical adventures. They give hints about magnificent tables, beautiful women, rapid journeys and special privileges. Simone supplies the credulity needed to complete the performance. The painters do not need to prove that the group exists. Each new detail increases the doctor's desire to be invited, and his response becomes their evidence that they can demand still more from him.

Simone begins giving presents and entertaining his supposed sponsors. He hopes that gifts will buy intimacy with a select brotherhood. The painters accept what he offers and prolong the initiation. Their invention grows more extravagant, decorated with language and ceremonious conditions. Finally they tell the doctor that he may attend a meeting and instruct him how to behave when he reaches the appointed place. He must be obedient and must not question the strange transport by which he is supposed to join the company.

At night, in a place chosen by the tricksters, the supposed adventure ends in a filthy ditch or cesspit. Simone is left covered in refuse rather than introduced to any secret society. The men arrange their exit and later provide an account that allows him to preserve some dignity without discovering how thoroughly he has been fooled. He can explain away the failure as the hazards of an occult initiation rather than admit that his acquaintances tricked him into their practical joke.

Lauretta's tale is more expansive and verbal than the Calandrino episodes. Bruno and Buffalmacco create an imagined institution with ranks, privileges and hidden customs; it is not enough merely to tell Simone one lie. They build a vocabulary in which every ordinary doubt appears to be proof that he has not yet earned the secrets. The physician's professional title makes the joke pointed. A man presumed to know the body and its ailments cannot diagnose the motives of two people he has allowed to manage his expectations.

We should resist turning the joke into a historical verdict on all medieval medicine or on a documented physician. This is a fictional Simone, exaggerated for comic effect, just as the painters are fictional characters with names associated with Florentine artists. What the tale observes is a recurrent human weakness: people who crave membership in a privileged circle may surrender the ordinary skepticism they apply elsewhere. The stranger and more exclusive the promise, the more flattering it can seem to believe it. The foul ditch is the unromantic fact at the end of a carefully narrated fantasy.
