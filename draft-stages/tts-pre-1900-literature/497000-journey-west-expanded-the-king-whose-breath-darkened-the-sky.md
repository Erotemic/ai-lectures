# The King Whose Breath Darkened the Sky

The company of lesser demons had retreated into Yellow Wind Cave with their drums broken and their flags torn. They told the king that Tiger Vanguard was dead and that Sun Wukong had dragged his body to the entrance. The ruler of the cavern did not at first rise from his seat. The tiger had been his own officer, sent to patrol the mountain. Now the monkey was not content with demanding his master's release. He had killed the officer as well.

Yellow Wind King called for his armor. The attendants brought him a golden helmet, a breastplate, and a three-pronged weapon. He came out with the appearance of a commander who believed his rank should command respect. Wukong stood at the gate beside the fallen tiger, hardly reaching the demon's chest.

The king stared at him. Was this the Sun Wukong whose name had traveled through Heaven and earth? He had expected a creature of extraordinary size. In front of him stood a small monkey, lean and fierce, with a dark staff in his hands. He mocked Wukong's stature. The Great Sage answered that height could be changed whenever he wished. If the king needed a larger adversary to take him seriously, he could provide one.

Yellow Wind King raised his fork and struck toward the monkey's head. Wukong bent and lengthened his body until he towered over the weapon. The king stopped in surprise. Wukong laughed at him for being so easily impressed and invited him to fight properly.

They fought first on the ground, then in the air above the cave. The king's three sharp points drove toward Wukong's chest; the iron staff twisted aside and returned toward the helmet. Blow met blow. For thirty exchanges neither opponent forced the other to yield. This was no unarmed roadside tiger, and Wukong had reason to respect the skill in the king's grip.

But respect did not lessen his determination. Xuanzang was still bound inside. Wukong had been appointed to guard him, and every moment of combat left the monk in the hands of captors. The Great Sage pulled a handful of hair from his body, chewed it, blew it outward, and commanded it to change.

Small Wukongs sprang into being all around the king. Each carried an iron staff. They rushed him from every direction. To the demon below, the single monkey had become a ring of attackers. There was no room to concentrate on a single weapon; every turn exposed another side to a blow.

The king had a defense of his own. He turned toward the quarter from which the wind would rise, opened his mouth, and blew. The first rush of air struck the circling monkeys, sending them spinning. Then came the true Yellow Wind. Dust leapt off the ground. Pines bent toward breaking. Rocks shook along the slopes. The cloud of small Wukongs whirled like scraps caught above a furnace, their staffs useless in the air.

The storm spread far beyond the cave. In the novel's great vision of the gale, rivers are troubled, ships struggle against their ropes, travelers lose their way, and even heavenly beings are thrown into confusion. A sun that should have shone overhead darkened. Places that had seemed securely fixed in the world trembled before the blast.

Wukong recalled his hairs. Their borrowed bodies vanished back into his own. He pushed forward alone, staff in hand, determined not to surrender merely because an enemy had changed the weather. The king leaned toward him again and sent another mouthful of wind directly into his face.

This time the injury was intimate. Grit and force struck the eyes that had endured the Eight-Trigrams Furnace. Wukong's fiery golden gaze squeezed shut. Tears flowed from the burning lids. He could hear the enemy; he could not see well enough to place his blows. He withdrew from the cave, not beaten by the fork but disabled by the wind.

Bajie was hidden among the rocks with the horse and baggage. From there he had felt the mountain shake. He had flattened himself against the ground while the gale roared overhead, praying that it would leave something standing. When it cleared he looked toward the cave and saw no fighters. He did not know who had won.

At last Wukong called to him. Bajie ran out and asked how a storm could have made so much destruction. Wukong answered through aching eyes. The king fought well, but his power lay in the wind, and the wind had injured him. Bajie asked what could be done about their master. Wukong spoke of finding a doctor for his eyes before attempting another attack.

The younger disciple stared at the ridges around them. A doctor, here? They had scarcely found a road or a place to sleep. Wukong knew that much, but he would not let the absence of a visible house decide the matter. They would seek shelter for the night and return in daylight. The demon had delayed eating Xuanzang precisely because he feared the disciples. There was still a chance to save him.

Bajie took up the baggage. Wukong kept his staff near one hand and the other near his painful eyes. The two left the cave behind, not victorious and not abandoning their master. For the first time since Bajie joined him, the Great Sage needed his brother to help him through the dark.
