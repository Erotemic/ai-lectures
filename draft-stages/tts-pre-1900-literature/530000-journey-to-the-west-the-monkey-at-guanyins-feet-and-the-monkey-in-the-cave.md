# Journey to the West: The monkey at Guanyin's feet and the monkey in the cave

## A complaint at the southern sea

Wukong had been dismissed. The argument that sent him away began with ordinary robbers, not a supernatural king or a monster in a cave. In protecting Xuanzang from armed thieves, he had killed men he might have driven off. When he repeated the violence and carried a severed head to his master, the monk could bear no more. He recited the spell that tormented Wukong beneath the golden fillet, then ordered him to leave the pilgrimage.

The monkey went not to his old home on Flower-Fruit Mountain, but to Guanyin's sanctuary at the southern sea. He had come to her in other crises, seeking help when an enemy could not be defeated by his own powers. This time the trouble was between master and disciple. He arrived weeping.

Guanyin's attendants led him before her. Wukong knelt and told her how much he had endured to protect Xuanzang. He had entered the mouths of danger, rescued his master from imprisonment, and faced enemies who feared neither celestial weapons nor the armies of Heaven. He had agreed to the journey because she had offered him a path away from the crimes of his earlier life. Now that same journey seemed closed to him. His master, Wukong said, repaid faithful service with suspicion and the torture of the headband.

Guanyin asked what had happened. Wukong gave the account of the robbers and the deaths. He did not deny the killings, but he insisted that the men had been violent and that he had acted in defense of the company.

The bodhisattva did not accept his defense. Robbers were wrongdoers, she said, but they remained human beings. Wukong had countless ways to stop them without killing them. To overcome a demon who preyed upon others might be counted as an accomplishment on the road to the scriptures; to kill mortals because they provoked him was a failure of compassion. If he wished to change his nature, he could not use his strength as an excuse for every impulse.

Wukong bowed. He was not ready to concede that Xuanzang had acted fairly, but he understood that Guanyin would not simply declare him innocent. He asked for another mercy. If the pilgrimage was over for him, she should remove the golden fillet. He would return to Water-Curtain Cave, live among his monkeys, and trouble no one on the road again.

Guanyin told him that there was no loosening spell. The Buddha had given her the fillets and the means to tighten them, but not a second formula that would remove them whenever a disciple wished to go free.

Then Wukong proposed going to the Buddha himself. Guanyin stopped him. She looked outward with her far-seeing knowledge and told him that Xuanzang was about to suffer another calamity. The monkey would be needed. He should remain in her presence until the matter came to him.

Wukong agreed to stay. The anger that had carried him from the roadside could not be discharged by a visit to his old cave. He stood beneath the lotus throne, still bound to the master who had rejected him.

## Two disciples and a thirsty monk

Far to the west, Xuanzang was traveling without him. Bajie walked at the horse's side; Sha Wujing carried the luggage. Wukong's quick eyes were gone, and the master felt the difference before a demon appeared. He was hungry and thirsty. Since they had departed Old Yang's home before dawn, the day had worn on with little food.

Bajie was sent to find a village. He flew up and surveyed the hills but could see only ridges. He returned, reporting that there was no house. Xuanzang then asked at least for water. Sha handed Bajie the begging bowl, and the pig disciple set off toward a mountain stream.

Time passed. The monk sat in the shade of the roadside, parched and increasingly faint. Sha had the heavier responsibility: the luggage and horse had to be watched, and the master could scarcely stand. But Bajie was taking so long that Sha finally tied the horse, set down the baggage, and went to find him.

For the first time in many chapters, Xuanzang was completely alone.

A familiar figure appeared and knelt before him with a cup of clear water. It looked like Wukong. The figure spoke in Wukong's voice and reproached the monk for allowing himself to grow thirsty when his former disciple could have helped him.

Xuanzang would not accept the water. He had dismissed the monkey for killing, and a cup of kindness did not erase the events on the road. He ordered the figure to go. The supposed Wukong insisted that the pilgrimage could not be completed without him. Xuanzang answered that whether it succeeded was no longer his affair.

At that, the visitor's tone changed. He cursed the monk, threw aside the cup, and struck Xuanzang across the back with an iron staff. The monk collapsed. The attacker seized the two blue-felt bundles which contained the travel documents and other essentials and departed into the air.

Xuanzang lay senseless beside his horse. Whoever had attacked him had worn the face of the disciple he had just dismissed. He could not yet know that another figure with the same face was sitting under Guanyin's watch at the southern sea.

## The missing bundles

Bajie, meanwhile, had discovered that his aerial search had been misled by the height of the mountains. Hidden in a hollow was a modest farmhouse. He believed its inhabitants would be frightened by a pig-faced stranger, so he changed himself into a sickly yellow-faced monk and asked the women inside for leftover rice. They had been cooking for family members working in the fields and gave him a generous bowl of the scraps. He returned pleased with his success.

Sha found him on the path. Why had he gone to beg for rice instead of taking water? Bajie explained that food was useful too. Together they arranged to carry the rice in the folds of a robe and fill the bowl at the stream. When they returned to Xuanzang, the monk was face down in the dirt. The horse was untethered and crying out. The bundles were gone.

Bajie believed they had arrived too late. He spoke of buying a coffin, burying the monk properly, and separating to find their own ways. Sha, whose devotion was often quiet and practical, could not accept that conclusion until he had examined his master. He knelt, turned Xuanzang over, and felt warmth and breath. The monk was alive.

They gave him water, supported him, and waited while he recovered enough to speak. Then he told them that Wukong had returned and struck him. Bajie was outraged. He threatened to go to Flower-Fruit Mountain and demand the bundles back. Sha reminded him that the master needed care first. They carried Xuanzang to the farmhouse, warmed the rice, and helped him rest.

The old woman of the house was bewildered by the arrival of yet more travelers claiming to come from the East. Had she not given food to a sickly pilgrim earlier? Bajie admitted his disguise. He showed her the rice, and she allowed them to remain. It was a small domestic comedy in the middle of a crisis, and it gave the party enough shelter to decide what to do next.

The travel document was essential. They could not pass through kingdoms by asking a ruler to stamp a pass that no longer existed. The clothes, begging tools, and other possessions in the bundles were also their means of survival. Their master was weak, and they needed to know whether the supposed attack was truly Wukong's doing.

Xuanzang decided that Sha Wujing should go. Bajie had once visited Flower-Fruit Mountain and offered to make the journey, but his blunt speech and old quarrels with Wukong might start another fight. Sha had been a steady companion. He should ask for the property politely, and if the monkey refused, he should appeal to Guanyin rather than fight alone.

Sha took his staff and traveled east across the sea.

## A second pilgrimage prepared in the cave

It took Sha days to reach Flower-Fruit Mountain. He crossed the eastern ocean and came to the familiar peaks of Wukong's old kingdom. The mountain was crowded with monkeys. They shouted around a stone seat, upon which sat Sun Wukong—or a figure indistinguishable from him at a glance.

The figure was reading a document aloud. Sha listened and recognized the Tang emperor's travel pass. It named Xuanzang as the monk sent west to obtain scriptures and recorded the imperial request, the stamps, and the names of the three disciples he had gathered along the road. The monkey on the stone seat was studying it, reading its words again and again.

Sha stepped forward and asked why the older brother was reciting the master's property. The seated monkey looked down as though the visitor were a stranger. He ordered the other monkeys to seize him and demand his name. Sha restrained his anger and explained who he was. He asked Wukong to remember the journey. If he no longer wished to serve Xuanzang, he should at least return the bundles so the others could continue.

The figure laughed at the request. He had not taken the document because he wished to retire, he said. He intended to travel west on his own, obtain the scriptures, deliver them to the East, and claim the achievement for himself. Why should the monk Chen Xuanzang take the glory? A mighty monkey could undertake the enterprise under his own name.

Sha replied that this was impossible. The Buddha had specifically appointed Xuanzang, the reborn Golden Cicada, as the recipient of the scriptures. Wukong's role was to protect the pilgrim, not replace him. Without the appointed monk, no amount of strength would compel the Buddha to hand over the sacred texts.

The figure had an answer. He had chosen another master. At his command, the monkeys brought out a white horse, a monk who looked exactly like Xuanzang, a pig-faced disciple, and another Sha Wujing carrying luggage. Here was a complete pilgrimage, with every role copied. If the original party would not take him back, he would build a new party and make it travel.

Sha's anger broke through. There was only one Sha Wujing, he declared, and the figure standing before him was not that person. He struck the imitation with his demon-subduing staff. The body fell, revealing a monkey transformed to look like Sha. The false Wukong rose in fury and ordered his followers to surround the visitor.

Sha fought his way out. The enemy did not pursue him far. Back in the cave, the figure ordered the dead monkey's body prepared for a meal, then appointed another of its followers to take the missing disciple's place in the counterfeit company. It was not merely trying to hurt Xuanzang or inconvenience the pilgrims. It was rehearsing an entire rival mission, down to the copied companions and the passport.

## The figure beneath Guanyin's throne

Sha turned south to ask Guanyin for help. He entered the bodhisattva's peaceful sanctuary, where her attendants already seemed to know that a problem involving Wukong would bring him there. Sha approached the lotus throne.

And there stood Wukong.

Sha saw the face he had just confronted on Flower-Fruit Mountain. His anger took control before he could speak. He raised his staff and attacked. The figure beside Guanyin dodged but did not strike back. Guanyin ordered Sha to stop and explain what had happened.

Sha described the attack upon Xuanzang, the theft of the bundles, the document being read at Water-Curtain Cave, and the counterfeit pilgrims. He accused Wukong of racing ahead of him from the mountain in order to deceive Guanyin with a false account.

Guanyin did not permit that conclusion. Wukong, she said, had been with her for four days. He had not left her sight. She had listened to his grievance over the slain robbers and ordered him to stay because his master would soon need him. The monkey at Flower-Fruit Mountain could not be the same being who had stood beneath her throne all that time.

Sha had seen the first with his own eyes. Guanyin had watched the second. Both reports seemed certain. It was not enough to call one witness foolish. Someone, or something, was wearing Wukong's appearance so completely that even Sha had mistaken it for his brother.

Guanyin told the genuine Wukong to accompany Sha back to Flower-Fruit Mountain. They must look upon the other creature together. Then they might discover which was true, which was false, and how a face so familiar could belong to two enemies.

The two disciples set out. Behind them the bodhisattva's sanctuary was quiet, and ahead lay the stone throne of the rival monkey. The false pilgrimage was being prepared. The real pilgrimage stood broken beside a frightened master. The question of who had struck Xuanzang and stolen the documents could not be answered by appearance alone.

At the end of this chapter, the two who would confront the impostor had not yet reached the cave. The test, the struggle, and the answer were still to come.
