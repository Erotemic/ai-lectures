# Madame Bovary II: style, reading, gender, debt, and the public trial

## Madame Bovary — Voice, Free Indirect Style, and Irony

One of the most influential achievements of Madame Bovary is a change in how a novel can make a reader hear a character's thoughts. Flaubert does not give Emma a diary that explains everything, nor does he simply step forward as an author to lecture us on her errors. His third-person narration often moves into the vocabulary and emotional pressure of a character without announcing the crossing. This method is called free indirect discourse, or free indirect style. Its importance is not that it was invented by Flaubert out of nothing. Earlier writers, including Jane Austen, used related methods. Flaubert makes the movement between narratorial speech and character language a central instrument of the book's irony.

Imagine three ways to report Emma's hope. First, direct speech: Emma said that at last she would know perfect love. Second, ordinary indirect report: Emma believed she would now experience happiness. Third, free indirect style: At last, then, love had arrived; everything would be transformed. That last sentence can be grammatically attached to a third-person narrative while carrying the rhythm of Emma's expectation. The author need not put quotation marks around her hope. The reader must decide how much of the sentence belongs to Emma and how much distance the surrounding narrative puts between the hope and the world. These sentences are illustrative paraphrases, not quotations from Flaubert or a particular translator.

The difference matters because Madame Bovary often presents claims that a character feels with immense conviction and that the wider scene questions. Emma's vision of aristocratic life at the ball is made powerful through sensuous detail, but the event does not prove that the guests live without boredom or constraint. Her romantic vocabulary at the beginning of an affair is emotionally real, but a practiced seducer can use the same language to obtain what he wants. The narrator's apparent restraint means that the reader must supply the ethical work. If we mistake the character's words for the author's endorsement, we have not heard the technique.

At the agricultural fair, Flaubert stages a more audible kind of irony. Public officials give formulaic speeches about agricultural merit while Rodolphe courts Emma with formulaic claims of personal destiny. The two conversations interleave. Their subjects are different, but their rhythms reveal a common dependence on phrases that sound valuable because they are familiar. It is a kind of verbal montage. The scene does not require the narrator to interrupt with a declaration that everyone is insincere. The arrangement of voices makes the comparison available to a listener who attends to the transitions.

The Rouen carriage scene uses withholding rather than a steady exchange of inner language. We know where the carriage travels and can infer what is happening inside, but the narrated attention stays outside. The action is not simply erased. It is constructed through what surrounding detail allows us to understand. This is a lesson in how absence can carry meaning. Flaubert makes the reader do part of the representational work, and the reader's participation became significant in the novel's censorship controversy. The form can suggest more than an explicit declaration while leaving room for contested interpretation.

Flaubert's dialogue also exposes character without a reassuring narrator explaining every irony. Homais produces speech that sounds scientific, progressive, and official, and his confidence repeatedly exceeds his judgment. Charles's expressions often signal genuine feeling and limited understanding at once. Rodolphe writes a breakup letter while trying to manufacture the appearance of reluctance. The words are actions. They help people acquire status, hide motives, or postpone consequences. The narrator's relative abstention from moral commentary does not create a morally neutral world. It asks us to examine how language works within it.

There is a risk in celebrating the style as if it automatically makes the author fair. Irony has a direction. The novel can invite readers to find Emma ridiculous while giving her frustrations emotional force. Its attention to provincial speech may be acute and sometimes cruel. A listener who notices the technique should also ask who is being granted an inner world and who appears mainly as an object of satirical display. The fact that a narrator does not announce a moral does not mean that the narrative arrangement carries no judgments. Critical sophistication begins when we see those judgments as things to examine rather than commands to obey.

When you read or hear Madame Bovary, pause whenever a sentence seems more ardent, more self-congratulatory, or more formulaic than the surrounding prose. Ask whether a character's inner idiom has entered the narration. Then look at what occurs immediately before and after. Does the scene confirm the emotional claim, merely allow it to be felt, or undermine it? That practice makes free indirect style more than a term to memorize. It becomes a way of hearing two voices occupying one sentence, and of understanding why Flaubert's novel changed the possibilities of literary narration.

## Madame Bovary — Reading, Romantic Scripts, and Disappointment

Emma Bovary has read romances, but the claim that books alone cause her ruin tells us too little. She is also a farmer's daughter who has encountered convent education, aristocratic spectacle, provincial marriage, commercial goods, and a narrowly arranged set of adult options. Her reading supplies images for longings that have many sources. The question is how those images teach her to interpret an ordinary day. A sentimental novel can give a person words for desire; it can also teach a person to expect that desire will announce itself in a particular scene, with particular gestures, in a life already arranged for an audience.

At the convent Emma is drawn to stories of grand emotion, and the language of religious devotion mingles with literary glamour. Her imagination moves among beautiful settings, suffering lovers, and promises of transformation. She does not approach these images as examples among many possible lives. She treats them as standards by which her own experience ought to be measured. When Charles offers affection and reliability, she struggles to recognize those qualities as the substance of a relationship. They lack the theatrical force by which she expects love to identify itself.

The ball at La Vaubyessard expands the same structure from reading to social observation. Emma sees elegant clothes, music, refined food, and confident gestures. She imagines that the visible scene gives access to the hidden lives of its participants. The ball becomes a memory she polishes until it seems more complete than the life she actually lives. But a brief visit is not evidence of the feelings that occupy the guests on other days. Her experience is genuine; her conclusion is less secure. The novel exposes a common habit: using the most photogenic moments of someone else's life as a standard for the whole of our own.

Rodolphe understands Emma's preferred emotional language and uses it. He does not need to invent a new theory of seduction. He has to repeat the promises of exceptional love at moments when she is disposed to hear them. The agricultural fair gives us the scene in which romantic ideality becomes an effective technique of persuasion. Emma hears a declaration she can inhabit. Rodolphe hears a means to begin an affair. Their words are not empty for both people in the same way, and that inequality matters. She is not simply duped by beautiful language; she supplies a powerful need for the language to be true.

The promise to run away is the decisive collision between imagined form and actual commitment. Emma sees a future drawn in vivid outlines. Rodolphe calculates the inconvenience and withdraws. His letter performs sadness without taking on the burdens of the decision. The resulting illness is not a theatrical punishment for reading. It is the devastation of someone who had made the imagined future essential to the meaning of her present. The romantic vocabulary has offered her an absolute; the other person was never bound by the same absolute.

Léon, initially shy and sentimental, seems at first a gentler possibility. When the affair begins years later, however, novelty turns into habit. Emma experiences the same impatience and repetition that had troubled her marriage. A new person cannot permanently provide the first intensity of a new story. This repetition is part of Flaubert's argument. The problem is not merely that she selected the wrong lover. It is that she asks a succession of relationships to prove an entire theory of happiness, one that leaves little room for the other person's independent wishes.

Still, the novel's irony should not be allowed to vindicate the provincial world without scrutiny. The men who speak confidently of progress often fail at their responsibilities. The town judges women by standards it does not apply evenly to men. Debt is sold as convenience before it becomes coercion. A woman may have more reason than her husband to dream of another life, even when the dream is harmful. Emma's romantic scripts mislead her, but her dissatisfaction is not proof that the world around her deserves gratitude. An attentive reader must be able to criticize the scripts and the constraints together.

One useful comparison is between a remembered scene and a practiced habit. The ball occupies a few hours but dominates Emma's memory for years. Charles's care occupies many ordinary days but does not become a spectacular event. Flaubert makes duration compete with intensity. If we listen only for grand declarations, we reproduce Emma's method of judgment. If we listen only for the virtues of routine, we risk ignoring what routine denies. Madame Bovary teaches us to ask what an image can reveal, what it cannot show, and what happens when someone tries to live inside the image instead of the world that produced it.

## Madame Bovary — Marriage, Desire, and Gendered Constraints

The title Madame Bovary names Emma through marriage. Her social identity is announced by a relation that does not capture the totality of her desires. She wants mobility, recognition, sensual experience, and a life whose possibilities are not exhausted by the household. Charles wants domestic affection and continuity. To describe this mismatch is not to make Emma's later treatment of him and Berthe harmless. It is to establish the conditions in which decisions become intelligible. The novel is interested in what a person imagines marriage promises, and in the damage produced when those promises cannot be openly discussed.

Emma's expectations draw from literature, religious feeling, fashionable images, and the limited examples of adult freedom around her. Charles expresses desire by being near her and providing care. He does not possess the vocabulary for asking why she finds the life intolerable. Emma experiences his satisfaction as a kind of accusation: if he can be content, perhaps the life is all she deserves. The conflict is not resolved by declaring one party correct. The two have different ideas about the relation between emotion and action, and neither is very good at discovering the other's inner arrangements.

Berthe's birth reveals how gendered possibility can enter a parent's imagination before a child has spoken. Emma wishes for a son because she imagines a boy could move more freely through the world. Her daughter arrives into a disappointment she did not cause. Later, Emma alternates between tenderness, distraction, and harshness, while other caretakers perform much of the work. The girl is not merely a symbol of Emma's limitations; she is a person who needs care regardless of what her mother has lost. The final account of her factory labor makes the cost of adult choices impossible to overlook.

Rodolphe and Léon occupy different positions in Emma's pursuit of love. Rodolphe knows how to exploit her longing for exceptional feeling; he has the social confidence to approach, seduce, and withdraw. Léon begins less assured but becomes attracted to the prestige of participating in a sophisticated affair. Neither is a liberator. Each enjoys some of the freedoms Emma wants, but neither has much reason to risk his entire social future for hers. Their asymmetry is not absolute: Emma also manipulates, commands, and deceives. The point is that a relationship that promises equality can remain built around unequal exposure to consequences.

The risk of discovery does not fall evenly across gender. A man's affair may harm his reputation, but Emma's position as wife and mother places her body, money, and mobility under public scrutiny. She cannot simply start another household with an independent career on terms available to many men of her setting. The village's judgments are hypocritical, but hypocrisy is not the same as powerlessness. It can still punish. The reader should see these constraints without turning every bad decision into an unavoidable consequence of them. Emma has agency; it is exercised within arrangements she did not design.

Charles, for his part, is neither monster nor perfect innocent. He is devoted and capable of gentle care, but he does not seriously enter the imaginative life that troubles his wife. His failures of medical judgment have consequences for patients. He can be comfortable in institutions that do not serve everyone well. His inability to perceive Emma's affair becomes tragic partly because he believes affection itself should guarantee mutual knowledge. It does not. A person can love another deeply and still fail to ask the questions that might reveal how distant they have become.

The notary's proposition during Emma's financial crisis makes the relation between money and sexual power explicit. Assistance is offered in a form that expects access to her body. Her refusal is a moral decision under pressure; it is not evidence that earlier choices were harmless. The scene strips away much of the romantic language surrounding her affairs. When she asks for help, she meets transactions. Even a supposedly private desire belongs to a social world that can set prices on vulnerability.

At the end the novel does not reduce Emma to a cautionary figure whose death proves an uncomplicated truth about fidelity. Berthe's fate, Charles's grief, Rodolphe's survival, and Homais's official recognition prevent the moral from closing so easily. The title remains an institutional label for someone whose desires resisted that label and whose actions injured others. Madame Bovary is most penetrating when we refuse to choose between acknowledging genuine structural constraint and holding a character responsible for what she does to people who cannot protect themselves from her choices.

## Madame Bovary — Credit, Commodities, and the Market

The tragedies of love in Madame Bovary are inseparable from a less glamorous story about bills. Emma buys clothing, furnishings, gifts, and objects that promise to turn a life into an image of refinement. Lheureux, the merchant, sells her goods and extends credit. His service seems generous because it lets her obtain the visible sign of desire before finding the money to pay for it. The goods arrive now; the obligation is postponed. This arrangement is not just background detail. Flaubert gives it the structural importance of an affair, because it is another relation in which immediate gratification is purchased through a promise about the future.

Lheureux knows how to present himself as useful. He does not announce at the beginning that he intends to ruin a household. He offers merchandise, understands tastes, remembers opportunities, and proposes arrangements that appear to solve short-term difficulties. Emma's imagination finds in each purchase a small restoration of freedom. The merchant's advantage is that he does not need to believe in the emotional value of the things he sells. He needs the signatures and the terms. When she changes her mind, the documents remain.

The distinction between price and value is central. A garment can carry the promise of becoming someone more admired. A household object can make the room appear closer to an imagined aristocratic environment. A gift for a lover can seem evidence that the relationship is real. Those meanings may be powerful to Emma and nearly irrelevant to a creditor. The same object enters different systems of valuation. Flaubert's prose lets the listener move between sensory attractiveness and economic consequence without treating either one as imaginary.

The story's accumulation of obligations matters more than any single extravagance. Notes are renewed; future money is pledged; old debts are covered by arrangements that make later debts worse. Emma does not merely forget to pay. She lives within a system that allows difficulty to be deferred and multiplied. Lheureux exploits that weakness, but the narrative also records the decisions by which Emma cooperates with him. The economic tragedy is made from a sequence of choices and incentives, not a mysterious punishment that descends on the last page.

Commerce also organizes the social world around Emma. The pharmacist Homais markets knowledge and personal authority. Professional titles, fashionable goods, and signs of distinction circulate as tokens people can display. At the agricultural fair, the village celebrates productive labor through official speeches and awards. Emma and Rodolphe talk of transcendent feeling beside the apparatus of public recognition. The coincidence is instructive. Both the market and the love affair distribute promises of an improved future, while hiding the ordinary labor and risks required to produce it.

When the creditor finally moves toward legal seizure, the consequences become visible. Domestic property is no longer a private collection of beautiful or comforting objects. It is an asset from which claims can be satisfied. Emma tries to obtain money from people she believed were attached to her emotionally, and their willingness to help proves far smaller than her hopes. The crisis converts private language into enforceable contracts. A creditor can be indifferent to the story of why an object was purchased and still have the machinery of the law behind him.

This does not mean the lesson of the novel is simply to avoid spending. Emma has limited routes to autonomy, and the sale of goods becomes a means of offering a simulacrum of choice. Lheureux uses the asymmetry between someone who wants an image and someone who manages obligations. The book neither erases Emma's agency nor pretends that consumer desire arose in isolation from the world around her. It shows a social economy producing aspirations and then profiting from their disappointment.

A practical way to read these scenes is to track three columns: the object Emma obtains, the emotion she expects it to create, and the future obligation attached to it. Add a fourth column for the people who will bear consequences if she cannot pay. The exercise makes clear why Berthe's fate is part of the same story as the dresses and gifts. Economic relations stretch beyond the moment of purchase. Flaubert turns the elegant surface of consumption around to reveal the signatures, dependence, and labor that were there all along.

## Madame Bovary — Homais, Medicine, and the Claims of Progress

Monsieur Homais likes the vocabulary of progress. He speaks as if rational modern knowledge can be measured by a person's confidence in using its phrases. Charles Bovary is an officer of health, or officier de santé, a historical form of authorized French medical practitioner with a more limited qualification than a doctor of medicine. Neither man's professional place should be confused with what a modern practitioner would be licensed to do. Flaubert's satire depends on the gap between the prestige of expertise, the limits of actual competence, and the ability of patients to consent to risks that they scarcely understand.

The case of Hippolyte reveals the gap. The stable worker has a clubfoot and has built a working life around it. Homais, eager for a modern surgical success that will reflect well on Yonville and on himself, urges Charles to operate. Charles studies descriptions of a treatment and accepts the role. Emma also encourages him, imagining that his success will make their family name famous and at last prove her husband worthy of admiration. Notice who is being promised what. The practitioner may gain status, the pharmacist may gain publicity, and Emma may gain pride. Hippolyte bears the bodily risk.

The operation goes badly. Complications develop, gangrene sets in, and another physician, Canivet, must amputate the affected leg to save the patient. Afterward Hippolyte survives with a far more disabling loss than he had before the procedure. The episode is easy to use as a symbol of Charles's inadequacy, because Emma herself sees it that way. But that reading risks repeating the novel's moral error. The most important consequence is not that a wife loses respect for her husband. It is that a person with less social power has been injured by an attempt made partly for other people's distinction.

The situation exposes several failures of reasoning. The promoters begin with a desired outcome and behave as though the probability of success can be inferred from the prestige of a fashionable technique. They emphasize the potential benefit to the operator and the town. They do not adequately weigh the patient's existing life, the practical skill required, the possibility of complications, or the uncertainty of the evidence. It would be a mistake to infer that all medical innovation is wrong. The scene criticizes the confusion of innovation's public appearance with evidence that a particular procedure is justified for a particular person.

Homais then pursues the publicity that professional triumph might provide. His language of science becomes inseparable from a concern for status. He knows how to make activities sound modern and useful, while his judgment can be guided by fear of losing influence. Flaubert's irony is sharper because Homais is not a simple enemy of knowledge. He admires it, reads about it, and uses its vocabulary constantly. The failure is not an explicit rejection of reason but a performance of reason without sufficient self-correction.

Charles is gentler than Homais and less adept at public self-promotion. Yet personal gentleness is not adequate protection for a patient. He accepts a dangerous undertaking beyond his competence, and his good intentions do not restore the lost leg. The novel refuses a comforting division between the caring but harmless practitioner and the vain but harmful enthusiast. Their different motives can contribute to one injury. Professional ethics requires more than a good heart and more than enthusiasm for technique.

The final honor bestowed on Homais brings the satire full circle. He receives the Legion of Honor while people who bear the costs of the town's decisions, such as Hippolyte and Berthe, receive no comparable recognition. The public institution rewards a man skilled at managing reputation. That ending does not mean everyone who earns an honor is a fraud. It means the novel has constructed one case in which the system's visible signs of merit are badly misaligned with the evidence of good judgment.

A productive reading question is to ask whose evidence counts in the operation episode. Homais offers theories and a newspaper story. Charles offers his professional effort. Emma offers ambition. Hippolyte's body provides the outcome. The narrator allows us to see the discrepancy among them. Flaubert's critique remains useful wherever prestige, publicity, and the language of advancement can crowd out attention to the person exposed to risk. It is a literary lesson in the ethics of evidence, not a replacement for the history of medical science.

## Madame Bovary — Close Reading: Scenes That Contradict Their Speakers

To read Madame Bovary closely, we can follow scenes in which speech promises one thing while the arrangement of detail reveals another. Flaubert rarely stops to tell the reader, in plain moral language, what conclusion to draw. He places voices beside acts and lets the difference matter. We will examine five scenes: the marriage and its aftermath, the aristocratic ball, the agricultural fair, the closed carriage, and the novel's final pages. The goal is to become able to hear irony without treating irony as a license to despise every character.

Begin with the marriage. Charles experiences domestic closeness as a fulfillment. Emma waits for the promised state of passion to transform ordinary life. Write down the daily evidence available to each person: Charles's care and satisfaction, Emma's boredom and longing, the routines of the house. Then ask which conclusions each adds. Charles assumes that being happy together must mean his wife is happy. Emma assumes that a marriage without overwhelming emotional intensity must be deficient in its essence. Both accounts have facts in them. Neither amounts to knowledge of the other's mind.

Next consider the ball at La Vaubyessard. Attend to the sensory detail: food, clothing, movement, the social grace of people who know how to inhabit a room. Emma sees surfaces, and the surfaces genuinely delight her. The close-reading question is when an observed detail becomes an imagined biography. She has seen an elegant dance; she comes to feel that an elegant life lies behind the scene, permanently different from her own. The novel does not have to declare the impression false. It gives us reason to doubt that a brief spectacle can support so large a conclusion.

The agricultural fair offers a different method. Listen to the official praise for labor while Rodolphe makes declarations about love. Mark the transitions. The speeches are not directly about one another, yet their shared dependence on respectable clichés is exposed by their proximity. The effect would be weaker if the narrator simply interrupted to announce that politicians and seducers both lie. In juxtaposition, the reader performs the comparison. A further question follows: do all the speakers know they are using formulas? Rodolphe is strategically manipulative, but officials may believe much of what they say. Formulaic language does not require identical intentions.

At the carriage scene in Rouen, change the question from what is said to what is withheld. We follow a vehicle through streets; the narrative largely excludes the intimate action inside. The geography of the city gives the reader a measure of passing time, while privacy becomes a moving enclosure. Ask what the scene requires the reader to infer. Then ask whether the inference is created by the prose or imported from an expectation about romance novels. Flaubert's composition guides inference with deliberate restraint. Translation choices in verb tense and rhythm can alter the effect, so a close reading across languages should keep the French wording nearby.

The death scene reverses romantic convention. Emma's suffering is physical, slow, and witnessed by people who do not understand how to help. The beautiful language of destiny has little place beside an actual body in crisis. Do not turn this into the punishment that a narrator plainly announces; no such straightforward sentence is needed. The scene makes us compare the fantasies of transcendent love with bodily limitation. Then the story continues past the funeral, refusing to make Emma's death the final image. What happens to Charles, Berthe, and Homais becomes as important as the death that caused it.

At the ending, list who has acquired status and who has lost protection. Homais receives official distinction. Berthe is sent into poor circumstances and factory work. Charles dies after grief and discovery. The contrast is so pointed that a reader can mistake it for a simple moral reversal in which the worst person wins. But ask what kind of social mechanism can produce that outcome. Recognition belongs to public rhetoric; vulnerability is borne privately. The narrative offers a pattern that reaches beyond the named characters. The satirical ending is social criticism by arrangement.

For any passage, try a four-part annotation: identify the visible action, the words used to describe it, the value judgment implied by those words, and the people excluded from the judgment. The method works with Emma's romantic dreams and with Homais's scientific speeches. It also protects us against our own cleverness. Readers who detect irony can become too pleased with their superiority, and the novel's social world is full of such pleased judges. A close reading is strongest when it can recognize a character's error without denying the genuine feeling or constraint that made the error possible.

## Madame Bovary — The 1857 Trial, Translations, and Influence

Madame Bovary appeared first in installments in the Revue de Paris between October and December 1856, and as a book in 1857. It quickly became a public controversy. Flaubert, the journal's manager, and its printer were prosecuted on charges involving public morals and religious propriety. They were acquitted in February 1857. These facts matter not only because they made the novel notorious. The dispute centered on what readers should infer when a story depicts adultery and desire without having the author appear to condemn them in an unmistakable voice. The trial was, in part, an argument about narrative form.

One side treated the novel's sensuous passages as invitations to share Emma's pleasure. Defenders argued that a work must be understood in the pattern of its whole, including the consequences of decisions and the distance between character and narrator. We should not pretend that the acquittal amounted to universal artistic freedom or that every objection was foolish. The prosecution shows the pressure placed on fiction to give readers clear moral instructions. Flaubert's method often refuses that instruction while leaving room for moral judgment. It asks a reader to work rather than wait for the author's verdict.

The publication history is more complicated than a simple movement from manuscript to magazine to definitive book. Flaubert revised extensively, and the periodical editors made or demanded suppressions. The 1857 book text differs from what appeared in the Revue de Paris. Scholars study these variations to distinguish authorial changes, editorial intervention, and the effects of censorship. For a lecture based on the story, we can state the principal events with confidence. For an argument about a particular sentence, paragraph break, or insinuation, we need to name the edition and, ideally, check the French witnesses. A claim that all readers have always encountered the same passage would be historically careless.

Translation makes another layer of choice unavoidable. A French sentence can move between a narrator's grammar and a character's emotional idiom in ways that do not transfer automatically into English. The translator must select rhythm, diction, degrees of familiarity, and structures for indirect thought. A version can make irony more conspicuous or more elusive. Neither a smooth modern English text nor an older translation should be mistaken for the bare unmediated voice of Flaubert. The practical method is to use a dependable translation for sustained reading and compare significant passages with the French text when making a strong claim about technique.

Flaubert's contribution to the history of realism is not the discovery that ordinary people lead ordinary lives. Earlier fiction had attended to domestic routines, money, social habits, and emotional misunderstanding. Madame Bovary intensifies the relation between precisely observed detail and a narration that withholds a direct authorial moral. Objects are chosen for their social meaning, conversation exposes habitual phrases, and free indirect style can make desire visible from within while allowing irony to form around it. The result is not realism understood as a camera recording facts. It is a highly constructed artistic experience of how people perceive and misperceive the world they share.

That construction mattered for later fiction. Writers and critics have treated Flaubert as a major figure in the shaping of impersonal narration and stylistic precision. His importance can be considered alongside, rather than in place of, Austen, Balzac, Stendhal, George Eliot, and other authors whose methods developed the representation of social experience and inner life. A single line of influence from Flaubert to every modern novelist would be too simple. What does endure is the question his work poses: how can narrative language hold a character's consciousness without agreeing with it, and how can form make criticism possible without a sermon?

The novel's influence is sometimes framed as a triumph of pure art over moralism. That formula also needs examination. The book is centrally concerned with whose lives carry the cost of illusions: Hippolyte loses his leg, Berthe loses protection, Charles loses the confidence that he understood his marriage. Its form does not remove ethical questions. It changes the position from which those questions are asked. Instead of receiving rules, readers must discover when a lovely phrase disguises another person's harm and when a conventional judgment disguises an unrecognized limitation.

Read the historical record and the fiction together but do not collapse them. The court's argument is evidence of public conflict around literature in 1857; it does not establish that every reader responded the same way. Flaubert's letters offer a record of his artistic ambitions; they do not automatically tell us what every sentence in the finished novel must mean. The novel is an object with textual variants, translated forms, and a long history of interpretation. The strongest measure of its importance is not merely that it was prosecuted and admired. It is that the arrangement of its voices still teaches us to hear a sentence twice: once as someone wants it to be understood, and again in the light of what the world around the sentence makes visible.
