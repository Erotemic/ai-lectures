# Anna Karenina — Consciousness, Perception, and Free Indirect Style

Tolstoy's narrator often places us extraordinarily close to a character without handing over the entire book to that character's first-person voice. A room can be described from outside, then suddenly be colored by the mind of the person entering it. We may read a sentence that has the grammar of third-person narration but carries a character's vocabulary, fear, or expectation. Literary scholars often call this free indirect discourse. The term is useful, but the effect matters more: the reader is invited into a consciousness without being told to trust every conclusion reached there.

At the ball, Kitty sees Vronsky's attention turn toward Anna. The scene has a public arrangement—people dancing, looking, and speaking politely—but for Kitty its meaning changes sharply. A glance becomes evidence that her expected future will not occur. Her interpretation is not foolish; it is grounded in real behavior. Yet it has been shaped by what she hoped to see. The narration can make us feel her humiliation while also showing the broader social picture. We have access to more than one participant's experience.

Anna's later perceptions become more volatile. A brief absence by Vronsky can acquire the meaning of abandonment; an ordinary comment becomes part of a feared conspiracy; passing strangers seem to display the selfishness of humanity. The narration records how fear alters attention. It does not turn the object of fear into confirmed fact. This difference between the intensity of a perception and the reliability of the belief derived from it is central to the novel's tragic power. We can understand Anna's suffering without endorsing every accusation she makes.

Levin's mental life uses a different rhythm. He may be moved by the physical pattern of mowing and then pulled into a theory about labor or faith. He attempts to generalize from immediate experience, often discovering that people do not behave according to his abstractions. The narrator can enter his elation and then expose a contradiction between what he thinks he has understood and what happens next. Such gentle correction does not strip him of dignity. It prevents his philosophical concerns from becoming the novel's unquestioned doctrine.

Karenin's inner life deserves similar care. His formal way of thinking about marriage can appear cold, but in Anna's near-fatal illness he responds with extraordinary forgiveness. A third-person narrative can show both the rigidity of habitual speech and the emergence of genuine compassion. If we judged him only by Anna's angry perception, we would miss his capacity to change. If we judged him only by that act of forgiveness, we would ignore later harshness. The narrator makes the difference between an individual action and an entire person difficult to simplify.

Tolstoy also changes speed. A brief gesture may receive prolonged attention, while months of ordinary living pass in a few sentences. The famous descriptions of work, childbirth, the deathbed, the ball, and the railway gain power from these changes of tempo. A sudden consciousness of mortality can make time slow almost to a stop. A social visit may be over before its participants realize what has changed. The form reflects experience without merely recording events in equal chronological units.

Translation complicates the analysis. The sentence rhythms, levels of formality, and markers of a character's inner language differ among English versions. Constance Garnett's public-domain translation gives one accessible entrance; modern translations may choose different words or degrees of literalness. An argument about a particular Russian expression should be checked against the Russian text and a named translation, not attributed indiscriminately to every edition. The broad formal patterns survive translation, but their exact texture does not remain unchanged.

To practice, choose Anna's arrival at the railway station, Kitty at the ball, or Levin during a day of mowing. Mark where the narration offers observable action and where it begins to sound like a character's private judgment. Then ask what someone standing nearby could verify. The point is not to expose the characters as liars. It is to see how a novel can respect an inner life while showing its partial knowledge. That achievement, more than any easy label of psychological realism, helps explain the book's continuing influence.
