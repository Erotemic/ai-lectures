# Frankenstein — The 1818 and 1831 Texts, Authorship, and Revision

Mary Shelley began the story that became Frankenstein in the summer of 1816, during a gathering near Lake Geneva where she, Percy Bysshe Shelley, Lord Byron, John Polidori, and others discussed ghost stories and challenged one another to write them. The surviving manuscript record shows Mary Shelley's sustained work transforming a short conception into a novel during 1816 and 1817. The published book appeared anonymously in three volumes at the beginning of 1818. Its anonymity contributed to early uncertainty about authorship. A new edition in 1823 named Mary Shelley, and she substantially revised the book for the one-volume edition of 1831.

The word 'the text' becomes complicated here. Readers may encounter the 1818 novel, the 1831 revision, or an edition that mixes features and silently modernizes spelling and punctuation. These are not interchangeable witnesses. In the 1818 narrative Elizabeth is Victor's cousin and member of the household from childhood; in 1831 Shelley gives her a different origin as a child taken into the family. The revised version also changes parts of Victor's early history and strengthens language of destiny or fatal influence in places where the earlier text can emphasize his agency. A lecture that explains these as if they were features of one fixed novel would misrepresent the evidence.

The manuscript history is richer still. Draft notebooks survive in Mary Shelley's handwriting, and the Shelley-Godwin Archive makes them available for inspection. Percy Shelley made suggestions and alterations during drafting and the preparation of the fair copy. Recognizing his role as a collaborator in revision does not transfer authorship of the novel to him. Nor does the surviving record permit a simplistic reconstruction of every sentence's origin. Scholarship can identify particular hands and changes while admitting gaps where pages and early drafts are lost.

The 1831 introduction recounts the famous origin of the story in a ghost-story competition. It is a valuable firsthand retrospective account, but it was written years after the events and shaped by the author reflecting on her earlier life. The manuscript chronology and correspondence can complicate the neatness of the memory. We should neither dismiss the account nor treat its every detail as an unmediated transcript of 1816. The habit of distinguishing source type from source authority is especially important in literary biography.

Mary Shelley's own statements about her 1831 alterations suggest that the core story remained intact. In a broad sense it did: Victor makes life, abandons the creature, loses his loved ones, and pursues revenge. But changes in characterization, emphasis, chapter structure, and causal language affect how readers judge events. The edition therefore matters for interpretation, not merely for bibliographic neatness. Readers who discuss Victor's moral freedom or Elizabeth's social position should know which textual witness supplies their evidence.

Frankenstein's long history of stage and screen adaptation has also changed what many people think the book says. Popular visual traditions often place a thunder-struck laboratory at the center and transfer the name Frankenstein from the creator to the creature. The actual novel gives little technical detail about animation and spends far more time on education, letters, testimony, and responsibility. The creature is not an inarticulate machine. Its capacity for speech is central to the plot. Adaptations can be works in their own right, but they are not substitutes for evidence from Shelley's text.

For a textual exercise, place the opening chapters of an 1818 edition beside those of 1831. Identify changes in Elizabeth's history and in Victor's retrospective account of his interest in science. Then compare the 1818 epigraph from Paradise Lost with the framing materials in the revised edition. Do not assume every variation expresses a single ideological program; changes can have different causes and effects. What the witnesses demonstrate beyond dispute is that Frankenstein was a developing literary work, shaped by its author over time. Studying its revisions improves rather than diminishes our understanding of Mary Shelley's achievement.
