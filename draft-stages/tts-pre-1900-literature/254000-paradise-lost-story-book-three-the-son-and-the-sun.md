# Paradise Lost — Book Three: The Son's Offer and the Sun

After two books among devils, the poem changes its light. Milton invokes holy Light and asks for illumination. The request is personal. He was blind when he composed the epic; he cannot see the daylight his poem describes. He turns from the outer brightness he has lost to an inward power of thought and imagination. The poet is not pretending that blindness gives him infallible knowledge. He calls upon a source of insight because the subject he has chosen lies beyond human sight, even for people whose eyes work perfectly.

In Heaven, the Father and the Son watch Satan approach the world of Adam and Eve. The Father knows what is coming. Satan will deceive the humans; they will eat the forbidden fruit; suffering and death will follow. This creates an immediate difficulty. If God knows the future, can those who act within it truly choose? The Father insists that they can. He made rational creatures free to remain faithful, and the advance knowledge of a choice does not compel it. The angels too had freedom; some used it to rebel. These are the Father's claims within Milton's drama, not a philosophical demonstration that ends centuries of dispute.

A distinction emerges between the fallen angels and the humans who will fall. Satan's rebellion was deliberate, committed with full awareness of the authority it challenged. Humanity will be deceived by an enemy. Its disobedience remains consequential, but divine mercy becomes possible. The Father asks who will accept the burden of rescuing the race from the death it will incur. The assembled heavenly host is silent.

The Son answers. He volunteers to take humanity's nature, suffer death, and defeat death through obedience and love. He does not offer to lead a more successful revolt. He offers himself for those who will fail. The heroic action at the centre of the poem has therefore been announced before the central human disobedience occurs. The Son's willingness to lose his life contrasts with Satan's determination to preserve his injured supremacy at the cost of other lives. Heaven answers the offer with celebration.

Then the story returns to its traveler. Satan has reached the outer regions of the created universe, where the scale of Heaven's work unsettles normal distance. He makes for the Sun and encounters Uriel, an angel charged with watching that brilliant place. Satan disguises his purpose. He does not demand permission to corrupt human beings. Instead he praises creation and claims to be a curious lesser angel who wants to see the new world and honor its Maker.

Uriel is taken in. The angel directs the stranger toward Earth. Milton does not make this mistake evidence that every good being is foolish. The qualities that make a society livable, including trust and openness to sincere questions, give deception a point of entry. Uriel is dealing with someone who has learned to mimic reverence while opposing its object.

Satan departs toward the garden. Two kinds of movement have occupied this book: a Son offering to descend for the sake of others, and an enemy descending to make others suffer for his own pride. The poem has placed both before the reader so that the word hero will not be settled simply by distance traveled, danger faced, or grandeur of speech. The Son's offer is announced before the story has shown us the human beings for whom it matters. We are about to meet them.
