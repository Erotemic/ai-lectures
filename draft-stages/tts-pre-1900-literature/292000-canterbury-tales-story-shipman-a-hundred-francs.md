# The Canterbury Tales — The Shipman’s Tale, a Hundred Francs

A rich merchant in Saint-Denis near Paris manages a prosperous household and spends much of his time counting money. He has a beautiful wife, a fine house and a familiar guest: a monk named Daun John, who calls himself a kinsman and enjoys the hospitality freely offered to him. The relationships appear secure because everyone knows the ordinary forms of courtesy. The merchant trusts the monk, the wife has access to household comforts, and the monk can pass between commercial and domestic spaces.

One day Daun John finds the wife alone. She complains that she needs a hundred francs to pay a debt, but cannot obtain them from her husband without difficulty. She promises the monk sexual favors if he will give her the money. He agrees. The transaction is unromantic in its accounting even if the language of affection is lavish. What the monk does next makes the plot possible: he asks the merchant to lend him a hundred francs, supposedly for his own temporary business. The merchant, about to leave on a trading trip, provides the money to the trusted guest.

Daun John gives the borrowed sum to the wife, and they sleep together. The merchant travels to Flanders, pursues his commercial affairs and returns expecting that the loan will be repaid. When he asks the monk for the money, Daun John says that he has already delivered it to the merchant's wife. The merchant, who cannot know the circumstances of that delivery, believes him. He asks his wife what became of the payment. She says she spent it on clothing and other things appropriate to a respectable merchant's wife, then offers to pay him back with affection in bed.

The merchant may think the exchange has been settled. The reader can follow every transfer: his own money financed the monk's liaison, the monk appears to have discharged a debt by handing the money to the woman who received the benefit, and the wife promises to repay the merchant with something he already expects from the marriage. If anyone has gained, it is not the man who supplied the funds. The humor arises from different kinds of value being treated as though they belong in the same ledger.

The story has the structure of a fabliau, a short comic narrative of sexual trickery, social appetite and embarrassment. Yet it is unusually precise about credit. The merchant has no idea what his money has purchased. The monk has used spiritual status and household intimacy to gain access to funds. The wife has solved a material problem without securing financial independence. Each participant has a different definition of payment, and the final agreement works only because the full accounts remain hidden.

The Shipman speaks as someone accustomed to risk, trade and bargains. His tale does not simply mock a cheated husband; it asks what trust means when friendship, hospitality, marriage and commerce overlap. When the Prioress takes the floor, the shift in tone will be severe. Her devotional tale offers the language of innocent childhood and heavenly intervention, yet contains a violent accusation against an entire religious minority. The pilgrim audience's enjoyment of stories cannot replace the need to judge what those stories do.
