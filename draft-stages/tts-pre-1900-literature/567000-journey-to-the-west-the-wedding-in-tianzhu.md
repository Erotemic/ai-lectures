# Journey to the West: The wedding in Tianzhu

## Three disciples before the throne

In the palace of Tianzhu, Xuanzang sat before a king who believed he was about to become his father-in-law. The monk had come with an imperial travel document, hoping for a seal and a quick departure toward the Buddha's mountain. Instead, a princess had thrown an embroidered ball from her marriage pavilion, struck him on the head, and made him the chosen bridegroom before a street full of witnesses. The ceremony was already being arranged. The real princess, if the woman imprisoned behind the wall of Gold-Spreading Temple spoke the truth, was still waiting for someone to hear her.

The three disciples entered the throne room when summoned. Wukong walked first, looking up at the walls and hanging lamps as freely as he looked at clouds. Bajie came behind him with his snout lifted and his ears moving. Sha Wujing followed, broad-shouldered, carrying himself with the formal calm of a former officer of Heaven. None of them knelt. Their appearance troubled the king more than their lack of courtly ceremony. A guard shouted that they must stand where they were and address the throne from a proper distance.

"If we can take a step forward, why should we take it backward?" Wukong asked. He went forward anyway, and his brothers came with him.

Then he saw Xuanzang standing at the side of the throne, waiting like an attendant. Wukong demanded to know why a man about to marry the king's daughter was not permitted to sit. The king had not expected to be corrected on royal etiquette by a monkey in a monk's robe. But he was trying to make the marriage look honorable, and Wukong's objection had its force. A stool was brought, and Xuanzang, who would gladly have exchanged it for his horse and the open road, was seated.

The king asked their names and what had brought them to this country. Wukong offered his history with the breadth of a court proclamation. He had been born from a stone on Flower-Fruit Mountain, mastered transformations, fought dragons and crossed out names in the registers of death. He had held office in Heaven, declared himself Great Sage Equal to Heaven, upset the celestial banquet, and been shut beneath a mountain for five hundred years. Then Xuanzang had freed him and given him a different task: to guard a pilgrim walking west for the scriptures.

The old events were not told as a boast alone. Standing in that palace was the creature who had once thought himself answerable to no one, now introducing himself as another man's disciple. The king had not expected his prospective son-in-law to travel with someone like that.

Bajie was questioned next. He described his former command of Heaven's river forces as Marshal Tianpeng, his drunken misconduct at a celestial banquet, his fall to earth, his rebirth as a pig-faced being, and his eventual promise to assist the pilgrim. He grew so delighted with the opportunity to describe himself that his ears and snout moved more vigorously with every sentence. Xuanzang told him to restrain himself. Bajie folded his hands and made an elaborate attempt to look scholarly.

Sha's account was quieter. He had served as the Curtain-Raising General in the heavenly palace. After breaking a precious vessel, he had been punished and cast down to the River of Flowing Sand, where he harmed those who crossed his path. Guanyin had offered him a new course. He was now Sha Wujing, carrying burdens and guarding the man who had taken him into the company.

The king had summoned three servants of a holy monk and discovered three former offenders of Heaven. He was delighted by their power and afraid of what they might do in a room full of court officials. His astrologers chose a favorable day for the wedding: the twelfth, four days away. He ordered rooms prepared in the imperial garden, generous vegetarian meals served, and a splendid celebration arranged. With the date fixed, the attendants behaved as if Xuanzang's consent had already been obtained.

## A garden that became a prison

At sunset the four companions were given a quiet pavilion and food. Bajie welcomed the change from roadside inns and demon caves. He ate until the servants could hardly keep the dishes filled. Then he sat back and wondered aloud whether marriage to a princess might involve breakfast as well as supper. Xuanzang turned on Wukong as soon as the courtiers had departed.

"I told you I wanted only the travel document," he said. "You brought me to the marriage pavilion. Now the king calls me his son-in-law. What am I supposed to do?"

Wukong did not immediately make light of the fear. He reminded Xuanzang of the story they had heard at the monastery: a girl claiming to be the genuine princess, held behind a locked door while another girl lived comfortably in the palace. The king's face, Wukong had noticed, looked shadowed by some unresolved trouble, and the supposed princess had not yet appeared close enough for him to inspect her. This was precisely why they had followed the marriage into court.

"When she comes out before her father," Wukong said, "I will see her face. Then we'll learn whether a wedding is the matter before us or something else entirely."

He added, with the mischief Xuanzang knew too well, that if the girl proved to be genuine, perhaps their master could simply remain a prince in the palace while the disciples fetched the scriptures. Xuanzang was furious. They had come through fire, flood, betrayal, winter roads and more false monasteries than he cared to remember. He was not going to abandon his vows for a king's painted pavilion. At the threat of the tightening-spell, Wukong dropped his joke and begged him not to recite it. If need be, he said, they would interrupt the ceremony and leave by force. But first they must give themselves a chance to discover the truth.

Bajie was ready for sleep. Sha tried to quiet the quarrel. Outside, the garden smelled of flowers, and bells sounded from farther rooms of the palace. To everyone else the next few days promised a festival. Xuanzang looked at the shuttered windows and heard the evening passing without bringing him nearer his destination.

In the morning the king invited him to inspect the garden. Its pools were fed by narrow channels. Bridges arched over water where fish appeared between the stones. There were pavilions for music, paths bordered by flowering trees, and galleries hung with paintings of the four seasons. The king meant to honor the man his daughter had chosen, and Xuanzang could not fail to see the care and wealth displayed. It did not make the arrangement any less threatening.

The king took Xuanzang to a table apart from the others. Bajie objected to being left elsewhere. He explained to the sovereign, with a frankness that caused a stir among the officials, that master and disciples had not spent years together only to be separated at the moment a marriage bargain was made. The king was determined to keep the ugly attendants away from his daughter, but could not afford to offend them. He assigned them another pavilion within reach and ordered more meals.

On the walls of the royal gallery hung paintings of spring, summer, autumn and winter, each accompanied by a poem. The king saw the monk examining the verses and asked him to compose answering poems. Xuanzang forgot the marriage for a little while. Here was the language he had learned long before he knew what a desert journey meant: seasons turning, blossoms opening, swallows returning, autumn fruit ripening, snow upon stone. He offered verses of his own. The king was delighted and had them written down so that musicians could set them to melody.

There was a quiet cruelty in that agreeable afternoon. The king saw the verses as evidence that his future son-in-law belonged among cultivated courtiers. Xuanzang saw them as a brief reminder that he was still a scholar, not only a traveler who slept on the earth and waited for his companions to save him. Neither meaning required a wedding. Yet the more gracefully he behaved, the more certain the king became that everything was proceeding well.

At the other pavilion, Bajie ate and drank and declared that life could hardly be improved. When the meal ended he proposed going straight to bed. Sha told him that sleeping immediately after eating so much was no discipline at all. Bajie answered with a proverb of his own invention, or close enough, insisting that a full belly required a good lie-down or the food would go to waste. Later he shouted across the garden about the advantages of having a royal in-law. Xuanzang threatened to discipline him; Wukong grabbed Bajie and made a show of holding him still. The pig fell to his knees and begged the prospective royal bridegroom for mercy. The courtiers had to intervene before the noise grew worse.

A ceremony was being prepared, and yet this family of monks could scarcely manage an afternoon of quiet. It would have been funny to Xuanzang if the king's daughter were not still waiting for him behind the next door in the calendar.

## The day of the wedding

Four days passed. The royal kitchens prepared hundreds of banquet places. Musicians rehearsed. Officials arranged the gifts and procession. Within the women's quarters, the supposed princess dressed among attendants who believed themselves devoted to her happiness. But she had noticed the faces of Xuanzang's companions. They were not frightened by her royal title, and the small one had stared at everything too closely. She asked the king to send the three disciples out of the city before the marriage. Their appearances terrified her, she said, and might disturb the ceremony.

The king found this sensible. He summoned Xuanzang and the disciples to receive the endorsed travel document. The documents were stamped and returned. Gold and silver were offered for the journey. Bajie moved toward the gifts by instinct, and Wukong accepted the formalities without showing his intentions.

Then the king announced that the disciples were free to go to the Buddha while their master remained behind. The words broke through Xuanzang's composure. He sprang from his place and caught Wukong by the hand.

"You're leaving me?" he whispered. "After all this, you're simply leaving me?"

Wukong squeezed the hand and gave him a look that could not be understood by the officials. Then he spoke loudly for the court to hear. The monk should enjoy the honors offered, he said; the disciples would seek the scriptures and return to visit when they had finished. Xuanzang could only stare at him. There was no comfort in the words until he remembered the whispered promise made in the garden.

The three disciples walked out with the officers of the court. They returned to the lodging house, where Bajie asked whether they were really going. Wukong ignored the question until the door was closed. Then he told Sha and Bajie to stay with the baggage and answer no unnecessary questions. He pulled out a hair, blew on it, and made an exact copy of himself to sit beside them. The real Wukong rose into the air and changed into a small bee.

He entered the palace through a window open to the warm garden. The king and Xuanzang were approaching the inner court. Gold ornaments rang softly on the attendants' clothing; the courtiers were preparing to welcome the bridegroom. Xuanzang's head was bowed. All the honors of the palace had become a weight he could neither accept nor put down.

A tiny bee settled on the rim of his monk's cap and crawled toward his ear.

"Master," came Wukong's unmistakable whisper, "I'm here. Don't be afraid."

The words reached Xuanzang alone. He lifted his head. He did not smile, not with the king beside him and the wedding drums sounding ahead, but he found the breath to follow the procession. The so-called princess waited beyond the next doors. Wukong would soon have his first close look at her, and the woman behind the monastery wall was still waiting for an answer.
