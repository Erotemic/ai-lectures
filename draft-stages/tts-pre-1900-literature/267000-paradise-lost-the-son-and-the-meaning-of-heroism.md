# Paradise Lost — The Son and the Meaning of Heroism

What does it mean to be the hero of an epic? In ancient poems the answer often involves a warrior of extraordinary courage whose actions change the fate of a people. Milton adopts many of the outward signs of that tradition: vast armies, formal speeches, catalogs of leaders, divine councils, battles, dangerous journeys, and a style elevated above ordinary conversation. But he refuses to make victory through force the highest achievement his poem can imagine.

Satan appears first in a role that resembles a heroic commander. He rallies a defeated host, plans a dangerous expedition, crosses a terrifying landscape, and challenges powers larger than himself. The signs are recognizable even to readers who have not read Homer or Virgil. Milton then changes the moral weight attached to those signs. The expedition targets an innocent couple; the council that sends him is politically manipulated; the enemy's most effective weapon will be persuasive deceit.

In Book Three the Son volunteers to redeem humanity. He has not been defeated, and he does not need to prove himself through revenge. His commitment is to suffer on behalf of others. The offer makes future self-sacrifice a positive form of action, rather than merely the absence of courage. A person may be most heroic not when defeating an enemy in a display of superior might, but when freely accepting a burden that protects the vulnerable.

The battle in Book Six complicates the contrast. The Son also enters the war in Heaven and expels Satan's host. He is not incapable of military power. Yet the war's conclusion reveals the limits of thinking about morality solely as successful combat. The Father has allowed angelic armies to struggle for days, and the Son's appearance ends the conflict decisively. If the poem merely wanted to celebrate a stronger warrior, it could stop here. Instead it turns toward a garden and a choice with no armies in sight.

The narrator's opening to Book Nine explicitly shifts the notion of heroism. The coming event, Adam and Eve's disobedience, is not a war of visible armies. The poet defends subjects traditionally judged less grand: patience, faithfulness, restraint, and suffering willingly undergone. This does not make the garden a minor arena. On the contrary, the smallest act of eating fruit has consequences wider than any battle narrated earlier.

Book Twelve joins the strands. Michael tells Adam that the promised redeemer will defeat death by accepting death, and will rise again. This is a Christian account of salvation in epic form. Readers need not share the theology to recognize the artistic argument: there are different kinds of triumph, and the poem asks us to resist identifying greatness with domination. The Son's action is heroic because it is directed toward the good of others rather than the enhancement of his own image.

One of the best tests is to compare three speeches. Satan speaks to the fallen host after a disastrous battle. Abdiel speaks alone against a large group prepared to mock him. The Son speaks when the Father asks who will rescue humanity. Each speaker risks something, and each has a different relation to audience approval. Satan needs his followers' recognition; Abdiel is willing to lose recognition; the Son offers action before anyone demands a display.

Milton does not eliminate all the pleasures of old epic; he uses them with extraordinary skill. But he asks the listener to inspect the purpose of the action, the vulnerability of those affected, and the truthfulness of the agent. Great deeds may involve warfare. Greatness itself cannot be measured by the height of the walls one breaks or the number of enemies one drives away.
