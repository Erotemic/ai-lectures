# Journey to the West: The doctor of Zhuzi

## The city beyond the stench

When the disciples at last emerged from Seven-Cut Mountain, there was an enormous pleasure in clean air. The foul road behind them had demanded more from Bajie's shoulders and appetite than he cared to remember. Ahead lay a paved approach, travelers in summer clothes, and the towers of a prosperous city. A yellow banner flew from the walls, its writing turned now and then toward the wind.

Xuanzang asked what kingdom they had come to. Wukong grinned and accused him of not knowing how to read. Had he not seen the banner? The master protested that he could read scriptures and imperial documents perfectly well. The banner was far away and moving too fast for him to distinguish the letters. Wukong announced that it said Zhuzi Kingdom. Bajie and Sha, who had no desire to quarrel about eyesight, told the master to ignore his elder disciple's boasting.

They went through the city gates, crossing bridges and streets lined with shops. The people wore fine clothes, spoke clearly, and traded briskly. Xuanzang saw signs of orderly government and was relieved to find a place where the next business might be as simple as obtaining a royal seal. But the shoppers were not used to his company. Bajie's snout, Sha's towering body, and Wukong's monkey face drew a crowd. Some people laughed; children ran close enough to throw stones and tiles. The master begged his followers not to react. Bajie, who normally had plenty to say in answer to insult, tucked his nose into his robe and hurried along.

They found the office for visiting envoys, the Hall of Common Intercourse. Its officers took Xuanzang's credentials, listened to the explanation of an imperial pilgrimage from Tang China, and offered the party a guest room and provisions. Wukong complained that such a master should have been entertained in the principal hall, not sent to a side chamber. Xuanzang pointed out that this was not a subject territory of Tang. The officials had other guests to receive. Hospitality was not the same as submission.

An attendant brought rice, flour, vegetables, tofu, dried mushrooms, and other food that the pilgrims could cook for themselves. Xuanzang asked whether the king would receive him that day. The attendant said that the king had been ill for a long time, but this happened to be an auspicious day on which he had been brought to court to issue a public notice seeking a physician. If Xuanzang wished to get his travel document signed, he should go immediately, before the court retired again.

Xuanzang put on his formal robe, took the passport, and went toward the palace. He left strict instructions for his disciples to prepare supper and cause no disturbance in the streets. Wukong listened attentively, as he always did before deciding on a plan that the master would not approve.

## The story told to a sick king

The king had scarcely strength to sit upon his throne. Attendants carried papers and cushions and spoke softly in his presence. Yet he welcomed the envoy from the eastern empire. He took the travel document and asked about Tang's kings, the rise and fall of earlier dynasties, and the circumstances that had led the Tang emperor to send a monk so far west.

Xuanzang answered at length. He began with legendary ancient rulers and passed through the competing states, unification under Qin, the long histories of Han and later dynasties, and the establishment of Tang. He then told the story connected with his mission: how a river dragon had been condemned after changing the amount of rain it was ordered to give, how the ruler had promised to protect him, and how the general who was destined to execute the dragon fulfilled his duty while appearing to sleep at a chessboard. The emperor had been drawn into a terrifying encounter with death and the otherworld. The Buddhist services performed afterward had opened the way for the journey to obtain scriptures.

The sick king listened with interest. The narrative connected distant dynasties, moral debts, and the hope that a religious journey could help the living and the dead. In Zhuzi, however, the king himself was being measured by a more immediate question: whether any physician could relieve a sickness that had kept him from governing for three years. The court's great historical curiosity could not make the king rise from his cushions.

While Xuanzang remained at court, Wukong suggested that he and Bajie go out to buy condiments for their meal. Bajie wanted to purchase the nearest suitable food and return. Wukong kept finding reasons to walk farther. One shop was too small, another too crowded, and surely the next street would have something better. Bajie complained, and the monkey coaxed him on.

Near the drum tower they found a thick crowd. Bajie refused to go into it. People had already stared and thrown things at him. He wanted no more handling by strangers. Wukong told him to stand quietly by a wall while he bought the food. The pig gave him the bowls and waited, facing the bricks so no one could see his snout.

Wukong slipped into the crowd and read the notice. The king had suffered a grave illness for years. Any person, local or foreign, who could cure him would be honored so greatly that the king was prepared to share his realm. Wukong immediately decided that this sounded like a fine opportunity. He had not come west to open a medical practice, but he did enjoy unsettling a court and proving that a problem was smaller than people thought.

He made himself invisible, removed the notice from the wall, and raised a gust that scattered the people. Then he returned to the place where Bajie was standing, found the pig half asleep, folded the royal notice, and slipped it into Bajie's clothing. Without waking him, he went back to the guesthouse.

## A pig accused of medicine

The officers responsible for guarding the proclamation searched frantically when the wind subsided. There had been eunuchs, guards, and spectators enough to see the notice, but no one could tell who had taken it. Then an officer noticed a strip of paper protruding from the garment of a strange monk by the wall. He asked whether Bajie had removed the proclamation.

Bajie looked up. The first guards fell backward at the sight of his face. Recovering, they seized his sleeves and demanded that he come to the palace and treat the king. Bajie took out the paper and understood the trick. He cursed Wukong for having left him to carry it. He said he knew nothing of treating kings and that the monk who had taken the notice was his elder brother.

The guards refused to believe him. The evidence was in his own clothing. They had been ordered to bring back whoever removed the notice. Bajie tried to leave. A group of men pulled at him and discovered that a dozen ordinary soldiers could no more move him than they could drag a rooted tree from the ground. He warned them not to provoke his temper. The older eunuchs asked more careful questions. He explained that he was traveling west with a monk from Tang, that the master was at court to have his passport stamped, and that Wukong was the mischievous disciple who had gone ahead to the guesthouse.

One of the eunuchs had indeed seen the foreign monk enter the palace. The story began to sound possible. Rather than argue in the middle of the street, the officials marched Bajie to the guesthouse. Along the way, the pig told them to treat Wukong with proper ceremony. If they came pulling and shouting, the monkey was likely to take offense; if they bowed and called him an honored elder, perhaps he would agree to help.

At the guesthouse Wukong sat comfortably, behaving as if he had expected the visit all along. The officers asked him to treat the king. He showed no embarrassment at having implicated Bajie. He accepted the notice as his own work and asked why the king had not come in person to request his aid. The officials explained that their lord was much too sick to travel. With elaborate courtesy they led the monkey toward the palace.

Bajie remained offended. He had been hauled through the streets by his sleeves while the true culprit returned to supper. Yet he also knew his brother's talent for turning a crowd, a notice, and a royal decree into a spectacle. He had become the unwilling first patient in the monkey's new career as a doctor: he needed treatment for wounded pride.

## The four ways of knowing a disease

When Wukong appeared before the sick king, the result was not encouraging. He advanced with confidence, loudly announcing himself. The king saw the sharp-faced monkey coming toward his bed, heard the harsh voice, and recoiled so violently that attendants had to carry him back into the inner palace. The officials blamed Wukong. No physician, they said, should frighten a weakened patient before examining him.

Wukong answered that courtly silence would never cure a disease. He recited the traditional four approaches used in medicine: observe the patient's appearance, listen to the voice and breathing, ask about the course of the illness, and feel the pulse. He described what a careful physician might learn from complexion, speech, appetite, sleep, the timing of symptoms, and the quality of the vessels under the fingers. The words impressed the royal doctors, though Xuanzang, who knew his disciple's habits, was already uneasy.

The king said he could not bear the sight of a stranger. Wukong proposed an examination without entering the sickroom. He would diagnose the pulse by strings stretched from the patient's wrists to his own hand, a method the court had heard of but never seen. The report was carried inside. Since ordinary treatment had failed, the king gave permission.

Xuanzang confronted Wukong when the monkey returned to the principal hall. Had he ever read the classic medical books? Had he actually learned the qualities of herbs, the names of diseases, and the methods of diagnosis? The master had followed his disciple through battles with demons and aerial journeys over seas, but he had never seen him sit with a medical text. What made him think he could undertake a king's treatment?

Wukong was untroubled. He claimed to have some simple remedies for difficult cases. If the king recovered, they might obtain a grateful escort out of the country. If not, he joked, what could the court do to an immortal monkey but accuse him of malpractice? Xuanzang did not find the joke reassuring. He feared the court would hold the entire party responsible if the king died.

The monkey took three hairs from his tail and transformed them into long, fine threads. He held them up before Xuanzang like the instruments of a physician. Each thread was made to reach between the hand of the patient and his own. The attendants came to summon him. Wukong followed them into the inner rooms while Xuanzang waited behind with growing concern.

The royal notice was no longer a mere piece of paper in Bajie's robe. The monkey had committed himself to an examination. The king's illness was not yet explained, no medicine had yet been prepared, and the master still did not know whether his disciple was making a brilliant diagnosis or inventing a new way to get them into trouble.
