# The Brothers Karamazov — Who Is Responsible for the Murder?

The factual answer to the mystery of Fyodor Pavlovich Karamazov's murder comes from Smerdyakov's confession to Ivan. Smerdyakov says he feigned incapacity, killed the old man, and stole the money. The legal answer is different: Dmitri is convicted. A third answer concerns the broader moral responsibility of people who hated Fyodor, expected violence, or encouraged dangerous ideas without wishing to confront their consequences. Dostoevsky's novel becomes powerful because those three questions are related but not identical.

Dmitri is the obvious suspect. He has publicly threatened his father, demanded money, competed with him for Grushenka, entered the property with a heavy weapon, and struck the servant Grigory. He has gone on to spend money whose source he explains poorly. These facts justify serious suspicion and investigation. They do not include proof that he struck the fatal blow. A court that mistakes a complete portrait of a turbulent man for direct proof of an unseen event risks condemning a person for being the kind of person it imagines a murderer must be.

Smerdyakov's agency is sometimes swallowed by the philosophical story. He tells Ivan that Ivan is the true murderer because Ivan's skepticism and departure from the house supplied permission. This is a morally aggressive claim by a man who has actually killed someone. Smerdyakov has grievances and has learned to speak in a vocabulary that exposes Ivan's weaknesses. But he also chooses, plans, deceives, and acts. We can investigate the effects of Ivan's language without allowing Smerdyakov to escape responsibility through a theory of influence.

Ivan's guilt is neither wholly invented nor straightforwardly criminal. He has disliked Fyodor, contemplated the relief of his death, and departed amid signs of danger. The knowledge that Smerdyakov may have taken advantage of his absence breaks him. Yet wishing someone absent, failing to prevent harm, and committing murder require different descriptions. The novel intentionally presses those descriptions together until Ivan can scarcely bear the boundary. A responsible reading must hold the boundary even while asking what duties he may have neglected.

Alyosha's moral responsibility is different again. He has tried to keep contact across the family and has sometimes been absent when a person needed him. Zosima teaches a broad responsibility for the suffering of others, but that moral vocabulary does not mean everyone deserves the same criminal punishment. A sweeping ethic of solidarity can animate care; a sweeping theory of guilt can become a weapon if it is used to ignore evidence, intention, or capacity. Dostoevsky dramatizes both possibilities.

The handling of the three thousand rubles shows how an act can acquire several narrative meanings. Katerina entrusted money to Dmitri; Dmitri spent part, claimed to preserve a remainder, and later displayed funds at Mokroe. Fyodor had separate money intended for Grushenka. Smerdyakov stole that money and presented it to Ivan. The prosecution turns Dmitri's visible spending and threats into a story of theft. Understanding these separate financial paths is not mere bookkeeping. It is a defense against mistaking a familiar kind of motive for proof of a specific transaction.

The trial is a public performance in which both prosecution and defense use language to make people legible. The jurors must act under uncertainty, but eloquent character sketches are capable of concealing gaps. Ivan's illness weakens his revelation of Smerdyakov's confession. Katerina's letter proves a threat, not an act. Dmitri's alternating remorse and anger make him an unreliable witness to his own condition, but unreliability about character does not automatically mean unreliability about every fact.

The novel does not offer a reassuring fantasy of infallible justice. It shows a legal institution making a consequential error, while the central characters continue to struggle with questions the court cannot decide. Smerdyakov is the murderer. Dmitri bears responsibility for many wrongs but not this murder. Ivan has reason for moral self-examination without being transformed by that reason into the killer. Holding these propositions separately is not pedantry. It is the foundation for any serious reading of the novel's idea that we are implicated in one another's lives.
