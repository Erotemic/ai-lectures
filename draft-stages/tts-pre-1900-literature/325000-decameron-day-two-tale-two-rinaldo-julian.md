# The Decameron — Rinaldo and Saint Julian

Rinaldo d'Asti is traveling on business when he falls into conversation with men he meets along the road. They ask how he prepares for a safe night's lodging. He tells them that he is accustomed to commend himself to Saint Julian, a saint associated with hospitality. He believes the prayer has helped him find shelter in many difficult journeys. His companions hear this with less devotion than interest. They are robbers, and they now know that Rinaldo has money worth taking.

The men wait for a favorable place, seize what he carries, strip him of his goods and clothing, and leave him exposed in the winter cold. One thief mocks Saint Julian, suggesting that Rinaldo may now test whether the saint can provide comfort. Rinaldo staggers toward Castel Guglielmo, searching for any opening into safety. A gate is closed. He finds what shelter he can and trembles outside a house. His prayer has apparently failed at the very moment when he needed protection most.

Inside, a lady is alone. She had expected a lover to visit her, but he has been delayed. Hearing the stranger shivering outside, she sends to discover what has happened. Rinaldo is brought in, warmed, clothed, and fed. The lady is attracted to him and invites him into a more intimate companionship. A night that began in fear and deprivation becomes, from Rinaldo's point of view, an astonishing reward. He is not made prosperous by his own planning. He has found hospitality because another person's plans went wrong, while the stranger's decency makes a place for him.

Meanwhile, the robbers are caught. Their crimes are discovered, and Rinaldo's goods are recovered. In the morning he can travel again with both his property and a story about how well Saint Julian has provided for him. The thief's mockery has been answered, at least to Rinaldo's satisfaction. The saint did not stop the robbery; the favorable end came by a chain of human actions and accidents.

Filostrato tells an adventure of providence and chance without forcing a reader to accept an explanation. One character interprets the entire episode through devotion. Another might see thieves who were unlucky, a lady who was generous and lonely, and a road on which one person happened to approach the right house. The narrator allows Rinaldo's gratitude to stand. The larger comedy lies in the mismatch between what he expected when he prayed for a comfortable lodging and the strange path by which he received it. Fortune can take a blessing that looked like protection and turn it into rescue after injury. The original loss is real, even though the ending restores more than possessions.
