# Journey to the West: The house outside the circle

## A mark around the travelers

The pilgrims had left Heaven-Reaching River behind them. The old turtle's shell had been steadier than the ice on which they had lost their master, and the promise Xuanzang made to him was now another obligation to carry west. The road rose into cold hills. Bare trees stood above frozen streams, and snow lay where the sun could not reach the ground. A winter day's journey grew short as the wind grew sharp.

They came to a stretch where no inn, village, or farmhouse could be seen. Xuanzang and the horse were tired. Bajie complained that his stomach was empty. Sha Wujing had little to offer from the traveling bags. To continue through the mountains without food seemed foolish, but to stop offered no supper either.

Wukong volunteered to search. He could cover miles in a moment, find a place where a meal was being cooked, and return while his companions waited. His first responsibility, however, was to make sure they would still be there when he came back.

He took his iron staff and drew a circle around Xuanzang, Bajie, Sha Wujing, the horse, and the luggage. It was a simple line scored in the ground, but he filled it with protective power. The boundary would keep out malign spirits while he was away. He made the rules plain: do not cross the line, do not follow anyone who offers a better resting place, and do not accept the appearance of comfort as proof that it is safe.

Bajie stared at the circle. He had spent enough nights exposed to the weather to hate the idea of sitting in snow while some imaginary household lay warm nearby. He wanted food, not a geometry lesson from his elder brother. Wukong told him that the line was more useful than a high city wall if the people inside obeyed the warning.

Xuanzang thanked him, though the monk was cold and weary. Sha Wujing promised to watch the luggage and stay with their master. Wukong leapt away.

The protection was strong. The difficulty was that it protected only those who chose to remain within it.

## A meal refused, and taken anyway

Wukong searched the hills until he found a dwelling where an elderly man was cooking rice. Smoke rose from the roof. A pot steamed over the fire. Here at last was something he could bring back to the waiting travelers.

He asked for food. The old man saw a stranger who had appeared without warning and did not welcome him. The monkey's manner did little to reassure a householder who had never met him. When Wukong pressed his request, the old man drove him away with a stick. The blows did not injure the Great Sage, but the refusal was unmistakable. The rice belonged to the man who had cooked it; no guest was entitled to enter and carry it off simply because he was hungry.

Wukong could have returned empty-handed. He could have searched longer. Instead he made himself invisible, slipped back inside, and took cooked rice from the pot. What the old man could not see, he could not defend. Wukong put the food into his begging bowl and hurried toward the hill where he had left his friends.

The theft was small beside the murders and battles of the pilgrimage, but it belonged to the same troubling part of Wukong's character. He could be deeply loyal to his master and still treat another person's property as an inconvenience in the path of his own plan. He had promised to bring food. Once the request had been denied, he preferred to take it rather than confess defeat.

He was pleased with himself as he returned. The food would silence Bajie's complaints and prove that the circle had kept everyone safe while he was away.

## The comfortable house that appears from nowhere

Inside the circle, the cold had begun to win.

Bajie shifted from one foot to the other, staring at the empty road. He said Wukong had been gone too long. Perhaps his elder brother had found a feast and was eating it by himself. Perhaps he had forgotten the rest of them. Why should the travelers remain in a small ring of dirt while darkness fell, when the whole country lay open around them?

Sha Wujing reminded him of the warning. The circle was no prison. It existed because their master had already been seized by monsters too many times. The White Bone Demon had used human appearances. Red Boy had used the form of an endangered child. The goldfish at Heaven-Reaching River had made a road of ice. A traveler who walked out simply because he saw something inviting would be repeating a lesson already paid for at terrible cost.

Then they saw a house.

Where the snowy hillside had seemed empty, a large and prosperous dwelling now stood within reach. Its walls were sound, its entrance welcoming, and the possibility of heat and hospitality almost painful to imagine. It looked like the very refuge they had been longing for.

Xuanzang hesitated. He knew Wukong had told them not to move. But perhaps the house had been hidden by the curve of the mountain. Perhaps the light had changed and revealed a building that had been there all along. Surely asking for shelter was not the same as knowingly walking into danger.

Bajie encouraged these thoughts. The monkey was always warning them against things that might or might not exist. Meanwhile the cold was real. The warmth of a house would be real too. If they waited until Wukong returned, the household might close its gates and refuse them, and Bajie would have endured another night without supper for the sake of a line in the snow.

Sha Wujing objected, but he was outnumbered by weariness and hunger. The group left the circle.

That crossing required only a few steps. Nothing exploded. No giant hand rose from the ground. The power Wukong had placed inside the boundary did not reach out to drag them back by their clothing. They walked to the splendid door and entered.

The rooms seemed ready for guests. Beautiful furnishings stood in order, bedding lay prepared, and garments of fine quilted cloth were laid where travelers might see them. Yet something was wrong. A body lay inside, as if a household's master had died and been abandoned. The splendor around the bed did not make the sight less disturbing. The house was too convenient, too suddenly found, and too still.

Xuanzang wished to leave what belonged to the household untouched. A pilgrim who had sworn himself to poverty could not go into a stranger's home and take fine clothes merely because winter had made him uncomfortable.

Bajie, however, had already noticed the warm garments. He fingered the padded cloth and imagined the relief of wrapping it around his body. Sha Wujing too was tempted by the thought of warmth, though he usually resisted Bajie's more reckless suggestions. The two disciples tried on the quilted waistcoats.

The clothes seized them.

What had seemed ordinary padded fabric drew tight and bound their bodies. Sleeves, seams, and ties became restraints from which their strength could not free them. Bajie struggled and cursed. Sha Wujing pulled against the cloth, but the more urgently they moved, the clearer it became that they had chosen a trap prepared for people who would take the garments.

The illusion broke. The warm house belonged to the designs of a monster. Servants and armed attendants emerged, and the three pilgrims were taken captive. Xuanzang had refused the clothes but had left the protective circle. His carefulness about the property did not save him from the enemy who had arranged the building. The demon carried them into his cave while Wukong was still searching for a meal.

## The circle with no one in it

When Wukong returned with the bowl of rice, the circle was empty. The line remained on the ground, its protective force intact, but the people it was meant to shelter had walked away. He saw tracks leading out. The horse was gone. The baggage was gone. There was no master to feed and no Bajie to complain that the portion was too small.

He stood for a moment over his work. He had not been defeated by a stronger spell. He had been defeated by the fact that his companions had disobeyed him. A boundary can exclude an enemy; it cannot make hungry friends remember why they should stay inside.

Anger came first. He had warned them. He had drawn the circle plainly enough for a child to understand. He had gone hungry and bothered a stranger for their benefit, and while he was away they had done precisely what he forbade. Then fear overtook anger. Xuanzang had been carried off too often for Wukong to assume he could simply follow a trail and arrive before the monster's dinner.

He called on the local mountain spirit and the guardian of the land. They knew the places ordinary travelers could not see. The spirits appeared and explained that the demon ruling the nearby region was known as the One-Horned King of Golden Cave, on Golden-Helmet Mountain. He had laid the illusion and taken the pilgrims into his stronghold.

Wukong demanded to know whether the spirit was powerful. They answered cautiously. A mountain could hold many kinds of being. Some stole bodies by brute strength. Others preferred magic, disguise, and traps. This king had both fighters and a device that made him especially dangerous. Wukong, eager to rescue his master, was more concerned with reaching the cave than with hearing a long account of its defenses.

He arrived before the entrance and called out the demon. The One-Horned King appeared as a formidable opponent, armed and ready. Wukong flourished his staff and demanded the release of the Tang monk. The demon replied with taunts and threats, and the two began to fight.

Their weapons rang across the mountain. They exchanged blows until the air was filled with dust and the servants of the cave watched from a distance. Wukong struck with all the skill he had developed in Heaven and on the roads of the western journey. His opponent survived, pushed back, and returned the challenge. The fight was no easy exchange of three blows followed by a retreat.

Then the One-Horned King produced something that looked almost too simple to matter: a white shining ring.

He threw it upward. The ring moved with a power that did not depend upon its size. Wukong raised his iron staff to strike again and felt the familiar weight vanish from his hand. The weapon flew toward the ring and disappeared into the demon's possession.

The Great Sage stood disarmed.

He had once taken his staff from the dragon kings of the sea. He had shortened it to a needle and carried it behind his ear; he had expanded it into a pillar heavy enough to terrify armies. It had survived furnace, battle, and mountain road. Now a demon had removed it with a single motion of a white ring.

Xuanzang was still inside the cave. Bajie and Sha Wujing remained bound by the clothing they had taken. The circle on the hillside was empty, and the bowl of rice Wukong had stolen for his friends was no use to anyone. To recover his master, he would first have to recover the staff he believed no enemy could take from him.
