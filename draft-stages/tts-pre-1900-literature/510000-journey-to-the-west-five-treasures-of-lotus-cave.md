# Journey to the West: The five treasures of Lotus Cave

## A treasure that vanishes

The Sharp-Witted Ghost and the Clever Insect had left their meeting with the old immortal convinced that they had secured an extraordinary bargain. Their masters had given them two priceless vessels, a purple-gold red gourd and a white jade bottle. They had traded both for a gourd that appeared to contain the sky. When the sun disappeared, they had stood trembling in the dark; when the light returned, the stranger's claim seemed to have been proved. What treasure could be greater than a vessel that swallowed the heavens?

Once the old immortal was out of sight, however, they decided to test their purchase. They threw the gourd into the air and repeated what they remembered of his spell. It fell directly back to the ground. They tried again, altering the words, and again the sky remained exactly where it had always been.

The Clever Insect wondered aloud whether the old immortal had been Sun Wukong in disguise. The Sharp-Witted Ghost objected. Wukong was trapped beneath three enormous mountains. How could he have been strolling about selling gourds?

Before they settled the argument, the gourd disappeared.

The Monkey King had made it from one of his hairs. Watching from above, he had grown concerned that the servants would discover the deception and raise an alarm while he was still nearby. He recalled the hair, and the gourd simply ceased to exist. Nothing fell to the ground. There was no crack, no puff of smoke, no broken shell. The two demons stared at their four empty hands.

They debated running away. Returning without the captured monkey, without their masters' vessels, and without even the supposed sky-containing treasure would surely earn them a beating. But fleeing would be little better. They went back to Lotus Cave and entered trembling.

Gold Horn and Silver Horn were drinking when their servants knelt before them. The kings asked whether they had captured Sun Wukong. The servants bowed lower. Asked a second time, they said nothing. At last they told the story of the extraordinary immortal from Penglai, the stolen darkness, the bad bargain, and the treasure that had vanished.

Gold Horn listened until he understood. It had been Wukong. The supposed immortal was another of the monkey's transformations, and the darkness itself must have been a trick.

The servants tried to protest. They had seen the sky swallowed. No ordinary monkey could do such a thing.

'You saw darkness,' Gold Horn replied. 'You did not see the sky inside a gourd.'

The loss was serious. But Lotus Cave was not empty of weapons. The brothers still possessed a sword marked by seven stars, a magical plantain fan, and a golden rope. The rope in particular was powerful, if one knew the spell to tighten it and the separate spell to loosen it. It lay with their mother, a nine-tailed fox who lived some distance away at Yalong Cave.

Gold Horn sent two messengers, Mountain Tiger and Sea Dragon, to fetch her. They were to ask the old lady to visit her sons, and to bring the rope with her. If the monkey could not be caught in the gourd or bottle, he could be bound and carried back like a bundle of firewood.

They hurried into the mountain.

Above the path, something small and harmless seemed to hover in the warm air. It was Sun Wukong, who had changed himself into a fly and followed every word of their errand. He had lost the advantage of surprise with the gourd, but now he knew of a third treasure, a mother who guarded it, and a road along which that mother must travel.

He flew ahead.

## The lady of Yalong Cave

The two messengers were not expecting to meet another of their own kind. A little mountain demon appeared on the path and asked where they were going so quickly. They told him, for the place was familiar and the road seemed safe. In another moment Wukong struck them down. He took the forms and the message they had carried and went to Yalong Cave.

The cave belonged to an aged fox demon with nine tails. She was the mother whom the Gold and Silver Horn Kings addressed with elaborate respect. The servants around her bowed as she came forward. Wukong, wearing the face of a messenger, bowed too.

He did not enjoy it.

The Monkey King would bow to the Buddha, to Guanyin, and, by a vow he had come to take seriously, to his master Xuanzang. He had once refused even the hierarchy of Heaven, naming himself Great Sage Equal to Heaven rather than submitting to an office appointed by the Jade Emperor. Yet here he was, kneeling before a fox spirit he intended to deceive.

He endured the indignity because the rope mattered more than his pride.

His message was urgent. The kings had captured the Tang monk and his disciples, but Sun Wukong was dangerous; the old lady must come and help her sons. She must bring the golden rope.

The fox was delighted. She had long wanted to see this famous monk, whose flesh the demons believed could grant immortality. She ordered her attendants to prepare a sedan chair and brought out the rope. Once everyone was ready, the little procession set off toward Lotus Cave: the old mother riding, servants carrying, the messenger running beside them.

Wukong watched the guards closely. He could not simply seize the rope while they stood packed together. He needed the old fox separated from the company.

He pulled a hair from his body and turned it into a great flat cake. He placed it where the carriers would see it, then shouted that some excellent food had been dropped on the path. The attendants, whose appetites were easy to awaken, rushed toward the bread and began to quarrel over it.

For the old fox, the last part of the journey was brief.

Wukong struck her dead. He took the golden rope and made himself look exactly like her, down to the aged face and the movement of her tails. From other hairs he fashioned attendants to carry the sedan. Then the procession continued as if nothing had happened.

When the false mother reached Lotus Cave, the two kings came out to greet her. They bowed, and Wukong accepted their reverence with the composed dignity of a mother inspecting her sons' housekeeping.

The scene was almost too pleasing. At the entrance the cave guards were binding prisoners, preparing food, and arguing over their master's guests. The Tang monk was somewhere within. So were Bajie and Sha Wujing, who had been captured after Wukong was crushed under the three mountains.

The fox mother glanced around with exaggerated interest. She asked whether the pig prisoner had particularly large ears. Those ears, she suggested, would make an excellent accompaniment to wine.

Bajie, hanging helplessly among the captives, heard the remark and looked at her.

He had lived beside Wukong long enough to recognize a familiar habit beneath an unfamiliar face. The fox's manner was suspicious, her joke too pointed. Then he saw what the disguise had failed to conceal.

'Aha,' he cried, 'the old lady has a monkey's tail!'

Wukong wished for a moment that Bajie could be packed into one of the gourds and left there until the rescue was finished.

Gold Horn turned toward his mother. Silver Horn moved closer. Before either could act, other servants came running from the mountain with dreadful news. They had found the real old lady's body beside the road, and the carriers were dead. The person seated in their cave could not be their mother.

The false mother leaped out of reach. Her face became the face of the Monkey King. In a flash he was through the entrance and into the air, carrying the golden rope.

The two kings had now lost three of their five treasures, and their mother was dead. Gold Horn's grief was mixed with calculation. He had underestimated the monkey repeatedly. Perhaps the safest course was to release the Tang monk and let the pilgrims go. But Silver Horn was furious. He would not forgive the killing, the theft, or the public disgrace.

He would pursue Wukong himself.

## The wrong spell

Silver Horn found the Monkey King outside and challenged him. The brothers fought with sword and staff until the mountainside rang like a smith's forge. Wukong could have struck an ordinary bandit into the ground, but Silver Horn knew spells and had the fierce confidence of a demon whose home had been attacked.

After a long exchange Wukong decided to use the new treasure. He drew the golden rope, cast it toward his opponent, and waited for the binding to take hold.

The rope was real. The problem was that Wukong did not know how to use it.

Silver Horn knew both the tightening and loosening formulas. He cried the appropriate words, and the golden cord reversed its course. Instead of circling the demon, it caught Wukong. The coils drew tight around his arms and legs. A moment earlier he had been the one with the stolen weapon; now he was a helpless captive.

Silver Horn dragged him to Lotus Cave amid the delighted shouts of the guards. At last the worst of their enemies had been caught. They searched him and recovered the red gourd and the jade bottle. They tied the rope around him and fixed him to a stone pillar. Nearby stood his master and brothers, who had no reason to believe this last arrival was part of a brilliant plan.

Bajie looked over the bindings.

'Brother,' he said, 'you were so interested in my ears a little while ago. Have you come to make sure they are seasoned properly?'

Wukong told him to keep quiet. He was thinking.

The rope could tighten, but it could not know every trick the monkey had learned. With another transformation he reduced his staff to a fine steel file. Patiently, while the guards attended to the kitchens and the masters argued about the prisoners, he worked through the coils that held him.

It took time. He could not make the rope simply disappear, as he had made his false gourd vanish; this was a genuine magical object. But he could damage it with the right tool.

When he could move, he left a hair-made double in his place. He changed into a small servant and began walking out of the cave, trying not to draw attention. Bajie looked up once more and recognized something about the backside of this particular servant.

'Look there,' he began.

Wukong silently cursed him. He stopped long enough to blacken his exposed rear with soot, then slipped away. Bajie had become an accidental enemy of every disguise Wukong attempted.

The monkey returned with a different deception. He arranged for the guards to replace the damaged golden rope with an ordinary binding, and left them with a rope of hair that would be worthless the moment he reclaimed it. By the time the demons understood what had happened, the prisoner at the pillar was another illusion and the real Wukong had vanished.

He was not finished with the two kings.

## The brothers named Sun

Silver Horn had the gourd again. His servants had seen what it could do, and he knew the fatal importance of a name. To draw a target into the vessel, the user had to call the target by name and hear an answer. Then the stopper and the sealing charm would do the rest.

Wukong returned outside the cave, announcing himself not as Sun Wukong but as a brother of Sun Wukong. He played with the order of his familiar name, Sun Xingzhe, meaning Sun the Pilgrim, until it became Zhe Xingsun. He sounded like another person, he thought, and perhaps another person's name would confuse the magic.

Silver Horn leaned from the entrance and demanded to know who dared challenge him.

'I am Zhe Xingsun,' the monkey replied. 'My elder brother has been troubled by your cave, so I have come to settle the account.'

Silver Horn raised the gourd and called the name. Wukong answered.

He vanished into the vessel.

The inside was narrow and airless, and the heat of the spell began to work. The demon was confident that this new brother would dissolve like any other creature trapped under the seal. But Wukong had endured forty-nine days inside Laozi's alchemical furnace long before the pilgrimage began. What would have reduced another being to liquid could not easily dissolve him.

He listened to the guards and made his voice sound weak.

'I am half melted,' he groaned. 'My head and shoulders have turned to soup. Only my legs are left.'

The servants laughed, then grew curious. If a prisoner dissolved so quickly, perhaps the king would want to see the state of the body. They removed the seal and lifted the stopper.

A small insect flew out before anyone understood what had happened. Inside Wukong left a scrap of hair, shaped to suggest the remains of a body. He darted behind the guards, took the form of a servant named Sea Dragon, and found a chance to exchange the genuine gourd for another imitation.

Once outside, he returned to his challenge. This time he gave a third name: Xingzhe Sun. If the demons had captured Sun Xingzhe and Zhe Xingsun, he declared, there was still one more member of the family waiting to avenge them.

Silver Horn was beginning to believe that the mountain was swarming with monkeys. One by one, these supposed brothers came out of the clouds with the same staff, the same face, and the same intolerable confidence.

He pulled out what he believed was the magic gourd. In fact it was the imitation Wukong had left behind.

Wukong held up the real one.

'Your gourd seems small,' he observed. 'Mine is its proper partner. There are male and female gourds, just as there are male and female creatures. Yours may be the male. Mine is the female. Shall we see which works better?'

He decorated his claim with stories of the goddess Nüwa repairing the heavens and immortal plants cultivated on Kunlun. By now Silver Horn had been robbed of so many treasures that he should have trusted nothing coming from the monkey's mouth. Yet the wish to win was stronger than his caution.

They agreed to test the vessels.

Silver Horn called the name Xingzhe Sun again and again. Wukong answered nine times. Nothing happened. The false gourd was a piece of hair without the power to catch so much as a sparrow.

Silver Horn began to suspect the wrong words or the wrong name. Before he could think further, Wukong turned the genuine gourd toward him.

'Silver Horn King!'

'What?'

The demon was pulled inside.

Wukong fitted the stopper and sealed it. There was no intervention from the cave, no clever reply from his prisoner, no reversal of the formula. The vessel grew hot. A liquid sound came from within. The Silver Horn King, who had escaped so many dangers by knowing the precise spells for every treasure, was overcome by the simplest rule of the one he had lost.

## Gold Horn's last army

Inside Lotus Cave, Gold Horn waited for news of his brother. What came was the news that Silver Horn had been captured and destroyed. The surviving servants were frightened; the families of those killed on the road and in the cave had begun to understand how dangerous the strange monkey truly was.

Gold Horn's face darkened. He had lost his mother and his brother, and the humiliation was greater for knowing how often Wukong had tricked them. Yet he still had the Seven-Star Sword and the plantain fan. He ordered his people to arm themselves. Hundreds of demons gathered under banners, and Gold Horn led them out to avenge Silver Horn.

The Monkey King saw the force coming over the ridge. He had faced armies before, but his first task remained the rescue of Xuanzang. Fighting every demon in a mountain would keep him busy long enough for someone in the cave to kill his master. He pulled a handful of hairs from his body, chewed them, and blew them into the air. They became a host of monkey soldiers carrying staffs like his own.

The battle spread across the slopes. Gold Horn's troops surged forward, shouting their king's name. The hair soldiers met them and drove them back. Staff struck sword. The thin path disappeared beneath running feet and fallen bodies. Wukong moved among the fighting as swiftly as a spark in dry grass.

Then Gold Horn opened the plantain fan.

This was no household fan, and it was not the great iron fan that would become important much later in the journey. It was a magical fan from an alchemical furnace, one that could raise terrible fire. Gold Horn swept it through the air. Flames rushed over the slope, reddening the sky and making the rocks seem to glow from within.

Wukong saw what would happen to his hair soldiers. A hair burned in that fire might not be recalled. He muttered a spell and gathered them back before the flames consumed them, then sprang clear himself. The fire cut across the field where his army had stood. The demons who had been close to him were scattered; others were felled by his staff as he searched for a way around the blaze.

Gold Horn believed the flames would drive Wukong away. Instead the monkey used the confusion to circle toward Lotus Cave. He found the entrance less closely guarded than before. The demon army was outside, and the prisoners remained within.

A strange light shone from a vessel in one of the rooms. For an instant Wukong thought the fire had entered the cave, but it was the white jade bottle. He stole it and slipped away. He did not yet have his master. He had another treasure, and Gold Horn was still armed with the sword and fan.

Outside, the demon king looked on the bodies of his soldiers and the ruins of his attack. He called for those who could still move. They followed him away from the charred ground, grieving for companions whose strength had meant nothing against the monkey's tricks. Gold Horn returned to the cave with a remnant of his force.

Wukong came again under cover of quiet. He passed a guard who could not distinguish one servant from another, then found the king exhausted after battle. Gold Horn had fallen asleep beside the weapons he had used. The monkey crept near the plantain fan, but his hand brushed the sleeping king's head. Gold Horn woke and sprang up. There was another chase, another clash, and another hurried retreat.

The king fled toward Yalong Cave, where his mother's relatives still lived. Wukong did not follow immediately. For the first time in many days the entrance of Lotus Cave lay open to him and its defenders were in flight.

He ran inside.

He found Xuanzang first, anxious and weary, but alive. He freed Bajie and Sha Wujing, who were delighted to be back on their feet. Bajie asked at once whether the kitchen had survived. Wukong, who had spent days being squeezed by mountains, bound to pillars, and dissolved in gourds, admitted that even a demon's kitchen could offer a reasonable dinner.

They ate what provisions could be made suitable for their master's vegetarian diet. They rested in the cave that had been built to consume them. Xuanzang thanked his disciple for returning, but the monkey explained that the Gold Horn King remained free. They could not yet call the road safe.

For one night they would be safer sleeping behind the doors of Lotus Cave than exposed on the mountain path. In the morning they would settle the account.

## The uncle who came to avenge the fox

Gold Horn rode to the relatives of his murdered mother and told them what had happened. His brother was lost. Their mother had been struck down beside the road. The treasure that might have protected them had been stolen by a monkey who wore faces as easily as clothing.

A fox lord, their maternal uncle, listened with growing anger. He was called Fox Aqi. His followers gathered, including fierce fox women who would fight for the old family. Gold Horn promised them revenge and led them back toward the cave.

The pilgrims heard the approach before the attackers reached the gate. Wukong took his staff and told the others to be ready. Bajie, refreshed by a night's sleep and food he had not had to pay for, came out with his nine-toothed rake. Sha Wujing lifted his staff and placed himself where he could shield their master if the enemy forced a way inside.

Fox Aqi declared his grievance. How could the monkey kill an aged mother who had done him no harm at the moment he struck her? Wukong answered that the fox had brought a weapon to help her sons imprison and eat a traveler. The exchange did not reconcile them. Their families and their loyalties were too far apart, and both sides carried the dead with them.

The battle broke open. The fox fighters tried to surround Wukong, while Gold Horn searched for a chance to retrieve the treasures. Bajie pushed forward with unexpected energy. He caught Fox Aqi with his rake and killed him. Sha helped drive back the followers, and the remaining fox demons scattered into the mountain.

Gold Horn tried to escape the field, but Wukong had kept the jade bottle ready. The monkey called the king by name. In his anger Gold Horn answered. The bottle drew him in, and Wukong sealed it. The last of the two brothers had been trapped by the very technique he had expected to use against the pilgrim.

With Gold Horn gone, the sword fell to Wukong's possession. The cave, the provisions, the rescued master, and the five treasures were now in the pilgrims' hands. They could gather their baggage and continue west.

It might have been the end of the matter.

But as they left the mountain, an elderly figure stood before them on the road. He was not a poor man from a village or another demon wearing a holy face. He was Taishang Laojun, the venerable Laozi of the celestial furnace.

Wukong knew him well. Long ago he had eaten the old master's immortality pills, been shut inside his furnace for forty-nine days, and emerged stronger than before. There had never been much affection between them.

'You have something of mine,' Laozi said.

Wukong immediately asked which thing. He had taken many treasures in his life.

All five, came the reply. The red gourd was used to store medicinal pills. The jade bottle carried water. The Seven-Star Sword was an instrument for subduing demons. The plantain fan fed the furnace fire. Even the golden rope had once been a belt around Laozi's robe.

These were not random weapons discovered by clever mountain spirits. The Gold Horn King and the Silver Horn King were two young attendants from Laozi's furnace, the gold boy and the silver boy. Their disappearance from Heaven had not been an accidental escape. They had been sent to the mountain as an ordeal for the Tang pilgrim.

Wukong stared at Laozi.

'You mean your boys stole your weapons, imprisoned my master, and tried to cook him, and all of this was arranged?'

Laozi explained that Guanyin had borrowed the boys three times to set this trial in the pilgrims' way. It was meant to test their resolution on the journey west.

Wukong thought of the old fox lying dead on the road, the servants killed in the hills, the demons struck down beside the fire, and the endless danger to Xuanzang. He had been asked to risk his master's life and spend his strength undoing an arrangement made by beings who could have stopped it at any moment. He demanded to know why the gods made such trouble for pilgrims they claimed to support.

Laozi did not offer the kind of answer Wukong wanted. He requested the treasures.

The Monkey King returned them, one by one. Laozi opened the gourd and bottle, released the essence of his two attendants, and restored them to their proper forms. The brothers who had been dissolved by the same vessels now stood as celestial youths again, no longer kings of Lotus Cave. Laozi gathered them and his possessions and departed for Heaven.

The mountain was quiet.

The prisoners had been rescued. The divine attendants had been reclaimed. Nothing brought back the dead fox or the servants, nor did any one of those revived servants appear to apologize for what had been done in the cave. Xuanzang had his three disciples around him once more, and the road ahead was open.

They turned west.

On the path, Wukong still had something to say about tests arranged from above. His master reminded him that the journey was not theirs to design. The monkey did not dispute that. He only wished the designers had to carry the baggage and face the cooking pots from time to time.

The cave receded behind them. Ahead lay open country, a monastery, and a quieter night that would prove to be the beginning of a different kind of danger.
