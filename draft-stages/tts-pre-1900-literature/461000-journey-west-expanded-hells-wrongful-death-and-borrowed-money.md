# The Hells, the City of Wrongful Death, and the Borrowed Money

Cui Jue led the Tang emperor away from the halls of the Ten Kings. Taizong had won permission to return to life, but the way back was not a quiet corridor. It passed through regions where the dead were gathered according to their deeds, their griefs, and their unfinished business. The emperor could no longer rely on the courtesies of a royal audience. He had to see what lay beside the road.

First came the dark country around the mountains of the underworld. From one direction rose wailing; from another came the clamor of punishments. The judge explained that the realms before them were places of consequence. People who had lied, stolen, tormented others, exploited the poor, or slaughtered living beings were made to endure suffering fitted to their conduct. Taizong saw the eighteen hells, with their instruments of violence, their terrible wardens, and their desperate inhabitants.

The names passed before him like places on a route he would never choose: prisons of fire, cold, grinding, knives, and cruel devices. He heard the cries of those whose lives had ended but whose wrongdoing had not been forgotten. The judge did not ask him to settle their cases. The message of the journey was to remember what he had seen and govern differently when he returned.

The path then reached the bridges associated with passage and rebirth. A golden bridge carried those of high spiritual achievement; a silver one served upright and worthy people. Another bridge stretched above a dreadful river. The water beneath it was dark and violent, and spirits struggled amid the current while fearsome guards watched. This was the bridge over the Nai River, the place where the cost of crossing could no longer be paid by rank or familiar privilege.

Taizong looked from one passage to another and asked where each led. Cui answered patiently. The emperor tried to absorb the details, but the sights were overwhelming. He had fought battles and ordered punishments in life; now he walked through an administration that remembered every injury and judged people by standards of its own.

Then came the city of wrongful death. Here the dead were not silent. Many had fallen in war, slaughter, and political violence. They recognized Taizong, or thought they recognized the cause of their suffering. As he approached, crowds moved toward him, crying for their lives back. They named him and called upon him to answer. Some were maimed, some carried the marks of wounds, and others reached from the crowd with the blind urgency of hunger.

Taizong recoiled. Here were lives that had disappeared inside proclamations of victory and accounts of campaigns. They did not praise the emperor who had secured a realm. They saw the man under whose armies they had died. He called out to Cui for protection. The judge said the spirits had no means to support themselves and no religious ceremonies to release them. They could not be driven away by imperial commands. Taizong would have to provide them with money and promise to perform rites for them in the world above.

The emperor had arrived in the underworld with nothing. He possessed neither treasury nor purse. Cui explained that someone living in the city of Kaifeng had credit in the realm of the dead: a man named Xiang Liang, who through offerings and good works had accumulated large deposits there. If Taizong would sign a document of borrowing, the judge could arrange payment from that account. The emperor agreed and gave his name to the debt.

The money was distributed. The hungry spirits took what they were given, and the crowd opened enough for Taizong to pass. Cui instructed him to remember the promise. He was to hold a great ceremony upon his return, with prayers and offerings for those who had died without care. The ritual would address the suffering that an ordinary court order could not reach. Taizong promised again. This promise, like the one made to the dragon, would matter beyond the moment when it was spoken.

Farther on he saw the paths of rebirth. Different classes of beings moved toward their appointed destinations. Some souls might pass into fortunate lives; others descended into harder conditions. There were humans, animals, spirits, and many kinds of travelers gathered for their next passage. Taizong watched their movement and was told to carry the lesson home.

At last Cui Jue took leave of him. Another underworld guide led the emperor toward the border of the living world. A saddled horse waited. They rode until they reached the Wei River, where bright fish moved in the water. Taizong paused, delighted by the sight after the misery he had seen. He lingered. The guide urged him onward, but the emperor would not move quickly enough. At last the guide pushed him from the horse into the river.

Taizong cried out as he fell. Water seemed to close over him. He felt the shock of drowning, the loss of balance, the rush of panic. Then the place around him changed. He was no longer beside an underworld river. He was in the body that had been laid out in the palace.

The court officials had been mourning for three days. Some wanted to proclaim a successor, while Wei Zheng insisted that they wait a little longer. They had begun to speak of the dead emperor in the past tense when a cry burst from the coffin. The voice shouted that someone was drowning him.

The mourners fled in terror. Only a few ministers kept their courage and approached the coffin. They opened it. Taizong was sitting up, dazed, still protesting about the river and the man who had pushed him from the horse. He had returned to life. He could once again speak to the people he ruled. And, as he would soon discover, he had brought back several obligations that neither a sword nor a royal seal could discharge.
