# The Decameron — Madonna Isabella and the Two Visitors

At her country house Madonna Isabella expects a visit from Leonetto, the young man she loves. She is married, and her privacy is precarious, but she has arranged a space in which they can meet. Another man, Messer Lambertuccio, also pursues her. He is powerful enough that his attention is hard to refuse safely. Isabella finds herself with one man whom she welcomes and another whom she fears, just when her husband is expected to remain away.

The evening collapses into a chain of arrivals. Leonetto comes first and is received. Lambertuccio then appears, pressing his claim. Isabella cannot allow the men to encounter one another openly, so Leonetto hides. Before Lambertuccio can leave, she learns that her husband is riding home. She has only moments to turn the presence of two concealed men into a credible scene.

Her solution is theatrical and exact. She orders Lambertuccio to draw his sword and rush down from the house, looking furious and threatening. The returning husband sees a violent man departing as though in pursuit of an enemy. Isabella then summons Leonetto from hiding and introduces him as the terrified victim of that pursuit: a stranger who has taken refuge in her rooms because Lambertuccio means to kill him. The husband's task now seems perfectly clear. He must offer hospitality and escort an endangered guest to safety.

The more generously he performs that duty, the more securely Isabella's invention works. He has no reason to ask why Leonetto was concealed in the house, because she has already supplied the answer in the form of an urgent rescue. The two lovers gain what they most need: time, an explanation and a respectable exit. Lambertuccio, whose earlier threats helped create the difficulty, is made to lend his temper to the fiction that releases them.

Pampinea's story shows a woman distributing roles to men who have greater obvious power than she does. Lambertuccio provides the visible danger; Leonetto provides the grateful fugitive; the husband provides the moral authority of the host. Isabella takes no public credit, but her rapid account gives each actor a part to perform. The husband becomes an unwitting participant in his wife's concealment, not by being idle but by behaving honorably within the invented circumstances.

The tale belongs to the day of wives' tricks, yet the two visitors are not interchangeable suitors. Leonetto is the lover she chooses. Lambertuccio's importunity creates pressure and risk. Treating all three men as equally amusing rivals would erase the reason Isabella needs more than a witty line. Her ingenuity is a form of navigation through unequal claims on her household and body. The ending is comic because no duel occurs and no one is exposed, but the underlying world remains one in which a woman's safety can depend on knowing exactly what story men will accept.
