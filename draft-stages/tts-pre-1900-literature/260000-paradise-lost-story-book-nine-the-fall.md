# Paradise Lost — Book Nine: The Temptation and the Fall

We have reached the act promised in the poem's first sentence. Milton begins Book Nine by saying he must now change from a story of conversation and divine instruction to a tragic event. He claims that the deepest heroism may lie not in military spectacle but in patience and moral endurance. The war in Heaven filled a book with weapons and armies. The catastrophe that changes the human world will occur during an ordinary day's work in a garden.

Satan returns after circling the Earth, searching for a means of approach. He chooses a serpent and enters its sleeping body. The transformation is an act of disguise and possession, not the serpent's independent decision to betray humankind. The enemy admires the beauty of the world, and, for a moment, of Eve herself. He almost forgets the hatred that has brought him here. Then he argues himself back into revenge, as though kindness were a lapse he must correct.

Adam and Eve begin the morning debating their work. The garden has grown abundant, and Eve suggests that they tend different areas so that they can accomplish more. Adam worries that separation may expose them to the enemy Raphael described. Eve objects to being treated as though virtue were valuable only when protected from trial. If she cannot stand alone, she asks, what kind of freedom has been entrusted to her? This is a serious question, not a mere excuse for vanity. Adam's caution is serious too. The poem does not offer the easy view that one of them possesses the entire moral truth. They part after extended discussion, with Eve going to work on her own.

Satan finds her. In serpent form he moves with unexpected elegance and addresses her as though she were a sovereign worthy of worship. Eve is astonished to hear an animal speak. The serpent explains that he was once like other creatures, until he ate fruit from a remarkable tree. It gave him reason and language. Why, he asks, should the same gift be denied a human being? His lie is effective because it begins with an observable marvel: here is an animal that seems to speak. He offers a false explanation for a real sight.

Eve follows him to the forbidden tree. She recognizes it and repeats the command. Satan changes tactics. If the fruit truly brings death, why does he stand before her alive and apparently improved? He appeals to ambition, to the possibility of becoming more like the gods, and to the suspicion that God is withholding power to keep humanity subordinate. He even recasts disobedience as a rational test of an inconsistent law. The reasoning is fraudulent because the witness is fraudulent. But it takes seriously desires that Eve already possesses: knowledge, freedom, and recognition.

She eats. The poem makes this small physical act a wound in nature. Afterward Eve feels exalted and begins calculating what her new knowledge might mean. Could she become Adam's equal or superior? Should she keep the benefit to herself? She fears, too, that if she dies, Adam may find another partner. Love, rivalry, and dread are already becoming difficult to distinguish. She resolves to share the fruit with him.

Adam understands immediately that something terrible has happened. He is not deceived by the serpent's argument. His motive is different. Unable to imagine life without Eve, he chooses to eat with her. In Milton's account this is not proof that attachment is evil; it is proof that love can lose its moral direction when the lover chooses shared wrongdoing over faithfulness to what he knows is right. The first human disobedience has become two distinct decisions, not a single undifferentiated temptation.

The fruit brings neither clear divinity nor a durable increase in happiness. The couple is swept into possessive appetite. They sleep and wake conscious of their nakedness in a new way. What was once innocent exposure now produces embarrassment and distrust. They make coverings from leaves. Then the accusations begin. Adam reproaches Eve for leaving; Eve hears in his reproach his failure to support her freedom and his own decision to eat. The conversation that began the day as an argument between partners trying to manage work ends as a fight between people trying to transfer blame.

The world has changed before a judge appears to pronounce sentence. Trust is no longer easy. Desire feels less like mutual delight and more like compulsion. Their minds are crowded with suspicion. Milton's tragedy is not only that a rule has been broken. It is that people who could talk and choose together are starting to regard one another as threats. The next book follows that damage beyond the garden, into nature, law, mortality, and the possibility of repentance.
