# What Does the Joke Ask Us to Forget?

A clever person makes a fool of someone else. That pattern appears throughout the *Decameron*, and often it produces excellent comedy. The listener knows about a disguise the victim cannot see. A character is trapped by his own boast. A false explanation converts disaster into triumph. But some tricks succeed by denying another person information they need to decide what happens to their body or life. Comedy is not automatically a statement that every consequence is harmless.

Take Alibech and Rustico in Day Three, Tale Ten. A young woman wants to learn a religious life and does not understand the sexual language in which Rustico describes their encounter. The tale invites its audience to understand a double meaning that Alibech does not possess. That difference makes the joke possible. It also makes a claim of informed agreement difficult: the participants do not share an understanding of the act being proposed. The point is not to deny the story's comic design, but to identify the ignorance on which it relies.

Day Nine, Tale Six presents a farce of shifting beds and mistaken identities. The mother has an encounter while believing the man beside her is her husband. The storyteller arranges a night of comic misunderstanding and an ingenious cover-up. A careful reader can laugh at the choreography and still observe that consenting to one person's presence is not automatically consenting to another's. The fictional narrator's interest in a clever rescue does not remove the underlying asymmetry in knowledge.

Day Ten, Tale Eight offers a still more consequential example. Titus takes his friend Gisippus's place in the dark bridal bed, and Sophronia believes he is the man she has agreed to marry. The tale later celebrates male friendship and extraordinary loyalty. But the friend's substitution does not become consensual because its outcome is absorbed into a story of noble generosity. Sophronia's ability to choose was compromised at the moment the identity was concealed. The distinction changes what we can admire in the tale.

A different sort of harm appears in Nastagio's story in Day Five, Tale Eight. Nastagio discovers a repeated infernal chase in which a woman is attacked and dismembered by a pursuing knight. He arranges for the woman he courts to witness the terrible spectacle, after which she agrees to marriage. The narrative's fantastic machinery makes coercion look like the solution to romantic rejection. The problem is not simply that modern readers dislike a medieval fantasy. The dramatic arrangement itself converts fear into assent. One can analyze how the tale persuades its fictional audience while refusing to call fear a free choice.

Rinieri's revenge on Elena in Day Eight, Tale Seven shows how far a comic retaliation may go. She leaves him waiting in the cold; he later contrives to expose her on a tower in brutal heat, to insects and exhaustion. The story's formal symmetry can make the revenge seem mathematically satisfying: winter answered by summer, humiliation by humiliation. The suffering is not equal. The symmetry of a plot device is not a moral measure of proportionality.

None of these examples requires assuming that Boccaccio was secretly writing a twenty-first-century ethical treatise. His narrators inhabit a different society, and the tales draw upon traditions of sexual joking, clerical satire, exemplary narrative, and revenge comedy. We should not attribute to a fictional narrator distinctions they never express. Equally, historical difference is not an argument for erasing what a story plainly says happened. Identifying deception, violence, or ignorance accurately is first a task of textual fidelity.

The *Decameron* makes the distinction among author, storyteller, character, and reader essential. Dioneo's delight in a bawdy outcome is a fact about how a narrator speaks. The author's decision to place that tale beside other stories of marriage, cruelty, and resourcefulness is a fact about the book's form. The listener's judgment is another act. They need not be identical. Literary criticism becomes impoverished when it assumes that recognizing an ethical problem means declaring the work worthless, or that admiring a work means denying the problem.

One practical method is to retell a comic tale twice. First follow the person doing the trick: what information do they conceal, what danger must they manage, and why is the solution satisfying? Then follow the person being tricked: what do they believe, what choices can they make, and what does the ending cost them? The plot is the same, but the center of knowledge moves. That shift does not destroy the joke. It lets us hear how the joke was constructed, and why its success may remain disturbing.
