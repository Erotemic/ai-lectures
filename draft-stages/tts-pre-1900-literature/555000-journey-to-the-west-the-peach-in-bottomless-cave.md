# Journey to the West: The peach in Bottomless Cave

## A lesson at the well

Bajie had been the first to go down the mountainside. Wukong and Sha Wujing remained above with the horse and luggage, looking toward a cliff which appeared to have no door. Below them the land folded into narrow gullies, and a path led past a well. Bajie followed it, expecting to find guards, a stone gate, or some sign that a demon had carried Xuanzang through. He found two women drawing water. Their hair was dressed in tall, old-fashioned coils; their carrying pole rested across the mouth of the well. It was not a threatening sight, except that Bajie had come looking for monsters and had already decided that anything he met must be one.

"Demons!" he shouted. "Tell me where you've hidden our master."

The women turned. They had never seen this stranger before, and he had addressed them as though they were creatures fit to be struck. They seized their carrying pole and gave him the answer their hands could provide. Bajie had tucked his rake away to climb down the path, and the heavy pole fell on his head more than once before he managed to retreat. By the time he reached Wukong he was rubbing his skull and declaring the whole ravine full of vicious female spirits.

Wukong asked what Bajie had said to provoke the attack. The pig answered with injured dignity that he had called them demons, nothing more. "And what did you expect them to call you?" Wukong asked. Bajie wanted sympathy. Instead he was given a lecture on courtesy. A soft piece of wood, Wukong said, might be carved into a revered image; a hard piece might end by receiving repeated blows in an oil press. Strength without the knowledge of when to bend could leave a man battered for his pains.

"You could have explained that before they hit me," Bajie complained. Wukong told him to return, bow politely, and ask questions. If the women were young, he should address them respectfully as young ladies; if older, as grandmothers. None of this meant they were relatives. It meant that a traveler who needed information could not obtain it by shouting insults at the people who possessed it. Bajie changed his appearance into that of a broad, dark-faced monk and went back toward the well.

The second conversation began rather better. He bowed so deeply that the women laughed and greeted him kindly. They asked where he came from. "Where I came from," he answered. They asked where he was going. "Where I am going." He had learned the form of civility without learning how to hold a conversation. Yet the women were amused instead of angry. Bajie noticed their buckets and asked why they carried clean water so far from their home. The reply made him forget every lesson he had received.

Their mistress, they said, had taken a Tang monk into her house. She was preparing a vegetarian feast to share with him, and after the feast there would be a wedding. The water was for the food and ceremonial wine. Bajie abandoned his disguise, hurried up the slope, and announced that the journey had come to its natural end: Xuanzang would marry, he could return to Gao Village, Sha could go back to the river, Wukong could reign at Flower-Fruit Mountain, and the dragon horse could find the sea again.

Sha stared at him. Wukong did not bother to argue at length. The master was being held in a place he could not escape. A demon who promised marriage had not become harmless by arranging a banquet. The two water carriers, however, had shown them a road. Wukong led his brothers down behind the women, keeping far enough back that they would not be noticed, until the carriers vanished beside a rocky platform.

## The mouth of a country beneath the earth

On the platform stood a handsome archway, painted and ornamented, bearing the name Trap-Sky Mountain, Bottomless Cave. The travelers went closer and discovered that the entrance was not a great arched gate at all. In a smooth expanse of stone there was a small round hole, no wider than the mouth of a large jar. Below it yawned a depth no ordinary traveler could measure. Bajie leaned forward, then immediately leaned back again. He did not wish to learn whether a heavy pig would reach the bottom before the next season.

Wukong looked down and understood why his master had disappeared so completely. The cave was like an inverted world, hundreds of miles across within, hidden beneath a narrow opening above. He made a plan. Bajie and Sha would stand guard at the entrance with their rake and staff. He would descend, find Xuanzang, and drive the captor upward. If the demon escaped him at the bottom, she would meet the other two at the top. It was a sound plan, provided a person could make his way through the passages and provided the demon did not alter the rules of the fight again.

He dropped into the opening. The darkness lasted only until the deep chamber widened around him. There, to his surprise, he found daylight, wind, flowers, trees, and another elaborately built residence. He might have fallen from the surface of one world into the courtyard of a second. His own Water-Curtain Cave had taught him that the earth could contain a hidden paradise. This place, too, had the charm of a dwelling built where no stranger would think to search. He became a fly and settled where he could listen.

The mistress was seated beneath a pavilion. She had changed her clothing since the forest and monastery, and now every ornament in her hair declared her the ruler of this buried household. Her attendants moved through the courtyard carrying dishes. She instructed them to prepare a feast for her Tang monk, her future husband. Wukong remembered Bajie's claim, which he had dismissed as another excuse to divide the luggage, and smiled to himself. This once the pig had reported the truth.

He flew from corridor to corridor until he saw Xuanzang sitting behind a latticed window. The monk was pale from sickness and captivity. He sat very straight, as though posture alone might preserve the boundary the demon wished to cross. Wukong landed on his head and spoke softly. Xuanzang recognized the voice at once. Relief nearly made him cry out; fear kept him quiet. He begged Wukong to save him before the marriage could be forced upon him.

The monkey tried to turn fear into a joke. The feast looked magnificent, he said, and it would be an easy life to remain here, produce a little family, and let the disciples carry the scriptures home without him. Xuanzang was furious. Since leaving Chang'an he had guarded his vows through hunger, storms, and mortal danger. What did Wukong take him for? The demon had seized his freedom; she would not seize his consent. He could endure the fear of death more easily than the thought of abandoning the pilgrimage he had undertaken.

Wukong dropped the joke. The trouble was the cave's shape. He could fight here, but carrying the monk straight upward through the narrow opening would be difficult, and the whole demonic household might fall upon them. His first plan was to hide himself in the bubbles of the wine. Xuanzang would offer a cup back to the woman, and when she swallowed it Wukong would enter her belly and compel her to lead his master out. Xuanzang disliked the cruelty of a plan based on torment from within. Wukong answered that sympathy for the captor was of little use to a prisoner who could not leave.

## A banquet nobody wished to attend

The mistress opened the monk's door and came to collect him. She spoke tenderly and took his arm. He answered her politely because he was frightened of what refusal might provoke, not because she had won his heart. Wukong remained concealed nearby. The woman had ordered only vegetarian foods: fruits in polished bowls, mushrooms, bamboo shoots, tofu, herbs, roots, and freshly cooked vegetables. In another house Xuanzang could have enjoyed such a meal with gratitude. Here every bright dish was another reminder that his body and will were not his own to direct.

She offered him wine. Xuanzang looked upward and silently called on the guardian powers of his journey. If the drink was permitted by his vows, he prayed that he might survive it; if it was not, he was drinking only under coercion. Wukong whispered from his hiding place that he should accept a little, then refill the cup for his hostess. The monk did so, managing to raise a cluster of bubbles on the surface. The monkey became a tiny insect and slipped into the froth.

But the demon did not drink at once. She spoke sweetly, exchanged ceremonial gestures, and let the cup rest while she admired her captive. The bubbles broke. A little insect appeared upon the wine. She noticed it and flicked it away with one finger. The first plan was ruined by a moment's delay. Wukong darted aside, changed into a hungry hawk, and swept through the pavilion. Attendants scattered. Cups fell, dishes shattered, and the beautiful meal became a confusion of broken furniture and frightened servants. The mistress shouted orders; Xuanzang was hurried back into his locked room.

For a while the monkey's fury did the work of an army, but the thing he needed remained beyond his reach. The woman still held Xuanzang. Her household was alarmed, not defeated. Wukong abandoned the hawk's form, returned as a small fly, and settled once more beside his master's ear. Xuanzang was in tears. The disorder had only made his keeper more determined to enforce the marriage. Now, he said, she would not even trouble herself to prepare a strictly vegetarian feast. Wukong's interference had turned inconvenience into danger.

Wukong accepted the rebuke, then saw the answer in the garden outside. A body might not be able to force its way out of the narrow cave with a prisoner in tow, but the mistress could lead Xuanzang out herself. The monkey would need to enter her belly after all; he simply needed another way in. The garden had peach trees. He told Xuanzang to ask for a walk, praise the fruit, and select a red peach that would be Wukong in disguise. The monk should offer the red peach to the mistress and take a green one in exchange. The fruit, unlike a fly in wine, would be something she wanted to eat.

Xuanzang asked once more why the monkey could not simply challenge her in the open. Wukong reminded him of the crowded cave, the hidden routes, and the round opening far above. Winning one battle would not necessarily bring the master home. He needed the mistress to become their unwilling guide. Xuanzang thought of the cold corridors through which he had been carried, the entrance he had never seen, and the promise he had made to return to Chang'an with the scriptures. At last he agreed.

## The red peach

When Xuanzang asked for fresh air, the mistress delighted in the request. She believed his desire for a garden walk meant that he was beginning to enjoy her company. She ordered the doors opened and the paths swept. Attendants followed them past pavilions, flowerbeds, pools, and shaded walks. Xuanzang could see the care with which this underground garden had been made. The woman pointed out its delights as though beauty might settle a question of freedom.

At the peach trees, Wukong signaled his master and slipped from his hair. In an instant he became a large ripe peach, red on the sunny side, hanging temptingly within reach. Xuanzang stopped and asked why some fruit was ripe and some still green. The mistress explained the difference with a pleased little lesson about sun and shade, yin and yang, and how growth depended upon their relation. The explanation was graceful. Her conduct toward the monk remained unchanged.

Xuanzang plucked the red peach. The mistress took a green one. He offered her the ripe fruit as a courtesy and suggested they exchange. She was charmed. To her it seemed a lover's gesture, an acknowledgment of the intimacy she had been trying to create. Xuanzang had learned to perform a gesture without surrendering its meaning. He held the green peach and waited as she raised the red one to her lips.

Before she could bite, Wukong rolled himself down her throat. The demon gasped. She had expected sweet flesh and a pit; instead something living moved inside her. From the darkness of her belly Wukong announced himself. He threatened to tear her organs apart unless she released the monk. When she asked Xuanzang who was speaking, he answered that it was his disciple, whom she had just swallowed as a peach. Her hands flew to her stomach. She understood at last that the fruit had been a trap.

The mistress pleaded that she had only sought a husband. Wukong answered with blows from inside. She collapsed in agony among the flowers and ordered her attendants to carry the monk out. Even that would not satisfy Wukong. She must take him herself, he said; a servant could lead him to some other prison or hide him in a side chamber. His master would leave the cave with the person who knew the way. Trembling, the demon raised herself, placed Xuanzang upon her back, and climbed toward the entrance she had so confidently thought no rescuer could find.

Above, Bajie and Sha heard movement from the depth. They raised their weapons. Then Xuanzang's voice called to them, and the two disciples cleared the entrance. The woman emerged bearing their master, still suffering the turmoil Wukong made inside her. Bajie reached forward to receive the monk. Sha stood ready to block a sudden escape. The plan had brought the prisoner to daylight, but it had not yet ended the struggle.

Wukong was still in the demon's belly. And she was still alive.
