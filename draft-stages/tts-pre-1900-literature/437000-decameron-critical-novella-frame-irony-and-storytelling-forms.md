# How One Hundred Stories Become One Book

Imagine finding one hundred short stories copied together with no indication of who told them, why they followed one another, or what their listeners made of them. Many might still be entertaining. But they would not be the *Decameron*. Boccaccio provides a second narrative in which the stories are told. Its daily schedule, narrators, themes, songs, and reactions change the meaning of what lies inside the individual plots. A frame is not simply a container. It is a machine for comparing interpretations.

The word *novella* is often translated as a tale or short prose narrative, but no single modern genre covers all the pieces in the collection. Some look like miracles or exempla, stories designed to teach by example. Some recall comic fabliaux, with bodily mishaps and trickery. Some take the shape of courtly romance or tragic love; others center on merchants and legal wit. This diversity is not an accidental assortment the author forgot to harmonize. The difference among forms is what permits the ten speakers to test competing values.

The book also names its own design. Its title points to ten days, and the introduction specifies one hundred tales told by seven women and three men. The short rubrics, or explanatory headings, describe the tales in advance. Modern thrillers often conceal an ending to preserve surprise. Boccaccio may tell a reader where the story will go and still hold their attention through the manner of getting there. If the outline is already visible, attention can shift toward how a plot is told, what its narrator admires, and what it means beside the preceding narrative.

Take the opening sequence. Ser Ciappelletto's false confession turns a scoundrel into an apparent saint; Abraham's journey to Rome converts evidence of clerical corruption into an argument for faith; Melchizedek's three rings avoid a dangerous declaration about religious truth. The tales share institutions and belief as concerns, but the response to deceptive appearances changes from one to another. Read separately, each might seem to deliver a compact moral. Heard in order, they become an argument about whether moral certainty can survive the forms in which evidence reaches us.

A narrator's choice is part of that argument. Emilia tells the Day Nine story that endorses a husband's violence against a wife; elsewhere the collection has allowed women to escape jealous husbands through ingenious tricks. Dioneo's license to depart from the day's theme makes his stories especially capable of changing the tone. The comic ending of Day Four follows a series devoted to unhappy loves. The final Griselda tale questions its day's emphasis on magnificence. Such differences do not need an imaginary authorial manifesto to make them significant. The sequence itself produces tensions readers must negotiate.

The storytelling frame also changes the meaning of speech. We hear a fictional woman recount a fictional man delivering an argument to a third person. When the man speaks, his claim belongs first to him. The woman narrator may approve it or enjoy it for another reason. The company may laugh or disagree. Boccaccio's construction of the scene adds yet another level. Readers who move too quickly from a character's words to 'Boccaccio says' erase the principal formal device that makes the book interesting.

Boccaccio interrupts his own concealment at the beginning of Day Four, addressing criticism of his subject and his attention to women. He appears again in the epilogue to anticipate objections to language, erotic material, and length. Most of the time the authorial voice is less prominent. The frame lets the author stage varied speech rather than constantly impose commentary. That contrast between an occasional self-defending author and many fictional speakers is a deliberate achievement of literary form.

There is also a temporal art. Some stories require a single swift reply, such as Madonna Oretta's complaint about bad narration. Others carry a traveler through years of adventures. The framing afternoon can contain either without requiring identical internal duration. A short story becomes long enough to make an encounter matter; a long one becomes concise enough to be held in a shared sitting. The scale of the book changes without tearing the company apart.

One of the best ways to read the *Decameron* is to choose an adjacent pair and ask what the first story teaches us to expect of the next. Does a trick that was harmless in one become violent in another? Does the generous gift of a rich man improve our judgment of a poor man's tact? Does an ending we initially applauded become more difficult after the next narrator speaks? This exercise treats Boccaccio as a designer of relations among tales, not just as their compiler.

The collection's integrity therefore does not depend on everyone agreeing. The structure gives disagreement a place. Ten voices can describe competing ways of living; the book's larger voice emerges from their arrangement. Boccaccio turns an old practice of telling exemplary, comic, and romantic stories into a complex artwork in which the reader must repeatedly decide what counts as evidence, success, justice, and generosity. That activity is the unity of the hundred tales.
