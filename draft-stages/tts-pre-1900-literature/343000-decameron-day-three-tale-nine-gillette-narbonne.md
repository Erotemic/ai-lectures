# The Decameron — Gillette of Narbonne

Gillette is the daughter of a physician from Narbonne, and she has learned something of her father's medical knowledge. She loves Bertrand of Roussillon, a nobleman whose rank places him above her in the marriage hierarchy. When the king of France suffers from an illness that his doctors cannot cure, Gillette approaches the court and offers a remedy. She asks no ordinary payment. If she succeeds, she wants the king to grant her Bertrand as her husband.

The king at first doubts that a young woman can accomplish what his physicians have failed to do. Gillette accepts the risk of failure and applies the treatment she has inherited. The king recovers and keeps his promise. He summons Bertrand and orders the marriage. Gillette has attained the rank and husband she sought, but the transaction has made something painfully clear: Bertrand does not want to marry her. He complies with royal command while regarding the match as beneath his station.

After the wedding, Bertrand leaves. He goes to Florence instead of remaining with Gillette, intending to pursue a military career and conduct his own affairs. He sends a message setting conditions that appear impossible: only if she possesses a particular ring he keeps close and bears a son by him will he accept her as his wife. The conditions are designed to make a legal bond ineffective in everyday life. Gillette has gained the formal title of wife without being welcomed into a shared household.

She does not remain at home waiting for him to reconsider. She governs his estate well enough to win the affection of its tenants, then travels to Florence under conditions that allow her to investigate his life there. She learns that Bertrand is courting a young woman of limited means. With the young woman's mother, Gillette arranges a substitution. The expected lover agrees to give the woman gifts; Gillette supplies the resources and secretly takes her place during their meetings. Bertrand gives up the ring and, without recognizing his wife, is intimate with her. Gillette conceives twins.

She returns with the children and the ring, presenting both before the man who declared them the conditions of their marriage. Bertrand can no longer deny that the terms he himself proposed have been met. Boccaccio concludes that he accepts her and comes to love her. Neifile gives us a wife who has pursued a seemingly hopeless objective by medical skill, household administration, negotiation, secrecy, and a carefully arranged encounter.

The shape of the story traveled far beyond the *Decameron*. Readers of Shakespeare's *All's Well That Ends Well* will recognize the healing of a king, an unwanted marriage, the ring, and the substitution of one woman for another. Shakespeare changes and expands the dramatic setting, but the troubling core remains. The heroine's competence is real, and the union she wins is not the result of mutual desire at the outset.

There is a reason to resist treating Gillette's plan as proof that determination can make love just. The king uses political power to assign Bertrand a spouse. Bertrand uses status and absence to humiliate the spouse. Gillette then obtains an intimate encounter by arranging for him to mistake her identity. The narrative's legal and dynastic logic celebrates that the right wife has borne children, but a contemporary account of consent would not equate compliance with that logic and informed agreement.

At the same time, Gillette is far more than the passive prize of a powerful man. She diagnoses an illness, earns a royal reward, rescues a neglected estate, makes alliances with women outside the aristocratic household, and turns Bertrand's own words into proof. Her actions demonstrate how narrow the ordinary channels of power are for someone of her birth. She must operate within the system that frustrates her to secure any meaningful position inside it.

Neifile has chosen this as her ninth tale because it makes the day's theme almost literal. A thing long desired is gained, appears lost, and is recovered through a sequence of exertions. What persists beyond the ending is the problem of how much personal freedom a romance may sacrifice to produce its promised happy conclusion. The story's power lies in our ability to admire Gillette's intelligence while continuing to question the bargain she has been forced to make with a world of unequal rank.
