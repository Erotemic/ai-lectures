# Journey to the West expanded III: Erlang, the furnace, and the Buddha’s palm

## Journey to the West — Guanyin, Erlang, and the Battle for Flower-Fruit Mountain

The celestial army had spent a day fighting on Flower-Fruit Mountain without taking its king. At night the generals counted their prisoners. They had captured wild creatures, animal rulers, and the demon leader whom Wukong had appointed to command his advance troops. They had not captured a single one of the Monkey King's own monkey followers. The mountain was surrounded by armed gods, but the heart of the rebellion remained inside Water-Curtain Cave. Li Jing and his commanders could not report a victory. They had to wait for morning.

Elsewhere in Heaven the trouble had interrupted a very different gathering. Guanyin, the bodhisattva associated with compassion and the rescue of beings in distress, had arrived for the Queen Mother's Peach Banquet with her disciple Hui'an. The hall was almost empty of guests and littered with the consequences of the previous day's disorder. There were no orderly lines of honored immortals taking their places; there was a ruined feast and a great deal of anxious talking. Guanyin heard what had happened and went to speak with the Jade Emperor.

The emperor recounted the whole affair. The Monkey King had been born from stone, cultivated magical powers, offended the sea and underworld authorities, accepted a low office, abandoned it, defeated the first expedition, and returned to Heaven under the grander title he had demanded. When given the Peach Garden, he had eaten its fruit, disrupted the banquet, stolen wine, swallowed Laozi's elixirs, and gone back to his mountain. A large army had been sent to arrest him. No report of success had yet come back.

Guanyin did not begin by ordering another army. She sent Hui'an down to learn what was happening. The disciple was also known as Muzha, the second son of Li Jing and brother of Nezha, so the military camp he reached contained his own family. Passing through the net of sentries, he found his father in the command headquarters. There he heard how the previous day's fighting had ended. The nine star gods had been beaten back. The generals had not managed to seize their target. The army had taken prisoners, but its principal mission was unfinished.

Then the Monkey King came out again to challenge the besiegers. Hui'an asked leave to fight. He carried an iron staff, a weapon more like Wukong's than the elaborate devices of some of the heavenly generals. He went to the open ground and called for the Great Sage. Wukong appeared, confident and unhurried. One more opponent had come to measure himself against the mountain king. If the stranger had come to arrest him, he could begin by showing whether he knew how to use his staff.

The fight was long enough to silence any idea that Hui'an was simply another officer who would run at the first blow. For fifty or sixty exchanges the staffs met. Each combatant shifted his grip, changed angle, closed distance, and recovered. Wukong was quick, and he was tireless in a way that ordinary fighters could not imitate. Hui'an finally felt his arms growing heavy and his strength failing. He made a feint, withdrew, and escaped to the camp.

He reported his defeat to Li Jing and then returned to Heaven with the request for reinforcement. A father and son who had both met the monkey in combat were in a better position than the emperor's ministers to know how hard he would be to seize. At court Hui'an told Guanyin that Wukong's powers were great and that the army could not bring him in. Guanyin considered the report and proposed a new kind of opponent.

There was a divine warrior living at the mouth of the Guan River, generally called Erlang, or the Illustrious Sagely Lord. He was the emperor's nephew, but he did not live as an ordinary courtier. He had his own worshippers, his own companions, and a substantial force of irregular divine troops. He answered imperial military orders while remaining apart from the daily ceremonial obligations of the palace. His powers were well known. Guanyin suggested that he was the one to send.

The emperor agreed and issued a formal commission. Erlang received the message at his sanctuary and called together the six companions known as the brothers of Meishan. These were not delicate ministers who made proposals while someone else fought. They were accustomed to weapons, hunting, and the command of a force that included twelve hundred grass-root divine soldiers. They came with bows, crossbows, hawks, and hounds. A very different sort of contingent was now descending on Flower-Fruit Mountain: less an army arranged for display than an armed hunting party prepared to follow a quarry wherever he ran.

Li Jing and the Four Heavenly Kings welcomed Erlang at the camp. Erlang asked to be allowed to fight Wukong himself. The nets should remain around the mountain, but the top ought to be opened so that the duel could take place freely. If he lost, his own brothers would help him withdraw. If he won, they would secure the prisoner. He also asked Li Jing to hold up a demon-revealing mirror in the sky, lest the monkey escape by taking another shape and slipping away from the watchers. These precautions showed what he had understood before the fight began. Wukong was not merely strong. He was difficult to keep in one place or one form.

When Erlang came to the cave, Wukong went out in armor with his iron staff. The two examined one another. Erlang was an imposing warrior, finely equipped, carrying a weapon with three points and two cutting edges. Wukong knew the stories told about the god's birth and famous feats. Erlang knew the name of the monkey whom the court had attempted to employ as a horsekeeper. Each tried to put the other in his place with words before either struck a blow.

Their first contest was a close fight. Neither was intimidated by weapons that would have shattered lesser opponents. The three-pointed blade moved against the iron staff. They attacked and withdrew, broke the rhythm of one another's blows, and met again. The exchanges multiplied into hundreds. The armies on either side shouted and watched without being able to settle the matter. At last Erlang enlarged himself into a terrifying figure of tremendous height, with a weapon as formidable as the mountain peaks. Wukong answered with the same power. His own body rose to giant proportions; his staff extended in his hands until the two warriors appeared to be fighting with pillars.

The sight shook the Monkey King's followers. The four marshals knew their leader could change his shape, but they also knew that the enemy had found someone who could match him. While the giants fought above them, the Meishan brothers carried out the other half of Erlang's plan. They released the hawks and hounds, attacked the monkey troops around the cave, scattered the defenders, and captured many. Bows and weapons drove the survivors back into the mountain or out among the rocks. A force that might have been able to resist an ordinary frontal assault had no protection against the sudden collapse of its position.

Wukong looked down and saw the confusion. His soldiers were running. Some were dropping their weapons. The marshals who had followed him since the earliest days of Water-Curtain Cave could scarcely hold the line. He had been fighting on equal terms with Erlang, but equality in a duel would not save a disintegrating army. The god had not needed to overpower him to put his mountain in danger. He had only needed his comrades to attack somewhere the king could not protect while occupied.

Wukong abandoned his giant form and withdrew. Erlang followed. The monkey raced toward the cave, but the Meishan brothers stood in his path. They had surrounded the approaches and were ready to seize him. For an instant he was a fighter with nowhere to run. Then he used the gift that had saved him in so many difficult places. He made his iron staff as small as a needle, tucked it into his ear, and changed himself into a little bird. A sparrow flitted up into the branches. The men who had been closing around Wukong suddenly found only empty ground.

For the first time the battle ceased to be a contest of armies or even two giant warriors. It became a game of recognition. If Erlang could identify what Wukong had become, he could pursue him. If Wukong could choose a form his enemy failed to recognize, he could escape. Somewhere among the leaves, a small bird watched the armed gods searching beneath it. Erlang arrived, looked upward, and understood what had happened.

## Journey to the West — The Sparrow, the Fish, and the Temple

The men of Meishan had surrounded Sun Wukong, and then he had vanished. They could see the weapons lying where the defenders had fled. They could see the trees above the approach to Water-Curtain Cave. They could not see the Great Sage. This was not because he had travelled a great distance. He was perched on a branch in the form of a sparrow, small enough to be overlooked by an army looking for an armored warrior.

Erlang had expected such a maneuver. He looked where the others had failed to look, recognized the disguised monkey, and transformed himself into a bird of prey. A small hawk burst toward the branch. Wukong flew away and became a larger bird, climbing toward the clouds. Erlang changed in answer, becoming a powerful long-winged hunter. The pursuit moved upward, across the air above the mountain, and then downward as Wukong abandoned the sky altogether.

He dropped into a stream and became a fish. The water was cold and moved swiftly over the stones. A fish could pass where a warrior could not follow on foot, and in the deep places it was difficult to tell one moving shape from another. Erlang came to the bank and searched. A creature had escaped into the stream; there were only so many forms it could have taken. He made himself a fish-catching bird and waited close to the surface.

The fish that was Wukong saw a hunter hovering over the water. Something was wrong with it. It had the general appearance of a fishing bird but not every mark of the creature it pretended to be. Wukong turned sharply away. Erlang saw a fish that also seemed imperfectly made, lacking the pattern that would have marked it as one familiar kind. They had recognized one another by the flaws in their disguises. Erlang dived. Wukong twisted free, sprang from the water, and became a snake slipping into the grass.

Erlang answered with a bird whose long beak could take a snake. Before the beak could close, Wukong became another bird, a low and awkward creature rather than a swift one, and stood on the bank. The tale makes a joke here about the social standing of that particular bird, treating it as a disreputable form which Erlang would not dignify by matching it. He returned to his own shape, drew his slingshot, and struck the bird with a pellet. Wukong tumbled down a slope and vanished again.

Below the slope, where there had been no building before, stood a tiny temple. Its door was open. It had windows, a place for an image, and a tall pole beside it. To anyone looking quickly, it might have seemed the shrine of some local spirit hidden among the stones. But Erlang looked carefully and discovered something wrong. The pole was standing behind the building, in the wrong place. The monkey had transformed his body into the temple, with his mouth as the doorway, his teeth as the doors, and his eyes as the windows. He had not managed to hide his tail, so he had turned it into a flagpole.

Erlang laughed at the construction. He would not walk through that door and allow the monkey to bite him. Instead he threatened to smash the windows and kick in the doors. Wukong immediately understood the danger. If Erlang struck the windows, he would be striking the monkey's eyes. If he broke the door, he would break his teeth. Wukong sprang away before the god could carry out the threat. The false temple was gone as quickly as it had appeared.

Erlang's companions searched the surrounding area again and found nothing. Wukong had made himself invisible and flown far beyond the battlefield. Li Jing, holding the demon-revealing mirror high above the nets, examined the distance and saw where he had gone. He had headed toward the Guan River, to the sanctuary where Erlang himself received worship. The god had pursued him across a mountain and through water; now the monkey had selected the most insulting possible hiding place, the god's own home.

Wukong arrived ahead of him and assumed Erlang's appearance. He descended among the servants and minor spirits who attended the sanctuary. They greeted him as their lord and allowed him to take the central seat. He examined the records of offerings: who had brought animals in thanks, who had prayed for children, who had promised a gift if an illness passed, and who had returned to fulfill a vow. The scene is almost as comic as the earlier story of the heavenly stables. Wukong had fled a serious battle and was passing the time by inspecting another man's accounts.

Then word came that their lord had arrived. The attendants were confused. Someone who looked exactly like Erlang was already seated inside. The real Erlang pushed into the sanctuary and found Wukong occupying his place. Wukong revealed himself and announced that the shrine belonged to the Sun family now. The joke was a declaration of war in miniature. A god's sanctuary was a center of reputation and obligation. To sit there checking the offerings was to claim his authority, even if only for the time it took him to be discovered.

Erlang attacked. Wukong drew his staff back out of his ear and returned to fighting in his own form. They fought through the sanctuary and into the sky, moving cloud and mist around them. Their struggle carried them back to Flower-Fruit Mountain, where the heavenly forces were waiting. The soldiers tightened their enclosure. The Meishan brothers came forward. This time Wukong had no straightforward path to the cave and no great distance in which to disguise himself.

Above the fighting, the Jade Emperor and his companions came to the Southern Gate to watch. Guanyin had recommended Erlang, and it was clear that he had succeeded in holding Wukong where other commanders could not. But holding the monkey and capturing him were different things. He was still armed; he was still fighting; and even after the long pursuit he had not surrendered.

Guanyin considered helping from above. She had a precious vessel and willow branch and proposed to use them to strike the monkey's head, knocking him down so that Erlang could bind him. Laozi, who stood nearby, objected that a porcelain vessel might break against the monkey's iron staff or miss its mark. He had something better suited to the work: a hard, enchanted ring that he wore on his arm. He could throw it from Heaven without needing to stand in the melee.

The ring fell. Wukong was so occupied with Erlang and the surrounding fighters that he did not see it coming. It struck him on the head, and for an instant he lost his footing. He recovered enough to rise, but Erlang's hound rushed in and bit his leg, dragging him down again. The men of Meishan closed on him. The ring did not destroy his powers; the dog did not defeat him in a fair duel; the brothers did not individually match him in strength. Together, those actions took away the moment he needed to escape.

They bound him and used hooks through the bones around his shoulders to prevent further transformations. The powerful body that had survived dragon palaces and the underworld could be tied, pierced, and held even if it could not easily be killed. The celestial army celebrated. Erlang was congratulated for the capture and gave credit to the emperor's fortune and the help of the other gods. His companions remained below to search the mountain while the officers took their prisoner to Heaven.

At the celestial court, the emperor's judgment was severe. The monkey had been summoned, rewarded, forgiven, and rewarded again. He had then stolen from its most sacred storehouses and defeated its armies. The emperor ordered that he be taken to the execution platform and cut to pieces. Wukong had escaped a low office and refused an empty high one. He had won almost every fight in which he chose the terms. Now he was tied beneath the instruments of Heaven's punishment, and his enemies were about to discover that seizing the Great Sage and ending his life were not the same undertaking.

## Journey to the West — Forty-Nine Days in the Eight-Trigrams Furnace

The executioners had the Monkey King bound at the place where condemned demons were to be destroyed. They used knives and axes. They thrust at him with weapons meant to open a body and sever its limbs. The blows struck. The body remained. Wukong's skin would not yield. They tried fire, expecting at least to burn what they could not cut. The fire did nothing. The officers of thunder were summoned to bring down a force that shattered mortal creatures, but their strokes made no more progress than the steel had done.

It was a humiliating problem for the government of Heaven. A prisoner had been captured in battle and sentenced by the emperor. The sentence could not be carried out. Every new attempt demonstrated the very power that the court meant to extinguish. The report reached the throne: the monkey had learned some extraordinary way of protecting his body. How were the officials to execute him if their weapons, fires, and thunderbolts left no mark?

Laozi proposed a method suited to the source of Wukong's protection. The monkey had eaten the peaches that conferred longevity. He had drunk immortal wine. Most of all, he had swallowed the elixirs that Laozi prepared with great care. The combinations had entered his body and made it hard as metal. Ordinary destructive force might no longer be effective. But the furnace in which those medicines had been made could also be used to separate them. Perhaps the intense heat and controlled changes of alchemical fire would recover the precious ingredients and reduce the monkey himself to ash.

The emperor accepted the proposal. The guards took Wukong to Laozi's palace. There the sage removed the restraints and pushed his prisoner into the great furnace of the Eight Trigrams. The furnace was more than a metal vessel with a flame beneath it. Its structure corresponded to the eight signs of the old cosmological system: Heaven and Earth, Thunder and Wind, Water and Fire, Mountain and Lake. Laozi's attendants managed its fires, adjusting the heat and tending the work as though preparing a transformation of substances. The prisoner was placed inside, and the doors were closed.

For Wukong, a creature who had spent much of his life escaping confinement, this was a prison without bars and without a way to look out. Its walls were made not merely to hold him but to change his body. There was no army to strike with his staff and no court officer to insult. He could not appeal to his title, and there was no audience to admire his cleverness. The fire was being maintained by those who expected him to be gone when it was finished.

Yet Wukong understood something of the furnace's arrangement. He found the position associated with the sign of Wind, where the direct heat would not strike him in the same way. The air there moved. Smoke passed through the place, and the smoke stung his eyes until they were red and irritated. But the flame did not burn his body away. The same monkey who had survived the blades of the execution platform now survived the alchemical chamber intended to finish him.

The fires were tended for forty-nine days, seven times seven, a period selected for the completion of the operation. Above the furnace the attendants waited for the time when Laozi would open it and take out what remained. In the world outside, days passed with none of the noise and excitement of Wukong's earlier battles. A dangerous prisoner had been transferred to a more specialized institution. Heaven could return to its usual ceremonies while experts managed the problem.

At last the appointed day came. The furnace was opened. Wukong heard the machinery and voices, saw light where there had been fire and smoke, and jumped. He burst from the opening, overturned the furnace, and struck down the attendants who tried to restrain him. Laozi himself reached for him and was thrown aside. The sage who had hoped to reclaim his medicines now faced a prisoner still alive, angrier than before, and free of the chains that had held him at the execution platform.

The monkey drew his iron staff from his ear. The weapon grew in his hands. He was not going back to his house, for he had no reason to believe that Heaven would offer him any peaceful arrangement now. Nor was he turning quietly toward the mountain where his companions had suffered the siege. His fury took him toward the greatest halls of the celestial court, and the gods who had helped arrest him moved to meet him.

The old battle had been a struggle around Water-Curtain Cave. This time the struggle was inside the guarded region of Heaven itself. The great star gods barred doors. The Four Heavenly Kings were unable to bring him down. Wukong passed from one protected place to another, swinging the staff against those who came forward. He was no longer an official returning from an appointment, or a visitor trying to learn the rules of admission. He was a prisoner escaped from execution, striking at the center of the power that had sentenced him.

Near the main audience hall he was met by Wang Lingguan, a powerful protector of the celestial palace. Wang Lingguan carried a golden whip and was prepared to use it. The two fought at the approach to the emperor's own seat. The palace guards watched a contest in which neither side could quickly force the other back. Wukong had overwhelmed many commanders, but he had not found a way past this guardian.

Additional thunder generals arrived, thirty-six of them, armed with a variety of blades, hooks, and other weapons. They surrounded Wukong and closed in. He answered by taking a form with three heads and six arms, multiplying his staff so that each hand could fight. Blows came from every direction. He caught them, turned them, and struck back. Those attacking him could not easily get through, yet he could not simply sweep away the entire formation and advance to the throne. The battle became a dangerous knot of bodies and weapons around the entrance to the imperial hall.

Reports reached the Jade Emperor. The monkey had survived execution, survived the furnace, escaped Laozi, broken through the outer resistance, and fought the palace guardians to a standstill. This was no longer a dispute over how to rank an immortal or how to recover stolen wine. The court's ultimate punishment had failed. Its military commanders could not restore order. The emperor sent messengers west to ask the Buddha to intervene.

Wukong knew nothing of the full meaning of that appeal. He was still moving the staff among the thunder generals, its blows sounding over the court, his smoke-scarred eyes burning from the days in the furnace. Those eyes, now known as fiery or golden eyes, would be part of his identity in adventures far beyond Heaven. But the fact that he could see through disguises and resist a furnace did not mean he could recognize every kind of power. He had fought weapons, officials, and armies. A very different opponent was approaching, and the question that opponent would ask was not whether the Great Sage could endure another blow.

## Journey to the West — The Buddha’s Palm and the Mountain of Five Elements

The summons reached the Buddha in the western realm while Sun Wukong was still fighting at the celestial palace. The messengers bowed and explained their difficulty. A monkey from Flower-Fruit Mountain had become an immortal, broken the rules of the celestial court, stolen sacred fruit and elixirs, defeated the emperor's forces, and survived every effort to execute him. Thirty-six thunder generals were now surrounding him near the emperor's hall, but none could bring the struggle to an end. The Jade Emperor requested assistance.

The Buddha heard the account, told his attendants to remain at their places, and came to Heaven. He did not arrive with another great army. He came accompanied by two disciples and proceeded to the place where the battle was raging. The thunder generals were ordered to withdraw and make room. Wukong lowered his weapons as a newcomer stepped forward and asked what sort of person had caused such a disturbance.

The question was almost comic after everything Heaven had attempted. The Monkey King was a figure whose name the entire celestial court now knew, yet this stranger wanted him to begin by explaining himself. Wukong demanded to know who had interrupted the battle. The visitor identified himself as the Buddha. Then he asked about the monkey's birthplace, his learning, and the claim that had led him into rebellion.

Wukong told the story with the pride of a man presenting his qualifications for office. He had been born from stone, discovered a home behind the waterfall, travelled in search of a teacher, learned ways of long life and seventy-two transformations, mastered a cloud that crossed one hundred and eight thousand li in a single leap, and raised himself above the rank assigned to him. He had no intention of spending eternity in the place Heaven had chosen for him. The ruler who occupied the imperial throne had enjoyed it long enough. Why should the position not pass to someone stronger?

The Buddha answered by asking what Wukong knew of the Jade Emperor's long cultivation. The office was not simply a seat that the strongest passerby might seize. The emperor's achievement had taken an immense succession of ages. Wukong had only recently emerged into the world and acquired his powers. The Buddha warned him that reckless ambition could destroy the fruits of his practice. The monkey was unmoved. Thrones changed hands in the world below. Why should Heaven be different? If the emperor would not surrender the place, Wukong would continue to disturb him.

The Buddha proposed a contest that seemed to favor the monkey. There was no need for more fighting. If Wukong could leap beyond the Buddha's palm, he would be acknowledged as the winner and the Jade Emperor would yield his place. If he could not, he must abandon his claim and return to the lower world for further cultivation. Wukong looked at the extended hand. It was a hand, not a mountain range or an ocean. He could cover an impossible distance in one somersault. He asked whether the Buddha could really guarantee such a bargain. The Buddha assured him that he could.

Wukong put away his staff, gathered his strength, and jumped. He travelled so fast that the world below became indistinct. The cloud carried him forward, and he kept going. It did not seem that a hand, however divine, could contain a leap of such magnitude. When he finally stopped, he saw five immense pillars rising into the sky. Beyond them there appeared to be no ordinary road. He believed that he had reached the edge of the world.

A clever victor, he thought, ought to leave evidence. If he returned with only a boast, the Buddha might claim that he had never travelled as far as he said. Wukong pulled out a hair, transformed it into a brush, and wrote his grand title on the middle pillar, adding that the Great Sage Equal to Heaven had come that far. Then, displaying the undignified humor the story never allows him to lose, he urinated at the base of another pillar. The mark and the smell would confirm his visit. Satisfied, he turned and travelled back with the same astonishing speed.

He landed in the Buddha's palm and demanded the throne. The Buddha told him that he had never left the hand at all. Wukong insisted that he had gone to the world's boundary. He had seen five great pillars and left his writing there. The Buddha invited him to look down at his own hand.

Across one of the fingers Wukong saw his writing. There was also the unmistakable evidence of his other mark. The five pillars had been the five fingers of the hand he thought he had escaped. He had rushed through what he imagined to be the outer distances of the world without recognizing the space in which he was moving. It is a startling reversal, and its joke depends on all the confidence accumulated in the previous chapters. The monkey who could visit dragon palaces, erase names from death's registers, and pass through celestial defenses could not tell the limits of a contest he had willingly entered.

He was not ready to accept the result. If he had failed the first time, he could leap again. He gathered himself for another somersault. Before he could escape, the Buddha turned his hand over. The five fingers became five peaks, associated with the five phases—metal, wood, water, fire, and earth. They fell over Wukong and held him beneath a mountain. He could not lift it. He could not pass through it. The space that had seemed so vast when he stood at the far end of his leap had become a prison from which speed provided no release.

In Heaven, the soldiers celebrated. The emperor came forward to thank the Buddha, and the interrupted banquet culture of the celestial court resumed in a different form. A new feast was prepared to celebrate the restoration of order. The gods brought gifts, the Queen Mother offered peaches, and distinguished visitors arrived with tokens of long life. The gathering received the name Banquet of Pacifying Heaven. After the stolen feast and the ruined campaign, the court was finally able to assemble around its tables without a monkey eating the dishes first.

But the victory was not quite as final as the guests imagined. Word arrived that Wukong was attempting to free himself. His head had appeared from beneath the mountain. The Buddha did not return to fight him. He took out a paper bearing a six-syllable sacred formula and directed a disciple to place it on the summit. Once the formula was fixed there, the mountain settled firmly. Wukong could breathe and make small movements, but he could not force the peaks aside. The last possibility of escaping through brute effort had been closed.

The Buddha appointed a local earth spirit and protective guardians to watch over the imprisoned monkey. They were to provide iron pellets when he was hungry and molten copper when he was thirsty. These are not the meals of a beloved king at Water-Curtain Cave, or even the fodder of the horses Wukong once tended. They are the provisions of a sentence. The Buddha also declared that when the monkey's appointed ordeal had ended, someone would come to release him. There was a future beyond the mountain, though no one had yet described it to the prisoner as the life he would eventually lead.

The hero of these opening chapters had gained almost everything he had believed he needed. He had found a home for the monkeys, obtained a name, learned immortality, acquired transformations and a weapon of immense power, defied the records of death, and forced Heaven to recognize his chosen title. Every gain had expanded the range of what he could do. None had taught him where to stop. At last he had encountered a power that did not need to match every trick with another trick. His leap had been vast, his confidence absolute, and his prison small enough, in one sense, to have begun within a single hand.

Beneath the Mountain of Five Elements, Sun Wukong remained alive. His eyes, hardened and stung by the alchemical furnace, could still see the world outside. Seasons would pass. The world below would change. Other people would undertake journeys of their own. The monkey who had travelled to escape death would have to learn how to endure time. The great pilgrimage for which the novel is named had not yet begun. Its most unruly future companion had been found, tested, and made to wait.
