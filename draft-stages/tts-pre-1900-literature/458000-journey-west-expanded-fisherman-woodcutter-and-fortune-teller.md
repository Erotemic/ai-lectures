# The Fisherman, the Woodcutter, and the Fortune Teller

Outside Chang'an, near the Jing River, a fisherman and a woodcutter walked home together. They had sold their day's goods in the city and shared a little wine. The fisherman was Zhang Shao. The woodcutter was Li Ding. They were friendly men, pleased with their work and with the chance to argue over which of them led the more fortunate life.

Li Ding loved the mountains. He spoke of trees, quiet paths, mountain winds, and the freedom to cut wood far from the duties that occupied important men. Zhang Shao loved the water. He spoke of his small boat, the fish that filled his nets, and the pleasure of returning to the river each morning. Neither could bear to let the other win. They exchanged verses about their trades and the changing seasons, praising the gifts of mountain and stream. Their poems were not the weapons of a serious quarrel. They were the entertainment of two friends who had time to talk.

At last they reached the place where their roads separated. Zhang Shao warned his friend to beware of tigers in the mountains. Li Ding took offense and replied that storms could overturn a boat as easily as beasts could seize a woodcutter. Zhang laughed. He said he had a way to keep ahead of the dangers of his trade. Each day in Chang'an he visited a diviner named Yuan Shoucheng, gave him a golden carp, and received a prediction about where he should cast his nets. The predictions had been so accurate that the fisherman could fill his basket almost without failure.

The friends said farewell and went their ways. Their conversation had been playful. But it had been overheard.

A water spirit serving the Dragon King of the Jing River had listened from beneath the bank. He hurried to the underwater palace and told the king that a diviner in Chang'an was directing fishermen precisely where the river's creatures could be found. The spirit worried that if the man could name every favorable pool and current, the fish under the Dragon King's protection would soon be taken. The king was furious. A human fortune teller, trading predictions for carp, was interfering with his dominion.

The Dragon King reached for his sword and prepared to go up to Chang'an and destroy the diviner. His advisers restrained him. If he rose in his true form, with rain and clouds around him, the city would be terrified and the heavenly court might hold him responsible. Better to transform himself into an educated gentleman and investigate. If the diviner was indeed a fraud, the king could humiliate him. If not, he would at least know what he faced.

He changed into a scholar in pale clothing and went to the western street where Yuan Shoucheng received visitors. The fortune teller sat quietly with his writing materials and implements of divination. The stranger asked a simple question: would it rain tomorrow? Yuan said yes. At what hour would the clouds gather? When would thunder sound? When would the rain begin, and how much would fall? Yuan gave a precise schedule and a precise depth. The Dragon King listened with growing amusement. He governed the river and its rain. What human could dictate such things to him?

The disguised dragon offered a wager. If the prediction proved accurate, he would bring fifty pieces of silver. If it proved false, he would break the diviner's sign and drive him from the city. Yuan accepted without fear. The visitor left, returned to the river, and told his household that the matter was settled. They laughed together. The master of the river, surely, could choose how much rain to give.

While they were laughing, a messenger came down from Heaven bearing an imperial decree. The Dragon King bowed and opened it. Tomorrow he was ordered to provide rain to Chang'an. The decree specified the very hours and the very quantity that Yuan had just named.

The Dragon King's pleasure vanished. He had made a wager against a man whose prediction appeared to be known in Heaven itself. To honor the Jade Emperor's decree would be to lose his wager. To win the wager would require him to alter what Heaven had commanded. His advisers offered the kind of cunning that wins a small argument at the cost of something larger. Change the starting time of the storm by one hour, they suggested. Shorten the rainfall by a small amount. Then Yuan's prediction would be wrong and the king could smash his sign.

The Dragon King listened. He knew the decree had come from the Jade Emperor. He knew it was not a suggestion. But he did not wish to kneel before a human fortune teller, and the court of fish and crabs around him was watching. The risk began to seem tolerable. He decided to adjust the rain.

The next day he ordered the wind, thunder, clouds, and lightning into position. They obeyed him, as they always had. But he did not let them act at the commanded hour, and he did not let the promised quantity fall. The city received its rain; Yuan's exact prediction was wrong. The king had won the wager in the narrowest possible sense.

He dressed again as a scholar and strode into Yuan Shoucheng's room. Without waiting for explanation, he threw down the sign, scattered brushes, and shattered the instruments. He shouted that the fortune teller was a liar. He had predicted rain that did not fall according to his schedule. The diviner ought to leave Chang'an and stop deceiving its people.

Yuan remained calm. He told the visitor that he knew who stood before him. This was no ordinary scholar. It was the Dragon King of the Jing River, who had altered a decree of the Jade Emperor to win a wager. The error in the prediction did not prove that Yuan lacked knowledge. It proved that the dragon had broken the law of Heaven.

The Dragon King dropped his arrogance. The punishment for such an offense, Yuan said, would be execution. The king knelt and asked for help. He had wanted to destroy a fortune teller's reputation. Now he needed that same man to tell him how to live.

Yuan said he could not cancel the heavenly order. But the executioner appointed for the following day was a human official, the Tang minister Wei Zheng. If the Dragon King could persuade the emperor to keep Wei Zheng from carrying out his assigned duty, perhaps he might escape. That night, the Dragon King would have to seek the mercy of the ruler of Chang'an.

The river creature left the broken room and returned beneath the water. Over a dispute that began with two friends praising their work, an imperial death sentence had been set in motion. The road to the scriptures was now winding through the smallest of human vanities: a wager made because someone could not endure being proved wrong.
