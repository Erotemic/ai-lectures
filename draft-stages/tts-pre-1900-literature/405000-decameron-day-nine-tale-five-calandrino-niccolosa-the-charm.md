# The Decameron — A Charm for a Fool in Love

Calandrino and his fellow painters are working on a house at Camerata when a young woman named Niccolosa arrives with the owner's son, Filippo. Calandrino sees her washing at the well and imagines a future in which she prefers him to everyone else. He is married to Tessa, a fact he hopes will remain outside the story. He approaches Bruno with a confidence entirely at odds with the evidence: Niccolosa must surely be smitten with him, and someone should help bring her within reach.

Bruno knows more than he admits. He recognizes Niccolosa and soon tells Filippo and the other painters what Calandrino has said. Together they arrange a comedy in which every apparent sign of affection is produced for Calandrino's benefit. Niccolosa plays along with the game. Calandrino reads her carefully managed glances as proof of his irresistible charm. His companions encourage him to talk, sing and admire his own imagined conquest. The more absurd his behavior becomes, the more secure he feels.

Bruno offers something better than ordinary courtship: a written charm. If Calandrino carries the paper and touches Niccolosa with it, she will follow him. Calandrino accepts the prescription, because it promises success without any need to understand her wishes. The actors arrange that Niccolosa will seem to obey the charm. To Calandrino it becomes the most direct possible evidence that magic works and that his feelings are returned.

But there is another audience to be managed. Nello, a relation of Tessa, has an easy way to reach Calandrino's wife. She learns of her husband's attempted adventure and comes to the appointed place. Calandrino is discovered not in the arms of a woman won by enchantment but in the middle of an elaborate practical joke. Tessa's anger and physical retaliation break his confident fantasy. The young woman and accomplices have made him a spectacle rather than a conqueror.

Fiammetta's story repeats the pattern established in Calandrino's earlier adventures. A claim of magic is believable because the supposed beneficiary badly wants it to be true. He imagined that an invisible stone would allow theft without detection; he accepted an impossible pregnancy and paid for a cure. Here he takes a scroll for a device capable of turning another person's desire into an automatic response. The underlying ambition is different, but the habit of belief is the same.

The tale's named woman is not the object of a genuine supernatural power. She is an actor in a staged response; her participation is part of the deception. Calandrino, who thinks the charm would make her follow him regardless of choice, does not understand the stakes of what he is asking for. The laughter of Bruno and the others exposes that failure, while Tessa's arrival recalls the marriage he was so eager to ignore.
