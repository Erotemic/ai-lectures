# The Decameron — Andreuccio’s Night in Naples

Andreuccio comes from Perugia to Naples with money to buy horses. He is inexperienced in the great city's ways, and he takes too little care to conceal how much he is carrying. A young woman learns of his money and arranges a deception. She claims to be a relative, draws him into her house, tells a story calculated to win his confidence, and offers him a place to stay. He believes himself fortunate to have found family in an unfamiliar city.

During the night he steps into a privy and falls through a badly placed opening into filth below. The house is closed against him, and his purse is gone. He knocks, protests, and discovers that the woman who welcomed him has no intention of admitting him again. His good clothing, his money, and his sense that he understands the situation have all disappeared. He wanders through Naples dirty, frightened, and angry.

A pair of men encounter him and propose a new venture. They plan to rob the tomb of an archbishop recently buried with valuables, but first they insist that Andreuccio wash away the smell of the latrine. They lower him into a well. When watchmen approach, the thieves scatter and leave him trapped. Other men come to draw water; the sudden weight upon their rope, and then the unexpected sight of a person rising from the well, sends them fleeing in terror. Andreuccio escapes yet again and somehow becomes involved in the planned robbery after all.

At the church, the thieves open the tomb and send him inside to search the body. Andreuccio has learned something from his earlier betrayal. He takes a valuable ring and conceals it before passing other objects up. His companions, suspecting that he is withholding treasure, close the tomb and abandon him in the dark beside the dead archbishop. He calls out in fear. Then a second group of thieves arrives, intending to plunder the same grave. One of them opens the tomb. Andreuccio seizes the man's leg, terrifying the intruders, and climbs free while they run.

By morning, he is battered, filthy, exhausted, and still alive. He returns home without the horses he meant to buy, but with the archbishop's ring. The adventure is sometimes called a lucky escape, though it has involved repeated robbery, fear, and a narrow passage through death. Fortune has not protected Andreuccio from humiliation. It has allowed him to survive it and to leave with a costly object he did not acquire honestly either.

Fiammetta makes Naples almost a second character. The city is a place of sharp distinctions between those who understand its hidden arrangements and those who do not. A stranger's claim to kinship is a trap. A well is both danger and means of rescue. A grave becomes a temporary prison, and the dead man's ring becomes the sign of life continuing. The tale has the pace of a long comic chase, but its humor depends on the transformation of a sheltered merchant into someone who has learned, by one terrible night, to distrust the apparent explanation of every door he enters.
