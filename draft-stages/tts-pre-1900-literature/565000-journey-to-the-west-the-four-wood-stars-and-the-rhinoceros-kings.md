# Journey to the West: The four wood stars and the rhinoceros kings

## A firefly in January

The three disciples reached Green Dragon Mountain while the last of the festival moon still lit the stones. Mysterious Flower Cave lay ahead with its doors shut, and Bajie was already lifting the rake to break them when Wukong made him stop. Their master might be tied close to the entrance. Smashing the door without knowing where the captors slept could kill the very man they had come to save.

Wukong put away his cudgel and changed into a firefly. Such a little light in the cold first month of the year was almost a contradiction, but it was small enough to enter the crack beside the stone door. Inside, several bull-headed servants slept where they had fallen after the long battle. The demon kings rested farther in. Wukong flew through the chambers and found Xuanzang chained to a column, lamenting the Lantern Festival that had led him to bow before impostors.

The monk lifted his face and saw the little flickering light. Why, he wondered, was a firefly moving through a winter cave? The light drew close and whispered, "Master, it is me."

Wukong took his proper shape. Xuanzang nearly cried out with relief. The monkey told him that Bajie and Sha were outside, that the doors could be opened, and that speed was more valuable than an elaborate plan. He touched the lock. The iron fell away. Xuanzang followed him through the corridors, stepping as softly as he could in the unfamiliar darkness.

A demon king woke and called for the watch. The sleeping servants scrambled up, banging their weapons and beating their night clappers. Two came down the corridor just as Xuanzang passed an open arch. They shouted that the prisoner was escaping. Wukong struck them down and tried to clear a path, but the alarm spread through the cave. More doors opened, more creatures ran into the galleries, and the three kings began dressing for battle. Wukong cut his way outside and called to his brothers. Xuanzang, slower and frightened, had been seized again.

Bajie looked past Wukong for the monk and saw nobody. The monkey explained the failed escape. The brothers demanded battle at once. The three horned kings came out carrying an axe, a sword, and a whip-like weapon. They fought together in a rhythm that left little space between their blows. Wukong met one, Bajie another, Sha the third. For a while the hillside rang with iron, and neither side gained much ground.

Then the demons called in their lesser troops. The water-buffalo and ox spirits threw themselves into the gaps. Bajie was pulled from his feet and bound. Sha tried to withdraw to protect him and was caught as well. Wukong knew what would happen if he let himself be trapped beside them. He flew free on a cloud and returned to Compassionate Cloud Temple alone.

## Asking Heaven for a particular kind of help

The monks were dismayed to hear that all three of Xuanzang's companions had failed. Wukong reassured them that he had not abandoned the prisoners. He wanted to know what the enemies truly were. The forms beneath the flags were those of three rhinoceros spirits. Their titles, Cold-Averting, Heat-Averting, and Dust-Averting, were names of power, but they were not names bestowed by the Buddha. If Wukong found a force able to master them by nature rather than by strength alone, the battle might end quickly.

He went to the western gate of Heaven. The Gold Star, Venus, encountered him among the celestial officials and asked why he had come. Wukong gave an account of the festival lamps, the costly fragrant oil, the three figures who had posed as Buddhas, and the captive monks. Venus listened and smiled at the difficulty. The Great Sage, he said, had recognized that the monsters were rhinoceroses but had not asked what could overcome rhinoceroses.

The three beasts had cultivated magic and possessed horns of great value. Their powers allowed them to move through air and water and perform the wonders used to deceive worshippers. But a set of four stellar beings called the Wood Creature Stars had a particular dominion over such creatures. Where Wukong's iron cudgel had met resistance, the mere appearance of these stars might turn the fight.

Wukong hurried to the Jade Emperor and requested their deployment. The emperor gave an order, and the four stars came to the gate with Wukong. They did not need an army of ten thousand or a grand celestial siege engine. Their power lay in a relation between kinds of beings, one recognized by the order of the heavens even when the earthbound traveler could not perceive it.

At Green Dragon Mountain Wukong called the three kings out again. The demons laughed at his return. Their servants spread to encircle him, expecting that a monkey who had fled twice would finally be caught. Then the Wood Creature Stars stepped into view and challenged them by name.

The kings looked up and changed. They recognized an enemy before whom their long practice and precious horns would not protect them. Their bull-headed followers broke into animal panic. The three kings dropped their weapons, returned to their four-footed forms, and fled toward the northeast. Wukong pursued with two of the stars, while the others set about clearing the cave and rescuing the prisoners.

Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha came out into daylight to find a different kind of victory than the one they expected. They had not been rescued by Wukong's staff breaking the gates. Celestial allies had frightened their captors into flight, and the lesser servants who could not escape lay defeated along the mountain paths. Bajie collected the valuables that had accumulated in the den, and he and Sha set it alight. Mysterious Flower Cave, home of the counterfeit Buddhas, was reduced to a black opening among the rocks.

## The sea that did not shelter them

Wukong had no intention of letting the three kings disappear into another district and repeat the festival trick. They ran to the western sea, using the magic of their horns to open a passage through water. The pursuing stars and the monkey followed them into the waves. Below, the fight continued in a world where sound came dull through the water and great currents rolled around the combatants.

The sea had its own watchmen. They recognized the Great Sage and his celestial companions and ran to tell Ao Shun, Dragon King of the Western Sea. The dragon called his son, Prince Mo'ang, and ordered the marine troops to cut off the flight. Turtles, fish, crabs, and shrimp soldiers came out from the crystal palace with weapons in their hands. The rhinoceroses found the road ahead blocked by soldiers of water and the way behind occupied by the stars.

They scattered. One was brought down and bound with a hook through its nose. Wukong shouted that he wanted the beasts alive to face the officials of Jinping, because their crime had been committed openly against a town. But fighting creatures did not always stop when the Great Sage gave a command. One of the Wood Stars caught a second rhinoceros and bit through its neck before Mo'ang could prevent the killing. The third pleaded for mercy as the other pursuers closed around it. It too was seized and bound.

The dead beast was stripped of its valuable horns. Wukong ordered the two living ones brought back to the prefecture to answer for years of fraudulent worship and the tax extracted from families who believed they were paying for blessings. The horns were trophies, but they were also material proof of the spirits' true shapes, unlike the shining Buddhist figures the crowd had knelt before.

They returned to Jinping, where the prefect and townspeople had waited with fear and hope since the festival. The captives were exhibited before officials, and the story of the lamps was told publicly. The three supposed Buddhas had been rhinoceros spirits. The expensive fragrant oil had been tribute stolen by creatures who could pose as holiness whenever the moon and the lamps made the imposture convincing.

The surviving rhinoceroses were condemned and killed. Their horns were divided as the authorities and divine helpers determined. The prefecture had not recovered every coin spent on the false ritual; many years of payments could not be gathered back into the homes that had made them. But the source of the demand was gone. The lamps no longer had to be filled for visitors who used sacred names to buy their own feasts. The town could keep the beauty of its festival without paying the tribute that had concealed a theft.

## The guests the city would not release

The people of Jinping welcomed Xuanzang and his companions as saviors. Families invited them to meals; officials brought gifts; the monks of Compassionate Cloud opened their doors and tables every day. Bajie looked upon this arrangement with satisfaction. His most persuasive argument for a long stay was that refusing hospitality would insult the people they had saved. The monk who had wanted to remain only for the full moon now found the festival becoming another obstacle to departure.

Xuanzang accepted a number of meals out of gratitude and courtesy. Then he watched the invitations continue to arrive and began to see no natural stopping point. Every wealthy household wished to honor the pilgrims; every invitation promised another. The people were generous, but the scriptures remained in the west and the pilgrim had a duty that could not be fulfilled by remaining a guest forever.

Before dawn one morning Xuanzang told Bajie to prepare the horse and Sha to take up the luggage. Bajie rubbed his eyes. Why leave a city with so many good vegetarian kitchens when so few of them had yet fed him? Wukong answered that they had eaten enough for the present journey. Xuanzang asked them to move quietly so that the monks would not have to choose between kindness and letting their guests depart.

They slipped through the monastery gate while the town still slept. The Lantern Festival's crowds and the counterfeit Buddhas were behind them. The four travelers entered the road with the quiet relief of men no longer trapped by celebrations, whether false ones contrived by demons or genuine ones prolonged by human gratitude.

Wukong looked west. The Buddha's country was nearer. That fact did not mean his master would never again be deceived by a sacred appearance. It meant the journey was about to test their judgment where religious memory and royal desire were woven together more closely than before.
