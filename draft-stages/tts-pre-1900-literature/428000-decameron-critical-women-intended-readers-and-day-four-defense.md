# A Book Addressed to Women

The *Decameron* makes a conspicuous claim about its audience. Boccaccio addresses women who suffer from love and have fewer means of distraction than men. Men, he suggests, can go about their business, travel, hunt, gamble, and enjoy varied public pursuits. Women are more often confined by domestic expectation. He offers stories as relief. The premise is an act of attention to women's constrained lives, yet it is also shaped by a male author's assumptions about those lives. We should hear both features at once.

Inside the story, seven of the ten narrators are women. The company begins because Pampinea voices a plan for escaping the plague, argues for its moral legitimacy, and persuades the others to act. Women choose themes, judge stories, and govern the group for most of its days. When women speak in Boccaccio's invented company, they do not speak as a single chorus. Fiammetta's choices differ from Emilia's; a storyteller of romantic patience can sit next to one who enjoys practical trickery. Their differences make it difficult to reduce the book to a simple statement that medieval women were either wholly powerless or unexpectedly free.

Look at Day Six, Tale Seven. Madonna Filippa is brought before a court under an adultery law that would subject a convicted woman to severe punishment. She does not deny the essential charge. Instead she asks why a law governing women was established without their consent and why consensual love should be punished under its present form. Her witty argument changes the crowd's response and leads to the law's modification within the fiction. The pleasure is not merely that she escapes. A woman turns the procedure of public judgment into an occasion for challenging the terms on which her body is judged.

Place her beside Ghismonda in Day Four, Tale One. Ghismonda argues with her father Tancredi for the legitimacy of her love for Guiscardo. Her eloquence does not protect her lover from death, and she chooses to die after his murder. The two stories do not show that eloquent resistance always succeeds. They show the power of speech under radically different political conditions. In one, an audience can be persuaded; in the other, private sovereign authority proves deaf to an argument it cannot bear to recognize.

Boccaccio interrupts the tale sequence at the beginning of Day Four to defend his own writing. He has been criticized, he says, for paying too much attention to women and to love. He tells the story of a boy raised away from women who, on seeing them, finds them irresistible despite his father's attempt to call them by an animal name. The so-called goslings anecdote mocks the idea that desire can be made to disappear through renaming or isolation. It also stages another male author's claim to describe women's power over men. His defense gives women attention, but it does not make them authors of the book itself.

The conclusion returns to the question of audience. Boccaccio imagines readers who may skip stories they dislike. That insistence on reader choice has a liberating possibility: the intended women readers are granted the power to judge rather than merely receive lessons. Yet within the tales, not every woman has that power. A bride may be traded through a family contract, a servant may be used in an impersonation, and someone who speaks frankly may suffer for it. The social location of the imagined female reader and that of the characters are not identical.

Scholars often discuss the book in terms of competing attitudes toward women, sometimes described by the pairing of praise of women and misogyny. The opposition is helpful if it opens a question, not if it supplies a final label. The same author who makes a woman an eloquent critic of law also allows Emilia to defend marital beating, and makes Griselda's obedience the object of admiration and debate. These contradictions need not be explained away as a hidden master plan. They are part of the moral range the frame permits.

A rigorous reading asks more exact questions than whether Boccaccio was for or against women. Who speaks? To whom? Who controls the money? Who can refuse a marriage? Who knows the real identity of a lover? Does the storyteller applaud the outcome, or is the outcome itself disturbing? The intended women readers, the fictional women who tell the tales, and the women inside them occupy different positions. Keeping them distinct allows us to hear the vitality of the book without pretending that a writer's claim to console is the same thing as full equality.
