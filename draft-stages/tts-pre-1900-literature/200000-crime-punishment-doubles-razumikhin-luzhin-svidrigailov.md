# Crime and Punishment — Three Counterlives

One reason Crime and Punishment can sustain a long philosophical argument without turning into a treatise is that ideas appear as people who make decisions. Raskolnikov is not the only character tempted by power, suffering, or the need to be exceptional. Luzhin, Svidrigailov, and Razumikhin offer contrasting ways to organize a life. Their similarities and differences expose what the protagonist cannot see about himself.

Luzhin calls his intended marriage to Dunya sensible. He offers money and status while expecting gratitude and submission. His calculations emphasize apparent mutual advantage but conceal a wish to possess the moral superiority of a benefactor. When Dunya refuses him, he tries to disgrace Sonya with a manufactured theft. The episode reveals how a man who speaks of rational arrangements can treat other people as interchangeable pieces in a plan to preserve his own importance.

Svidrigailov appears less interested in respectable explanations. He often acts as though desire and boredom justify whatever follows. He uses money freely and sometimes provides real help, including assistance for the Marmeladov children. His generosity cannot erase the coercion he exercises toward Dunya. If Raskolnikov's idea of exceptional action sounds heroic in his own thoughts, Svidrigailov makes us see what exemption from the demands of other people can look like when stripped of noble language.

Razumikhin is the most obvious counterexample. He is neither wealthy nor brilliant in any certified, world-changing sense. He works, makes friends, cares for Raskolnikov during illness, and helps the mother and sister he has only recently met. His actions are not perfectly calculated, and he is capable of embarrassment, excitement, and error. His moral strength arises from a willingness to remain available when another person's needs interrupt his plans.

The three men clarify Raskolnikov's divided character. Like Luzhin, he imagines family sacrifice as a question he can settle from above. Like Svidrigailov, he is tempted to believe that a person can create a private law. Like Razumikhin, he is sometimes moved to immediate acts of generosity. None of the comparisons fully explains him. That is the value of a counterlife: it isolates possibilities without reducing the central character to a single hidden essence.

Notice the role money plays in each case. Luzhin offers financial security as a claim to obedience. Svidrigailov can give money while withholding respect for another person's independence. Razumikhin has little to spare but uses time and labor to provide help. Raskolnikov kills while citing economic benefit, then hides the stolen objects without using them. The novel is not saying that money is inherently corrupt. It shows how the meaning of help depends on the terms under which it is given.

Porfiry can be included in this network of comparisons, though he occupies a different institutional role. He, too, tries to control conversations and foresee another person's reactions. The distinction lies in the responsibility attached to that control: he pursues a charge and eventually urges a confession, while Raskolnikov wants to make himself the sole judge of an act against others. The magistrate's methods are not beyond criticism, but they cannot be understood simply as the opposite of the criminal's intelligence.

Sonya refuses the terms of the whole competition for exceptional status. She does not try to prove that she deserves sovereignty over anyone else. Her attachment to particular people and insistence on truth make Raskolnikov's choice more visible. What he calls weakness in ordinary moral bonds is also the condition for recognizing another person's separate existence.

A close-reading exercise is to choose one scene for each of the three counterlives. Compare Luzhin accusing Sonya, Svidrigailov confronting Dunya, and Razumikhin assisting Raskolnikov's family. Ask who controls the setting, what each person can refuse, and whether aid comes with a demand for surrender. Dostoevsky's philosophical argument is carried by these concrete differences of conduct, not only by the debates about Napoleon.
