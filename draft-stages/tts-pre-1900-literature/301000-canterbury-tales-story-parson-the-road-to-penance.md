# The Canterbury Tales — The Parson’s Tale, the Road to Penance

It is late in the day. The travellers have heard stories of love, theft, saints, violence, deceptive speech and marvelous transformations. Harry Bailey asks the Parson for a tale to complete the entertainment. The Parson refuses to tell a fable. He will speak in prose, he says, about the journey that matters beyond every earthly road: the way toward repentance and salvation. His contribution is not a plot in which an individual hero acts through a sequence of events. It is an extended account of how a person recognizes wrongdoing and attempts to change.

The Parson begins with penitence. A person must feel genuine contrition, confess the truth and make satisfaction as far as possible. Such acts cannot be reduced to embarrassment or fear of being caught. Contrition requires seeing sin as a corruption of the relationship with God and with one's neighbors. A catalogue of religious categories follows, including the seven deadly sins and the virtues that counter them. Pride is answered by humility, envy by love of neighbor, anger by patience and meekness, sloth by spiritual effort, greed by generosity, gluttony by temperance, and lust by chastity.

Each category expands into an account of ordinary life. Pride can attach to clothing, rank or the desire to dominate another person. Envy can thrive in conversation that delights in another's misfortune. Greed can become the wish to hold more wealth than one needs, or the exploitation of someone who cannot defend himself. These are not abstractions detached from the pilgrimage. Readers who have heard the Reeve's revenge, the Merchant's possessive husband, the Pardoner's sales techniques and the Friar's corrupt visitor can recognize patterns of wrongdoing without being given a list of which pilgrim must be condemned.

The Parson also reflects upon confession. Speaking truth is difficult because the sinner must surrender the self-image that made wrongdoing tolerable. Confession should not become another performance for advantage; the Pardoner's admission of fraud was brilliantly clear and yet did not lead him to stop selling. Nor is penitence merely a private feeling. Satisfaction includes trying to repair the harms one has caused. That claim connects the treatise with Prudence's advice to Melibee and with the losses that cannot be repaired, such as Virginia's death.

The discourse is long and demanding, and it lacks the quick reversals of comedy or romance. Its placement can therefore feel like a deliberate challenge to the Host's original contest. The pilgrimage to Becket's shrine has always had a religious destination, though many stories seemed to forget it. The Parson brings spiritual pilgrimage into the language of explicit instruction. He insists that the company has been moving through questions of conscience whether or not the tellers intended to do so.

But an edition's decision to place this treatise near the end is not evidence that Chaucer finalized the order of every fragment. The poem survives as an unfinished collection whose larger architecture remains uncertain. We can appreciate the resonance between a literal road and a moral path without assigning an authorially guaranteed conclusion to all preceding tales. This is a final voice in many manuscript arrangements, not a verdict that cancels earlier voices. After the Parson's prose comes a retraction in which the author asks forgiveness for writings that may have encouraged worldly vanity. That closing gesture will raise one final question: can the creator of such lively fiction also ask his readers to renounce it?
