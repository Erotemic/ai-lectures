# Journey to the West: The ring that stole Heaven's weapons

## A monkey without his staff

Wukong had stood before emperors, argued with kings of the underworld, and fought troops that filled the skies. Once, long ago, the Jade Emperor had sent the armies of Heaven to seize him, and Wukong had laughed while his iron staff scattered them. The weapon had become so familiar that he hardly thought of it as something he could lose. Yet in Golden Cave a one-horned demon had thrown a white ring into the air, and the staff had flown away from its master's hand.

Now the monkey stood outside the cave with empty fingers. Within were Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha Wujing. The three had left the circle he drew to protect them, tempted by the false warmth of a grand house, and the monster had captured them. Wukong had gone to fight and had discovered that his enemy possessed a device which could steal the very means of fighting.

For a while he was overcome with sorrow. He had not lost his master entirely; no one had yet told him that Xuanzang was dead. But he had lost the staff that gave him confidence in a direct fight. He sat and wept, speaking bitterly of his companions' disobedience and his own failure to rescue them. Pride was difficult to maintain when the object on which so many victories had depended was no longer his.

Then he remembered that power did not reside only in the iron staff. There were gods who owed him favors, officials who feared him, and celestial institutions whose job was to know when beings and treasures had gone missing. A demon strong enough to rob him might be a fugitive whom Heaven could identify.

He went upward, leaving the snowy mountain behind.

## The emperor's registers

At the heavenly court, Wukong demanded an audience. He did not arrive with the careless boldness of his first rebellion, proclaiming himself equal to Heaven. He came as a disciple responsible for a human master, and he had a particular complaint: a demon had seized that master and stolen his own iron staff by means of a white ring. The Celestial Emperor ought to know whether some dangerous being had escaped from his appointed place.

The Jade Emperor summoned the officials responsible for examining the heavenly ranks. Registers were checked, stars and divine attendants accounted for, and word returned that no celestial officer was missing. The mountain demon could not simply be identified as a member of the heavenly court absent without leave.

This information did not tell Wukong who the creature was. It only removed the easiest answer. He had hoped to discover an escaped official who could be ordered home. Instead he found that the ordinary records offered no explanation for the one-horned king or his ring.

Wukong asked for soldiers. The Jade Emperor had once sent many of them against the monkey himself; surely a few could be spared to recover a Tang monk from a cave. He obtained the assistance of Heavenly King Li and the prince Nezha, with other celestial fighters assigned to support them.

Wukong led the new company back to the mountain. He had been alone when he lost his staff; now he stood with an army's authority behind him. The king of Golden Cave was summoned out again to face the combined force.

Nezha had a reputation earned in battles beyond the experience of ordinary mortals. He could assume a form with three heads and six arms, each hand carrying a different weapon. His spears, swords, rings, and other instruments moved together in a storm of blows. Against a conventional enemy the display would be overwhelming. One fighter had become as dangerous as several, striking in directions no single human body could guard.

The One-Horned King faced the attack without surrendering. At the right instant he raised the white ring.

The weapons flew from Nezha's hands.

It was not a matter of the prince being slower than the demon. No blade had been knocked aside by a staff; no spear had shattered upon armor. The ring simply took the weapons, just as it had taken Wukong's. Six armed hands became six empty ones. Nezha, astonished, could do little but withdraw. Heavenly King Li's soldiers had seen their champion disarmed before the conflict had properly begun.

Wukong was furious. He had brought warriors from Heaven to solve a problem of missing weapons, and the first result was a larger collection of missing weapons. The demon now possessed Nezha's arms along with the iron staff.

## Fire answered by a ring

Another approach might succeed where metal had failed. The celestial fire powers knew a force that could not be grasped like a sword or club. Wukong called for assistance from the fire officers, hoping that flame and heat would drive the demon out even if the ring could snatch away ordinary arms.

The fire deity came with his attendants and instruments. They arranged themselves around the cave. Their arts filled the mountain with burning forms, flames shaped and directed by heavenly command. The cold air brightened. Snow and frost gave way to radiating heat. Even the demon's soldiers looked uneasy at the approach of so much celestial fire.

Wukong watched with renewed hope. This was no spear for the ring to catch; it was the element itself. The enemy might resist it, but surely he could not put a river of fire inside a little white circle.

He was wrong.

The One-Horned King raised his ring. The instruments of fire, the burning projectiles, the devices through which the power had been brought to bear—all were taken from their owners. The attack collapsed. The fire officials recoiled from the spectacle of their own forces being drawn away. Another attempt had enriched the demon rather than weakened him.

Wukong looked from one celestial ally to another. Heaven had given him the power to fight an adversary. The adversary had collected the equipment as though the gods were delivering gifts.

## The Yellow River in half a basin

If fire could not be brought against the cave, perhaps water could. Wukong sought the water officials, who understood the courses of rivers and the immense forces they contained. The Water Earl of the Yellow River was consulted, and the plan grew extravagant. A half-basin of river water, prepared and commanded by divine power, could be poured upon the mountain. It would be enough to inundate the cave and force the demon from his place of safety.

The measure of water was impossible by ordinary standards. No mortal could gather a great river into a household vessel or carry it into the hills. But celestial offices were accustomed to powers ordinary mortals could not command. What seemed small in the hand could represent a torrent on the earth.

Wukong stood by while the water deity approached the cave and released the flood. A great rush surged toward the entrance. The mountain sides trembled with its force. The monkey expected to see the demon's defenses dissolve beneath the weight of the water. After fire had failed, water ought to succeed by sheer mass.

The ring intervened again. The door of the cave remained protected. The torrents could not be driven where Wukong wanted them. Instead the water spread outward across the surrounding country. Fields were drenched. Low ground flooded. The force which was supposed to rescue a single monk began to harm the people who lived below the mountain.

Wukong was alarmed. He had summoned the flood, but he had not meant to destroy farmland in its path. He demanded that the water be taken back. The Water Earl explained that what had been poured out could not simply be scooped back into the basin and undone. A river is not a blade one can pick up after a failed blow. Its water follows the land.

The experiment had supplied Wukong with a new difficulty. His master remained imprisoned, the demon remained secure, and the countryside had suffered from the very method intended to defeat him. The monkey could not go back to the villagers whose crops had been flooded and explain that this was only an unsuccessful attempt at a larger good.

## What remains without a weapon

Fire had failed. Water had failed. A prince armed with six weapons had failed. Wukong decided to try the one resource that could not be hung in the demon's armory: his own body.

He challenged the One-Horned King to fight without weapons. If the ring could steal an iron staff, it might have nothing to seize when two opponents grappled, struck, and dodged with bare hands. The monkey and the demon closed on one another in a contest of fists and feet, each trying to find a gap in the other's guard.

The One-Horned King was strong enough to endure the assault. Wukong, accustomed to victories achieved by the weight and reach of his staff, now had to fight at arm's length. He could still leap, twist, kick, and strike. He could still use all the stubbornness that had made him a terror to celestial armies. But the demon would not be toppled merely because the monkey announced a new rule for the contest.

Wukong plucked hairs and turned them into copies of himself. The air crowded with monkeys who pressed the enemy from every side. They did not need to carry his iron staff to be troublesome. The One-Horned King, however, knew the trick. The white ring rose again, drawing away the forms or the hairs that gave them substance. The swarm vanished as suddenly as it had appeared.

Wukong understood that he had learned something, even though he had not won. The ring could take not only metal but the magical means by which his body multiplied its reach. It was not simply a magnet for swords. Whatever the One-Horned King held, it was powerful in a way that none of Wukong's companions had encountered in their ordinary battles.

He needed to learn where the demon kept what he stole.

## The fly in the enemy's house

The great cave that had seemed impossible to attack from outside now became a place Wukong might enter by stealth. He reduced his body until he was small enough to slip into spaces the servants scarcely noticed. In this shape he passed through the entrances and crevices of Golden Cave, moving silently among the demons who believed their king had defeated the most troublesome pilgrim on the road west.

Inside were the signs of victory. Servants had brought in weapons taken from celestial fighters. Objects that had been terrifying in their owners' hands lay among the spoils of a single enemy. The ring, still with its master, could be used again; the confiscated treasures were no longer on the battlefield to threaten him.

And there stood Wukong's iron staff.

He recognized it at once. No other weapon could be mistaken for the pillar he had carried from the dragon palace through innumerable fights. It had been taken from him by a trick, but he had not forgotten how to handle it. A thief might carry the staff into a cave; only its true owner knew its weight, its changing size, and its relationship to the creature who commanded it.

Wukong approached, restored his proper form, and seized it. At once the old confidence returned. The familiar weight settled into his hand. He could make it short enough to conceal or long enough to break through obstacles. He had come into the cave without it and now held it again before the demon could arrange another contest.

He made his escape, striking a path through the confusion of the monster's servants. The stolen staff had been recovered, but the white ring had not. The One-Horned King remained a dangerous master of his own cave. All the celestial fighters who had lost their weapons still wanted them back. Xuanzang, Bajie, and Sha Wujing had not been freed. Wukong could threaten the entrance again, but he could not yet stop his enemy from lifting the ring.

Outside, the mountain looked much as it had before. The winter wind still moved among the rocks. The cave still held his friends. Wukong, with his staff in his hand, had learned that a weapon could be regained without the battle being won.

He would need a better answer to the ring than another army, another flood, or another blow.
