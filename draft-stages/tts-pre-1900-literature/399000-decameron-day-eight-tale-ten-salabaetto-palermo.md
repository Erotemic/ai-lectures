# The Decameron — Salabaetto and the Sicilian Woman

A young Florentine merchant, Salabaetto, travels to Palermo to sell woolen goods. His business requires him to turn stock into money and send the proceeds home. In the city a woman known as Jancofiore, or Biancafiore in some translations, notices him and devises a more profitable affair than a simple courtship. She surrounds him with signs of wealth, attention and refinement. Salabaetto takes the performance for proof of sincere affection and gradually allows the commercial judgment that brought him to Sicily to be governed by the desire she has encouraged.

After their meetings have made him confident of her love, Jancofiore invents an urgent need. She must have five hundred florins, she says, and only a man devoted to her can help. The sum is effectively the money Salabaetto has received for the merchandise entrusted to him. He lends it. She delays repayment, then becomes difficult to find. He discovers that the expensive-looking household and its apparent emotion were arranged to convince him to part with his funds. Losing the money threatens both his pride and his obligations as a merchant.

He leaves Palermo for Naples, where an older acquaintance, Pietro Canigiano, helps him think through the fraud. Pietro's counsel is not to appeal to the woman's pity or to try to recover the money through public shame. Salabaetto must create a better prospect of profit than the one she has already taken. He returns to Palermo and lets it be known that he has access to a far larger shipment, worth thousands of florins. The woman hears of the opportunity and renews her friendship. To gain his trust, she returns the original five hundred.

Salabaetto now presents a fresh difficulty. He needs another thousand florins to release his goods from a supposed emergency. As security he offers access to a warehouse believed to contain the valuable shipment. Jancofiore, judging the apparent merchandise more valuable than the loan, advances the money. But the warehouse is filled with goods of little value, arranged to look like the expected cargo. When she discovers the truth, Salabaetto has escaped with the cash and recovered more than she once took from him.

Dioneo's long concluding story is a fitting answer to the brief financial trick that opened the day. Gulfardo made a payment count as the repayment of a debt. Salabaetto, instructed by Pietro, makes worthless stock count as security for a loan. In each case the decisive object is not merely money but an interpretation of money: what a transfer proves, what inventory represents, what a promise can make another party imagine it owns.

The story also gives a different form to the anxieties that run beneath the country-house comedy. Merchants depend on information, credit, strangers and distant transactions. A handsome face and a staged household can become part of a counterfeit balance sheet. Salabaetto must first learn how his judgment was captured before he can exploit Jancofiore's desire for a larger gain. The revenge is clever; it also teaches the same deception rather than establishing an open standard of fair dealing. Two frauds end with one fraudster triumphant.

After the story, Lauretta hands the crown to Emilia. Emilia will allow a new change of pace: the ninth day's company may tell stories without a prescribed common subject. The movement from Day Eight to Day Nine is therefore also a release from the strict rhythm of moral themes. With eighty tales told, the company has practiced almost every manner of disguise and reversal. Yet every teller still depends upon the same basic tool: a listener willing to inhabit a world that words have made.
