# Journey to the West: The river of mothers and the water of release

## A meal that was not a trap

Someone was calling from beside the road, and the travelers had every reason to distrust the invitation. Not long ago they had entered a splendid house and awakened as prisoners inside Golden Cave. The small white ring they had met there had beaten every force Wukong could summon, until Laozi came to claim his runaway blue ox and the diamond snare it had stolen.

But this voice was not another demon's. The mountain and earth spirits came forward with a steaming bowl and explained that Wukong had obtained the food earlier, before the companions left his protective circle. They had kept it ready and warmed it when they knew the rescue was over.

Xuanzang accepted it with fresh gratitude for his disciple. He admitted that he had dismissed a warning and suffered the consequences. Wukong could not resist making the lesson memorable. His master had stepped out of one circle because he did not believe in it, only to fall helplessly into the power of another. The ring around Golden Cave's king had been stronger than any armed guard.

Bajie, for once, had little interest in disputing the point while a meal was being shared. The four rested, thanked the spirits, and went on. Spring was beginning to fill the valleys. Birds called above new leaves. The road wound toward an ordinary-looking river with an old ferry crossing. Nothing along its banks looked especially dangerous.

Across the water they could see cottages under the willows, but no boat. Bajie shouted for the ferryman. Eventually a small vessel emerged, steered by an elderly woman. Wukong asked where her husband was. The woman smiled and offered no explanation. She brought them safely across, received a few coins without arguing over the fare, and walked toward the houses.

Xuanzang was thirsty. The river looked clear and cold. He asked Bajie to fill the begging bowl. The monk drank some, and Bajie finished the rest. Wukong and Sha Wujing did not join them.

It was one of the most consequential drinks of water on the entire journey.

## A pain neither had expected

Not long after they resumed traveling, Xuanzang began to groan in the saddle. Bajie was suffering too. Their bellies swelled, and beneath their hands something seemed to move. The pain became so severe that the party stopped at the first cottage they saw. Wukong hoped the household might provide hot water and medicine for what looked like a violent illness.

An old woman sat outside, working with hemp. She listened to their account and began to laugh. When they told her they had drunk from the river, she called other women to come and see. Wukong's temper rose. His master was in pain, Bajie could hardly stand upright, and their hosts seemed to regard the sight as entertainment.

The old woman finally explained. This was the land of Western Liang, a kingdom inhabited entirely by women. The river was the Mother-and-Child River. Its water caused pregnancy without the presence of men. Women of the kingdom drank it when they were old enough to bear children, then went to a special spring to determine whether they had conceived. The travelers had drunk it without knowing what it did.

Bajie looked down at his swelling belly. Xuanzang was horrified. The old woman did not mean to describe a mere stomachache. Within days, she said, the two travelers could give birth. They were already feeling the growth of the strange pregnancy.

Wukong made jokes, but he did not mistake the suffering for harmless comedy. Xuanzang begged for medicine to end the condition. Bajie, who usually found a way to joke about his appetite or his size, wanted relief just as urgently. The old woman knew only one remedy: water from the Abortion Spring at a place called the Child-Breaking Cave on Mount Jieyang. A few mouthfuls would dissolve the unnatural pregnancy.

There was a difficulty. A religious adept known as Ruyi, the True Immortal, had taken control of the spring. He had turned the nearby cave into an establishment of his own and demanded offerings from people who needed the water. Rich gifts of wine, food, and ceremonial goods were now the price of what local people had once been able to draw for themselves. A begging monk with no money would have trouble buying even a bowlful.

The spring was some thirty li away. Wukong asked for a vessel. The old woman found a clay bowl and asked him to bring extra water, if he could. There were other women who would need it. He told Sha Wujing to guard the sufferers, gave the household a warning to treat them decently, and went into the air.

## The price of a spring

The mountain was lovely: wooded slopes, flowers, clear streams, and the quiet buildings of a hermitage. Wukong descended with his bowl and greeted a disciple at the entrance. The man asked his business. Wukong said that his master had accidentally drunk the river water and that he wished to obtain some of the spring to relieve him.

"Where are your offerings?" the disciple asked.

Wukong had none. He suggested that the master of the place might make an exception when he heard who had come to ask. The disciple went inside to announce the visitor.

Ruyi had been playing the zither. At the mention of Sun Wukong's name, he stopped. Whatever fee he might normally have demanded was no longer the central issue. He came to the door armed with a long metal hook, enraged before he had heard a second word.

Did Wukong know the child called Red Boy, he demanded? Did he remember the demon who had lived at the Fire-Cloud Cave and nearly burned the pilgrims to death?

Wukong knew him very well. Red Boy was the son of the Bull Demon King, Wukong's former sworn brother. Guanyin had taken the child into her service as Sudhana, the Youth of Good Fortune. From Wukong's point of view, the child had received something better than life as a kidnapper and fire-breathing ruler of a cave.

Ruyi was the Bull Demon King's brother and Red Boy's uncle. To him, the old freedom of his nephew had been destroyed. He wanted to avenge the boy's humiliation. That the same monkey should arrive at his door and ask for a favor seemed intolerable.

"The child lives and serves a bodhisattva," Wukong argued. "How does that make me his murderer?"

"He was free before," Ruyi answered. "What is freedom worth in another's service?"

The question was not one Wukong could talk away. The immortal lunged with his hook. Wukong defended himself with the iron staff. After a sharp fight the monkey drove him back and tried to draw water, but Ruyi avoided a decisive battle and returned whenever Wukong bent over the well. A hook caught Wukong by the leg and sent him sprawling. Another attempt ended in another fall. Wukong could defeat his opponent in a fair exchange of blows, yet he could not fill a bowl while fighting him.

He was angry enough to break the spring's defenses and punish the gatekeeper. Then he remembered that the people who required the water would suffer from any damage he did to it. He needed not a bigger blow but a second pair of hands.

## Wukong's distraction

When Wukong returned without water, Xuanzang could hardly bear the disappointment. Bajie groaned beside him. The old woman offered to watch them while another disciple joined the search. Sha Wujing was reluctant to leave the master suffering, but the household promised care and the plan was clear. He took a bucket and rope and flew back with Wukong.

They landed beyond the hermitage. Wukong gave his instructions quietly. Sha was to hide with the vessel. Wukong would challenge the True Immortal at the front. When both fighters were too occupied to watch the spring, Sha was to slip inside, draw as much water as he could, and return immediately.

Wukong banged at the gate and demanded the water again. Ruyi emerged, surprised that the monkey had returned to an encounter he had already found inconvenient. They began to fight, staff and hook flashing in the air. Wukong did not try to finish his opponent quickly. Each time Ruyi gave ground, the monkey pursued; each time the immortal attempted to lead him away from the well, Wukong followed. That was the point. The guard who claimed to own the spring believed he was controlling the battle, while the real visitor was already behind him.

Sha Wujing crossed the courtyard and reached the well. The disciple at the door objected. Sha did not stop to debate. He struck with his demon-subduing staff, breaking the man's arm, and filled his bucket. He spared the disciple's life, then carried the water out under cover of the noise of the duel.

"Brother," he called from the air, "I have the water. Leave him."

Wukong turned on Ruyi with the satisfaction of a man whose troublesome errand was finally complete. He explained that the fight had been a diversion and that Sha had taken what their master needed. He also said he was sparing Ruyi partly because he had not actually committed a capital offense, and partly for the sake of the Bull Demon King. But Ruyi, unable to swallow the insult, struck once more. Wukong avoided the hook, knocked the immortal down, took the weapon, and broke it into pieces.

He warned the keeper not to withhold the spring from those who came in genuine need. Then he left the mountain with Sha. The old quarrel with Red Boy's family remained unresolved, but the water was in their hands.

## A bitter medicine, a good supper

The old woman's household greeted the returning disciples with relief. They were astonished by the speed of their flight and by how much water Sha had brought. For Xuanzang and Bajie there was only one question: would it work?

Each drank. Soon their bodies began to cramp again. The pressure released in an undignified series of urgent visits to the household's privies. The swelling sank away. They were no longer in danger of giving birth. Xuanzang, embarrassed and grateful, recovered enough composure to accept the porridge the women prepared. Bajie, after announcing that he was quite well and needed no nursing, ate bowl after bowl until Wukong warned that a feast might make his belly swell for an entirely different reason.

The women asked whether they might keep the remaining spring water. Bajie was finished with it. The household carefully stored the precious supply. The old woman said it was valuable enough to provide for her funeral expenses. A medicine that should have belonged to a common source had become a costly treasure because a powerful guardian had placed himself above it.

That night the travelers rested beneath a roof where their hosts had first frightened them by laughing and then saved them by sharing what they knew. The river had not been a demon in disguise. It had been exactly what it was, and the mistake was drinking without understanding the country.

By morning Xuanzang and Bajie were strong enough to travel. They thanked the household and set off toward the capital of Western Liang. There, they would discover that the kingdom's river was only the beginning of a confrontation with its customs, its rulers, and its hopes for the men who had arrived from the East.
