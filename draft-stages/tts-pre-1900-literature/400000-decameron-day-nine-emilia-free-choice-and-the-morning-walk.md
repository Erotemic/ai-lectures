# The Decameron — The Last Open Day

The company wakes to a day without an appointed subject. Emilia, newly crowned queen, has refused to confine the speakers to a single kind of tale. Their conversations have already ranged through religion, misfortune, thwarted love, tricks and countertricks. Now, after the eighth day's elaborate rivalries, she lets every storyteller choose. This freedom is not the absence of order. There is still a queen, a sequence of speakers, and the expectation that ten stories will carry the afternoon. Only the topic is released.

In the morning Emilia rouses the young women and men and leads them into a nearby wood. Deer and goats move through the trees without their ordinary fear. The narrator offers a striking explanation: during the pestilence, human hunters have ceased to be the constant danger they ordinarily are. The animals move freely not because every creature has forgotten mortality but because the usual human threat has been suspended. The company's escape from Florence offers a related, more fragile sensation. They are alive amid a larger catastrophe, enjoying a space that cannot be guaranteed to last.

The friends gather leaves and aromatic plants, walk, sing and return to the house. Food, music and the labor of their servants maintain the pleasant rhythm of their temporary society. In the afternoon they settle down to stories. Filomena begins with a widow who has two unwanted admirers. The series moves between convents, travelers, painters, an inn and a household where a husband tries to interpret a dream. The narrators return to familiar people, especially Calandrino, whose eagerness to believe bad evidence about himself has already made him a standing comic character.

But open choice does not make the day's contents neutral. One story turns a supposed medical diagnosis into a way of extracting money. Another begins with a girl's attraction and ends in marital anger. Emilia herself chooses a story that expressly defends husbands beating supposedly disobedient wives. Her argument is not a universal judgment delivered by the novel from above. It is a speech by one of its fictional storytellers, heard after many earlier tales exposed the cruel effects of husbands' jealousy and power. The collection makes the audience hold these stories together without providing a single consistent doctrine.

Dioneo, exempt as usual from the fixed speaking order, ends the day by pretending that an absurd sexual encounter is an attempted magical transformation. His joke returns the company to the worldly body after Emilia's moralizing advice. When the crown passes to Panfilo, the last king, the tenth day receives a definite subject: acts of great-heartedness and magnificence. The change matters. The storytellers will measure what human beings do with wealth, power and the possibility of giving something away. Emilia's free day gives one final view of their mixed world before the more elevated closing theme.
