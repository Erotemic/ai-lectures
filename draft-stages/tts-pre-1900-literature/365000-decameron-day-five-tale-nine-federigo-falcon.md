# The Decameron — Federigo and the Falcon

Federigo degli Alberighi has everything required of a splendid Florentine gentleman except the love of Monna Giovanna. He holds tournaments, entertains guests, and gives away wealth in an effort to be noticed by her. Giovanna neither solicits this expenditure nor rewards it with affection. Federigo impoverishes himself pursuing a woman who has given him no promise. At last he retires to a small country property, where he survives with difficulty. His remaining treasure is a magnificent falcon, both companion and means of sport.

Giovanna's life changes. Her husband dies, leaving wealth to their son and, should the boy die without heirs, to her. She moves to a country estate near Federigo's. The boy gets to know the impoverished gentleman and falls in love with the falcon. He becomes seriously ill. When his mother asks what might make him happier, he says he wants the bird, but he knows it is precious to Federigo and has not dared to demand it.

Giovanna faces a painful decision. The falcon is almost all Federigo has left. She knows he loved her and that she did not return his love; to appear now and ask for his best possession seems cruel. Yet she is desperate to help her child. She visits Federigo, accompanied by another woman, and asks to dine with him before explaining her request.

Federigo is overjoyed and ashamed. His visitor is someone he longs to honor, but his household has no food fit for a guest of such standing. He searches for a way to provide a meal. Nothing seems adequate except the falcon. Without knowing what Giovanna has come to ask, he kills the bird, has it cooked, and serves it. The women eat. Only afterward does Giovanna explain that she hopes to take the falcon home to her sick son.

Federigo breaks down. He has given away the very thing she needed, not because he refused her request, but because he had no opportunity to hear it before trying to show courtesy. He explains, and brings the bird's remains as proof. Giovanna first reproaches him for sacrificing such an excellent creature to provide a meal. Then she recognizes the generosity that poverty has not destroyed. She returns home without the falcon. Her son dies soon afterward; the narrative does not establish that obtaining the bird would have saved him.

Time passes, and Giovanna's brothers urge her to marry again. She would prefer to remain a widow, but their pressure persists. She declares that if she must marry, she chooses Federigo. Her brothers object that he is poor. She answers in favor of the value of a good person over mere possessions. She brings wealth to the marriage; Federigo, now responsible for a household again, lives with greater care in managing it.

There are two kinds of giving in this tale. Federigo's early spending is magnificent and ineffective, driven by hope that wealth will buy affection. His final gift is sincere but tragically misdirected. He kills his falcon because he believes a guest deserves the best of the house, never knowing that his guest has come specifically for the living bird. Generosity alone is not sufficient; it must meet another person's actual need. The story's poignancy depends on the failure to communicate before acting.

The ending, too, should not erase its losses. The child dies. The falcon dies. Giovanna's new marriage is made possible by widowhood, grief, kinship pressure, and a judgment she forms about Federigo's character. Federigo is rewarded in the romance, but not because every act he undertook was wise. Indeed the final line emphasizes his improved management of money, a quality absent from his earlier courtship.

Fiammetta tells this as the ninth tale of her own day. Compared with the terrifying spectacle that produced Nastagio's marriage, her story proposes a different measure of worth: conduct that remains generous after status and fortune have disappeared. The most cherished gift cannot be given twice. That irreversibility gives the novella its sorrow, even as its concluding union brings a form of happiness.
