# The Decameron — Zima and the Palfrey

In Pistoia, a young man known as Zima is in love with the wife of Messer Francesco Vergellesi, a knight who is preparing to leave for a public office in Milan. Zima is admired for his elegance, and he owns a fine riding horse, a palfrey. The knight wants the horse but is unwilling to pay what it is worth. Zima offers a bargain: the palfrey is his if he will allow Zima to speak privately with his wife in the knight's presence.

The husband accepts. Perhaps he is so sure of his wife's obedience that he regards the interview as an empty concession. He instructs her to listen but not to respond. The men have negotiated an occasion in which her silence will make the husband's acquisition look risk-free. She is placed before Zima like someone whose presence can be exchanged as a favor.

Zima begins to speak. He tells the woman of his long devotion and asks her to offer him some sign that he is not hopelessly pursuing a dream. She obeys her husband's instruction and says nothing. Zima then performs the tale's extraordinary turn: he begins to answer on her behalf. In an imagined reply he gives voice to the sympathy and permission he hopes she will feel, explaining that after her husband has left for Milan, a pair of towels placed in a window could signal that the garden gate is open. He has made a one-sided declaration into a complete dialogue, supplying both question and answer.

The knight, delighted by what he takes to be his wife's steadfastness, praises her silence and goes away with the palfrey. Zima complains, in a final witty exchange, that a conversation with a stone is not what he paid for. Meanwhile the wife has heard the proposed plan, and she has also heard how insistently he imagines her responding. After the knight departs for Milan, she considers the attention she has received, her husband's absence, and her own wishes. She chooses to place the towels in the window. Zima sees them and enters by the indicated route; the pair begin an affair.

The story turns on an important distinction. The husband's permission for Zima to speak is not permission for the wife to answer. Nor is Zima's invented reply evidence that she has actually consented to a meeting. At the moment he gives his imaginary speech, it remains a fantasy designed to persuade. The decisive act occurs afterward, when she privately decides whether to signal him. Boccaccio's plot is constructed so that the husband thinks he has purchased a horse for nothing, while the wife receives an opportunity to act that he did not intend to grant.

Elissa is interested in the power of language to create an event. The double dialogue is a verbal invention: Zima rehearses the future he wants until the woman can imagine entering it. The palfrey becomes a visible marker of the husband's greed and overconfidence, while the towels at the window become a private sign of a decision that only the two lovers understand.

This is not simply a story about eloquence conquering resistance. A person who is ordered not to speak has to be offered something more than an opportunity to answer aloud. Here the husband treats her as trustworthy because he believes she has no independent voice, and his certainty enables the very choice he believes he has ruled out. Zima gains his desire, but what makes the story more than a clever sale is the wife's eventual action after the bargain has ended.
