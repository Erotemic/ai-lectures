# Journey to the West: The robbery, the trial, and the returned host

## The men who watched the procession

The pilgrims were a little way beyond Diling when the sky darkened and rain began to fall. They had been accompanied that morning by drums, banners, servants, monks, Daoists and a wealthy household's tearful farewell. Now the road was empty. They took shelter in a ruined building once dedicated to the divine radiance of an older shrine, its roof broken but still sufficient to keep some of the weather off the scrolls and clothing. Xuanzang had thanked Kou Hong for feeding them and had promised to return after receiving the scriptures. He did not know that the procession had been watched by men who cared nothing for the old man's vow.

In the town, a band of thieves discussed where to find money. They had spent what they owned on gambling and pleasures, and they wanted the next night to be easier than the last. A prosperous household had been displayed to them in the very streets: Kou's house, where four foreign monks had been fed and escorted with enough ceremony to occupy half the town. The robbers reckoned that riches worth stealing must be stored behind those gates. They gathered knives, clubs, ropes, torches and ladders, waited for the streets to empty in the rain, and broke in.

Kou Hong's servants scattered. His wife hid beneath a bed. His sons fled to protect themselves. The robbers opened boxes and cupboards, taking silver, gold, clothing, ornaments and anything else easily carried. The old man saw the goods being taken and could not bear it. He had spent years opening his house to strangers; that night strangers entered without asking and wanted to empty it.

"Please," he begged, stepping into their path. "Take what you need, but leave an old man something for the rest of his days."

They kicked him down. He died on the floor of his own house while the thieves carried their bundles into the rain and escaped over the wall of the town. The next household sounds were cries of grief. The family gathered around the body of the man who had spent so long feeding monks. His vow was complete. His life, they believed, had ended before he could know whether the travelers ever reached their goal.

## A false accusation

Grief and anger sought an explanation. Kou Hong's wife remembered how the visitors had resisted remaining longer, and how lavishly her husband had displayed his wealth in sending them away. Her resentment turned into a story she could lay before the magistrate. She declared that the robbers had been the very monks they had entertained: Xuanzang with the torch, Bajie with a knife, Sha carrying the treasure, and Wukong striking the fatal blow.

Her sons believed her. They knew the travelers had stayed long enough to learn the passages and rooms of the house. If a mother said she had recognized them in the torchlight, why should her children doubt her? They wrote a petition naming the four guests as robbers and murderers and took it to the prefectural court. The official hearing the complaint knew something of Kou Hong's public feast and the foreign monks. He sent men after them at once, believing he was pursuing criminals of a particularly cruel sort.

Meanwhile the actual thieves, fleeing west, had stopped in a sheltered hollow to divide their plunder. The rain had ended. The pilgrims appeared on the road ahead, four travelers with a horse and burdened packs. The robbers thought they had found another easy source of money, perhaps even more than the man they had just killed. They hurried out and blocked the path with their weapons.

Xuanzang trembled. He had lost count of the times a road that looked safe had produced men or monsters demanding his life. Wukong stepped forward and asked what they wanted. They demanded payment for using the road. He asked what they had in their bundles. They boasted of their success and revealed that the goods had come from the rich man who had entertained the monks.

Wukong recognized the danger. He could have struck the thieves down. The same monkey had killed men on earlier roads, an act for which Xuanzang had driven him away. Now he used cunning and control instead. With a few movements and the aid of his magic he subdued the thieves and seized their stolen goods. When the master saw jewels, clothing and money that plainly belonged to Kou Hong, he insisted they be taken back to the household.

"He gave us everything we needed," Xuanzang said. "We cannot leave his family to suffer this loss."

Wukong had wanted to punish the thieves severely. He looked at Xuanzang and did not strike. He let the robbers go, carrying none of their loot, while Bajie and Sha loaded the recovered property onto the horse and their shoulders. The four turned back toward the town. Their gratitude was about to lead them straight into the accusation made against them.

## The court mistakes what it sees

They met the officials on the road. The pursuers saw the monks, their conspicuous faces, and the wealth of the murdered Kou household in their possession. It appeared to confirm the complaint. They surrounded the pilgrims and seized them before the contents of the bundles could be explained. The goods intended for restitution became evidence of theft. By the time Xuanzang had been brought before the prefect, the story of his guilt had already taken the form of an official record.

He protested that Kou Hong had fed them and that the goods had been recovered from highwaymen. He offered the travel document, bearing seals that traced his journey from the Tang lands. The prefect asked why the monks had not detained the robbers and brought them in. Why were there no criminals in custody except the monks themselves? And if the travelers were innocent, why had the dead man's sons named them? An officer could construct a convincing case without ever discovering what had happened.

Xuanzang's explanation weakened under the weight of a written accusation, an official's certainty and the treasure laid out before the court. Wukong saw that the hearing might turn into a beating before the truth had a chance to appear. When the officers threatened to tighten a torture cord around his master's head, he declared that if anyone had committed the robbery, it was he alone. He claimed the torch, the weapon, the stolen goods and the killing.

The prefect, pleased to hear what sounded like a confession, ordered the cord applied to the monkey instead. The officers pulled. The cord snapped. They tried again. It snapped again without making Wukong's forehead so much as wrinkle. Their instruments had not been designed for a stone-born disciple of immortals.

The proceedings were interrupted by news of an important official approaching the city. The magistrate ordered the monks confined in prison until he could resume questioning. The prisoners were led away. The keepers fastened them into restraints and began to beat them, demanding money to stop. Xuanzang had never been able to bargain calmly when threatened with a blow. Wukong told him that the guards wanted a gift and suggested surrendering the beautiful brocade robe. Xuanzang recoiled. The robe had come to him from Guanyin and belonged to the mission. Yet the guards were already hurting them, and he allowed Wukong to offer it.

When the prison keepers opened the wrapping, light from its ornaments filled their dark room. They quarreled over who could claim it. One man's greed briefly interrupted another man's violence. In that grim little comedy, Wukong found a way to get through the night and keep his master alive.

## Three messages in the night

Wukong's body could not be held by a magistrate's ropes if he chose to escape. But he did not simply break through the prison wall and leave the accusation behind. Kou Hong was dead, the household believed the monks had killed him, and the officials thought the evidence pointed in one direction. They needed an answer that would survive Wukong's departure.

He moved through the city at night, working on the fears of those who had made the accusation and those who believed it. To the Kou household he sent the appearance of the murdered man, compelling his family to reckon with his fate. To the prefect he sent a dreamlike warning, in the name of one who could speak for the dead. And to the local officials he sent a celestial messenger warning them that they had imprisoned good people and would be held accountable if they did not release them.

By morning the magistrate was in no mood to tighten another rope. Officers hurried to open the cell. The prefect and his assistants received the four prisoners with apologies, ordered their horse and baggage restored, and tried to explain that the matter had been a misunderstanding. Wukong did not allow the explanation to stand alone. He demanded the return of every item entrusted to the officials. The horse was led back. The bags were brought out. Even the precious robe was recovered from the keepers.

Xuanzang understood that the result still rested on supernatural warnings and the officials' fear, not on a full account of the crime. He asked to go to Kou Hong's house so the matter could be examined there. Wukong agreed, but proposed a better witness than any of those already heard.

"Let me ask the dead man," he said. "He was there."

## Kou Hong returns

While the others waited at the house, Wukong rose into the air and went to the underworld. Its judges were accustomed to the great monkey's visits and to his willingness to demand immediate answers. He asked where the soul of Kou Hong had gone. The judges told him that the charitable gentleman had been taken to Kshitigarbha, the great bodhisattva associated with the suffering and release of beings in the realm of the dead. Wukong went there directly.

Kshitigarbha knew Kou Hong's name. The old man's life had ended when his allotted span reached its limit, but his generosity had been remembered. He had been given work keeping a record of good causes. Wukong explained that the man's family had made a terrible false charge, that the real robbers had escaped, and that the host needed to speak for himself. Kshitigarbha agreed to let Kou Hong return to the world for another twelve years.

The old man was bewildered when he saw Wukong. He remembered the attack and the abrupt end of breath. He had not expected the monkey he had fed to come looking for him among the dead. Wukong told him what had happened after the robbery and brought him back.

At the house, the coffin was opened. Wukong returned the soul to the body. Breath came. Kou Hong rose, alive where his family had been mourning him. He saw Xuanzang and his disciples, the prefect, his wife and sons, all gathered under one roof. He thanked Wukong for bringing him home. Then he learned that his family had accused the guests of murder.

"They did not kill me," he said. "The men who came with torches took our goods. One struck me down when I begged them to leave something. These monks had nothing to do with it."

The household could no longer maintain its accusation. The wife and sons bowed in shame. The magistrate, who had nearly tortured an innocent monk into admitting a crime, allowed the family to escape further punishment. The case ended with thanks, embarrassment, renewed respect for supernatural power and no adequate reckoning for the ease with which an official had trusted a false petition and the sight of stolen property.

Kou Hong wished to entertain the travelers again. He had been granted twelve further years and wanted to begin them by feeding the four men whose encounter had returned him to life. But Xuanzang feared another round of delay. He thanked the family, acknowledged the generosity that had helped them all along, and asked to continue west. Kou Hong prepared another farewell, smaller in intention though still rich with gratitude. The old man watched them depart a second time, alive to stand at his own gate.

The pilgrims went on. Xuanzang carried no gift from the house except a debt of kindness; Wukong carried the memory of a court that had mistaken evidence for understanding; Bajie carried whatever food could be packed before the farewell; Sha carried the bags and kept them in order. Beyond the next roads lay the mountain whose name had sustained their journey. The last human wrong had been set right as far as the novel allowed, though it had not been simple or entirely just.
