The novel's original three-volume divisions are not an automatic three-act formula. The story lectures divide the narrative at logical pivots rather than pretending each one reproduces a whole physical volume. Full plot spoilers precede criticism.

## Distinguish testimony, motive, and fact

- Wickham's claim that Darcy unjustly deprived him of a church living is **his persuasive account**, not a narrative finding. Darcy's later explanation, the Georgiana episode, and Wickham's conduct require Elizabeth to revise the judgment.
- Darcy's letter **admits** separating Jane and Bingley while offering an explanation; it is neither an unqualified apology nor proof that the harm to Jane was justified.
- Elizabeth's initial judgment of Darcy is not wholly invented: he did behave arrogantly toward her and later insulted her family while proposing. Her error is granting Wickham's attractive story too much authority.
- The Longbourn entail is a **particular restriction on this estate**. It does not mean English women were universally barred from owning or inheriting any land. The legal description of Longbourn can itself be debated by specialists; the novel's operative premise is that Mr. Collins expects the estate on Mr. Bennet's death.
- Lydia's marriage to Wickham averts immediate disgrace but is deliberately **not** the same narrative outcome as Jane's and Elizabeth's marriages. The epilogue documents continuing financial disorder and failing affection.
- Pemberley does not prove character by wealth alone; the changed conduct toward the Gardiners and the private rescue of Lydia are independent tests of Darcy's claimed transformation.

## Print and literary history

- [Jane Austen's House](https://janeaustens.house/online-exhibition/six-winters-online/six-winters-scene-4/) dates the published book to 28 January 1813 and explains the late-1790s *First Impressions* draft and extensive later revision. The complete discarded early draft does **not** survive in a form permitting full reconstruction.
- [University of Nebraska's Austen Said project](https://austen.unl.edu/background) demonstrates free indirect discourse with specific scenes. The narrator's style may momentarily borrow a character's diction; this does not make every seeming third-person assertion an independent narrator's endorsement.
- [JASNA on entail, elopement and marriage law](https://jasna.org/publications-2/persuasions-online/volume-44-no-1/hall/) examines the economic stakes. [JASNA's legal discussion](https://www.jasna.org/publications-2/persuasions-online/vol38no1/collins/) cautions against importing a technically oversimplified entail explanation.
- [Tom Keymer's study](https://academic.oup.com/book/38558/chapter/334240524) discusses voice and free indirect discourse; only its public abstract was consulted in this pass.

## Questions to use with the audio

1. Which independent facts initially support Elizabeth's belief in Wickham? Which details rely exclusively on his word?
2. Why can Charlotte rationally choose a marriage Elizabeth rightly regards as unattractive?
3. Where does Austen allow an elite household's 'polite' speech to disguise contempt or ambition?
4. What action, rather than confession, best demonstrates that Darcy has changed?
5. Why does the epilogue continue with Lydia and Wickham after the two happiest marriages?
