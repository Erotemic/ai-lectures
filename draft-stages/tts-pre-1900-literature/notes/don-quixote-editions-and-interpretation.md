# Don Quixote: editorial and source notes

## Narrative boundaries

The 1605 First Part has 52 chapters; the 1615 Second Part has 74. The first
four story manuscripts divide chapters 1–22, 23–52, Part II 1–41, and Part II
42–74. Those divisions are editorial and intended for continuous listening.
The two books should be understood as a literary work whose publication was
separated by a decade, not silently rewritten as a modern single-volume plan.

## Evidence and literary form

- The windmills, sheep, basin, boy Andrés and galley slaves belong to Part I;
  the story of Cardenio and Dorotea, the *Curious Impertinent* and the captive's
  tale are distinct narratives integrated in the later Part I chapters.
- The fictional Moorish historian Cide Hamete Benengeli is an authorial device,
  not an actual historiographic witness to the characters' adventures.
- The printed Part I becomes part of the characters' lives in Part II; Sancho's
  lie about Dulcinea becomes a recurring plot mechanism; the puppet show, Cave
  of Montesinos, and ducal pranks complicate the simple 'reality versus
  fantasy' formula.
- The duke and duchess create the staged governorship of Barataria; Sancho
  exercises practical judgment and later resigns.
- Avellaneda's pseudonymous 1614 sequel preceded Cervantes's 1615 second
  part. The false continuation is explicitly encountered within Part II;
  Barcelona and the printing shop help stage that argument about authorship.
- Carrasco, as Knight of the White Moon, defeats Quixote in Barcelona; the
  latter returns, renounces chivalry and dies as Alonso Quixano.

## Texts and reception

The Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes hosts Spanish reading editions,
facsimiles of original 1605 and 1615 printings, and the public-domain English
John Ormsby translation. The Biblioteca Nacional de España collects edition
history. The University of Glasgow Special Collections describes early
printings, an awkwardly placed attempted correction concerning Sancho's donkey,
the English translation of Part I (Thomas Shelton, 1612) and the Avellaneda
book. Anthony J. Close's Cambridge chapter provides scholarly context for the
novel's relationship to the picaresque; only its abstract was consulted.

The major narrative sequence and bibliographic facts were checked against
accessible texts and reference pages. No exhaustive chapter-by-chapter
collation of the complete early modern Spanish witnesses or competing modern
translations is claimed. Analysis of women's agency, social rank, humor and
friendship is identified as criticism rather than the narrator's final verdict.
