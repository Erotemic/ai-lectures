## Money and evidence: do not collapse distinct sums

- **Katerina's three thousand rubles** were entrusted to Dmitri; he used part and later claims to have kept the rest sewn in a cloth. The exact proportion he spent is central to his defense and confusion.
- **Fyodor Pavlovich's envelope of three thousand rubles** was prepared as an inducement for Grushenka. Smerdyakov takes this sum after killing Fyodor and later shows it to Ivan.
- Dmitri's separate **paternal inheritance grievance** is also a motive in the prosecution's story. It must not be mistaken for either of the two envelopes above.
- Dmitri does **strike Grigory** and threatens his father. Those are real wrongs. Smerdyakov's confession identifies him as **Fyodor's murderer**. The court convicts Dmitri of that murder because the physical and circumstantial evidence is persuasive while Ivan's testimony is undermined by illness and Smerdyakov is dead.
- The case shows a distinction between **legal guilt**, **moral responsibility for known actions**, and **the broad ethic of solidarity** taught by Zosima. Do not treat these as synonyms.

## Speakers and genre boundaries

- *The Grand Inquisitor* is an embedded fiction invented by Ivan. Its claims are neither the chronicler's historical report nor a direct statement by the author.
- The skeptical formula often paraphrased as 'everything is permitted' passes among voices and later readers. Do not attribute it as an unambiguous freestanding quotation without checking translation and dramatic context.
- Ivan's encounter with a 'devil' is narrated through fever and disordered consciousness. A supernatural interpretation is possible, but it should not be silently asserted as a plain factual event.
- Smerdyakov's possible illegitimate paternity is reported through family gossip. Avoid asserting a legally established genealogy that the text does not furnish.
- The text contains philosophical dialogue, family farce, monastic biography, criminal inquiry, trial speeches and a schoolboy narrative. **No character is an automatic authorial mouthpiece**.

## Publication and textual witnesses

- Serialized in *Russkii vestnik* in **1879–1880**. A separate book edition appeared at the end of **1880**, although early copies may bear an **1881** imprint. Dostoevsky died in early 1881.
- His preface anticipates a further narrative about Alyosha, **never completed**. The course describes the ending of the extant novel without constructing an unwritten sequel.
- The accessible complete English base is **Constance Garnett's 1912 translation**, as transcribed by Project Gutenberg. Different translations vary in tone, Russian diminutives, speech registers, and famous phrases. Exact wording-dependent interpretation should consult a specified edition and preferably the Russian.
- Scholarship on active love and on the poetics of polyphony can illuminate scenes without eliminating their critical ambiguities. The source records separate primary access, institutional bibliography, and critical interpretations.
- No complete collation of original serial, first book, revised Russian editions, and all English translations was attempted.
