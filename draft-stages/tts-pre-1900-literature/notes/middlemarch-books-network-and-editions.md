## Edition and historical boundaries

- **Publication:** *Middlemarch* appeared in eight separately sold books in 1871–1872, followed by bound editions. This is not the same as a uniformly weekly magazine serial. The [New York Public Library's eight-part collection](https://old-digitalcollections.nypl.org/collections/middlemarch) identifies the surviving parts and book titles; [Cornell's exhibition](https://exhibits-prod.library.cornell.edu/womenLit/feature/george-eliot-1819-1880) records the 1871–1872 issue and later four-volume format.
- **Reading text:** [Project Gutenberg ebook 145](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/145) provides a complete later English reading text. The digital text is not a facsimile of the original eight paperback issues, and it is not evidence that the variants between every lifetime printing have been collated here.
- **Historical setting:** The fiction is largely set in 1829–1832, at the approach of the Reform Act. Characters' campaign and railway debates are scenes in a novel, not an exhaustive account of parliamentary or technological history.
- **Author and narrator:** George Eliot was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans. Narrator judgments and fictional characters' thoughts must not be quoted uncritically as biographical statements made by Evans outside the work.

## Interpretive and factual controls

1. **Bulstrode and Raffles:** Distinguish the secret historical wrongdoing, the act of failing to carry out instructions during Raffles's illness, and an allegation of premeditated murder. The novel establishes a serious failure of care and temptation; do not overstate legal findings.
2. **Lydgate:** The temporal proximity of Bulstrode's loan and Raffles's death fuels public suspicion, but it does not show the doctor accepted a payment for wrongdoing. Keep reader knowledge distinct from provincial rumor.
3. **Mary and Featherstone:** Mary refuses to burn a will; she does not determine the final inheritance by willingly suppressing a document. Her choice is primarily about integrity and legal vulnerability.
4. **Casaubon's codicil:** The property provision penalizes Dorothea if she marries Will. The accusation implied by the codicil is not proof of adultery; the novel distinguishes appearance, motive, and fact.
5. **Lydgate and Rosamond:** Their marriage is not reducible to a single villain or a single cause. Debt, unspoken expectations, losses, pride, and power shape the trajectory.
6. **Finale:** Eliot recounts Lydgate's death at fifty and the lives of Dorothea/Will and Fred/Mary. Describing the Finale as merely an endorsement of domesticity erases its simultaneous interest in limited public opportunities.
7. **Class and labor:** Caleb Garth's skill and esteem for work do not prove that estate hierarchy is equitable. The novel's narrator grants unequal interior access to landowners and laborers; critical readings should recognize the distinction.
8. **Exact wording:** For a language-based claim, name the edition, paragraph, and chapter. These lectures analyze rhetorical methods largely without depending on long quotations.
