## Evidence distinctions

- **Magwitch and Compeyson:** the men have criminal histories, but Magwitch's account reveals differential treatment tied to appearance, language, and class. Do not treat either his account or Pip's initial revulsion as a simple independent court record.
- **Miss Havisham:** Pip's idea that she is secretly grooming him to marry Estella is his inference, **not** a promise established by Jaggers or Miss Havisham. Her manipulation of Pip and Estella does not make Pip's fantasy true.
- **Estella:** she states limits on her own emotional availability. Pip's determination to interpret her as his destined wife should not be mistaken for her consent to that expectation. Her childhood training is relevant to, but does not fully erase, her agency.
- **Joe and Biddy:** their marriage is their own decision, not a prize available to Pip when he finishes his education.
- **Molly and Estella:** Pip reconstructs Estella's parentage through limited sources. The story's emphasis on her ancestry challenges easy class assumptions but does not supply Molly's complete independent testimony.
- **Magwitch at death:** Pip's care for him is the climax of his change of judgment. Legal confiscation of Magwitch's fortune should not be conflated with a voluntary gift by Pip.

## Publication and ending

The [Dickens Project chronology](https://dickens.ucsc.edu/resources/dickens-chronology/) records serialization in *All the Year Round* from **1 December 1860 to 3 August 1861**. A [weekly installment inventory](https://www.victorianweb.org/victorian/authors/dickens/ge/geserialisation.html) enumerates the 36 issues and chapter spans. These facts support broad claims about serialized suspense; no claim is made that each weekly chapter ends with the same type of cliffhanger.

The [Wisbech & Fenland Museum manuscript account](https://www.wisbechmuseum.org.uk/dickens/) documents Dickens's earlier, bleaker ending and its revision following a suggestion by Edward Bulwer-Lytton. The original conclusion was not published as the book's conclusion in Dickens's lifetime. It was subsequently printed in John Forster's *Life of Charles Dickens*; [The Victorian Web transcribes that version](https://www.victorianweb.org/victorian/authors/dickens/ge/ending.html). In it Estella has remarried and she and Pip separate after an encounter in London. In the revised, first-published ending they meet at the ruined Satis House and the closing image suggests a possible future together without explicitly narrating marriage. The manuscript's once-present phrase **“but one”** after “no parting” was not retained in the standard published final line. Those are distinct manuscript/publication states, not a vague universal inconsistency between every printed book.

**Transportation context:** The [UK National Archives research guide](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/criminal-transportation/) and [National Library of Australia account of tickets and pardons](https://www.library.gov.au/research/family-history/family-history-research-guide/convicts-research-guide/tickets-leave) distinguish different degrees of colonial freedom and limitations on returning to Britain. [Leon Litvack at Queen's University Belfast](https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/139141883/PRISONS.HTM) discusses Magwitch and penal transportation. The novel describes a fictional case, not a statistical sample.

## Discussion questions

1. What evidence does Pip actually have when he identifies Miss Havisham as his financial benefactor?
2. When does the older Pip admit shame, and when might his confessional manner itself invite sympathy?
3. What can Joe and Biddy do that rich characters cannot? What can they not do because of their social positions?
4. How does Magwitch's transportation to Australia complicate the apparently domestic story of financial ascent?
5. Compare the two endings without presupposing that the revised line promises a wedding.
