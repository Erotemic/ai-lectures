## Edition history

The **Herman Melville Electronic Library** distinguishes the October 1851 British edition (*The Whale*) and the November 1851 American edition (*Moby-Dick; or, The Whale*). The British edition was reset from proofs, contains editorial cuts and changes, and omits the epilogue. Melville's manuscript and proof sheets have not survived in a form that resolves every alteration. Publication order must not be conflated with composition order. The companion uses the American epilogue, which explains Ishmael's survival. Its chapters are read through a Project Gutenberg transcription, not a scholarly diplomatic facsimile.

## Factual and ethical distinctions

- **Ahab's interpretation is not the narrator's finding.** The novel records his belief in a malevolent intention behind the whale; it does not establish a whale's supernatural motive as a fact.
- **Ishmael is not an ordinary consistent eyewitness.** Dramatic soliloquies and narrated private scenes exceed what a shipmate can have witnessed. This is a literary method, not verified testimony.
- **Pip's suffering is not a freely chosen experiment.** His prolonged exposure at sea follows a dangerous labor assignment and an abandonment, producing psychological harm.
- **The Rachel's captain is looking for his missing son.** The ship rescues Ishmael after the Pequod sinks; that rescue does not assert the boy was recovered.
- **Queequeg's coffin becomes the ship's life buoy** and then sustains Ishmael. Ahab's specially forged harpoon does not guarantee victory and the line draws him to his death.
- **Melville's classification is historical and literary.** The narrative's cetology, anthropology and claims about other peoples are not current biological or ethnographic authorities.
- **Whaling is represented through commercial work and violent killing.** A book's admiration of seafaring skill is not evidence that the industry was sustainable or ethically benign.
