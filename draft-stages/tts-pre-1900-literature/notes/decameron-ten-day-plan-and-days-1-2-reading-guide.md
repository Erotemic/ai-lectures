# Boccaccio's *Decameron*: ten-day map, text sources and reading guide

**Editorial status:** the manuscripts in this overlay cover the opening plague
frame and **all of Days One and Two** (20 tales, lecture IDs **312000–333000**).
Days Three through Ten and the critical studies remain **planned**, not authored.
The ten storytelling days are spread over roughly two weeks with scheduled
non-storytelling days; they are not ten consecutive nights. The book has a
proem, the plague frame, an authorial intervention at the opening of Day Four,
individual day introductions and conclusions, and the author's conclusion.

These thematic descriptions are editorial orientation, not a constraint on
Dioneo's later special privilege to tell last and diverge from the day's theme.
A separate critical section should follow the **complete** narrative, covering
Boccaccio's career, the 1348 plague as history and fiction, Florence and Naples,
commerce and credit, the church and anticlerical humor, Jewish and Muslim
characters, coercion and sexual double standards, the novella form,
the narrators' voices and rulership, translation, manuscripts, and subsequent
influence on Chaucer, Shakespeare and later European prose.

## Literary and historical cautions

- The narrative begins during the **plague of 1348**, but the ten speakers and
  their organized retreat are a literary frame, not independently documented
  persons offering diary testimony. The description of Florence provides
  precious historical perspective but not a complete modern medical history.
- The title is sometimes glossed as *Ten Days*; the hundred stories are
  arranged in ten sets of ten, each with its own governing ruler and literary
  relationships. The frame narrative and transitions matter as much as the
  best-known stories.
- Abraham's conversion (I.2) is written within a Christian conversion plot;
  Melchisedech's ring parable (I.3) answers a threatening question without
  deciding doctrinal truth. Neither passage is an ethnographic report about
  Jewish belief or Muslim governance.
- Alatiel's story (II.7) includes coercion, intoxication, murder and repeated
  removal from one household to another. Describing the end as fortunate
  reflects the narrative's public marriage conventions and should not turn
  the preceding experiences into uncomplicated romance.
- Bartolomea (II.10) is abducted by Paganino before choosing to stay with him;
  the latter choice does not erase the original act of force. Zinevra (II.9)
  proves that evidence of access to a bedroom is not proof of consent.
- Rigg's **1903 translation** is freely available but sometimes archaic in
  diction and can carry nineteenth-century interpretive habits. The lectures
  are modern original retellings, not claims about a single definitive English
  rendering of every Italian phrase.
- The Berlin manuscript **Hamilton 90**, dated to about 1370 and accepted as
  an autograph witness, is important to establishing Boccaccio's involvement
  in the book's written design. It does not mean our retellings reproduce the
  manuscript letter for letter.

## Further listening questions

1. Compare Ciappelletto's confession (I.1), the ring story (I.3), and the
   staged proof against Zinevra (II.9). Which speakers know more than their
   listeners? What counts as credible testimony?
2. Compare the Marchioness's banquet (I.5) and the Gascon lady's appeal (I.9).
   When can indirect speech protect someone with less political power?
3. Compare Bergamino's story (I.7) and Landolfo's chest (II.4): does the first
   depend upon a patron's change of mind, the second upon chance, or both?
4. The second day promises fortune after misfortune. For whom is Alatiel's
   ending a successful restoration? For whom is Bartolomea's? Who controls
   the interpretation of these endings?

## Scope and evidence

Story outlines and consequential text details are grounded in the primary
English text available at Brown and in the complete Rigg index, with additional
university and scholarly context where available. This is **not** an
exhaustive line-by-line comparison of Italian manuscript witnesses, nor are
interpretations asserted to be the sole readings permitted by the book.
Spoken word counts are descriptive, not a lecture-length acceptance rule.
This contribution contains source text only, no pictures, recordings or binary assets.
