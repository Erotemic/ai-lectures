## Facts that adaptation shorthand often obscures

- **Frankenstein is Victor's surname**, not a proper name given to the creature in the novel. The creature speaks at length, reads literature, evaluates evidence and reports its own crimes.
- The animation method is not a disclosed scientific recipe. Historical electricity and physiology debates are background, not a canonical mechanism.
- The creature **kills William and frames Justine**. Victor did not directly perform those acts, but his initial abandonment and subsequent silence remain relevant to responsibility.
- The creature reports saving a drowning girl and being shot. That report is nested testimony, not an independently witnessed scene.
- Victor destroys a female being **before animating her**. The dispute concerns his promise, the first creature's isolation, and the prospective independence of a being whose consent cannot be known.
- The creature's wedding-night threat is interpreted by Victor as directed at **him**; he leaves **Elizabeth** alone and she is murdered.
- Walton **returns home rather than continue the expedition**, despite his desire for discovery.
- The creature announces an intention to destroy itself. **The promised death is not shown**; it disappears into Arctic darkness.

## Editions, biography, and authorship: source discipline

- [Shelley-Godwin Archive's Frankenstein page](https://shelleygodwinarchive.org/contents/frankenstein/) and its [notebooks introduction](https://shelleygodwinarchive.org/contents/frankenstein/the-frankenstein-notebooks-introduction/) document the 1816–17 draft and later edition witnesses. The extant draft is incomplete; no single complete original '1816 story' survives.
- [Shelley-Godwin Archive's chronology](https://shelleygodwinarchive.org/contents/frankenstein/frankenstein-chronology/) dates the first three-volume publication to January 1818, a new named 1823 edition, and the revised 1831 edition.
- **Elizabeth Lavenza** is Victor's cousin in the 1818 text. The 1831 text changes her childhood origin to a foundling admitted to the Frankenstein family. Do not silently blend the two versions.
- In several places the 1831 revision changes language about Victor's early life, destiny, and personal choice. Interpretative claims about fatalism versus responsibility must identify a textual witness.
- **Mary Shelley is the author.** Percy Shelley's visible contribution to draft and fair-copy revision is a legitimate matter of textual scholarship; it is not a reason to misattribute the novel. The [Frankenstein Variorum](https://frankensteinvariorum.org/viewer/MS/box_c56_from_chapter_1) displays comparison across manuscript, 1818, Thomas, 1823 and 1831 witnesses.
- The 1831 introduction is Mary's retrospective narrative of the 1816 ghost-story challenge. As reminiscence, it should be compared with the manuscript chronology rather than assumed verbatim.
- [National Library of Medicine exhibition](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/frankenstein/exhibition1.html) gives the history-of-science context. A [scholarly Oxford Bibliographies entry](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62337/reference-article-abstract/554127238) outlines interpretive debates in its public extract; the full entry was not used.

## Prompts for the listener

1. What does Walton witness himself, and what has passed through the accounts of Victor and the creature?
2. In the first moment of life, what actions by the creature can be observed independently of Victor's disgust?
3. Why is Justine convicted? Who supplied the incriminating evidence and who had reason to remain silent?
4. Is Victor's proposed companion primarily a remedy for the first creature, a new independent life, or both?
5. Which narrator claims helplessness against fate while also choosing secrecy or retaliation?
6. What difference separates Walton's last decision from Victor's earlier decisions?
