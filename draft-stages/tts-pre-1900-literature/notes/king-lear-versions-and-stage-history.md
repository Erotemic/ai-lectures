# King Lear: Story Map, Textual Witnesses, and Editorial Boundaries

**Content range:** 038000–047000. **Primary author:** William Shakespeare. **Work:** *King Lear*, written around 1605–1606; first quarto printed in 1608, Folio in 1623.

The four story lectures precede all criticism, are complete through the ending, and follow a pragmatic composite sequence. These are original spoken retellings, **not dramatic recordings** or substitutes for performance of the Shakespeare text.

## Verified starting points and differences between early texts

- The **1608 Quarto** and **1623 First Folio** vary significantly. The Folio contains roughly 100 lines not in the first Quarto and omits roughly 300 present there, according to the Folger Library's introduction. The relationship may involve revision, theatrical adaptation, printing processes, or more than one of these. No one account is treated as proven.
- The **1619 Quarto** is another witness; it is largely a reprint with corrections and new errors. It does not erase the distinction between the earlier Quarto and the Folio.
- The early versions assign the concluding speech differently: often **Albany in Quarto, Edgar in Folio**. Avoid presenting one allocation as the sole surviving reading.
- Some early texts omit or alter scenes in Dover, including the Quarto-only scene 4.3. Modern editions may conflate the witnesses. The spoken plot describes the common pedagogical story without asserting that each detail appears in every version.
- The final comment about a "poor fool" does **not** demonstrate that Lear's Fool was literally hanged. The Fool disappears from the action; Cordelia is hanged by Edmund's order. A theory of double casting Cordelia and the Fool is possible but not documented as a fact of the 1606 staging.
- Gloucester dies **offstage**, reported by Edgar, after recognition. The servant who protests the blinding wounds Cornwall and is killed; Cornwall later dies of the wound. Edmund attempts to recall the execution order too late. These differences between action, report, and intention matter to the ending.

## Historical boundaries

- A court performance on **26 December 1606** appears on the 1608 Quarto title page and is described in the Royal Shakespeare Company's stage history. Reconstructed blocking, scenery, and exact audience reaction are conjectural.
- Shakespeare's source tradition includes legendary King Leir accounts and the earlier anonymous play *The True Chronicle History of King Leir*, plus a Gloucester-like plot known from Sidney's *Arcadia*. The lectures avoid claiming a single source mechanically determines the finished tragedy.
- The political world of James I, including English-Scottish union debates, is a relevant context but the dramatic kingdom is not a literal diagram of the Stuart state.
- **Nahum Tate's 1681 adaptation** cuts the Fool and supplies a happy ending. **Macready's 1838 revival** helped restore the Shakespearean tragedy onstage. These are facts of reception rather than evidence that the original 1606 audience understood the play as modern critics do.

## Interpretive boundaries

1. Cordelia refuses false rhetoric but is not simply speechless, passive, or unquestionably "right" in every imaginable sense; analyse what her words do within a coercive ceremony.
2. Goneril's initial complaints about the armed retinue can be practical even as her subsequent choices become abusive. Regan and Goneril have distinct trajectories.
3. Edmund suffers under illegitimacy rules; this is context for, not excuse for, his forgery and fatal orders.
4. Edgar's fake cliff saves Gloucester from an immediate death; its ethical legitimacy remains debatable.
5. Lear's disordered speech and Edgar's acted madness should not be assigned retrospective medical diagnoses as if the play were a case history.
6. Gloucester's blinding is physical violence, not a claim that disability is inherently necessary for insight.
7. Cordelia's death is not balanced by a secure providential system of just reward. The play supports argument; interpretations remain interpretations.
