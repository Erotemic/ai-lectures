## Evidence hierarchy and authorial voices

- **Primary work:** Boccaccio’s Italian *Decameron*. Brown’s online Italian text follows Vittore Branca’s 1992 edition. The direct links in the individual lecture notes are to J. M. Rigg’s English translation, first printed in 1903. Plot events in tales are **fictional** unless independently evidenced.
- **Autograph witness:** Berlin Hamilton 90, written by Boccaccio circa 1370, preserves a later authorial text with corrections and omissions. An authorial manuscript is not a complete record of every prior version.
- **Historical context:** Brown’s chronology, social-history notes, and plague resources, plus an institutional Cambridge chapter summary, are scholarly sources for questions of plague, biography, and institutions. Literary evidence does not itself prove a citywide mortality figure, a business event, or a saint’s actual life.
- **Interpretation:** A tale’s protagonist, its fictional narrator, the ten-person audience, and the historical Boccaccio are distinct voices. A fictional narrator’s endorsement of a character does not prove the author’s endorsement.
- **Reception:** Petrarch’s own letter is primary evidence of his response to Griselda. Harvard’s Chaucer site explains Petrarch’s role as Chaucer’s direct source. Folger distinguishes accepted sources from parallels and possibilities in its Shakespeare scholarship.

Do not imply full-text consultation.

- [Medieval Merchant Culture](https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/Decameron/society/structure/merchant_cult.php) — Brown University, Decameron Web. Historical context; the three-orders model and mercantile change are interpretive generalizations.
- [Medieval Merchants and Trade](https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/Decameron/society/structure/trade.php) — Brown University, Decameron Web. Context for Mediterranean commerce and the mercantile city, not proof of fictional adventures.
- [The Decameron](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/boccaccio-decameron/decameron/BE19BC6EB7005755DC3041DB3793AC95) — David J. Wallace, Boccaccio: Decameron, Cambridge University Press (1991; online 2012). Accessible chapter summary on authorial book design, title and authored rubrics; chapter may require access.
- [Review of The Decameron: A Critical Lexicon](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/33889) — Valerio Cappozzo, The Medieval Review (2022), on Forni, Bragantini and Kleinhenz (eds.). Published scholarly review discussing rhetoric, irony, laughter, representation and philogyny/misogyny; a review is not the whole book.
- [Sex: Themes and Motifs](https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/Decameron/themes_motifs/module/sex/index.php) — Brown University, Decameron Web. Theme index categorizes desire, affairs and sexual trickery; not a proof that every narrated encounter is consensual.
- [The Clerk's Tale: Contextual Notes](https://chaucer.fas.harvard.edu/pages/clerks-tale) — Harvard University, Geoffrey Chaucer Website. Identifies Boccaccio X.10 and Petrarch's Latin adaptation as Chaucer's literary source chain.
- [Letters of Old Age XVII.3: To Giovanni Boccaccio, on the Tale of Griselda](https://chaucer.fas.harvard.edu/pages/francis-petrarch-1304-1374-letters-old-age-xvii-3) — Francesco Petrarch; translated primary letter hosted by Harvard Chaucer Website. Primary evidence of Petrarch responding to and adapting Griselda; English translation, not Petrarch’s original Latin.
- [Decameron and the Philosophy of Storytelling](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/decameron-and-the-philosophy-of-storytelling/9780231136082/) — Richard Kuhns, Columbia University Press (2005). Publisher description and contents of a scholarly book; do not infer details from unread chapters.
- [Berlin Staatsbibliothek — Hamilton 90](https://www.enteboccaccio.it/s/ente-boccaccio/item/380) — Ente Nazionale Giovanni Boccaccio, manuscript authority. Catalogs the surviving authorial copy circa 1370, corrections, decoration and later interventions.
- [Italian and English Texts of The Decameron](https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/Decameron/texts/) — Brown University, Decameron Web textual project. Identifies Branca 1992 as Italian base and J. M. Rigg 1903 as English translation.
- [Giovanni Boccaccio (1313–1375)](https://chaucer.fas.harvard.edu/pages/giovanni-boccaccio-1313-1375) — Harvard University, Geoffrey Chaucer Website. Presents analogues and qualifies conjectures about Chaucer's direct knowledge of the Decameron.
- [Francis Petrarch (1304–1374)](https://chaucer.fas.harvard.edu/pages/francesco-petrarcha-1304-1374) — Harvard University, Geoffrey Chaucer Website. Explains Petrarch’s Latin Griselda and its use in Chaucer’s Clerk’s Tale.
- [All's Well That Ends Well: Source Material](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/All%27s_Well_That_Ends_Well) — Folger Shakespeare Library, Folgerpedia. Identifies the Giletta narrative reaching Shakespeare through William Painter's Palace of Pleasure.
- [List of Sources for Shakespeare's Works](https://folgerpedia.folger.edu/List_of_sources_for_Shakespeare%27s_works) — Folger Shakespeare Library, Folgerpedia. Lists sources, possible sources and analogues separately; includes Cymbeline and the Decameron.

## Cross-reading exercises

1. Compare the opening plague introduction with the departure. What social duties are restored when the company goes home, and what forms of dependence did the garden conceal?
2. Reconstruct the women’s speaking positions from the authorial Proem, Pampinea’s proposal, Madonna Filippa’s trial, and Emilia’s argument for wife-beating. Identify who is entitled to consent in each.
3. Read Cisti (VI.2), Andreuccio (II.5), and Salabaetto (VIII.10) as stories about what it takes to make a transaction trustworthy.
4. Contrast Ciappelletto’s false confession (I.1), Abraham’s Roman visit (I.2), the three rings (I.3), and Fra Cipolla’s relic improvisation (VI.10). How does each tale make testimony credible or suspect?
5. For Alibech (III.10), Nastagio (V.8), the three-bed farce (IX.6), and the bridal substitution (X.8), record whose knowledge is missing and which characters bear the consequences.
6. Compare acts of release by Nathan (X.3), the suitor and magician (X.5), Torello and Saladin (X.9), and Gualtieri (X.10). Who actually gives up something that belongs to them?
7. Compare Griselda in Boccaccio X.10, Petrarch’s Latin rewriting as described in his letter, and Chaucer’s *Clerk’s Tale*. What happens to the narrator’s attitude as the tale travels?
8. Compare Giletta (III.9) with Shakespeare’s *All’s Well That Ends Well*. What can a dramatic adaptation make more difficult than a brief tale’s asserted happy ending?
