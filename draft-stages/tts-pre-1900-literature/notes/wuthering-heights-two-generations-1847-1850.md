## Names and family map

- **The elder Catherine**: Catherine **Earnshaw**, later Catherine **Linton** when married to **Edgar Linton**; Catherine and Heathcliff had grown up together at the Heights. Her daughter **young Cathy** is also Catherine Linton by birth, and becomes **Catherine Heathcliff** after a coerced marriage to **Linton Heathcliff**.
- **Heathcliff**: a foundling brought from Liverpool by Mr. Earnshaw; the text does not establish his biological ancestry or the source of his wealth after his return. Do not present conjectures about his ancestry as settled fact.
- **Hindley Earnshaw**: Catherine's brother, married to Frances, father of **Hareton Earnshaw**. Hindley degrades Heathcliff; Heathcliff later degrades Hareton.
- **Edgar Linton and Isabella Linton**: siblings at the Grange. Edgar marries the elder Catherine; Isabella marries Heathcliff and is mother of **Linton Heathcliff**. Linton Heathcliff and young Cathy are **cousins** and are not to be confused with Edgar and the elder Catherine.
- **Nelly Dean**: housekeeper, witness, caretaker, decision-maker and long-range narrator. **Lockwood**: outer narrator, tenant at the Grange in 1801. His social inferences are frequently mistaken; Nelly's recollection is detailed but not detached from the conflict.
- **Joseph**, **Zillah** and the other servants also help mediate the household to readers. Joseph's Yorkshire dialect is meaningful; editors differ on rendering it.

## Causality and interpretation

- The earlier generation's love story does not close at Catherine's death. The story continues through a designed campaign of property acquisition and intimidation directed at the next generation.
- Marriage and property are connected through specific nineteenth-century claims, debt and inheritance; do not substitute a universal statement of modern family law. Edgar attempts to protect his daughter's interests, but Heathcliff maneuvers through his son and the imposed marriage.
- Isabella's experience is an essential check against romanticizing Heathcliff's violence. Understanding his mistreatment as a child is different from justifying mistreatment of Isabella, Linton, Hareton, and young Cathy.
- Cathy and Hareton's shared literacy provides a concrete alternative to Hindley's and Heathcliff's repeated use of deprivation as a form of control. Their relationship is not a replay of the elder Catherine's bond with Heathcliff.
- Ghosts are left interpretively open. Lockwood's first nightmare and his final description of the graves do not confer the authority of an all-knowing narrator.

## Publication and source witnesses

- **Primary continuous reading text:** [Project Gutenberg 768, Emily Brontë, *Wuthering Heights*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/768). Useful for plot and passages, but not independently certified as a diplomatic transcription of the 1847 edition.
- **Initial publication:** [Bodleian Libraries, Great Writers Inspire, *Wuthering Heights*](https://writersinspire.podcasts.ox.ac.uk/content/wuthering-heights-0) identifies publication in **December 1847**, under the pseudonym **Ellis Bell**, with the 1850 Charlotte-edited posthumous edition. The original 1847 three-volume set joined *Wuthering Heights* (two volumes) with Anne Brontë's *Agnes Grey* (one volume).
- **Form and critical interpretation:** [The British Library, *Wuthering Heights* overview](https://www.britishlibrary.cn/en/works/emily-bronte-s-wuthering-heights/) describes the nested perspectives, literary setting, and the novel's interlocking economic and emotional forces. It places the first publication in 1847.
- **Edition history:** [W. W. Norton, *Wuthering Heights*, fifth Critical Edition](https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9780393284997-wuthering-heights/features) explains that the 1847 first printing contains defects and did not incorporate every correction Emily made; a posthumous 1850 text contains Charlotte's interventions. A sentence's precise punctuation or Joseph's dialect may differ materially between editions. The [Brontë Parsonage Museum](https://www.bronte.org.uk/the-lives-of-the-brontes/the-bronte-novels) records contemporary reactions; one page dates the book 1848, conflicting with the 1847 first-publication record in Bodleian and British Library sources, so the lectures follow the dated first-edition evidence.

**Editorial scope:** Plot retelling is grounded in a public-domain reading text and supported by institutional scholarship; it is not a collation of the 1847 and 1850 texts, all extant proof corrections, or all nineteenth-century legal records. Economic interpretations are identified as readings of the novel, not as exhaustive statements of the law.
