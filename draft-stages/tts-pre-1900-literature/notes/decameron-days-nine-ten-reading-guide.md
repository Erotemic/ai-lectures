# Boccaccio’s *Decameron*: Days Nine and Ten — source and reading guide

> Organization note (16 November 2026): the current playback inventory and work order are in [the pack README](../README.md). Numbered parts and former counts in this source/edition dossier describe the original writing components; their narration and evidence now live inside the combined manuscripts. They are not additional playable lectures.

**Coverage:** 400000–423000, the fifth narrative installment of this work in
our pre-1900 literature track. Days One–Eight occupy 312000–399000. This
installment completes **all 100 individually narrated tales**. Each of ten
day introductions has a narrated manuscript, while the return to Florence
and authorial epilogue have their own lectures. This completes narrative
coverage, **not** the planned critical and historical study of the work.

The direct links below point to the relevant primary-text passages in the
English *Decameron Web* mirror (J. M. Rigg, 1903), not to a secondary
synopsis. Brown's Italian text uses a critical edited text. The English
translation is a separate witness, not a claim that modern scholarship
endorses every interpretation in these lectures.

**Day Nine:** Emilia is queen, with no compulsory storytelling theme.
Filomena opens, followed by Elissa, Filostrato, Neifile, Fiammetta, Panfilo,
Pampinea, Lauretta, Emilia and Dioneo. Calandrino returns in **IX.3** and
**IX.5**; Simone returns as a willing accomplice in **IX.3** after the
fraud against him in VIII.9. Day Nine's conclusion crowns Panfilo and
assigns magnificence and generosity for Day Ten.

**Day Ten:** Panfilo is king. Neifile opens, followed by Elissa, Filostrato,
Lauretta, Emilia, Fiammetta, Pampinea, Filomena, Panfilo and Dioneo. The
company departs on the morning following the tenth storytelling day and
returns to Santa Maria Novella in Florence. The **authorial epilogue**
is after the frame concludes; do not treat it as another tale or as a speech
by one of the ten fictional storytellers.

## Plot, interpretation and evidence cautions

- **IX.1, Francesca:** Alessandro actually enters the tomb and Rinuccio
  lifts him as a supposed corpse. The night patrol interrupts both, allowing
  Francesca to refuse their further pleas without claiming the men failed
  for lack of courage. Her lack of romantic interest is crucial.
- **IX.2, Abbess:** the garment is the priest's breeches, placed on her head
  in the dark. The nun exposes the superior's hypocrisy; it is not a record
  of a specific historical convent.
- **IX.3, Calandrino:** Nello, Bruno and Buffalmacco recruit Doctor Simone
  to pronounce a fabricated pregnancy. Calandrino finances his nonexistent
  cure with capons and money. The doctor has his own earlier comic history.
- **IX.4, the Ceccos:** distinguish Cecco degli Angiulieri from Cecco
  Fortarrigo. The latter loses money through gambling and fabricates a
  robbery to gain the other's clothes and horse.
- **IX.5, Niccolosa:** Filippo and the woman cooperate in Bruno's stage-managed
  deception. Calandrino's paper has no actual magical power. Tessa discovers
  the arranged meeting; the tale is not evidence for a historical spell.
- **IX.6, three beds:** track both the baby's cradle and the movement between
  the beds. The mother's encounter with Adriano occurs because she takes him
  for her husband; do not erase the mistaken identity when discussing consent.
- **IX.7, wolf:** the predictive dream is a literary fact of this story, not
  a documented claim about the scientific reliability of dreams.
- **IX.8, Ciacco and Biondello:** trickery over a meal leads to a revenge
  that recruits another man's violence. The injury is unequal to the insult.
- **IX.9, Solomon:** Emilia's explicit defense of wife-beating is part of the
  primary text; do not attribute it directly to Boccaccio or silently soften
  the violence while explaining the story's literary argument.
- **IX.10, Dom Gianni:** the supposed mare conversion disguises a sexual act.
  It is a trick devised by the priest, not an actual animal transformation.
- **X.1, Alfonso:** Ruggieri chooses between two chests, one treasure and one
  earth, and is unlucky. The king gives him the treasure anyway.
- **X.2, Ghino:** the Abbot of Cluny is seized and placed on a restricted
  diet, then released and becomes Ghino's advocate. The named persons are not
  documentary evidence for the described medical treatment.
- **X.3, Nathan:** he meets Mitridanes without being recognized, offers advice
  for killing himself, then refuses vengeance when his identity is clear.
- **X.4, Gentile:** the supposedly dead woman survives and gives birth in
  Gentile's care; he publicly gives her back to her husband. The banquet's
  analogy about possession of a servant deserves close reading.
- **X.5, Dianora:** the January garden exists by necromancy in the fiction;
  Ansaldo later releases Dianora, and the magician waives his promised fee.
- **X.6, King Charles:** the king refrains from pursuing Neri's very young
  daughters and arranges marriages. Its chivalric praise is not a guarantee
  that the daughters had equal freedom in choosing spouses.
- **X.7, Lisa:** her message reaches King Pedro through musician Minuccio;
  he publicly honors her and arranges a marriage to Perdicone.
- **X.8, Titus/Gisippus:** Titus substitutes for Gisippus in the dark bridal
  bed; Sophronia believes the man she sleeps with is Gisippus. The friends'
  later extreme loyalty does not retroactively supply informed consent.
- **X.9, Torello/Saladin:** Saladin is first received in disguise as a guest;
  their recognition comes during the Crusade, and the enchanted night journey
  returns Torello before his wife can marry a new husband.
- **X.10, Griselda:** Gualtieri makes Griselda *believe* their children are
  killed; both are secretly living. The teenage supposed second bride is her
  own daughter. Dioneo explicitly calls the marquis's conduct folly, not a
  model for a husband. Petrarch and Chaucer adapt this story in distinct ways.
- **Departure and epilogue:** Ten days of tales are set within a longer
  approximately fortnight-long stay. The end of the frame is the return to
  Florence, while the author's epilogue replies to criticism of language,
  storytelling, indecency and length, arguing for readers' power of choice.

Some stories use names or events associated with real history, but the
fictional telling itself is not enough to establish those episodes as actual
biography, a scientific result, or an account of medieval institutions that
applies without qualification. Cross-check historical claims against specialist
scholarship before teaching them as history.

## Questions for reading across the ending

1. What changes when Emilia removes the day theme? Does apparently unrestricted choice produce a more plural moral landscape?
2. Why does Day Nine repeat Calandrino twice, using Doctor Simone first as an earlier victim and now as a paid collaborator?
3. Compare the supposed consent in IX.6 with the wedding-night substitution in X.8. Who knows the facts necessary to decide?
4. Compare Emilia's moralizing IX.9 to Dioneo's final X.10. Does the collection itself permit or resist a settled doctrine of obedience?
5. Which action of Day Ten costs its giver the most, and which supposed generosity is actually exercised at another person's expense?
6. How does Saladin's disguise test whether Torello's hospitality depends on status?
7. Why does Gualtieri insist on testing Griselda after her worth has become apparent? What does the testing accomplish for him?
8. What does the choice to return to Florence, without a reported end to the plague, tell us about the collection's project?
9. How does the author's defense of long, varied and sometimes bawdy tales change the way we interpret the book's ideal audience?
