The story sequence **252000–263000** retells all twelve books of Milton's
revised **1674** text in book order. Critical studies **264000–274000** follow
rather than interrupt the story. The spoken manuscripts are **original
retellings and criticism**, not Milton's verse and not biblical texts.
Reading the story lectures does not require prior reading of the poem.

## Important textual and interpretive distinctions

1. **Publication history:** The **1667 first edition** has **ten books**;
   the **1674 second edition** has **twelve**. The later organization was made
   mainly by dividing two of the original long books and adding linking
   text; it is not two new books of narrative added in 1674. Title-page
   dates **1667, 1668 and 1669** in surviving issues do not always indicate
   independently typeset editions. The reading text should always be
   identified when citing book and line numbers.
2. **Genesis is not Milton's full plot:** The infernal council, Sin and Death
   at the gates, three-day war with cannon, Raphael's conversation, many
   psychological motives, and Michael's expanded vision are **poetic
   constructions or drawn from interpretive traditions**, not direct
   transcription of Genesis 1–3. The creation, garden, serpent, Fall,
   judgment and expulsion are central scriptural starting points.
3. **Satan is rhetorically attractive, not simply endorsed.** Different
   critics have read his apparent heroism differently. Neither a slogan
   about an accidental hero nor a declaration that no reader may admire
   his courage resolves Milton's contradictions.
4. **Eve and Adam make distinct decisions.** Eve is deceived in the
   serpent scene; Adam eats knowing the command has been broken. Their
   separation debate also addresses marriage, labor and consent.
5. **Freedom, foreknowledge and grace are presented through the poem's
   theology**, not conclusively settled as philosophical facts. Interpretive
   alternatives remain live.
6. **Milton's political biography matters**, but no single historical
   figure is a straightforward key to Satan. The poet defended republican
   government and served the Commonwealth and Protectorate. Readers should
   compare the political diction in the poem with Milton's actual polemics.
7. **The final outcome is exile with hope.** Adam and Eve's repentance is
   meaningful, but Eden is not restored in the poem's last scene; Michael's
   history includes future suffering and the promised redemption.

- The **1674 twelve-book text**: [Wikisource, *Paradise Lost* (1674)](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost_(1674)).

  transcription connected to a scan, **not** a diplomatic collation of all
  printing states.

## Four reading exercises

- Compare the infernal council's language of freedom (Book II) with
  Satan's actual manipulation of that council. What would a genuinely
  free decision procedure have required of him?
- Follow the word **knowledge** from Raphael's lessons (Books V–VIII)
  through Satan's false demonstration (Book IX). Where does the speaker
  promise justified inquiry, and where an unwarranted leap from evidence?
- Read the Edenic encounter in Book IV, the separation debate in Book IX,
  and the reconciliation in Book X. Where is Eve heard directly, and where
  is her life conveyed through Adam or an angel?
- Put the epic's vast opening invocation beside the small movement of the
  couple leaving Paradise. What does the change of scale do to the ideas
  of freedom, punishment, and hope?

## Scope of review

The manuscripts were developed from the named 1674 reading text, a
university book-by-book summary, and selected historical and scholarly
references. Particular plot and high-stakes bibliographic distinctions
were cross-checked. This is **not** a complete line-by-line textual
collation, a confessional exposition of Genesis, an attempt to settle
contested Milton scholarship, or a recording. No binary images are
included. English is the language of Milton's original poem, and there
are no unpublished translated passages in the spoken content.
