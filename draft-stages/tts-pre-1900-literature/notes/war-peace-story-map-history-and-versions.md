This is a **non-spoken source companion** to lectures 114000–129000. The spoken lectures are original explanatory retellings and criticism; they do not reproduce a copyrighted edition and should not be represented as an unabridged performance of the novel. There is no target duration.

## Publication and textual witnesses

- Tolstoy's material appeared in *Russkii vestnik* in the 1860s under the earlier title **1805**; the first complete book edition emerged in **1868–1869**. Later editions altered text and sometimes the position or language of historical arguments. Do not say there is one unique immutable text.
- The accessible Project Gutenberg text is the **Louise and Aylmer Maude** English translation. It is a useful complete reading text but not identical to Tolstoy's Russian and not a definitive record of every early version. A modern revised Maude edition, the Pevear–Volokhonsky translation, and other English versions differ in transliteration, headings, register, and preservation of French.
- Tolstoy incorporates **French** into Russian aristocratic conversations. Its handling can change meaning: translations that flatten languages into English lose a visible class and political marker. See `cl-tolstoy-rudn-translingual` and the primary reading text.
- The philosophical account of generalship and history is **Tolstoy's argument**, not an independently established empirical result. His portrayal of Kutuzov and Napoleon should be compared with historical scholarship if presented as military history. See the Cornell source abstract.
- The **first epilogue** ends with the fictional households and political disputes around 1820. The **second epilogue** moves into a sustained account of causation, power, freedom, and necessity. One should not silently omit the latter or call its theses proven.

## Critical boundaries

1. Major plot strands are selected for oral intelligibility. Not every named character, secondary love affair, campaign order, or action of the 15-book sequence is recounted.
2. Pierre, Natasha, Andrei, Marya and Nikolai are fictional characters. Napoleon, Alexander I and Kutuzov are historical, but their thoughts and conversations in the novel remain literary portrayals.
3. The Rostov and Bolkonsky estates depend on an unequal system of **serfdom**. A sympathetic portrait of an owner does not demonstrate that the institution was benign or that all peasants held one view.
4. Pierre's moral education, Andrei's forgiveness, and Natasha's changing roles are open to interpretation. The account of gender in the first epilogue should not be silently conflated with a universal description of Russian women.
5. The ending does not narrate the Decembrist uprising of 1825; political hints and the historical date of that later revolt belong to commentary.
6. Public-domain text and selected institutional/scholarly pages were used as orientation sources, **not** exhaustive Russian manuscript collation or comprehensive documentary verification of the Napoleonic campaign.
