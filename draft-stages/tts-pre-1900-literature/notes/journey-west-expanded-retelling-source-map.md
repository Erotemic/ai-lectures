# *Journey to the West*: Expanded Retelling — source, scope, and continuation map

This is a separate literary retelling of the conventional 100-chapter Ming novel, and not a revision of the World History series 048000–054000. The existing six narrative lectures give a concise complete-plot survey; the new retelling is intended to let characters, incidents, reversals, and the changing relationships develop at listening pace. The length of each episode follows its material.

## Text and evidence decisions

- Original literary work: *Xiyou ji* (*Journey to the West*), traditionally attributed to Wu Cheng'en, but the attribution is historically debated. The earliest complete surviving printed edition is associated with the 1592 Shidetang printing; the accessible online chapter text is a later transmitted text, **not** a claim to reproduce that printing leaf for leaf.
- The fictional Stone Monkey's powers, dragon court, underworld registers and divine bureaucracy are narrated as events within the novel, **not** independent history.
- The detailed retelling draws narrative order from the original-language chapter text. Anthony C. Yu's revised annotated complete English translation is an orientation and a check on naming and research; the manuscripts do **not** reproduce it.
- A modern chapter-by-chapter web guide is included only as an aid for locating scenes, not as independent evidence.
- Lecture 054000 in World History separately covers Xuanzang's historical pilgrimage, transformations of the tale, edition variations, and the uncertain authorship.

## Progress and next chapter group

The expanded literary telling now includes chapters **1–7**, from the stone birth through the imprisonment under the Mountain of Five Elements. Lectures 444000–451000 use natural scene transitions within chapters 4–7 rather than claiming new divisions in the source novel.

The next natural story arc is chapters **8–12**, which establish the westward scripture mission and the Tang court and develop the historical-and-fictional groundwork for the pilgrim monk. Chapter numbering and arrangement may differ among editions, so verify those chapter boundaries against the chosen primary witness. The following long pilgrimage has repeated characters, journeys, reversals, and delayed consequences: let those developments govern episode boundaries rather than setting a fixed length or one-chapter-per-lecture rule.

Once the narrative reaches the end of the 100-chapter novel, separate criticism can address the textual tradition, Buddhist and Daoist language, Ming satire of institutions, religious ritual, the novel's representation of outsiders, the relationship between Sun Wukong and the monk, and its later adaptations.
