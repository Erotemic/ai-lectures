It should not be used to claim exact first-edition punctuation or unmediated variant history.

## Interpretive cautions

- Emma's consumption of romance literature **does not, alone, explain** her marriage, desires, debt, or death. The novels give shape to hopes also conditioned by gender, social rank, commerce, and constrained options.
- Charles is an **officier de santé**, a historically regulated qualification to practice healing distinct from being a *docteur en médecine*. Do not identify him unqualifiedly as a fully trained physician or attribute modern licensing rules to provincial nineteenth-century France. See [the regional French medical order's history of officers of health](https://conseil76.ordre.medecin.fr/sites/default/files/domain-361/1/24.pdf).
- Hippolyte has a clubfoot, undergoes a dangerous attempted correction by Charles, develops severe infection, and then loses a leg. The suffering of the worker is not merely a symbol of Charles's professional failure. Homais urges and promotes the intervention while expecting social credit.
- Rodolphe's declarations and refusal are his actions; Emma's ideals do not absolve his manipulation. Léon is neither a villain identical to Rodolphe nor the liberator Emma imagines.
- Berthe's fate matters: the ending moves beyond Emma's death to a surviving daughter's economic vulnerability. Homais receives the **Legion of Honor**, but his prize is not evidence that the narrator validates his judgment.
- The Rouen carriage scene works by controlling what readers can see; a claim about its *specific* wording must identify a French text or a named translation. Avoid treating paraphrases in these lectures as French quotations.
- Free indirect discourse is **not** an invention that begins exclusively with Flaubert. The novel's significant achievement is the sustained, subtle use of mixed narrator/character voice to create distance and irony. See [Oxford's account of the technique](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61883/chapter-abstract/547772880) and [Yale's close-reading discussion](https://campuspress.yale.edu/modernismlab/madame-bovary/).

## Publication, trial, and textual witnesses

The [Bibliothèque nationale de France work record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb11938746n) confirms serial publication in *Revue de Paris*, **1 October–15 December 1856**, and an 1857 book edition issued by Michel Lévy. [Fondation Napoléon's trial history](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/close-up/a-close-up-on-the-flaubert-madame-bovary-trial) identifies the February **7, 1857 acquittal** of Flaubert, the journal's owner, and printer. This was a case about moral/religious offenses associated with publication. The court decision is a historical source for the prosecution, not a shortcut to Flaubert's intent.

For differences between serialization, editing, and the book, see the [published historical study of editorial suppression and authorial reconstruction](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1743872107076383) and the [Oxford chapter abstract on Flaubert's writing under censorship](https://academic.oup.com/book/9510/chapter-abstract/156475899). In translating the French, different editions may alter voice, rhythm, and moral cues. Compare exact claims of style with their chosen French witness; do not declare a universal best translation without evidence. [Michael Lucey's scholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/75b7r776) also cautions that the reader's pleasure in detecting subtle irony can reproduce a hierarchy of literary sophistication.

## Listening and reading prompts

1. Identify where Emma's emotional vocabulary enters third-person narration without an explicit quotation frame.
2. Track the words of Rodolphe's seduction against the simultaneous official speeches at the agricultural show.
3. For the carriage episode, distinguish the narrated city route from the action inferred inside the vehicle.
4. List Lheureux's promises beside their later financial consequences; whose future is put up as security?
5. Follow Hippolyte and Berthe through the narrative. Whose suffering is displaced by the principals' ambitions?
6. Why does the final public honor of Homais change the significance of the chapter after Emma's funeral?

**Limits:** Selected accessible primary text and reliable academic/institutional notes were consulted, not all publication witnesses or translations collated sentence by sentence. Historical facts, primary events, and interpretive argument are distinguished in manuscript and source records; the files are not a performance of the copyrighted modern translations.
