**Coverage:** 378000–399000, the fourth installment of this work in the
pre-1900 literature track. Days One–Six occupy 312000–377000. With this
installment **80 of the 100 tales** have individual narrated manuscripts,
as do the opening frames of the first eight storytelling days. Days Nine and
Ten and work-level historical and literary studies remain to be written.

The table links *the relevant primary-text passage*, not a secondary synopsis.

**Day Seven:** Dioneo is king. The company visits the Valley of the Ladies
for the whole day, with breakfast under the trees and afternoon storytelling
by the lake. Emilia opens the ten tales. The theme, inspired by Licisca's
kitchen argument during Day Six, is tricks wives play on husbands.

**Day Eight:** Lauretta is queen. At the close of the seventh day she rejects
simple retaliation (husbands deceiving wives) and orders tricks among women
and men in any direction. The company returns to its normal fountain setting,
after a visit to a nearby church. Neifile opens. Lauretta's concluding
transfer of the crown to Emilia introduces the ninth day's freer storytelling:
Emilia chooses no compulsory subject.

## Recurring characters and running questions

The first new Calandrino tale is **VIII.3**, after Maso del Saggio's false
stone-lore sets the deception in motion. **Bruno and Buffalmacco** stage an
invisibility illusion at the Mugnone. **VIII.6** returns to the same three
painters, this time over a stolen pig and a rigged swallowing test. **VIII.9**
then reuses Bruno and Buffalmacco but replaces Calandrino with Master Simone,
a physician whose desire to enter a secret society makes him another eager
subject of their performances. Day Nine will return to Calandrino.

These stories are *literary* episodes. The presence of names associated with
Florentine painters is not independent documentary evidence that the pranks
occurred. The point of treating the episodes as a recurring fictional series
is to trace developed character dynamics, not to turn comedy into biography.

## Reading cautions and close-reading suggestions

- **VII.1, Tessa / Gianni / Federigo:** distinguish the ass's-head signal
  from the emergency prayer. The apparent exorcism is actually an instruction
  to the lover, who has arrived after Gianni's unexpected return.
- **VII.2, Peronella:** the husband has a genuine buyer at five florins;
  Peronella claims a sale at seven. The hidden lover pretends to inspect the
  barrel, then uses the husband's cleaning of it to continue his encounter.
- **VII.3, Fra Rinaldo:** godparenthood provides both the friar's access
  and the stated religious objection to his pursuit. The child's supposed
  illness is invented to explain his presence when the husband returns.
- **VII.4, Tofano / Ghita:** a stone dropped into the well creates the false
  drowning. Ghita switches places with the husband at the locked door, then
  uses public shame to change their balance of power.
- **VII.5, the jealous confessor:** the husband impersonates a priest,
  the wife recognizes him, and an apparently truthful confession creates the
  false premise he watches for at the front door.
- **VII.6, Isabella:** Lambertuccio's armed exit and Leonetto's supposed
  peril are a coordinated fiction. Keep Lambertuccio's unwelcome pursuit
  distinct from Isabella's chosen relationship with Leonetto.
- **VII.7, Lodovico / Anichino:** the disguised servant wins Egano's trust
  before winning Beatrice's love. The husband accepts a garden beating while
  wearing the wife's disguise because he treats it as proof of loyalty.
- **VII.8, Sismonda:** a maid, not Sismonda, receives a severe beating and
  haircut in the bed. The servant's injury is not erased by the husband's
  subsequent humiliation or by a joking narration of the episode.
- **VII.9, Lydia and the pear tree:** separate the earlier injuries to the
  hawk, beard and tooth from the garden trick. A false report from the tree
  teaches Nicostratus to dismiss a true sight as an illusion.
- **VII.10, Tingoccio / Meuccio:** the ghost's pronouncement is a statement
  *within the tale*. It is not an authenticated report from purgatory or a
  historical theological ruling on spiritual kinship.
- **VIII.1, Gulfardo:** a payment made to Guasparruolo's wife can also be
  narrated as a loan repayment to her husband. The public description of the
  money controls who gets to keep it.
- **VIII.2, Belcolore:** the priest's cloak is a pledge for payment; he
  maneuvers to recover it. The mortar and pestle provide a bawdy closing
  verbal reversal and also reveal the woman's frustration at being cheated.
- **VIII.3, Calandrino and heliotrope:** the stone of invisibility does not
  exist in the narrative world as an actual tested power. The painters make
  their friend infer invisibility from staged conduct, and he then beats
  Tessa, who had no part in the fraud.
- **VIII.4, Fiesole rector:** the widow's refusal precedes the invitation;
  the invitation is a trap. The maid bears an intimate part of the danger,
  and the bishop's presence turns apparent privacy into public exposure.
- **VIII.5, the judge:** the body exposed on the bench is not proof of a
  particular wrongful judgment. The tale is about the fragility of a public
  costume of authority.
- **VIII.6, Calandrino and the pig:** the thieves operate the supposed test
  for theft and deliberately produce their victim's failure to swallow.
  The test is rigged, not an actual medical or judicial detector.
- **VIII.7, Rinieri / Elena:** Elena's freezing-night humiliation is
  followed by a more dangerous summer ordeal planned by Rinieri. The
  storyteller's language of deserved revenge should not be silently
  treated as an ethical verdict binding on the listener.
- **VIII.8, Zeppa / Spinelloccio:** consider who is free to consent when
  two men claim that swapping sexual partners settles their betrayal.
- **VIII.9, Master Simone:** Bruno and Buffalmacco persuade the physician
  to buy access to an imaginary society; the end is a cesspit, not a genuine
  mystical initiation. The characters' names are not historical testimony.
- **VIII.10, Salabaetto / Jancofiore:** follow both financial transactions:
  first five hundred florins lost, then returned to gain trust, and finally a
  further advance made against worthless goods. Pietro Canigiano supplies
  the plan that makes the counterfraud possible.

## Questions for the listener

1. Why are the Valley of the Ladies' servants necessary to the pleasure of
   the company, though they do not receive turns as formal storytellers?
2. Compare the "prayer" of VII.1, the confession of VII.5 and the supposed
   supernatural message of VII.10. In each case who decides what counts as
   religious or truthful evidence?
3. Compare the servant injured in VII.8 with the maid recruited in VIII.4.
   Does a trick remain clever if it transfers danger onto a less powerful
   third party?
4. Compare VII.9's tree and VIII.6's truth test. How is the wrong theory
   installed before an apparently decisive observation?
5. Why does Calandrino want to believe in invisibility? Why does Simone
   want to believe in the secret society? Which desire is most useful to
   the people designing the trick?
6. In the opening and closing tales of Day Eight, how does money move?
   Would the deception work if all parties had the same account of the
   transaction?
7. Compare VIII.7's retribution to VIII.8's agreement to share partners.
   Do either of these endings give all participants equal freedom to decide?
8. What is the value of letting fictional narrators disagree in tone,
   especially where their jokes involve real suffering within their tales?
