# Dante's *Commedia*: sources, textual boundaries, and editorial decisions

> Organization note (16 November 2026): the current playback inventory and work order are in [the pack README](../README.md). Numbered parts and former counts in this source/edition dossier describe the original writing components; their narration and evidence now live inside the combined manuscripts. They are not additional playable lectures.

This dossier accompanies lectures **016000–037000** of `pre-1900-literature`.
It is non-spoken. The lectures are original explanatory prose, not recitations or
substitutes for one particular edition of the poem.

## Base text and scholarship

- **Primary poem and commentary:** the [Princeton Dante Project](https://dante.princeton.edu/pdp/commedia.html)
  provides a cantica/canto interface to the Italian text and commentary. Its
  electronic text follows Giorgio Petrocchi's critical edition, with punctuation
  changes documented in its project notes. The site does **not** authorize
  redistribution of Petrocchi's Italian text; it is used only for reference.
- **University teaching edition:** the [World of Dante](https://worldofdante.iath.virginia.edu/)
  (University of Virginia) provides the three cantiche, navigational aids and
  historical notes. The source is useful for locating a figure, region, and canto.
- **Commentary disagreement:** [Dante Lab](https://dantelab.dartmouth.edu/)
  provides many centuries of commentary and distinct interpretive positions.
  Modern readings do not settle all contested allegorical identifications.
- **Public-domain English comparator:** [Longfellow's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1004)
  (1867), identified in [Dante Lab's FAQ](https://dantelab.dartmouth.edu/faq).
  It is useful for speech comparison but is not the Italian original and is not
  reproduced in this overlay.
- **Context:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dante/)
  discusses Dante's life, philosophy, political engagement and exile; the
  [Cambridge Companion's chronology](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/44307/frontmatter/9780521844307_frontmatter.pdf)
  dates the fictional journey to 1300 and Dante's exile to 1302.
- **Textual transmission:** [Cornell](https://exhibits-prod.library.cornell.edu/visionsofdante/feature/dante-from-manuscript-to-printed-book)
  and [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/dante-in-context/textual-transmission/2F3718D65BE012F4BAEC855314866A91)
  document the lack of a surviving autograph and the manuscript tradition;
  [Cornell's Foligno exhibit](https://exhibits-prod.library.cornell.edu/visionsofdante/feature/incunables-as-books-of-transition)
  identifies the 1472 first printing.
- **Title:** the Princeton Dante Project [Toynbee entry on *Comedìa*](https://dante.princeton.edu/cgi-bin/dante/DispToynbeeByTitOrId.pl?INP_ID=212131)
  distinguishes Dante's usage from the familiar later title *Divina Commedia*;
  the adjective entered a printed title in 1555.
- **Public commentary:** Catherine Keen's [institutional UCL study](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10076200/1/Ethics-Politics-and-Justice-in-Dante.pdf)
  documents Boccaccio's publicly supported Florentine lectures beginning in 1373.

## Deliberate distinctions

1. **Fictional narrator versus author:** the pilgrim experiences the journey in
   1300, whereas the author writes with knowledge of the political events of
   1302 and later. Predictions are poetic retrospection, not documentary
   evidence of prophecy.
2. **Historical figures versus invented dialogue:** a real person named in a canto
   may be assigned a speech Dante invented. The speaker's claims require their
   own scrutiny; no speech in the poem is a historical deposition.
3. **Medieval doctrines versus current claims:** exclusion from salvation, the
   categorization of sexuality, religious polemics, and medieval cosmology are
   presented as features of Dante's fictional Christian universe, not as
   universal ethical or scientific conclusions.
4. **Difficult literary debates:** exact identifications of the beasts,
   symbolism of the chariot, rationale for individual judgments, and Ugolino's
   hunger are not treated as settled beyond what the text shows.
5. **Translation:** no modern copyrighted translation was copied into the
   narrated text. Quotations are avoided where a prose paraphrase can be more
   transparent; the lecture on terza rima directs readers to compare editions.
6. **Purgatory's goal:** the souls on the mountain know the end of their journey
   will be good, unlike the fixed conditions represented in Hell; an account
   of the poem's theology is not a prescription for medical or psychological
   treatment.
7. **Editorial status:** episode ranges, historical context, selected canto
   claims and printed-book history were cross-checked against the sources
   listed above. **No full line-by-line collation** of all one hundred cantos,
   Petrocchi variants, or every scholarly commentary was undertaken. Claims
   should not be marked as exhaustively edition-verified.

## Future editorial review opportunities

- For any audio release intended for scholarly quotation or a companion showing
  exact verse, collate the intended Italian passage with a named critical
  edition and record the line numbers, translation credit and rights.
- If listeners request deeper narrative density, extend selected canto groups
  without altering the stable lecture IDs already used for recording playback.
- Critical comparison with Boccaccio, Chaucer, Milton, and later literature
  should be a separate substantial section; avoid asserting influence solely
  on the basis of similar motifs.
