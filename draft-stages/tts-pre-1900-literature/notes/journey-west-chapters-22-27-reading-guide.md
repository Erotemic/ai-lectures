# Journey to the West — chapters 22–27: reading and evidence guide

**Prepared 16 November 2026.** This is the source companion for four playable manuscripts `502000`–`505000`, covering **six consecutive selected-witness chapters**, not six separate compulsory lecture durations. The established retelling runs continuously from chapter 1 through chapter 27. The preceding manuscript `493000` ends with the rescue at Yellow Wind Ridge; the next continuation starts in chapter 28 after Wukong's dismissal.

Narrative arcs rather than numerical chapter boundaries determine manuscript length. Chapters 24–26 deliberately remain together so that the initial theft is neither resolved offstage nor split from the cost of Wukong's attempt to repair it. ## Scene sequence and source checks

### Chapter 22 — Flowing-Sand River

1. An impossibly wide river interrupts the westward road. Its inscription describes a crossing eight hundred *li* wide and water that will not bear even a goose feather or a reed flower. The stone is a literary description, not a physical claim about buoyancy.
2. A monstrous river-dweller fights Bajie. The travelers do not yet know that he is the former heavenly Curtain-Raising General, punished for breaking a crystal/glass vessel at a celestial banquet, cast down into the Flowing-Sand River, and forced to endure repeated punishment.
3. Bajie is able to fight underwater; Wukong cannot simply strike down a submerged enemy without risking the crossing's purpose. The monkey's impatience frustrates attempts to draw the creature out and elicit a surrender.
4. Guanyin's disciple Hui'an, also called Muzha, explains that the demon has been appointed in advance to the pilgrimage. Crucially, the creature was not told clearly who the passing pilgrims would be. An encounter that should have become recognition has become combat.
5. The former general accepts Xuanzang as master and becomes Sha Wujing, also called Sha Seng. A gourd, together with the nine skulls worn by the river-dweller, forms the miraculous ferry by which Xuanzang and the others cross. The skulls' earlier significance is not confused with the human body of the living historical Xuanzang.

### Chapter 23 — The Four Sages' test

1. The newly enlarged party has three disciples and a master; the dragon horse is also a transformed former celestial offender. Bajie needs repeated reminders that his new duty does not include abandoning the journey whenever domestic comfort appears.
2. A rich widow, associated with the surnames Jia and Mo, offers the travelers accommodation and eventual marriage with her three daughters Zhenzhen, Aiai, and Lianlian. The names and wealth are part of a crafted temptation.
3. Xuanzang refuses marriage. Wukong refuses, Sha stands firm. Bajie tries to obtain privately what the others reject openly. Wukong, transformed into an insect, witnesses his secret approach.
4. Bajie fails a blindfolded bride-selection game and agrees to put on an embroidered garment. The garment becomes a magical restraint.
5. At dawn the mansion vanishes; the visitors are identified as the Old Mother of Mount Li, Guanyin, Manjushri, and Samantabhadra. Chapter 23 ends with the companions hearing the bound Bajie's cries. His untying and repentance open chapter 24 and belong to the next manuscript's first scene.

### Chapter 24 — The ginseng fruit

1. Zhenyuanzi, the Daoist immortal who tends the ginseng fruit tree, is away at a religious assembly. He orders his boys Qingfeng and Mingyue to welcome the approaching Buddhist pilgrim because of an act of courtesy in an earlier life. The account concerns the novel's religious cosmology, not historical interfaith diplomacy.
2. The tree flowers, fruits, and ripens over a very long cycle. Its child-shaped fruit inspires Xuanzang to believe that he is being offered babies. He recoils, thinking it cannibalism. The two fruits are eaten by the attendants after the monk refuses them.
3. Wukong takes a fruit-beating tool. The first fruit falls into the earth and disappears because of its magical relation to the five elements. He gathers three more, and Wukong, Bajie, and Sha each eat one. Bajie swallows his almost before tasting it and demands another.
4. The boys count their missing fruit and confront the guests. Their abuse provokes Wukong to uproot or fell the tree. The damage is not a minor theft; it endangers a sacred life that cannot easily be replaced.

### Chapter 25 — Pursuit and negotiated repair

1. Qingfeng and Mingyue shut the gates. Wukong escapes with the party, but Zhenyuanzi returns and pursues them.
2. Zhenyuanzi captures all four travelers with his vast sleeve. More than one escape attempt fails. Wukong takes the blows meant for his master; a willow-tree trick and other substitutions preserve the others under punishment. The immortal's power cannot simply be dismissed by Wukong's reputation.
3. The threat of hot oil and the substitute stone lion make the physical contest both comic and serious. Xuanzang remains at risk.
4. Wukong commits to finding a cure for the destroyed tree. This changes the problem from who wins a fight into whether what has been broken can be restored. The agreement creates the opening for the third chapter in the episode.

### Chapter 26 — The remedy

1. Wukong searches the immortal islands, visiting immortals who may prolong human life but cannot restore the particular tree. The journey distinguishes individual longevity from repairing a damaged living root.
2. Guanyin knows the method. Her willow-vase dew can revive the tree, but the water must not be collected in containers associated with interfering elements; jade cups are used.
3. The tree rises and bears twenty-three fruit. The newly visible fruit resolves the count: one originally fell into the ground; the three disciples ate three; the boys had wrongly accused them of stealing four to eat.
4. A feast reconciles the parties, including Xuanzang's first acceptance of a ginseng fruit. Zhenyuanzi and Wukong form a bond. Do not retell this solely as an argument about gluttony: it includes hospitality across religious households, the limits of physical force, and restitution.

### Chapter 27 — The White Bone Demon

1. When Xuanzang grows hungry in the mountains, Wukong searches for peaches. In his absence the White Bone Demon, who desires the monk's flesh, approaches as a young woman bringing rice and vegetarian food.
2. Wukong sees through the deception and strikes. A false corpse remains while the demon escapes. Food that appears nutritious changes into loathsome creatures, but Bajie can explain that transformation as Wukong's fabrication. Xuanzang recites the tightening spell and threatens to dismiss the monkey.
3. The demon appears next as an old woman searching for her daughter, then as an old man searching for his wife and daughter, reciting a scripture. Each role makes Wukong's apparent violence more damning. Wukong's second strike again leaves a seeming human corpse; only in the third encounter does he arrange for the local mountain guardians to prevent escape.
4. The true white bones remain, marked with the name White Bone Lady. Bajie still argues that a monkey capable of transformations could have produced this evidence. Xuanzang agrees: the fact that the monkey can do magic makes the proof harder, not easier, for him to trust.
5. Xuanzang writes a formal dismissal. Wukong uses three hair-created doubles to make the last respectful bow from all four sides when Xuanzang refuses to receive it. He asks Sha to guard his master, warns Bajie, and turns east toward Flower-Fruit Mountain in sorrow. Chapter 28 begins after this separation.

## Edition and interpretation cautions

The primary citations above are **selected transmitted Chinese Wikisource chapter texts**, not a reconstructed or facsimile 1592 first edition. In the larger strand, the chapter-9 River Child text has known transmission and insertion issues. The chapters here are tracked against their selected witness, and all English dialogue, transitions, and descriptions are newly composed prose, not verbatim translation. Other editions and English translations may divide or name episodes differently.

The historical Buddhist pilgrim [Xuanzang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzang) and the novel's Tang Sanzang must not be treated as interchangeable biographical subjects. The chapter accounts of reincarnation, supernatural fruit, demon corpses, and celestial bureaucracy are **events inside a fictional literary narrative**. The novel invokes Buddhist, Daoist, and popular religious traditions; the characters' disagreements about morality are part of its drama and should not be flattened into an authoritative statement of any one doctrine.

The White Bone Demon's trick concerns **competing interpretations of the same staged evidence**, not simply a foolish monk ignoring an infallible friend. Wukong has a real history of impulsive violence, Xuanzang has a real duty to avoid harming innocent people, Bajie has grounds to resent the monkey, and the demon exploits all three features of their relationship. The narrative shows how those true observations can combine into a devastating false conclusion.
