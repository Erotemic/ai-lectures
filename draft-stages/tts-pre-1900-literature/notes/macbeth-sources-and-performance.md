## Source and historical boundaries

- **Primary text:** Folger Shakespeare Library's *Macbeth* is an edited reading text grounded in the **First Folio of 1623**. There is no surviving early quarto of *Macbeth*. Modern stage directions, punctuation, spelling, and interpretation must not be presented as a documented 1606 performance. See [Folger text](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/macbeth/read/) and [editorial introduction](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/macbeth/an-introduction-to-this-text/).
- **Dating:** Scholars often date composition to around 1606, especially given Jacobean themes and possible echoes of debates after the Gunpowder Plot, but exact composition and earliest performance dates are uncertain. Simon Forman described a performance in **1611**. Royal Shakespeare Company [dates and sources](https://www.rsc.org.uk/macbeth/about-the-play/dates-and-sources).
- **Textual dispute:** Some scholarship attributes material around Hecate or other passages to Thomas Middleton. That attribution is contested and the lectures do not identify any specific line as certainly Middleton's work. The Folio may reflect theatrical revision or adaptation, but not every hypothesis about revisions is a fact.
- **Historical Macbeth:** The eleventh-century ruler is not identical to Shakespeare's character. Holinshed's *Chronicles* provided major narrative material; Shakespeare reorganized it. In particular, Banquo's chronicle role differs from the version shown on stage. The Macbeth-Banquo-Stuart genealogy was an available tradition, not a modern verification of biological ancestry.
- **Macduff's birth:** The play's revealing phrase is **"from his mother's womb untimely ripped"**; the lectures explain that Macduff's birth falls outside Macbeth's ordinary interpretation without imposing one medical reconstruction in every production.
- **Lady Macbeth:** Her death is **reported**, not dramatized. Suicide may be inferred from later language, but the precise event is not explicitly staged as a verified cause of death. Her sleepwalking should not be turned into a retrospective clinical diagnosis.
- **Supernatural:** The ghosts and witches work as theatre; how far a production displays them and whether the audience should assign a single metaphysical mechanism remain interpretive choices. The prophecies do not instruct Macbeth to kill Duncan or Macduff's family.
- **State violence:** Duncan is murdered offstage by Macbeth. Macbeth kills the servants after the discovery, claiming outrage; their guilt was manufactured. Banquo is killed while Fleance escapes. The assault on Lady Macduff and her children is Macbeth's order, not an action of Malcolm's invasion.

## Reading prompts

1. Compare Banquo's and Macbeth's responses to the witches before any murder has happened. Where does an idea become an action?
2. Reconstruct the chain of testimony and staged evidence after Duncan's death. Whose death eliminates a witness?
3. Place the language of manhood in Lady Macbeth's first persuasive speech beside Macduff's response to family loss. What changes?
4. Compare the opening discussion of future time with Macbeth's meditation after hearing of Lady Macbeth's death. How does the same speaker experience time differently?
5. Use a staged production to ask what the audience sees of the dagger, banquet ghost, or moving wood. What changes if those signs are invisible?

**Audit scope:** Folger's text and editorial account and RSC's plot/dates references were consulted. Full witness collation and exhaustive independent historical review were not performed. Anchors in `metadata/` identify precise claims; commentary and critical synthesis are distinguished from demonstrable stage action. No fixed lecture length was imposed.
