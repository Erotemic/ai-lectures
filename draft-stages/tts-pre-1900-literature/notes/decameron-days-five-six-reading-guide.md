## How the two days are organized

Fiammetta rules Day Five and prescribes successful love after severe
misfortunes. This does not entail that every tale is ethically reassuring:
V.1 and V.8 deploy force or terror to bring about the weddings that their
tellers celebrate. VI.10, from Dioneo, is deliberately a different kind of
comic ending. The transition to Day Six changes scale: Elissa prescribes
quick sayings and shrewd actions that avert danger, disgrace, or loss.
A short tale can have a complete arc because its turning point is verbal.

The courtly women and men do not work alone in their villa. The kitchen
argument between **Licisca and Tindaro** before VI.1 makes servants audible.
Elissa reprimands Licisca, even as Dioneo sides with her interpretation;
her remarks about marriage then inspire Day Seven’s proposed theme.
At the end of Day Six the women visit the Valley of the Ladies and swim.
These narrative-frame actions connect the smaller anecdotes to the continuing
fiction of a company surviving by ordered leisure outside plague Florence.

## Close-reading passages and cautions

- **V.1 Cimon/Iphigenia:** Cimon's education transforms his abilities, but
  his seizure of Iphigenia is not presented as a clearly negotiated marriage.
  Compare the opening scene, when she fears him, with the eventual weddings.
- **V.2 Gostanza/Martuccio:** separate what Martuccio earns through counsel at
  Tunis from what Gostanza receives through luck and the hospitality of
  strangers. Her venture to sea is born from misinformation, not knowledge.
- **V.3 Pietro/Agnolella:** a wrong road turns a private elopement into a
  sequence of armed captures and shelters. Locate the acts of care performed
  by the older couple and other helpers.
- **V.4 Caterina/Ricciardo:** the nightingale is a euphemism and a device for
  arranging privacy. The father's consent to marriage turns a discovery
  into a comic ending; social rank helps explain that possibility.
- **V.5 Guidotto's ward:** old wars and uncertain parentage enter the home
  through the girl's guardianship. The revelation of kinship redefines the
  rival suitors' prospects but does not establish her absolute autonomy.
- **V.6 Gianni/Restituta:** trace the raiders' transfer of a woman to a king
  and the king's intended public execution. A recognized family identity,
  rather than a legal hearing of her preference, triggers rescue.
- **V.7 Teodoro/Violante:** the character first called Teodoro is renamed
  Pietro after being captured and sold. A scarlet mark and his recognition
  of Armenian lead to reunion with Fineo. The contrast between the threatened
  death and the wedding exposes the force of rank and birth.
- **V.8 Nastagio:** the rider's explanation of supernatural punishment is
  spoken within the tale. Nastagio uses a repeated killing as a deliberately
  staged threat; the chosen bride's fear cannot be edited out of the ending.
  The narrative was used in Renaissance wedding imagery, including a series
  associated with Botticelli; distinguish an image's commission from a claim
  that the author's coercive judgment is obligatory for present-day readers.
- **V.9 Federigo:** distinguish extravagant courtship from hospitality and
  the accidental destruction of a requested gift. The boy dies; the falcon
  does not save him; Giovanna chooses marriage after grief and family pressure.
- **V.10 Pietro di Vinciolo:** the comedy depends on concealment in a marriage
  that does not reflect the spouses' desires. Analyze hypocritical public
  condemnation separately from same-sex attraction.
- **VI.1 Oretta:** her reply inherits the gentleman's image of a ride.
  It is both critique and a face-saving exit for the incompetent storyteller.
- **VI.2 Cisti:** the baker controls a prized craft product and uses a
  pointed reply to teach a noble guest discernment without demanding an
  impossible erasure of social rank.
- **VI.3 Nonna de' Pulci:** the marshal's earlier coercive bargain and
  counterfeit payment are the suppressed facts that make her reply sting.
  Do not retell the sexual exploitation as a harmless flirtation.
- **VI.4 Chichibio:** the cook has given Brunetta a crane leg; when the
  cranes lower their resting legs after Currado shouts, his new reply saves
  him by turning the master's proof into a fresh joke.
- **VI.5 Giotto/Forese:** a joke about shabby clothes reveals the weakness of
  inferring intellectual or artistic skill from appearance. Giotto is a
  historical person; the narrated exchange is not documentary testimony.
- **VI.6 Scalza/Baronci:** the proof of family antiquity is a comic false
  argument that uses physical differences to ridicule an absent family.
- **VI.7 Filippa:** the alleged ordinance is a fact *of this fiction*, not
  proof of a recorded Prato statute. Its revision does not enact equal
  sexual rights for all women.
- **VI.8 Fresco/Ciesca:** the mirror joke is linguistically sharp but fails
  to persuade its intended target, separating verbal brilliance from effect.
- **VI.9 Guido Cavalcanti:** the tomb is the dead men's house, interpreted by
  Betto after Guido's escape. Do not attribute theological views to the
  historical poet merely because the riders mock him with a rumor.
- **VI.10 Fra Cipolla:** pranksters replace a feather with charcoal; the friar
  claims it is another relic. Distinguished rhetorical agility from truth of
  miraculous assertions; his account proves neither the relic's origin nor
  any general statement about medieval belief.

   texts](https://cds.library.brown.edu/projects/Decameron/texts/).
   The Italian text is based on Vittore Branca's 1992 edition; the site's

   the same as an Italian critical collation.

   Textual navigation to days and tales, including the day frames and
   narrator transitions.

   A teaching plan corroborating the focus of Days Five and Six.

## Questions for listeners

1. Compare V.1 and V.8. In each, what makes the planned marriage appear
   fortunate to its pursuing lover, and what remains unsettled about the
   pursued woman's freedom?
2. Compare the three recognitions in V.5, V.6 and V.7. Is an identity
   discovered, a social position changed, or both? Who is protected by rank?
3. In V.9, what is the difference between a gift given without knowledge of
   the receiver's need and a gift that actually meets that need?
4. Compare VI.1, VI.4 and VI.10 as performances of narration. Which speaker
   finds truth, which an exit, and which a new explanation that cannot be
   tested within the scene?
5. Why can Cisti and Nonna answer without a direct accusation, while
   Filippa appears to challenge the law itself? Which positions are risky?
6. What does the placement of Licisca's kitchen speech before a day of
   elite verbal performances suggest about class, sexual knowledge and
   the right to tell a story?
7. How does the Valley of the Ladies change the company’s imaginative
   geography, and how is its privacy sustained?
