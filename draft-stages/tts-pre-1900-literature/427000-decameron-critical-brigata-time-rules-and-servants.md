# Ten People Make a Schedule

Why do seven women and three men, newly escaped from a dying city, spend so much effort deciding who may speak, when to eat, and how to behave? They could have told stories whenever they felt like it. Boccaccio instead shows them creating a government in miniature. Every day has a ruler, appointed tasks, time for recreation, and a formal sequence of speech. Their freedom is achieved through arrangements, not through the disappearance of arrangements.

The company, or *brigata*, begins with seven women in Santa Maria Novella. They are joined by three men, each already connected to members of the group. Pampinea proposes that they leave the city, preserving honor and companionship instead of surrendering to fear. Their decision is bold but carefully defended. In their society, women and men traveling together outside ordinary supervision can invite suspicion. The narrators understand that how they appear to others matters. The orderly household they form is therefore partly a moral experiment and partly an answer to possible criticism.

A king or queen presides each day, and the crown moves through the group. Among the women are Pampinea, Filomena, Neifile, Fiammetta, Elissa, Lauretta, and Emilia. The men are Panfilo, Filostrato, and Dioneo. These names do not function as biographies of verifiable Florentines. They are fictional identities whose recurring preferences, choices, and reactions become parts of the frame. A particular story is not simply Boccaccio stating what he thinks. It is Boccaccio making a character say something before other characters who may have heard an incompatible tale yesterday.

The topics give the ten days a recognizable architecture. The first day is open. The second concerns reversals of fortune that end well. The third turns to achieving a desired goal. The fourth tells unhappy loves. The fifth returns to lovers who survive hardship. The sixth concentrates on apt and witty replies. The seventh concerns tricks played by wives on husbands. The eighth broadens to tricks more generally. The ninth releases the theme. The tenth asks for examples of magnificence and generosity. These descriptions are not watertight genres. Dioneo's privilege to tell the final story outside the stated theme, and the behavior of individual narrators, keep the architecture from becoming a mechanical classification system.

There are ten days of narration, but the company stays away from Florence for approximately a fortnight, with days set aside for worship and other activities. This distinction can seem trivial until one notices how carefully Boccaccio uses time. A terrible event has broken continuity in Florence. The friends invent rhythms they can keep. The daily turn, the crown, the dining table, the music, the place in the garden, and the evening movement give each person reason to expect a next gathering. Order allows variation to become intelligible.

The experiment is not equal in every respect. The company's servants do not receive an equal vote on the stories or a crown. They cook, clean, carry news, and maintain the places in which refined leisure occurs. In the Day Six framing episode, an argument among servants spills into the governing attention of the company. It is a reminder that the tidy circle of ten never entirely contains the social world sustaining it. A story about a temporary republic of voices may depend upon a highly unequal household economy.

Nor does the rotating crown create a consensus of ethical beliefs. One speaker admires a deceiver whom another might deplore. Day Nine includes Emilia's earnest endorsement of a husband's violent authority over his wife. That opinion does not become the inevitable final teaching of the book simply because a queen says it. Dioneo can answer the seriousness of one day with a bawdy joke, and the final day can praise generosity while ending in a tale about a husband who exerts extraordinary cruelty under cover of a test.

The frame is best understood as a place where judgments can be compared. Ten speakers do not produce ten consistent moral systems. They produce a moving conversation, including disagreement by implication, irony, echo, and contrast. The point is not that every opinion is equally good. It is that readers must listen for the difference between the person who tells a story, the person who acts within the story, the audience who hears it, and the author who has assembled them all.

At the end of the tenth day, the company chooses to return. Its society was never intended to replace Florence permanently. This ending rescues the frame from an easy fantasy of flight: the world still requires returning citizens, households, duties, and judgments beyond the circle of ten. The friends have practiced making a temporary structure for pleasure and thought. That practice may not save a city from plague, but it offers an answer to the question of how people might preserve deliberation when fear invites abandonment of every bond.
