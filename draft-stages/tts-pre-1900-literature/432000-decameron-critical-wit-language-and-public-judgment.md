# A Sentence Can Change the Situation

Madonna Oretta agrees to ride on horseback with a man who promises to entertain her with an excellent tale. As he begins speaking, the story becomes difficult to endure. He stumbles in its telling and mars what might otherwise have been good material. Oretta asks him to let her dismount, explaining that his horse trots too roughly. The joke is the comparison: the journey is the story itself, and the rider cannot bear the way it is carried. Her remark solves a social problem without a long accusation.

Day Six is filled with such moments, when speech does something. Its short tales show characters altering social circumstances by choosing a sentence that makes another person see an event differently. Wit is more than clever phrasing. It is a response adapted to a particular audience, opportunity, and imbalance of power. Oretta cannot simply order everyone to stop enjoying the man's company. She can make her discomfort legible through a graceful image that leaves him a route out of embarrassment.

Cisti the baker demonstrates another form. His wine is excellent, and a high-ranking gentleman ought to appreciate it. Cisti's behavior enables that appreciation without provoking an affront across class lines. His capacity to understand the rules of courtesy becomes a means of winning recognition. A person who lacks social rank can still possess the knowledge needed to guide someone who has it. The tale does not abolish hierarchy; it reveals an opening within it.

Chichibio the cook uses a more desperate verbal turn. He serves a crane with one leg missing, having given the other to a woman. Challenged by his master, he insists that cranes possess only one leg. The next morning real cranes seem to support him while standing on one leg, until the master makes a noise and they extend the other. Chichibio answers that the cooked bird would have displayed its second leg too if the master had frightened it in the same way. His remark does not change what happened to the dinner. It changes his master's emotional reaction, converting anger into laughter.

Madonna Filippa in Day Six, Tale Seven raises the stakes. She faces an adultery accusation before a court. Her response questions the legitimacy and application of the law itself. A voice endangered by institutional power becomes persuasive to the crowd. The story grants rhetoric a capacity to alter not merely a private opinion but a public rule, at least within the fictional episode. The triumphant conclusion also leaves a question for real societies: how often does a person without Filippa's eloquence receive the same hearing?

Guido Cavalcanti in Day Six, Tale Nine escapes an unwelcome group of companions with an answer that turns their surroundings, including the tombs, into an intellectual insult. The scene asks the hearer to supply something of the meaning rather than stating it plainly. Wit can thus mark social distance. It makes a community of those who understand while reminding others that they have been excluded. Verbal elegance is not always the same as kindness.

Fra Cipolla's story closes the day with a speech almost entirely manufactured in response to disaster. His supposed sacred relic has been replaced with charcoal. Rather than confess the substitution, he improvises an explanation that turns the charcoal into another object of religious importance. The audience admires the speed and ingenuity of the performance even while knowing it is false. The speaker's authority survives because his listeners accept his rhetorical transformation of the evidence.

These cases show why the phrase 'words have power' is too blunt to be useful. What kind of power? An elegant rebuke can preserve dignity. A well-placed joke can avert punishment. A legal argument can persuade a crowd. An allusive remark can enforce status. A rehearsed deception can manipulate believers. The differences depend on who speaks, who listens, and which institution gives the speech consequences.

Boccaccio also makes the reader experience wit in time. A remark works at the instant the listener grasps what was meant. Tell the joke too early and the effect is lost; explain it afterward and the social situation has already changed. That is one reason the short tales of Day Six cannot be treated merely as compressed versions of the romances. Their endings depend on timing, shared assumptions, and an audience's sudden new perception. To understand their art, listen not only to what is said but to why it could be said at just that moment.
