# The Decameron — Fra Rinaldo and the Child

Fra Rinaldo has long known a married woman in Siena. Their acquaintance changes when he becomes spiritual sponsor to her child. That relationship, which should bring the friar into the family's life under a special obligation of trust, also gives him a pretext for frequent visits. He begins to press his desires on the mother. She reminds him that his position as the child's godfather creates a close spiritual relationship, and that sexual relations across that bond are forbidden. Rinaldo answers with the shameless invention of a convenient distinction: a friar's own spiritual standing, he implies, should remove the obstacle. His argument is not independent theology. It is a suitor's self-serving attempt to undo a refusal.

Eventually the woman agrees to see him privately, and the two establish their affair. One afternoon he visits with a young companion, who helps keep the household occupied while he goes to the mother's room. The husband unexpectedly returns. Before he can understand what has been happening, the household must produce an explanation for a friar being found so close to his wife.

The wife begins the performance with an urgent claim: their child had been dangerously ill. Fra Rinaldo has saved him through prayers or a remedy for worms. The arrival of the husband is transformed from a threat of discovery into an occasion for gratitude. Rinaldo understands the fiction and reinforces it. His role as godfather, which he had used to gain access to the household, becomes the respectable reason for his presence. The mother and friar speak as though a narrowly avoided tragedy has just made them all indebted to him.

The husband is relieved, thanks the friar and treats the supposed rescue as a miracle. The episode is sometimes told with the husband's desire to commemorate the event by an image of the saint or the healer's intervention. What matters is the direction of belief: he does not simply fail to see evidence; he is invited to interpret a scene of betrayal as evidence of care for his child. A frightening alternative has been supplied, and the husband's love for the boy makes that alternative persuasive.

Elissa's comedy has two targets. The first is a sanctimonious friar who can speak the language of duty while exploiting intimacy and trust. The second is a household order in which the husband takes religious authority as a guarantee of truth. Rinaldo is trusted because he is a friar and a godfather, yet these positions are precisely what allow him to arrange a private encounter. The inconsistency is not accidental to the plot; it is its engine.

The spiritual kinship question also returns at the end of the day, when Dioneo's story about the dead raises it again in a different register. Boccaccio sets the two arguments beside one another without requiring the listener to accept either invented doctrine. The story is not a factual record of a miraculous cure. It is a tale in which the power to describe a child's illness protects adults from being answerable for what they have done.
