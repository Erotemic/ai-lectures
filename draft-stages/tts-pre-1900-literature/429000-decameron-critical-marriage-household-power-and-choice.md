# Marriage Is Never Just Two People

A young woman loves someone her family considers beneath her. A husband believes he has an exclusive right to know his wife's movements. A powerful lord decides that his wife must prove her obedience by surrendering what she values most. These are not identical situations, but they share a social fact: marriage joins bodies and affection to property, rank, lineage, and power. Boccaccio's tales repeatedly force these different meanings of marriage into collision.

In Day Four, Tale Five, Lisabetta loves Lorenzo, an employee of her merchant brothers. They kill him to end the relationship and protect what they understand as family honor. The brothers speak through an action, not a philosophical essay. Their conduct tells us how easily a woman's attachment can be converted into a perceived threat to household standing. Lisabetta's grief survives in the basil pot she tends. The story's tenderness has meaning precisely because the commercial and familial reasoning around her has become murderous.

Ghismonda's confrontation with her father in Day Four, Tale One makes the conflict verbal. She rejects the claim that nobility of birth determines moral worth and defends her choice of Guiscardo. Tancredi's emotional dependence on his daughter does not enable him to grant her freedom. His love becomes possessive; its cost is paid by Guiscardo and Ghismonda. The familiar language of parental protection can thus hide a determination that the daughter must not belong to any life beyond the father's control.

The tricks of Day Seven offer other strategies. Peronella improvises a ruse around a barrel, arranging for her lover to escape discovery while her husband is made to accept a new interpretation of the household scene. The tales are funny because the husband, who expects knowledge and command, is defeated by someone forced to make decisions under his gaze. But a successful prank need not prove that the wife enjoys durable independence. She may win an afternoon while remaining inside the constraints that made secrecy attractive or necessary.

Day Nine, Tale Nine turns the imbalance into declared doctrine. Emilia tells a story in which Solomon supposedly teaches a man to beat his wife, and she endorses a model of female obedience. The unpleasantness of that narration is not a modern insertion into an otherwise gentle collection. The tale's force comes from the approval it receives within its own speech. It also demonstrates why narrators must be distinguished from Boccaccio's historical person. The fictional queen's message clashes with earlier tales of husbands whose jealousy and control lead to ruin.

At the other end of the book stands Griselda. Gualtieri, Marquis of Saluzzo, marries a poor young woman and then subjects her to a series of cruel trials. He makes her believe that their children have been murdered. He publicly dismisses her and requires her to help prepare for his supposed new marriage. The children are alive; the intended bride is their daughter; the tests are an invention of the husband's power. There is no uncertainty about who controls the conditions. Griselda can show patience but cannot make the tests stop.

Some readers have treated her constancy as spiritual allegory; others have stressed the husband's brutality. The story's final narrator, Dioneo, explicitly criticizes the marquis's conduct as folly. It is not accurate to say the original tale straightforwardly recommends cruelty in marriage. Nor is it sufficient to say that because the narrator condemns the husband, the suffering no longer matters. The episode invites argument about whether obedience can count as virtue when the other party has made the relationship into a theater of domination.

Marriage in the *Decameron* is sometimes a source of tenderness, sometimes an avenue to property and security, and sometimes an institution that protects power from challenge. Wives also deceive husbands, not every lover is honorable, and the causes of betrayal vary. The book's interest lies in a series of specific situations rather than an abstract verdict. One reader may applaud a woman's stratagem while questioning the fate of a servant recruited to support it. Another may admire persistence in love but wonder whether the beloved had a genuine choice.

A good comparative reading returns repeatedly to the question of who could have said no. Could Lisabetta have married Lorenzo? Could Ghismonda have made Tancredi listen? Could Peronella have desired anyone without concealment? Could Griselda have refused without losing livelihood, children, or the roof over her head? The answers are not supplied by the wedding at the end of the story. They are discovered in the distribution of power before the wedding and in the actions that follow it.
