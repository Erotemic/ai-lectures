# Anna Karenina — Story: Levin's Work and Anna's Illness

Two kinds of work occupy the center of Anna Karenina. Levin attempts to manage his estate and understand the labor of the peasants who work its land. Karenin attempts to preserve a marriage through the formal procedures available to a senior official. Both are highly educated men, and both discover that the methods that serve them in ordinary life do not necessarily explain the people closest to them.

Levin's agricultural efforts are practical and often frustrating. He joins workers in mowing, feels the pleasure of coordinated physical labor, and tries to improve how farming is organized. His experience in the fields does not make him a peasant, and the workers' views are not simply extensions of his own. They have families, customs, judgments, and incentives that his abstract plans often fail to include. His wish to create a more just and productive arrangement runs up against his dependence on owning the land. Tolstoy makes the problem of moral improvement concrete rather than allowing Levin a painless escape into good intentions.

Levin remains hurt by Kitty's rejection. At one moment he glimpses her in a passing carriage and realizes that the affection he thought he had buried is still alive. The encounter is brief; it does not transform their lives immediately. His brother Nikolai, ill and estranged, also draws him away from the self-contained routines of estate life. Nikolai's poverty, illness, and quarrels disturb Levin's wish to make problems soluble by work. No scheme of agricultural improvement can settle what a family owes a suffering brother.

Anna's position grows more desperate. Karenin intends to control scandal through separation without openly dissolving the marriage. Anna, however, is pregnant with Vronsky's child and refuses to treat the affair as a secret that can be filed away. She is frightened for her son, Seryozha, and increasingly trapped by the impossibility of keeping both relationships in the form she wants. Vronsky's prospects in military and fashionable society are altered by the affair, but he retains freedoms unavailable to Anna. The mismatch becomes a recurring source of strain.

As Anna prepares to give birth, her health collapses. She becomes seriously ill and seems close to death. Karenin arrives expecting betrayal and accusation, but he encounters suffering that disrupts his usual moral categories. In an extraordinary scene he forgives Anna and Vronsky. Vronsky, overwhelmed by humiliation and despair, attempts to shoot himself; he survives. Anna also survives her illness and gives birth to a daughter, usually called Annie. What briefly appeared to be a final reconciliation cannot easily become a lasting household. Forgiveness in an emergency has not changed the laws governing marriage, divorce, and children.

Kitty and Levin meet again under changed conditions. She has experienced disappointment and recovered some independence; he has learned that wounded pride cannot determine the rest of his life. Their renewed closeness grows through small gestures rather than an elaborate public display. In one scene they communicate through the initial letters of words, discovering that they understand each other's wishes before the full sentences have been supplied. Their engagement does not mean that their future will be free of doubt. It means they are willing to begin a shared life while still imperfect.

Stiva's role in arranging meetings and smoothing social discomfort contrasts with Dolly's daily work of keeping her family together. He can assist Levin and Kitty while remaining unable or unwilling to correct the pattern of neglect in his own marriage. This is one of Tolstoy's sharpest social observations: charm may be genuinely helpful in one setting and profoundly irresponsible in another. The same person can be liked and trusted in public while leaving private debts, financial and emotional, for others to pay.

Anna and Vronsky eventually leave Russia together with their daughter. Karenin remains with Seryozha. The departure gives Anna a form of freedom but also separates her from the child she loves and leaves her uncertain status unresolved. Vronsky can imagine a life abroad as a new beginning. Anna carries a loss that foreign travel cannot repair. The difference in their burdens will become more visible when they return.

By now the two main paths of the novel have crossed a threshold. Levin and Kitty are moving toward marriage after learning something about the limits of romantic display. Anna and Vronsky are moving into a life outside recognized marriage after discovering that powerful feeling cannot abolish family bonds or social institutions. Tolstoy refuses to make either path simple. The task of living truthfully has become, for both pairs, a problem of other people's needs rather than a private feeling alone.
