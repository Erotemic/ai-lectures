# The Decameron — Gerbino and the Princess of Tunis

Gerbino is a young Sicilian nobleman, the grandson of King Guglielmo, known for courage and splendor. Reports of his qualities reach the daughter of the King of Tunis. Through messengers, gifts, and letters, the two form an attachment before they have met. Their love is nourished by descriptions and promises, a relation established across the water by speech rather than shared daily life.

The princess is promised to the King of Granada. Her father prepares a ship to carry her to the marriage. Hearing rumors of Gerbino's passion, the King of Tunis asks Guglielmo to guarantee that the vessel will pass safely. The Sicilian king gives his word and sends his glove as a physical pledge. The diplomatic meaning is clear: the ship will not be attacked by those subject to the king's authority.

The princess sends Gerbino a message announcing her departure and urging him to prove his love. He knows that his grandfather has made the promise, but he arms two galleys and goes to sea in the hope of intercepting her. He persuades his men with language that joins love to the prospect of valuable plunder. For himself, he says, he seeks only the woman; the rest of the ship's wealth may be theirs. The expedition is therefore an act of passion and an armed venture with material rewards.

The ship appears. Its crew displays Guglielmo's glove and invokes the pledged safe conduct. Gerbino dismisses the token and demands the princess. The defenders refuse. A fierce battle follows, in which arrows and stones fly across the water. Unable to take the ship quickly, Gerbino tries a more destructive tactic, using a burning vessel to force the crew's surrender.

The defenders decide that their king's daughter will not become Gerbino's prize. They kill her before his eyes and throw her body into the sea. Gerbino boards in a rage and kills many of the people who opposed him. He can recover her body and bury it, but not restore her life. The expedition he imagined as a rescue has made her death possible.

The King of Tunis demands an accounting. Guglielmo has given his word. Though he loves his grandson, he judges that the breach of safe conduct cannot be excused by private passion. Gerbino is arrested and executed. The old king chooses the preservation of a political pledge over the life of his heir. Elissa ends with a second loss produced by the first: the princess has died in the attack, and Gerbino dies because he ordered it.

This is a tragedy about competing obligations. The lovers use the language of a promise to each other; the rulers use the language of a promise between kingdoms. Neither kind of promise is trivial, but Gerbino assumes that desire can annul the public undertaking and that force will be able to repair the difference. It cannot. His courage, so attractive in reports carried across the sea, becomes the means by which he defeats the very possibility of being with the woman he loves.

We should also notice how little freedom the princess possesses at either end of the story. Her father arranges her marriage, and Gerbino plans to seize her vessel. She sends a request for help, but once fighting begins, the men controlling the ship decide what to do with her body. The poem-like aura of long-distance love must not conceal the violence of the institutions surrounding it. She is neither a diplomatic gift nor a military prize from her own point of view.

Boccaccio places the glove at the center of the conflict for a reason. A small object stands for an agreement larger than any individual. Gerbino scorns it as irrelevant to his urgent feeling. The result shows why a pledge that protects travelers can matter more, not less, when emotions run high. The tale asks whether courage without fidelity to a just promise deserves praise when others must bear its costs.
