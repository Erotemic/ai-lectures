# The Emperor’s Return and the Messenger with Melons

When Taizong spoke from inside his coffin, the palace did not immediately recover its composure. The emperor's attendants had prepared for a funeral. The officers had argued over succession. The court women had put on mourning garments. Now the man they were ready to bury was sitting upright, describing a river, a horse, and the sudden shove that had brought him back to his body. The ministers steadied him and summoned physicians. After food, rest, and medicines, the emperor regained enough strength to tell the story.

He described the city of the dead, the officials of the Ten Kings, the complaint of the Dragon King, the intervention of Cui Jue, and the register that allowed him twenty more years to live. He described the halls of punishment, the bridge above the dark river, and the crowd of victims in the city of wrongful death. His ministers heard how a ruler might have honors in one world yet stand helpless among those killed in the wars that founded his kingdom.

When he had recovered, Taizong began to act upon the promises he had made. He issued proclamations urging mercy and good works. The story says that he released a great number of condemned prisoners so that they might return home before appearing again for their appointed punishment, and arranged for thousands of women in the palace to leave its service. The court's proclamations imagined a kingdom in which acts of generosity might lighten the sufferings that filled the underworld.

Yet declarations were not enough. Taizong had promised the Ten Kings a gift of southern melons, and he had borrowed a fortune in the underworld from a man he had never met. Both debts still waited. He sent an officer to find Xiang Liang in Kaifeng and deliver the money corresponding to what had been borrowed. He also asked for someone who would carry melons into the land of the dead.

A man named Liu Quan volunteered for the second task. The circumstances of his life were painful. His wife Li Cuilian had given food to a monk in charity, and Liu Quan had berated her for acting without his approval. In distress she had died by suicide, leaving young children. Liu Quan could not endure the household after her death. He offered himself for the embassy into the underworld, knowing that the undertaking required him to die and carry the fruit as a spirit.

The court received his offer. He was given the melons and ritual instructions, and he took a fatal draught. His soul reached the gates of the dead with the promised offering. When a ghostly guard challenged him, Liu Quan answered that he was the emperor's messenger. The Ten Kings accepted the fruit and praised Taizong for keeping his word. They asked the messenger's name and reason for coming. He told them about his wife, his sorrow, and the children he had left.

The judges ordered the records of Liu Quan and Li Cuilian examined. They learned that the appointed span of their lives had not yet run its course. Liu Quan's journey, intended as a final offering, had brought him to the one realm where his lost wife could be found. They were reunited in the court of the dead and given permission to return to the living world together.

But another difficulty arose. Liu Quan's body had only recently died, and it could receive his spirit again. Li Cuilian had been dead longer; her body was gone. Where could her spirit live? The judges consulted the registers and determined that a woman of the Tang royal household, Li Yuying, was due to die. Her body could be used. The decision was carried out through the strange procedures of the underworld.

The story moves between tenderness and unease. Liu Quan and his wife find one another, but their return depends on taking over the body of a princess. The narrative treats this as a miraculous arrangement by the judges. It does not ask the living woman for her approval. The royal household will discover that a familiar body now speaks as someone else.

The emperor meanwhile awaited news, having sent one man to settle a monetary debt and another to carry fruit into a place where no ordinary courier could return. He had seen enough of the underworld to believe that government included obligations to the dead. He was learning that even in a tale of miracles, duties accumulated. A promise to one frightened dragon had started the chain of events. A promise to hungry spirits and the Ten Kings would now draw all of Chang'an into a solemn religious undertaking.

When Liu Quan returned, he would bring more than word that the melons had been delivered. He would bring a wife in a borrowed body, and a new question for the emperor about the reach of earthly authority. The city of the living was about to receive a story that was as strange as anything in Taizong's own account of death.
