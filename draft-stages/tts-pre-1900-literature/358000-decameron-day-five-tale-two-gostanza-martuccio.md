# The Decameron — Gostanza and Martuccio

The island of Lipari supplies Emilia with a story that begins in poverty, not at court. Gostanza loves Martuccio Gomito, an enterprising young man who wants to marry her but lacks the resources her family expects. Her father rejects the match. Martuccio leaves to seek wealth, becoming a raider at sea. At first his ventures prosper; then he encounters defeat. His ship is overcome and he is taken prisoner. News returning to Lipari says he has been killed.

Gostanza accepts the report as the end of her future. She does not arrange a new courtship or even try to protect herself. She goes down to the shore alone, takes a small boat, and sets out without proper preparation for a crossing. She means to abandon herself to the sea. Fortune, which has already falsified the news of Martuccio's death, now carries her toward the coast of North Africa rather than destroying her. She comes ashore in the region of Susa, alive but unable to explain herself to the people she meets.

A woman gives Gostanza shelter and helps her enter a household where she can recover and remain safe. This detail matters. Many of the day's romances depend on the intervention of strangers who have no reason to act except compassion or recognition of distress. Without such hospitality, a girl alone in a foreign port would be in much greater danger than the plot's concluding prosperity makes easy to remember. Gostanza learns enough of the local circumstances to discover that Martuccio is not dead. He is in Tunis, and his fortunes have changed astonishingly.

Martuccio, taken captive after his defeat, has become useful to the ruler through intelligent advice about warfare. His expertise helps secure a victory; rather than remaining a prisoner, he earns the king's favor and an honorable position. Emilia has drawn a second trajectory beside Gostanza's. She is carried helplessly by the sea and preserved by welcome. He is carried as a captive into a court where judgment and skill can make him valuable. Their paths now converge in a city neither expected to call home.

When the lovers are reunited, Martuccio is no longer the poor young man whose proposal could be dismissed. He can arrange a marriage and a wealthy return to Lipari. Their reunion satisfies Fiammetta's day's theme in the clearest way: a reported death proves false, two separated people recognize each other, and material circumstances no longer bar the match.

It is tempting to hear the ending as a lesson that suffering is always rewarded. The story offers no such guarantee. Gostanza could not know that a storm would preserve her life, or that a stranger would protect her, or that Martuccio would rise in a foreign court. Nor could Martuccio reasonably predict the benefits of captivity. Good fortune operates precisely where the characters lack control. By contrast, hospitality and useful intelligence are human acts whose value can be described without pretending that fate is fair.

Emilia's romance allows a reader to feel relief without forgetting the risks that made relief necessary. Separation begins in the unequal power to decide a marriage; reunion depends on changes that neither lover could command. Their happiness is genuine within the tale. Its arrival is a combination of endurance, help, competence, and chance, not a proof that the world naturally delivers such endings.
