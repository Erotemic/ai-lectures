# The Canterbury Tales — Retraction and the Unfinished Journey

At the end of many editions of The Canterbury Tales stands a brief prose text known as Chaucer's Retraction. The author thanks Christ, Mary and the saints for whatever in his writing has led people toward devotion and asks forgiveness for works of worldly vanity. He names several of his own poems and stories. The piece seems, at first, to contradict the noisy book that precedes it: after hundreds of pages of quarrels, erotic jokes, romances, sermons and false claims, a writer asks that anything spiritually harmful be forgiven.

How should a listener receive this? One possibility is to hear the Retraction as a genuine devotional statement by an aging author, concerned about the moral implications of his career. Another is to notice that literature in the collection has repeatedly tested the relation between a speaker and a statement. The Pardoner condemns greed without reforming; the Wife of Bath uses scriptural learning while mocking those who use it to silence her; Chaucer the pilgrim is himself interrupted for telling bad poetry. The Retraction may be sincere and still exist within a literary structure that prevents us from reducing the preceding voices to one lesson.

The actual pilgrimage is not completed in the surviving collection. We do not watch the full company arrive at Becket's shrine, receive the promised supper after the return journey, or determine a winning tale. The host planned a large competition with outward and homeward storytelling. What survives is twenty-four tales, some unfinished, grouped in ten fragments that contain internal connections but no certain final arrangement of every group. Chaucer died without delivering the neatly completed book that Harry Bailey's rules seemed to anticipate.

The absence of an ending has shaped every generation of readers. Copyists and editors arranged fragments, chose where to place links, and sometimes supplied additional works in the hope of filling gaps. A modern edition has to make practical decisions about sequence, but those decisions are not identical with proof of Chaucer's final intentions. The distinction makes the book more revealing rather than less. It has become an object that readers, scribes and scholars must continually assemble, and therefore continually interpret.

The journey's central idea remains powerful. People from different walks of life agree to travel together and, while sharing a road, tell stories. They do not become morally identical through the act of pilgrimage. They compete for attention, expose one another's hypocrisy, delight in incompatible genres and recognize different forms of authority. Some use stories to injure, others to advise, and many do both at once. The text offers no single voice capable of explaining all the others away.

If we return to the opening spring morning, the movement is clear even without a final supper. The road made strangers into a public audience. At the inn they could have exchanged names and occupations; as tellers and listeners they reveal grudges, fantasies, fears and methods of judgment. Some stories teach readers how to recognize patterns of harm. Others force readers to ask why a joke once considered harmless can wound people excluded from its audience. Every teller is situated within a social world, and every listener has a stake in what is said.

The last surviving movement, then, is neither a completed travel itinerary nor a straightforward triumph of one literary form. It is an invitation to continue thinking after the texts stop. The pilgrims' proposed contest raises the possibility of a best tale; the unfinished collection leaves us with the more demanding task of deciding what kinds of excellence, truth and responsibility can coexist in stories told by people who do not agree. That is a more lasting achievement than a prize dinner would have been.
