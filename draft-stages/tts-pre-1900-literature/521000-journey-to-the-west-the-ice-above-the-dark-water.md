# Journey to the West: The ice above the dark water

## The Great King comes to dinner

In the shrine beside the Heaven-Reaching River, two children sat waiting to be eaten. Their parents were miles away in a house full of people who dared not speak loudly. The villagers had conducted the annual tribute as they always did. The incense was lit, the offerings placed, and the fearful people withdrawn. Only one part of the rite had changed. The little boy was Sun Wukong; the little girl was Zhu Bajie.

Bajie disliked every part of the arrangement. The girl's clothes were uncomfortable, the platter was too small, and the smell of the surrounding food made him hungry. He had been told not to move until the creature appeared. The whole plan depended on the river spirit mistaking two experienced fighters for a pair of helpless children, which required Bajie to remain quiet longer than he ordinarily could.

A presence entered the temple. The supposed river god, known to the villagers as the Great King of Numinous Response, had arrived to claim his tribute. He appeared in a splendid warlike shape, wearing a shining helmet. He had come expecting the familiar terror of two small children and the familiar feast that followed. Instead he heard an argument.

Bajie, frightened at being chosen first, objected in a little girl's voice that the boy ought to be eaten before the girl. Wukong, still shaped like Guanbao, had no intention of helping him preserve this self-serving disguise. The pig's protest was already enough to make the spirit suspicious. The children did not behave as they should.

The Great King moved to seize his meal. Bajie could bear no more. The child vanished, and a mighty rake swept through the air where a little girl had sat. Wukong leapt up as himself, iron staff ready. The monster cried out and fought them. He had expected an altar meal and found two armed enemies.

Under the blows, two large bright scales came loose from his body. Bajie struck hard enough to remind him that the children were no longer helpless, but the fight was brief. The spirit slipped away toward the water, leaving the two pilgrims in the shrine with the broken remains of the ceremony and the scales that had fallen from him.

Wukong examined them. The creature was not a river god beyond all nature. His body bore the signs of a fish. That was important. If he was a fish spirit, he could be hunted, driven out, or called to account by someone with greater command of the waters. The children had been saved, but the river's ruler was still at large, and Xuanzang still needed a passage to the western bank.

Bajie thought the night's work had earned him a meal. Wukong, however, knew that wounding the spirit was not the same as freeing the village. A creature that had claimed children for years would not easily surrender a rich source of tribute because two travelers had struck it with weapons. The struggle would move from the altar to the water itself.

## The court beneath the river

Far below the surface, in a palace whose halls were washed by green light, the Great King received the news of his failure as an insult. He had gone to the shrine to eat and had been battered by two of the very disciples whose master was traveling west. The fish scales torn from his body proved how close he had come to being overpowered. He was angry at the villagers, angry at the two false children, and frightened of the monkey who had once challenged Heaven.

A female mandarin-fish attendant listened to his account. She understood that the pilgrim band had not come to remain beside the shrine. Their purpose was to cross the Heaven-Reaching River and go on toward the western scriptures. The master himself was a human monk, incapable of swimming the vast stretch of water and unwilling to abandon his horse and companions.

If the Great King could not seize the disciples, she reasoned, he might take the master. There would be another way to get the strangers into the river.

She proposed cold.

Although the season had not yet advanced to the depth of winter, the monster could command a sudden turn of weather. He could bring frost and snow, thicken the river with ice, and make its broad surface seem like a road. The people on shore would tell the travelers that the ice was solid enough to bear weight. A few convincing figures crossing with goods would make the deception more persuasive. The pilgrims, anxious to continue their journey, might leave the safety of the bank of their own accord.

Once they reached the center, the spirit could break the ice and seize the one traveler he wanted.

The plan appealed to him. A direct assault had failed because Wukong and Bajie carried powerful weapons. The monk, who spent his days teaching compassion and trying to keep his disciples from quarrelling, did not possess an iron staff or a rake that could drive an enemy off. Draw him out and the others would have to come begging.

Outside, the weather altered. A bitter wind rushed across the river. The waves quieted, then thickened. Snow blew over the roofs of the Chen family houses. Ice appeared where water had been the day before, spreading far into the distance. The villagers woke to a world that looked as though a season had passed in a single night.

## A road that should not exist

Xuanzang could hardly believe the change. Only recently he had stood at the bank and seen water beyond sight. Now the same expanse was hard and white. A continuous plain of ice reached toward the distant sky. Men who knew the river came to tell him that a crossing might be possible. Travelers seemed to be moving on the frozen surface, carrying goods as though it were an established winter road.

The Chen family urged caution. A river eight hundred li wide was not a village pond. Ice could conceal deep channels, weak places, and currents that no one could see. A boat built for the crossing would be safer when the weather changed. Their guests had saved the family's children. The elders would gladly shelter them for as long as it took to find another way.

But Xuanzang had begun to count the years. He had left the Tang emperor with the hope of returning after three years. The journey had already taken far longer than that. Every river, mountain, and demon seemed to add another delay. He feared the court might have ceased expecting him, and he knew he had made promises that could not be discharged by waiting in a generous stranger's house.

Wukong did not share the urgency. He had lived long enough to know that a promise measured in years was not worth dying for on one foolish day. The ice had appeared too quickly and too conveniently. A monster had just escaped to the river. These facts deserved more attention than the encouraging signs of an apparent caravan moving over the frozen surface.

Bajie was of two minds. He liked the Chen family's food and had no desire to fall into cold water, but he was also tired of waiting. If the river truly offered a road, why should they sit on its bank? He carried his rake onto the edge and struck the surface, finding it hard. He walked farther, tested again, and returned announcing that it could bear weight.

The Chen elders still pleaded for patience. Sha Wujing, who had crossed dangerous rivers before, looked at the white expanse with concern. Xuanzang answered that the purpose of their pilgrimage was to continue toward the western heaven. A man who feared every obstacle would never arrive. The travelers could take precautions. They could wrap the horse's hooves with straw so it would not slip. They could carry their weapons crosswise to steady themselves if the surface gave way.

Wukong did not consider those measures sufficient, but he could not persuade his master to stay. The old conflict between their temperaments had returned in another form. Xuanzang called persistence a virtue. Wukong called suspicion a way of staying alive. Neither thought the other's advice was the whole truth.

They thanked the Chen family, gathered their belongings, and stepped onto the ice.

## The white plain gives way

At first the crossing seemed possible. Straw bound around the horse's hooves gave it some purchase. The men moved slowly in the cold, placing each foot before the next. The air made their breath visible. Snow lay in ridges across the surface, hiding the darker ice underneath. The river seemed silent, but beneath that silence enormous volumes of water still moved in unseen channels.

Xuanzang leaned forward on his horse, watching the distant horizon. Bajie was burdened by his clothes and equipment. Sha Wujing kept near the luggage. Wukong moved around them, scanning the ice and the sky. Behind, the near bank dwindled. Ahead, the opposite shore remained invisible.

Somewhere below, the Great King of Numinous Response waited.

The travelers had ventured far enough that retreat was as difficult as continuing. The spirit ordered the ice to break. What had seemed a firm white road groaned and split. Water surged through fissures and lifted whole sheets of ice like doors wrenched from their hinges. The horse stumbled. The disciples scrambled to keep their footing as the surface fell apart beneath them.

Xuanzang was the one the spirit sought. The monk could not leap into the sky, change into a bird, or throw aside the laws of his own body. In the confusion he was dragged into the dark water. The river closed above him.

Wukong and the others fought to reach him, but the attack had been arranged for precisely this result. The monster disappeared below the broken surface with his captive. The three disciples were left above the river, alive but unable to see where their master had gone. Every moment mattered. They had crossed onto the ice against warnings from the house that had sheltered them, and now Xuanzang was lost in the water his friends had feared.

The monster took him down to the hidden palace. There was no celebration of the kind that might have followed the successful capture of a warrior. The prize was a bound and terrified pilgrim, a man in wet robes whom the Great King intended to keep until he decided what to do with him. He ordered Xuanzang shut into a heavy stone chest or coffer and kept below the water, where the disciples above could not reach him without entering the spirit's domain.

It was a second kind of sacrifice. The spirit had failed to eat the two children promised by the Chen family. He had instead drawn a holy man onto a road made of ice and taken him into the river. The village had been spared its yearly offering for one night, but the power that demanded such offerings remained intact.

On the broken ice, Wukong looked down into the water. His master was alive somewhere below; the monkey's task was to find him before the fish spirit returned for its feast. He knew how to threaten the gates of Heaven. A palace at the bottom of a river was another matter. He would have to work with his companions, learn the creature's defenses, and discover the one place where the trapped man could be reached.
