# Frankenstein — The Creature’s Education, Language, and Social Prejudice

When the creature first wakes, it does not know language or the social meanings of its own body. It experiences light, cold, hunger, and confusion. Its later eloquence is therefore not a miraculous inheritance of educated speech. The novel gives a long account of how it learns. By hiding near the De Lacey cottage and observing the family, it discovers recurring sounds, gestures, needs, and patterns of affection. By watching Felix teach Safie, it acquires language and receives a simplified history of nations and human institutions. Language opens a social world that has already refused to receive it.

The creature's education is both practical and literary. It learns to avoid taking the family's food after recognizing their poverty. It gathers wood for them. It also reads Plutarch, Goethe, and Milton, finding models through which to interpret social conduct and personal suffering. These books do not agree with one another. The political virtues of historical lives differ from the passions of a sentimental novel and the theological drama of Paradise Lost. The creature draws on each because it lacks the ordinary social relationships that might allow meanings to be negotiated with others.

The encounter with the blind father De Lacey is the book's central test of appearance. The creature believes that a listener who cannot see it might respond to its words. The conversation begins to realize that hope, but Felix returns and beats it away. We should not say blindness grants the father perfect moral insight; he has too little information to know who is speaking. The scene instead isolates one mechanism of prejudice. The people who can see the creature react to a form they have already learned to fear, rather than to its conduct or testimony.

The being's response to rejection changes over time. At first it tries to avoid harm and seeks opportunities to help. After repeated violence and flight, it interprets the world as an enemy. It destroys the cottage after the family leaves and sets out to confront its creator. When it kills William and frames Justine, it crosses from victimhood into deliberate cruelty. The transformation should not be narrated as though education mechanically produced murder. The creature learns sympathy and language before choosing vengeance. What drives the catastrophe is the interaction of unjust treatment, wounded expectation, and decisions for which it remains responsible.

Safie's role is often reduced to a plot convenience because the creature overhears her lessons. But her movement across borders, her desire for independence, and the conflicts in the De Lacey family's history widen the novel's view beyond Victor's laboratory. At the same time, the portrayal of Safie's Turkish father and of cultural differences draws on European ideas of the period that deserve critical examination. A serious reading should distinguish what characters say about national or religious identity from a universal description of the people so labeled.

The language of monstrosity itself is unstable. Victor repeatedly names the being through disgust, but it can explain its motives, remember kindness, and criticize injustice. It also uses language to threaten and manipulate. The fact that a speaker is eloquent does not establish that every claim is true; the fact that the speaker looks frightening does not establish that it is false. Shelley repeatedly splits appearance, testimony, and conduct so that readers cannot settle the ethical question by examining a face.

For an active listening exercise, trace the creature's first three decisions that affect someone else's welfare: whether to take food, whether to gather wood, and whether to ask the De Laceys for friendship. Then compare them with its later decisions concerning William and Justine. The chronology reveals a being capable of moral learning who nonetheless becomes destructive. The novel's question is not whether humans are naturally good or naturally evil. It is how social recognition, injury, education, and choice interact in the making of a person.
