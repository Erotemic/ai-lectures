# Taizong Before the Ten Kings of the Dead

Taizong was dead in the palace, but the story did not leave him there. His spirit found itself outside the world he had ruled. At first it seemed that he was still surrounded by soldiers on an imperial excursion. Then they vanished, and the landscape became strange and empty. He did not know where he was going. A figure called to him from a distance, addressing him as the emperor and asking him to come closer.

The stranger was Cui Jue, the judge to whom Wei Zheng had written. He wore the clothing of an official, carrying the assurance of someone who understood the records and procedures of the underworld. Taizong produced the letter from his minister. Cui read it and welcomed him warmly. He knew Wei Zheng, and he promised to help the emperor return to life if the laws and ledgers of the dead allowed it.

They went together toward the city of ghosts. At its gate Taizong saw figures he had not expected to meet again: his father Li Yuan and his brothers Li Jiancheng and Li Yuanji. The brothers came toward him demanding what had been taken from them. In the human world, Taizong's power had survived the violent struggle that made him emperor. In the underworld, the dead remembered it differently. They seized at him, and he could not simply command them to stand aside. Cui called upon an attendant to drive them back, and only then could Taizong continue.

Past the gate stood halls and offices of astonishing grandeur. The underworld was not a wilderness where ghosts wandered without order. It had officials, petitions, clerks, summonses, registers, and judges. The king of the living world was being led into another government. Before its throne stood the Ten Kings of the Dead, each responsible for a portion of the vast administration of souls.

They received Taizong with elaborate courtesy. He was a king among human beings, and they were kings within their own realm. Yet the formal welcome did not mean that he could decide his own case. The dead Dragon King had brought a complaint against him. Why, asked the judges, had Taizong promised rescue and allowed the execution to proceed?

The emperor explained everything. The Dragon King had come in a dream. Taizong had promised help. He had summoned Wei Zheng, kept him in attendance, and engaged him in a game of go. Wei Zheng had not left the room. The execution had nevertheless occurred while the minister slept. The emperor had tried to keep his word and had failed to understand that the appointed executioner could act through his spirit.

The Ten Kings were satisfied with the account. They already knew the Dragon King's allotted death had been recorded in the heavens. The dragon's complaint had required Taizong's appearance for a formal hearing, but it did not make him the murderer. The matter was closed. Still, a legal question remained. The emperor was dead. Was his time among the living truly finished?

The judges ordered the records consulted. Cui Jue went to examine the book that named the spans of mortal reigns. The entry for the Tang emperor appeared to allow only thirteen years, the very number of years Taizong had ruled. Cui recognized the danger. If this entry stood unchanged, the underworld officials would have no reason to release his guest.

So Cui performed an act that belonged as much to the world of clerks and documents as to the world of miracles. He took his brush and added strokes to the written number. Thirteen became thirty-three. The court saw the amended record and concluded that twenty years of earthly life remained to Taizong. The judges declared that he should return.

Taizong expressed his gratitude. Before he departed, he asked about the members of his household. The judges reassured him about most of them but hinted that a royal woman's life would soon be cut short. They also accepted his offer of a gift from the living world. Asked what fruit the underworld lacked, they answered that they had eastern and western melons but no southern melons. Taizong promised to send them the fruit they desired.

This was a strange scene for an emperor. A decision about his life rested upon an altered character in a ledger. He was invited to send produce as a token of thanks. The dead did not seem bound by the dignity that surrounded him in court; they treated him as a guest, a petitioner, and a debtor, sometimes all at once.

Cui Jue and another underworld official were assigned to take him back. But the route home did not pass directly from the court to the palace. The judge would show him what the emperor's former wars and the living world's misconduct had left behind. There were more gates to cross, and not all the people waiting beyond them would bow at the sight of imperial robes.
