# The Decameron — A Friendship Across Two Cities

Titus and Gisippus grow up together in Athens and become devoted friends. Titus is a Roman, Gisippus an Athenian; their families and cities give them different obligations, but their affection is presented as nearly complete. When Gisippus is betrothed to Sophronia, Titus sees her and is overwhelmed by love. The young Roman wastes away with the conflict between his desire and the loyalty he owes his closest friend.

Gisippus learns the cause. Rather than reproach him, he offers to give up his bride. He argues that he can find another wife more easily than another friend. This is the moment Filomena expects her audience to read as extraordinary liberality. The gift, however, is not an object Gisippus owns. Sophronia is a person whose family has already negotiated her marriage and who has not agreed to the exchange. To prevent the families from interrupting the plan, the two men proceed deceptively.

Gisippus completes the wedding ceremonies, but on the wedding night Titus takes his place in the dark. Sophronia consents to the man she believes she has married. She discovers later that the partner was someone else. The friends appeal to marriage law and the fact of consummation to defend the arrangement. Her relatives are furious and threaten consequences. Titus uses the authority of a Roman citizen and the prospect of political retaliation to force acceptance. Sophronia goes with him to Rome; Gisippus remains behind in Athens.

The fortunes of the friends reverse. Gisippus loses his standing, is exiled and arrives in Rome in destitution. He sees Titus pass and believes he has been ignored. In despair he allows himself to be accused of a murder he did not commit, hoping to die. Titus recognizes him and declares himself the murderer to save him. The real killer, hearing the two friends claim guilt, comes forward. All three are spared. Titus then welcomes Gisippus into his household, shares his wealth and arranges for him to marry his sister.

The second half supplies the repayment for the first. Titus risks his own life for the man who relinquished a marriage for him. Filomena's audience is encouraged to see a chain of friendship stronger than self-interest, poverty or death. Yet the first half remains difficult to assimilate to generosity. The friends consider the loss and rescue of one another but make Sophronia the medium of the transaction. Her mistaken identity of the bridegroom means that the first sexual encounter occurs without informed consent.

This tale therefore puts the day's problem in its starkest form. The men can be genuinely devoted friends and still wrong a person who has no comparable voice in their compact. Ancient settings, Roman legal claims and the elevated language of loyalty may make the action sound noble to the narrator. A listener can admire the later willingness to face execution and still ask whose desires the original gift required the story to disregard.

Titus and Gisippus are not a simple pair of exemplary men. They are figures in an argument about what loyalty means. Their willingness to sacrifice for each other is real within the fiction. So is their willingness to arrange another person's life. Filomena's long narrative makes those two judgments occupy the same space, which is why it remains more interesting than a mere illustration of friendship.
