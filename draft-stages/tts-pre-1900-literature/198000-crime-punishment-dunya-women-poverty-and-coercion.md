# Crime and Punishment — Women, Poverty, and Coercion

Crime and Punishment is structured around a man who claims the right to rise above ordinary restraints. Around him are women whose choices are constrained by money, social power, and the sexual entitlement of others. Dunya, Sonya, Katerina Ivanovna, and Raskolnikov's mother do not respond to these pressures alike. Their stories prevent the book's philosophical arguments from floating free of lived economic conditions.

Dunya's proposed marriage to Luzhin is a clear example of a respectable bargain with unequal terms. He imagines that a woman grateful for his financial position will be obedient. Raskolnikov sees that his sister may sacrifice her autonomy to help the family. His indignation contains genuine love but also a wish to control the decision himself. Dunya is the person who must decide whether to marry Luzhin. Her refusal becomes most meaningful when she hears the terms in his own voice and rejects the idea that financial assistance entitles him to command her loyalties.

Her earlier employment in Svidrigailov's household exposes a second danger. A woman paid to work in another family's home may be vulnerable to an employer's desire and to accusations that follow even when she resists him. Dunya's reputation depends partly on what other people choose to believe about events they did not witness. Later, Svidrigailov attempts to use knowledge of her brother's crime as leverage to force her into submission. The scene is not a courtship at the edge of tragedy. It is coercion, and her resistance is essential to its meaning.

Sonya's poverty has a different form. She earns money through prostitution because members of the Marmeladov household cannot otherwise survive. Her father's account recognizes her sacrifice while revealing his own failure to stop drinking. She is subjected to contempt by a society that often treats poverty as evidence of vice. Luzhin exploits this stigma when he plants money and accuses her of theft. He has selected a target whose public credibility is already weakened.

Katerina Ivanovna's decline is not simply a personality failing. She struggles to preserve domestic dignity while facing illness, debts, hunger, and the needs of children. Her demand for respect can make her fierce and unreasonable, yet the circumstances are genuinely intolerable. When she takes the children into the streets after Marmeladov's death, the spectacle is disturbing because social performance is being used as a last defense against destitution.

Pulcheria Alexandrovna, Raskolnikov's mother, has a different kind of dependence. She sends money and hopes her son will resume the studies for which the family has sacrificed. Her love does not give her access to the truth. She interprets fragments of his behavior in ways that allow her to hope; he conceals information to protect her or to avoid her judgment. The eventual distance between them is one of the costs of his violence.

These women are not simply embodiments of faith, pride, or maternal affection. Dunya makes a consequential refusal. Sonya challenges Raskolnikov to confess. Katerina cares for children while damaged by circumstances and her own errors. Pulcheria acts through letters and economic support, despite limited knowledge. Their decisions shape the plot and have consequences independently of what the central man thinks of them.

Compare the bargains proposed by Luzhin and Svidrigailov with Raskolnikov's theory. The details differ, but each man is tempted to treat another person's freedom as subordinate to his own purposes. Luzhin seeks gratitude as obedience; Svidrigailov wants desire to create entitlement; Raskolnikov imagines historical greatness as permission to kill. In each case, the person subjected to the claim is the crucial moral reality that the theory or bargain tries to erase.

Dostoevsky's representation also has limitations we can discuss. Sonya's suffering can become idealized as redemptive, and the women do not have equal narrative access to their inner lives. A critical reader can appreciate how the novel exposes exploitation while asking whether its religious and gender assumptions restrict what alternative futures it imagines for those characters. Understanding a literary work includes recognizing the questions its form makes difficult to ask.
