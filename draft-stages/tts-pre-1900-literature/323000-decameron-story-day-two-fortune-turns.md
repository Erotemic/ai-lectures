# The Decameron — Day Two Begins, Fortune Turns

Morning comes to the country retreat. The members of the company rise, walk through the gardens, eat, and enjoy the ordered pleasures that now mark their days. The beauty of the setting is no accident. Boccaccio places this calm against the memory of Florence, where the plague had interrupted the most ordinary acts of trust. These ten people cannot remake the city by telling stories. They can create, temporarily, a household in which a meal follows work, an invitation can be accepted, and one person's speech receives another's attention.

The wreath has passed from Pampinea to Filomena, the new queen. Instead of leaving the subject entirely free, she asks for stories about people who are beset by dangers and misfortunes but unexpectedly reach a happier outcome. The theme is Fortune: the instability of possessions, health, rank, travel, and desire, and the possibility that a change which looks disastrous might lead somewhere no human plan could have reached. The day's tales will move through roads and seas, palaces, markets, burial chambers, exiles, shipwrecks, and changing political regimes.

It would be a mistake to suppose that this theme makes every person happy in the same way. One traveler escapes a robber; another becomes rich by piracy before losing everything again. Some men and women survive because a stranger gives shelter. Others are brought into a wealthy marriage by coincidences so dramatic that the storyteller's hand is nearly visible. A reunion that the tale names fortunate may depend on a character's humiliation or lost freedom. The ending cannot erase every injury suffered along the way.

This is also the point at which the group's internal rules become more distinct. Dioneo is permitted to speak last and to take greater liberty with the subject assigned to the others. That privilege is a formal piece of playfulness: even a carefully governed company finds room for a voice that resists its pattern. Each other narrator must work within the queen's theme, though the ways they understand it differ. The story that feels most like an adventure may be the one that most clearly shows Fortune mocking the confidence of merchants and kings.

We have already heard that clever words can rescue a person from danger. Now we will hear how little cleverness sometimes matters when a ship breaks apart, a ruler dies, or a rumor reaches the wrong ears. But we will also find people who act resourcefully amid uncertainty. The distinction between chance and choice will become less tidy the more we listen. Neifile begins in a city excited by miracles, with a performer who thinks he can deceive a crowd and discover that the crowd is much more dangerous than he imagined.
