# The Decameron — Bartolomea and the Pirate

Dioneo's concluding story returns to marriage, but with a sharper and more openly bawdy voice than the other narrators have used. Ricciardo di Chinzica is a prosperous older judge who marries a much younger woman, Bartolomea. He has made a socially advantageous match. He also finds reasons to avoid intimacy with his wife. A great many calendar days, he tells her, are reserved for religious observance or some other prohibition. His legal learning supplies restrictions when she seeks affection.

During a trip along the coast, Bartolomea is seized by a pirate, Paganino da Monaco. The abduction is an act of force, even though the tale proceeds to shape their subsequent relationship as one of mutual sexual pleasure. Ricciardo discovers where she has been taken and travels to seek her return. Paganino receives him and agrees to restore Bartolomea if she wishes to go. The judge may have expected his position as husband, and perhaps his status as an educated and wealthy man, to make her decision automatic.

Bartolomea refuses. She describes a marriage in which her desires were routinely postponed behind a calendar of exemptions, and contrasts it with the attention she receives in Paganino's household. She sees no reason to return to a husband who made a solemn legal bond but neglected its emotional and physical substance. Ricciardo can argue, bargain, and invoke the language of duty, but he cannot persuade her to leave. He goes home alone, dies not long afterward, and Bartolomea eventually marries Paganino.

The final tale of the day is called fortunate from the woman's point of view, at least as its narrator chooses to present it. Yet the plot begins with a kidnapping. That fact should not vanish merely because later scenes give Bartolomea a choice and pleasure. The story's bawdy humor depends on a medieval literary convention that makes sexual satisfaction a way of judging a marriage. Its comic justice falls on the husband who has taken the benefits of marriage without attending to his wife's needs. It does not offer a careful account of the power relations through which the pirate acquired his partner.

Dioneo is deliberately testing the boundaries of the company's assigned theme. The other narrators have given us shipwrecks, exile, threatened death, lost children, and identity concealed or recovered. Here the storyteller makes the unhappy turn appear as a rescue from a lawful marriage. What counts as good fortune depends on whose life is being described. Ricciardo believes he is the injured party. Bartolomea describes herself as the one who had been deprived. Paganino profits from violence which the narrator's ending does not judge with equal severity.

The tale closes Day Two with a provocation rather than a settled moral. The book's orderly company has granted Dioneo the right to speak from an angle that makes their other stories newly arguable. One man's restoration may be another woman's captivity; a judge's legal claim may coexist with his failure of care; a satisfying comic ending may rest upon acts that remain troubling. The question of Fortune has grown complicated. It can rescue the endangered, but its gifts are not distributed according to virtue, and storytellers do not agree on how to decide who has been lucky.
