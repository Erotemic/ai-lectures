# Journey to the West: Zhu Bajie joins the pilgrimage and the Heart Sutra warning

## Journey to the West — The Nine-Toothed Rake and Marshal Tianpeng

The fleeing figure reached Mount Fuling as a red glow in the darkness. Wukong followed close enough to see the light collect at a cave mouth. The pig demon changed back to his own shape, darted inside, and emerged carrying an immense rake. It was not a peasant's tool borrowed from a stable. Its nine teeth gleamed with worked metal, its shaft bore signs of extraordinary craftsmanship, and it was held by someone who had plainly been trained to fight.

Wukong halted in the air with his staff. A moment earlier the creature had been trying to slip away from a woman's bedroom. Now he stood before a mountain cavern as a warrior, braced to meet another warrior. Wukong called down for his name and origin. How did he know the Great Sage's reputation? Where had he obtained a weapon such as that? A pig's snout was not an explanation for a fighter's ability.

The demon answered with a long account of himself. At first, he said, he had lived lazily. He had not understood what happened to creatures who wasted their lives and came at last to the courts of death. Then an immortal had instructed him in cultivation. He learned to regulate breath and bodily forces, to bring together elements that appeared opposed, to prepare the elixir that promised transformation. His teacher guided him through the demanding practice until he no longer belonged simply to the world below.

When his labor was complete, clouds bore him to Heaven. The immortals were assembled there. He received an appointment that gave him responsibility for the heavenly river and its forces. His title was Marshal Tianpeng, the heavenly marshal. He had commanded celestial troops and carried a weapon fit for his rank. He was not, as Gao's neighbors supposed, merely a wild pig that had stumbled out of the woods.

Wukong knew what it meant to receive a title from Heaven. He also knew that offices did not make their holders virtuous. The pig demon was not finished with his account.

At a peach celebration he had become drunk. He went into the palace associated with the moon and seized at Chang'e, a celestial woman who did not want him. She refused repeatedly. He tried to force her to remain with him. The disturbance reached the heavenly authorities, and the officer who had once commanded troops stood accused of assault and disorder.

The punishment decreed for him was death. Taibai, the Gold Star official who had intervened before in the affairs of Heaven, spoke on his behalf. The sentence was reduced, but not made light: he received two thousand blows and was cast down from the celestial world. In the course of his descent and rebirth he entered the wrong womb. Instead of returning as a human, he became a creature with the body and features of a pig.

Thus, he declared, the former marshal had come to the earth as Zhu Ganglie. He had known a splendid office, made his offense, been punished, and found himself reduced to a household servant and unwanted husband. His past could explain his skill. It could not undo what he had done to Chang'e, and it did not give him the right to imprison Cuilan.

The story still mattered to Wukong. Here stood someone whose life had crossed some of the same thresholds as his own: the grandeur of Heaven, the humiliation of official judgment, an exile from the place where divine titles were distributed. But Wukong had not been banished for the same offense. He had challenged heavenly authority and been imprisoned under a mountain. The marshal had used his rank and strength against someone who refused him. They could recognize one another's fame without pretending their histories were identical.

The pig demon lifted his rake and laughed with bitterness. He remembered the monkey from the heavenly disturbances. Wukong had once been given the lowly post of keeper of horses, and he had made such trouble that many other officers had suffered the consequences. What right had this former horse keeper to invade another's home, interfere in a marriage, and call himself a righteous servant of religion?

Wukong answered with his staff. To him, the important point was no longer whether they had stood in the same heavenly court. The creature had locked away a woman and made her family afraid. He had boasted of his ability and fled when confronted. If he wanted to argue about ancient titles, he could do it while defending himself.

The rake flashed toward him. Wukong met it with iron. The collision rang above the cave, and the night filled with a fight between two figures who had once received honors in Heaven and now stood outside a remote mountain dwelling, with very little honor between them.

As sparks flew from staff and rake, the pig demon called Wukong a destroyer of families. Wukong answered that it was the imprisoning of Cuilan that had destroyed this one. Neither fighter yielded the argument. Each was sure the other was the intruder. They went on striking, rising and descending through the dark, until the boundary between insult and combat could hardly be found.

## Journey to the West — A Night of Fighting and a Morning at Gao Village

The two exiles fought long after the households below had gone to sleep. Wukong's staff moved with the confidence of someone who had faced a heavenly army. Zhu Ganglie's rake was not inferior in workmanship, and its wielder had once commanded water troops. The weapons met above trees and rocks, then came down again in rushes of sparks. The monkey had the advantage of speed; the pig demon kept turning the rake to hold him back.

They traded accusations as readily as blows. Zhu Ganglie said that wrecking a marriage was a great wrong. Wukong said that no household was safe while a strong man could imprison a daughter and command her as property. The words did not settle the fight. They gave it heat.

Through the middle of the night the duel continued. At last the eastern horizon began to pale. Zhu Ganglie's arms grew heavy. He could still fight, but the strength needed to match Wukong's unflagging assaults was leaving him. He changed into a violent wind and swept toward the cave, shutting himself inside before the monkey could follow. The entrance bore the name Cloud Pathway Cave. Wukong stood outside, staff in hand, calling for the defender to emerge.

No answer came. He could have begun breaking the doors at once, but daylight had arrived, and Xuanzang had been waiting at Gao Village since the previous evening. The monk knew only that his disciple had set out to confront a demon and had not returned. Wukong decided to report before carrying the fight further. He sprang onto his cloud and crossed the distance in a moment.

Back at Gao's house, the elder men who had been invited to keep Xuanzang company had passed a sleepless night. They had eaten, talked about old events and current troubles, watched the lamps burn lower, and looked toward the courtyard every time there was a sound. Xuanzang had kept his place among them. He had been through too many separations from Wukong to assume that every delay meant disaster, but neither could he know the outcome until his disciple returned.

Wukong dropped into the courtyard and called to his master. The squire and the relatives hurried out, grateful and anxious. Where was the monster? Had Wukong killed him? Would Gao's family be free of him?

Wukong told them that the creature was no ordinary wild demon. He had been Marshal Tianpeng, an official of Heaven, and had come down into a pig's body through punishment and mistaken rebirth. Wukong described the nine-toothed rake, the night-long battle, and the retreat to a cave whose doors were now shut. He could return and break them open, he said, but had come first because his master might be worried.

The old squire fell to his knees. He was not satisfied with the creature's departure for a single night. He had already seen what happened when exorcists failed and went away. The monster might simply return after the pilgrim's party moved on. Gao offered a reward of money, land, and a portion of the property if Wukong would make the removal permanent. The family wanted no bargain that left its daughter vulnerable to being locked away again.

Wukong did not immediately accept. He turned the conversation in a direction that startled the old man. Was the son-in-law really as useless as the household now said? He had worked the fields, repaired the buildings, cultivated the land, and contributed to the family's prosperity. His food had not come without labor. The squire was scandalized to hear his hired rescuer defend the intruder.

The argument was not quite serious. Wukong delighted in upsetting people by taking a claim and turning it around. But the objection also exposed something complicated about the family's account. Gao had praised the son-in-law when he needed his labor and wanted him gone when he became socially embarrassing. None of that justified the confinement of Cuilan. The daughter who had been shut up behind copper was the person the agreement had most grievously failed to protect. Yet the squire spoke again and again about the family's reputation, the shame of being known for a demon son-in-law.

Gao was adamant. People would say that his house had married into monsters. The creature's work could not make that story bearable. He would not risk another night in which his daughter disappeared into the rear rooms.

Xuanzang put a stop to the debate. Wukong had already fought the demon. The task should now be brought to a proper conclusion. If they left without settling the matter, they would have accepted shelter and hospitality while leaving the village to the same fear that had greeted them. The monk did not wish to linger in a household quarrel, but neither did he wish to abandon it halfway.

Wukong laughed and said he had only been teasing the squire. He would make an end of the business. Gao should keep his master well provided for another short while. If Wukong could not bring the creature back, the old man would at least know that this was not another empty promise bought with silver.

He put the staff behind his ear, crossed the courtyard, and rose again into the sky. The monkey knew the cave, the creature's name, and the quality of his weapon. He also knew that the demon was tired and had no good reason to come out to meet him on equal terms. The next encounter would begin at the cave doors, not in Cuilan's bedroom.

At Mount Fuling the sun had risen above Cloud Pathway Cave. Wukong approached the entrance with every intention of making its occupant answer. The first battle had lasted the night and ended in a retreat. The second would not be decided until the pig-faced warrior heard a name he had been waiting years to hear.

## Journey to the West — The Cave, the Iron Head, and the Vow to Travel West

When Wukong returned to Cloud Pathway Cave, there was no answering shout from within. The former marshal had shut himself behind the door, hoping that the enemy from the night would grow bored or be called away. Wukong had returned precisely because he was not inclined to leave the matter unsettled. He struck the entrance and demanded that the inhabitant come out.

Zhu Ganglie heard the challenge. There was little point pretending he was not at home. He brought his nine-toothed rake, came out to face the monkey, and launched into a description of the weapon's extraordinary origin. It had been forged from marvelous metal, worked by high divine artisans, fitted with symbols of the heavens and the seasons, and bestowed upon him as an officer's emblem. He had carried it in the palaces of Heaven and down into the world below. Its teeth could tear into mountains and overturn creatures of the water. He had not picked it up from a farmyard.

Wukong listened with an amused expression. The rake was magnificent, but he had heard enough speeches about weapons to know that speeches were not the same as blows. When Zhu finished promising to crush his enemy's head, Wukong lowered his staff and offered a demonstration.

He stood still, bent forward, and presented his skull. Strike here, he said. Strike as hard as you liked. If the rake really possessed the advertised power, it should make short work of one monkey's head.

Zhu Ganglie did not refuse the invitation. He swung with all his strength. The metal teeth struck Wukong's head and sent out a shower of sparks. The monkey did not move. Zhu stared at the spot he had hit. The weapon had not pierced skin or even raised a bruise.

Wukong explained what the celestial officers had tried during his rebellion. Axes, swords, fire, lightning, and heavy blows had all failed to break him. Laozi's furnace had been no better at killing him. The fire had hurt and altered his eyes, but his head had endured. A nine-toothed rake was impressive in the hands of an ordinary opponent. Against that head it was a tool for making noise.

Now Zhu wanted to know why the famous rebel, last heard of centuries before, had appeared at the home of his father-in-law. Had Old Gao sent messengers all the way to Flower-Fruit Mountain? Had the monkey become a professional destroyer of marriages?

No, Wukong answered. He had left his old life and was now protecting a Buddhist monk, a servant of the Tang ruler named Xuanzang or Tripitaka. This monk was traveling west to seek scriptures from the Buddha. He had stopped at Gao Village only for shelter. When the family's trouble came to light, Wukong had agreed to deal with it.

The change in Zhu was immediate. The rake dropped from his hands. He ceased arguing about his rights as a son-in-law. Instead he bowed and asked where the scripture-seeking monk was. Would Wukong take him to meet the traveler?

The monkey was suspicious. This was the same creature who had threatened him with a rake a moment before. Why should hearing about the scriptures change everything? Zhu explained that Guanyin had appeared to him years earlier, instructed him to repent, and given him Buddhist precepts. He was to wait for a pilgrim coming from the east and accompany that pilgrim to the western land as a means of making amends for his wrongdoing. The waiting had gone on so long that he had almost ceased to expect the traveler. He had lived on vegetarian food and continued to remain at Gao Village rather than take the road himself.

Wukong remembered Guanyin's journey east to recruit helpers. She had spoken to the dragon who became Xuanzang's horse, and to other beings whose offenses had brought them out of favor. This pig-faced ex-marshal had evidently been one of those recruited. But a story about receiving vows was easy to tell. Wukong wanted proof that the creature meant to honor them.

Zhu fell to his knees and swore by the powers of Heaven that he sincerely intended to serve the scripture pilgrim. If he was lying, he called down terrible punishment on himself. Wukong studied him for a moment, then set a harder condition. A man who meant to leave his former life should no longer keep a demon's cave as his retreat. Burn it, Wukong said. Do it now, so there is no convenient refuge to which you can return once the road becomes difficult.

Zhu gathered brushwood and reeds and set the cave alight. The fire spread through the entrance and its chambers, ruining the dwelling that had concealed him after his flight from Heaven. Flames leaped where the giant rake had stood only moments before. When Zhu turned to Wukong, he declared that he had nothing left there to detain him.

Wukong accepted the offer, but not the promise of complete trust. He asked Zhu to hand over the rake. The former marshal obeyed. Then Wukong plucked out a hair, breathed upon it, and changed it into a sturdy rope. He bound the newcomer's arms behind his back, took hold of one large ear, and began hauling him toward Gao Village.

Zhu complained about the grip. His ears were not handles, and Wukong was being needlessly rough. Wukong answered that an obedient creature had nothing to fear from being brought before the master he claimed to seek. They could discuss the niceties of welcome after Xuanzang heard the whole story.

Together they rose through cloud and haze. Wukong carried the rake, tugging the huge ears whenever Zhu slowed. The two creatures who had fought through the night were returning side by side, but the difference between them was still sharply drawn. One had already entered Xuanzang's service and wanted the new recruit tested. The other had at last named the mission he was supposed to have been awaiting.

At the Gao household a family was still wondering whether the demon would come back and claim his place. The answer came from the sky, with his hands tied and his former rival gripping his ear.

## Journey to the West — Zhu Wuneng, Zhu Bajie, and the Road Out of Gao Village

A movement in the courtyard brought the assembled Gao relatives to their feet. Wukong descended from the air with a bound creature in tow. Even from a distance nobody could mistake the face: the long muzzle, large ears, and solid body belonged to the son-in-law who had frightened the household for years. But his arms were tied behind him, his rake was in the monkey's possession, and he was walking where Wukong directed him.

Squire Gao cried out that the man had been caught at last. The relatives crowded nearer. Xuanzang remained seated, waiting to hear why his disciple had brought the creature back alive.

Wukong hauled Zhu to the front of the hall and demanded that he recognize the monk sitting there. Zhu immediately knelt and bowed. If he had known the scripture pilgrim was staying with his father-in-law, he declared, he would have greeted the monk himself and saved everyone a great deal of trouble.

Xuanzang asked what this meant. Wukong nudged the prisoner with the handle of the rake and told him to explain. Zhu repeated his account of Guanyin's instruction: he had received precepts, promised to follow the eastward pilgrim when he passed, and intended to seek forgiveness through service on the road. The monk listened without interruption.

When the story was finished, Xuanzang asked for incense. An altar table was brought out. He washed his hands, lit the offering, and bowed southward in gratitude to Guanyin for having prepared another helper. Wukong unbound the former marshal. Zhu bowed again, this time freely, and declared his willingness to travel west. He also bowed to Wukong as the senior disciple, the one who had entered their master's service first. The monkey, delighted by the advantage, accepted his new younger brother with an air of great authority.

Xuanzang proposed to give the newcomer a religious name. Zhu replied that Guanyin had already named him Zhu Wuneng. The name went well beside Sun Wukong: one suggested awakening to ability, the other awakening to emptiness. Both men were now expected to put their powers to a purpose greater than appetite or pride.

That expectation was immediately tested. Zhu, hearing that his new master had accepted him, suggested that the occasion might call for a feast. He had kept to vegetarian food for the sake of Guanyin's precepts, but perhaps his vows could now be relaxed? Xuanzang refused. If he had undertaken to avoid forbidden food, he would continue to do so. The monk then gave him an additional familiar name, Bajie, the one who observes the eight precepts. Zhu accepted it cheerfully enough, though his desire for food had plainly survived his conversion.

Old Gao saw the danger depart in a new form: the demon was becoming a pilgrim. The household arranged a feast of vegetarian dishes. Squire Gao produced wine made without meat, but Xuanzang declined because of his monastic observance. Wukong and Bajie protested that they had not renounced every kind of drink. The master permitted them a limited amount, warning that no drunken misbehavior could be tolerated on the road. It was an awkward beginning for a family whose second pupil had once lost a heavenly office through drunken misconduct.

After the meal the squire tried to repay the travelers. He brought a red lacquer tray bearing silver and gold, along with clothing for their journey. Xuanzang would take neither money nor fine garments. He was a pilgrim who sought food at settlements and trusted to the road. To accept a fortune for removing a demon was not the kind of exchange he had undertaken. Leftover bread and fruit would be enough provision until the next village.

Wukong saw Gao Cai, the servant who had been seized on the road, and took a handful of coins from the tray to reward him. The fellow had been hunting for a specialist when the specialist had captured him. Wukong joked that if he found more demons requiring attention, he should continue to bring them his way. Gao Cai, who had spent weeks suffering reprimands for his failures, received the gift with enormous relief.

Bajie had his own claim. He had worked in the household for three years, he reminded his father-in-law. If the others did not want anything, surely he had earned food or clothing in place of wages. Wukong had torn his outfit during the ambush, and his shoes needed replacing. Gao supplied a garment and a new pair of shoes. The great pig-faced pilgrim put them on and looked pleased with his departure arrangements.

Then he requested that Cuilan be brought out to make her farewells to the monks and family. Wukong cut him short. A man entering a Buddhist household, he said, could not keep talking about having a wife as though he were still a resident son-in-law. The marriage and the obligations of the Gao household were not arrangements he could carry west under a robe.

Bajie nevertheless asked his father-in-law to treat Cuilan well. If the scripture journey failed, he mused, perhaps he could come back and resume his former place. Wukong rebuked him loudly. It was a remark made in jest or wishful self-protection, but it was not reassuring in light of the way Cuilan had been kept. Her own words do not accompany the departure in this chapter. The company settles the problem by taking the dangerous son-in-law away, not by asking her to publicly forgive him.

Xuanzang brought the conversation to an end. There was a long road ahead and enough daylight left to begin it. They packed a load of provisions and baggage. Bajie took the carrying pole onto his broad shoulders. Xuanzang mounted the white dragon horse. Wukong went in front with his staff.

The three disciples' household had become a larger company, though only two of them walked as men beside the horse. There was the human master, who could direct them but could not defend himself from many of the dangers ahead; the monkey, who could fight almost anything and obeyed with difficulty; the white horse, carrying the monk as a consequence of his own wrongdoing; and now the pig, possessing strength, skill, an enormous appetite, and vows he had only just begun to practice.

They left Gao Village behind. Old Gao and his relatives watched the burdened newcomer walk away beneath the baggage. The family had their daughter back within the household. Whether that would give her peace was not said. For the pilgrims, the new companion was another chance for a powerful offender to make himself useful through a long and uncertain service.

A month later, after roads and villages had carried them beyond the borders of the district, the three walkers and the horse approached a mountain where Bajie knew a teacher. There, Xuanzang would receive a text more lasting than the food or money offered at Gao Village.

## Journey to the West — Crow-Nest Chan Master and the Heart Sutra

For a month after leaving Gao Village, the party traveled without a great disaster. The road still demanded effort. Xuanzang rode slowly enough for the load-bearing Bajie to keep up. Wukong moved ahead and back, watching the mountain paths, testing the distance to the next settlement, and teasing his new companion whenever the burden made him complain. The white horse carried its master patiently. They were beginning to look like a group that might remain together, though no one could yet say how they would behave under a serious threat.

A high mountain appeared ahead. Xuanzang drew the horse to a halt and warned the disciples to watch carefully. A wooded hill was not necessarily a safe hill, and the journey had already proved that a monastery could be dangerous and a familiar-looking husband could be a monster.

Bajie knew the place. It was Pagoda Mountain, he said, and a Chan master called Crow-Nest lived there. The monk asked what sort of teacher he was. Bajie answered that the man possessed religious attainment and had once invited him to become a disciple. Bajie had declined. It was difficult not to hear in this admission another example of a good turn from which the former marshal had shied away. But he spoke respectfully of the teacher, and Xuanzang wanted to meet him.

They climbed through a mountain of pines and cypresses, flowering trees, clear water, birds, and deer. There were no bustling markets or crowded temple courts. A nestlike structure of branches and grass had been built up near the boughs of a fragrant tree. Birds gathered around it, and animals moved nearby without alarm. The man who lived there was at home among the trees rather than within walls of brick and wood.

When Crow-Nest saw the travelers approaching, he came down from his dwelling. Xuanzang dismounted and bowed. The master greeted him courteously. Then he recognized Bajie.

The former Marshal Tianpeng, once called Zhu Ganglie, had indeed returned in a different company. Crow-Nest was pleased that Guanyin's direction had finally led him to the scripture pilgrim. Bajie replied that he had accepted the bodhisattva's advice and meant to accompany the monk west. Here, for once, the creature whose history was so often disclosed as a cause for alarm was being welcomed as a penitent.

Crow-Nest looked at Wukong and asked who he was. Wukong laughed at the question. Why did this old Chan master recognize the pig and not the monkey? The answer was polite and unhelpful: the two had not been sufficiently acquainted. Xuanzang introduced his senior disciple by name. The exchange was small, but Wukong did not forget that the teacher seemed to know more than he chose to say.

Xuanzang had not climbed the mountain only for courtesies. He asked how far they still stood from the Great Thunderclap Monastery, where the Buddha's scriptures awaited. Crow-Nest gave him little comfort. The distance was very great. Tigers and leopards were plentiful; the roads were hard. The destination could be reached eventually, but the pilgrim should not expect the obstructions of delusion and supernatural danger to disappear simply because his intention was sincere.

Then the teacher offered something he could carry without adding weight to Bajie's pack. He knew the Heart Sutra, a short teaching on the perfection of wisdom. Xuanzang knelt and asked to hear it. Crow-Nest began to recite. The mountain air was quiet enough for the words to be heard without effort.

He spoke of Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva who, practicing deep wisdom, sees the five aggregates as empty and passes beyond suffering. He addressed Sariputra: form is not separate from emptiness, nor emptiness from form. Form itself is emptiness; emptiness itself is form. So it is with feeling, perception, the movements of the mind, and consciousness. The things of the world, understood through emptiness, are neither simply born nor simply destroyed, neither stained nor purified, neither increased nor diminished.

In emptiness there is no fixed form to possess, no fixed feeling or perception, no eye or ear, no nose or tongue, no body or mind to grasp as a permanent self; no sights, sounds, odors, tastes, touches, or ideas that stand alone. There is no ignorance set apart from its ending, and no old age and death set apart from their ending. There is no suffering, no cessation, no path to claim as one's own, no wisdom that can be kept in a box, and no attainment to hold like a prize.

Because there is nothing to grasp in that way, the bodhisattva relies on perfect wisdom without obstruction of mind. Without obstruction, there is no fear. Deluded dreams lose their power. The Buddhas of past, present, and future awaken through this wisdom. Crow-Nest ended with the mantra of going beyond, going beyond again, and arriving together at awakening.

It was not a chant about which mountain pass to use or how much food to pack. Its words placed the journey inside a different scale of effort. Xuanzang had set out to obtain sacred writings in the west. Here, a teacher he had never met spoke a teaching into the open air, and the monk listened as one who might need every syllable. The novel says that Xuanzang, possessing deep religious capacity, remembered the scripture after hearing it once.

Xuanzang had set out in search of written scriptures. Here, unexpectedly, a teacher in a bird's nest gave him a teaching spoken into the open mountain air. He could not carry a library out of that encounter, but he could carry remembered words. A road filled with things to fear would offer repeated occasions to discover whether those words could change the way he met fear.

Crow-Nest prepared to return to his nest. Xuanzang stopped him and asked again for a more definite description of the way west. How long? Which paths? How would he know where to turn? The teacher responded not with a map but with a series of verses about the dangers ahead.

There would be cliffs that seemed to reach the sky and narrow passes where feet must be set carefully. Woods would hold cunning creatures; cities and palaces would contain monsters dressed as officials. Wolves might serve as magistrates, tigers sit in judgment, lions and elephants wield royal authority. These were not routes that could be made safe merely by recognizing the word mountain on a sign.

Then the verse changed its tone. A wild pig would carry the baggage. A water monster was still waiting along the way. An old stone monkey would have no cause to be angry. If the travelers wished to know the road, they could ask the one who was already with them.

Wukong heard enough. He understood the mention of the old monkey as a jab, and the line about the pig struck him as mockery of his new brother. He laughed sharply and told Xuanzang that there was no need to consult a stranger for directions when the Great Sage himself was present. Crow-Nest rose as golden light toward his dwelling. Wukong sprang up beneath it and swung his staff at the nest.

He could hit mountains and break stone gates. He could not reach this one small shelter. Lotus blossoms seemed to multiply around it, and mist veiled the branches. Again and again the staff struck at a place that refused to be injured. Xuanzang called for him to stop. The teacher had given a sacred text and spoken of the road. Why assault him?

Wukong complained that the old master had insulted them. Xuanzang had not understood the verse in that spirit. Bajie, who was generally quick to bristle when mocked, spoke more calmly this time. Crow-Nest appeared to know something of the future. The reference to a water monster might be worth remembering. They should let him alone.

Wukong lowered the staff. If a teacher who lived among branches wished to stay out of reach, there was nothing useful to be gained from striking mist. He returned to the road with the others.

Xuanzang rode on with the Heart Sutra held in memory. Bajie carried the bags and a new title that asked more of him than his old one had. Wukong led them away from the nest, still irritated by the teacher's knowing smile. Farther west lay the river monster that Crow-Nest had mentioned. None of the pilgrims had yet met him, but the story had already given a place to his coming.

They descended the mountain and continued toward the unknown. The second disciple had joined; the teaching had been received. The party was stronger than when Xuanzang had first crossed the border, and still very far from the scriptures he sought.
