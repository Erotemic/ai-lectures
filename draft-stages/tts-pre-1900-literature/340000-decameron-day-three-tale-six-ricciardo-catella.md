# The Decameron — Ricciardo, Catella, and the Bathhouse

In Naples, Ricciardo Minutolo desires a married woman called Catella. She loves her husband, Filippello, intensely and rejects Ricciardo's approaches. The important fact is not merely that she is married. She has made her own refusal plain. Ricciardo learns that she is jealous of her husband, and he decides to use that vulnerability instead of respecting the answer he has received.

First he makes a show of transferring his attention to another woman. People begin to believe he has abandoned his pursuit, and Catella allows him into ordinary social company again. At a gathering near the sea, he hints that Filippello has arranged an affair with Ricciardo's own wife. Catella presses him for details. Ricciardo pretends to be reluctant, exacts a promise of secrecy, and then tells her that the lovers will meet in a particular bathhouse the following day. He offers a plan by which she can catch them without being seen.

Catella's jealousy supplies the urgency he needs. She goes to the indicated place, where the rooms are darkened and her visitor is expected. Ricciardo has arranged to occupy the position she thinks belongs to Filippello. She enters believing she will confront or expose her husband and, in the darkness, encounters Ricciardo instead. Only later does she learn whom she has been with. The trick has transformed her mistrust of Filippello into an opportunity for someone who already knows she does not want him.

Fiammetta tells the deception with the elaborate timing of a comedy: rumors planted in public, a whispered confidence, a concealed room, and the revelation after the trap has worked. The narrator's frame treats Ricciardo's success as the attainment of a difficult desire. Catella is distressed, and Ricciardo attempts to manage the consequences by controlling what she can say about the incident. The story's conventions encourage an eventual accommodation, but the path to that accommodation has been arranged without her informed choice.

It is worth stating the central difference without euphemism. Catella's consent, if the scene is described in those terms, was based on a false belief about the man's identity. Ricciardo secured access by impersonation and deception. Her earlier refusal remains a refusal. A reader can observe Boccaccio's narrative technique without approving the act that the technique makes successful.

The story also shows why jealousy is such a powerful engine of intrigue. A person preoccupied with another's possible betrayal may neglect evidence about the supposedly helpful informant. Ricciardo provides Catella with a story that confirms her fear and a route to act on it. The more intensely she wants to know what her husband has done, the less she asks why Ricciardo has brought the knowledge to her.

Compare this with the woman who used a friar as an unwitting messenger. In that case the initiator manipulated an institution to contact a desired partner. Here a man manipulates a woman's fear to overcome a stated refusal. Both plots celebrate ingenuity at the level of construction. They are not ethically interchangeable. The difference is important precisely because Neifile's theme groups them together under the attractive language of obtaining what one desires.
