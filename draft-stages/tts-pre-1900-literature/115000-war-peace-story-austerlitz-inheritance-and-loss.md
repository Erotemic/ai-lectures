# War and Peace — Story: Austerlitz, Inheritance, and Irreversible Loss

In the campaign of 1805, Russian troops are fighting alongside Austria against Napoleon. Tolstoy repeatedly brings us close to men who do not know what is happening beyond a hill or a line of trees. Reports arrive late. Units move for reasons their members cannot see. Kutuzov, the Russian commander, is often more aware of the limits of command than the younger men who want everything to be decided by a brilliant stroke. Prince Andrei, however, still imagines that greatness might take the form of a single heroic deed witnessed by history.

Nikolai Rostov's first encounters with danger make a mess of his picture of gallantry. Fear is a physical experience, not a sentiment fit for a story told at dinner. The soldier he thinks he should be and the soldier whose horse carries him away from danger do not perfectly coincide. He admires his regiment and loves the emperor with a sincere fervor. Tolstoy gives that feeling its force while showing the distance between a sovereign's image and what a wounded man can actually see.

At Austerlitz, the allied armies enter battle with plans that confidently describe the enemy's future conduct. The plans break down. Andrei seizes a standard and rushes forward, achieving for a moment the image he has long desired. Then he is wounded. Lying on the ground, he looks up into the immense, indifferent sky. The clouded heavens make the heroic scale of his former ambition feel small. Napoleon comes near him, but the conqueror who once seemed almost superhuman no longer fills the horizon of his thought. This is not a tidy conversion to a settled creed; it is the abrupt collapse of a fantasy.

Back in Russia, Pierre's transformation into a rich count places him in a different predicament. Prince Vasili and his circle lead him toward marriage with Helene. She is magnificent to look at, but Pierre does not know whether he loves her, and the pressure to make the expected declaration substitutes for his own decision. Soon their marriage is filled with suspicion and embarrassment. Helene's rumored intimacy with the officer Dolokhov becomes a public insult. Pierre challenges Dolokhov to a duel, although he has no real experience of the code he invokes. When he wounds his opponent, he is horrified by what he has done. Even a man who considers himself humane can be brought to violence by pride and social convention.

Andrei returns home on the very night his wife gives birth. Lise dies in childbirth. The new baby, Nikolai, will survive, but Andrei cannot repair what he withheld from Lise while she was alive. Her expression seems to accuse him of a wrong he can no longer answer. He does not emerge simply ennobled by loss. He withdraws, works on his estate, and tells himself that ambitious public life is over. His old father continues to rule the family. Marya must comfort and mediate, as she has always done, now under the pressure of a death.

The Rostovs encounter more ordinary dangers: expense, gambling, the manipulation of honor. Dolokhov, the survivor of Pierre's duel, courts Sonya and is refused. He then wins a ruinous amount of money from Nikolai at cards. Nikolai's humiliation is not the grand shame of a failed battle, but it threatens the household just as surely. He returns to a home where the singing of Natasha briefly makes life seem worth living beyond its debts and self-reproaches. The moment is powerful precisely because music does not cancel the debt. It gives him something by which to measure it.

Watch the different kinds of damage across these stories. A duel makes Pierre confront the violence hidden in manners. A battle makes Andrei see how little glory can justify. An unlucky card game exposes the Rostovs' fragile resources. A death in childbirth reveals the cost of Andrei's emotional distance. Tolstoy does not arrange these as moral lessons followed by compliant students. Pierre will make more mistakes; Andrei will become proud again; Nikolai will remain pulled between impulse and obligation.

The narrative also begins making a claim about what a historical novel can know. The famous battlefield is not presented as an omniscient diagram in which every movement had one visible cause. It is rendered through misunderstanding, bodies, delays, rumors, and extraordinary moments of perception. The intimate scenes work the same way. The words spoken at Pierre's wedding and the look on Lise's dead face are no less consequential than the decisions at headquarters. Tolstoy's book is too large to belong to one hero because the world it represents cannot be held in one person's mind.
