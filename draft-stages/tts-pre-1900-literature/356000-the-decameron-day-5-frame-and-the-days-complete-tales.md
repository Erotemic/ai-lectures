# The Decameron · Day 5: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Day Five: Fiammetta and Love After Misfortune

Morning comes to Boccaccio's country house after a day devoted to unhappy love. Ten young Florentines are far from a city damaged by plague. There is food, music, a garden, and a fountain. Their place of refuge is not immune to chance, but it gives them something the plague-stricken city scarcely allows: the power to arrange a day and choose what sort of stories will fill it. Fiammetta, now queen of the company, decides that after yesterday's deaths and losses, she wants another direction.

Her subject is lovers who pass through severe trials and nonetheless reach happiness. That sounds like an order for consolation. But it does not mean that every difficulty will be reasonable, or that every happy ending will distribute happiness evenly. The distinction becomes apparent almost as soon as Panfilo begins. His hero Cimon acquires refinement by falling in love, then carries the woman he loves away by force. Learning and desire are treated as achievements, while the desired woman's own intentions are in danger of disappearing from the account.

Fiammetta first leads her companions across open country in the cool morning air. The sun rises; they return to their villa, share refreshments, and amuse themselves until the meal. A siesta separates the heat of noon from the afternoon gathering. Only then does the company assemble at the fountain for storytelling. This daily arrangement—walking, food, rest, narration—has become their answer to broken public routines. The tales are emphatically not reports of happenings at the villa. They are fictional performances by individual members of the group, chosen under a temporary rule.

The trials awaiting the day's fictional lovers are various. Gostanza risks a voyage after believing Martuccio dead; Pietro and Agnolella are separated by armed men and lost in a forest. A young couple's midnight meeting ends in a household negotiation disguised by the joke of a nightingale. Other stories turn on disputed parentage, abduction by sea raiders, the exposure of a secret pregnancy, and the chance arrival of a father at the very moment his son is being led toward execution.

Two tales near the end deserve sustained attention. Nastagio sees a terrifying supernatural punishment, then uses it as a spectacle to frighten a woman into marrying him. Federigo, ruined by extravagant courtship, sacrifices the only possession that might have saved a sick child. In the first, the desired marriage is produced by fear. In the second, a generous act and a disastrous failure of communication reveal the generosity of a man whom wealth had nearly destroyed. Neither story allows us simply to substitute the word happiness for the actual events.

And Dioneo, whose license permits him to depart from the chosen subject, will finish not with courtly consolation but with a quarrel inside a marriage. His comedy exposes how a household can be legally constituted yet emotionally incoherent. The final tale also prepares us for the verbal nimbleness that will dominate the following day.

When people describe the Decameron as a collection of happy and sad stories, that is true but insufficient. Boccaccio repeatedly asks who does the counting. Does happiness mean a wedding, survival, freedom from punishment, recovered wealth, or a decision a character would freely have made? Fiammetta supplies the subject. Each narrator supplies a different test of its meaning. We will hear ten attempts to turn suffering into the ending that the day's queen has requested.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 1: Cimon and Iphigenia

On the island of Cyprus lives a wealthy gentleman, Aristippus, whose son has disappointed every plan for his education. Called Cimon by those who judge him coarse and incapable, the young man takes little interest in books or the civilized accomplishments expected of his birth. His father sends him to the countryside, where rough labor seems better suited to him. One spring day Cimon walks through a grove and sees a young woman sleeping near a fountain, with attendants nearby. Her name is Iphigenia.

He is astonished by her beauty. When she wakes, she knows him and is alarmed by his stare. She asks him to keep away. He follows her nonetheless, then returns to his father's household with a determination his family has never seen. He wants fine clothes, instruction, conversation, music, riding, and every accomplishment associated with an educated gentleman. For years he applies himself until the youth dismissed as incapable becomes distinguished in the arts of peace and war. Panfilo presents this transformation as the power of love to unlock capacities that instruction alone could not release.

But Cimon's education does not create a reciprocal love. Iphigenia is promised to another man, and her family intends her to marry abroad. Cimon resolves not to yield. When the ship carrying her toward Rhodes sets sail, he and armed companions intercept it at sea. Iphigenia is taken by force. At this point the story of cultivated virtue has become an adventure of maritime violence. A storm drives the captors toward Rhodes, where Cimon is arrested and imprisoned, while Iphigenia is returned to the household waiting for her marriage.

Another man in Rhodes has his own grievance. Lysimachus, a powerful official, loves Cassandra, who is also about to marry someone else. He can release Cimon and gain an experienced partner for an assault on the wedding celebrations. The two agree to combine their aims. At the appointed moment, they enter with armed followers, seize Cassandra and Iphigenia, and escape by ship. They reach Crete, where they marry the women and eventually obtain a settlement allowing their return to their homes.

The finished tale calls the outcome fortunate. Two determined lovers gain the women they pursued, and the law that briefly imprisoned one of them yields to wealth and negotiation. Yet the narrative repeatedly leaves us with the same absent question: what do Iphigenia and Cassandra want? Iphigenia's fear in the grove is directly narrated. The violence of her abduction is directly narrated. Neither becomes an obstacle to the plot's assurance that marriage will solve the problem.

Panfilo's story blends several familiar forms: an education in courtly virtue, a sea adventure, the competition of suitors, captivity, rescue, and final reconciliation. Its pace carries the listener from an apparently miraculous awakening of intelligence to a succession of raids. The transformation is real within the fiction, but its meaning is divided. Cimon learns eloquence and command. He does not thereby learn to respect a refusal.

Reading with that distinction makes the tale stronger rather than weaker. Boccaccio allows us to admire a man learning skills and acting decisively, while showing that accomplishment can be put to destructive ends. The romance's final weddings describe the winners' fortune. They do not settle every question raised by the means of winning it.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 2: Gostanza and Martuccio

The island of Lipari supplies Emilia with a story that begins in poverty, not at court. Gostanza loves Martuccio Gomito, an enterprising young man who wants to marry her but lacks the resources her family expects. Her father rejects the match. Martuccio leaves to seek wealth, becoming a raider at sea. At first his ventures prosper; then he encounters defeat. His ship is overcome and he is taken prisoner. News returning to Lipari says he has been killed.

Gostanza accepts the report as the end of her future. She does not arrange a new courtship or even try to protect herself. She goes down to the shore alone, takes a small boat, and sets out without proper preparation for a crossing. She means to abandon herself to the sea. Fortune, which has already falsified the news of Martuccio's death, now carries her toward the coast of North Africa rather than destroying her. She comes ashore in the region of Susa, alive but unable to explain herself to the people she meets.

A woman gives Gostanza shelter and helps her enter a household where she can recover and remain safe. This detail matters. Many of the day's romances depend on the intervention of strangers who have no reason to act except compassion or recognition of distress. Without such hospitality, a girl alone in a foreign port would be in much greater danger than the plot's concluding prosperity makes easy to remember. Gostanza learns enough of the local circumstances to discover that Martuccio is not dead. He is in Tunis, and his fortunes have changed astonishingly.

Martuccio, taken captive after his defeat, has become useful to the ruler through intelligent advice about warfare. His expertise helps secure a victory; rather than remaining a prisoner, he earns the king's favor and an honorable position. Emilia has drawn a second trajectory beside Gostanza's. She is carried helplessly by the sea and preserved by welcome. He is carried as a captive into a court where judgment and skill can make him valuable. Their paths now converge in a city neither expected to call home.

When the lovers are reunited, Martuccio is no longer the poor young man whose proposal could be dismissed. He can arrange a marriage and a wealthy return to Lipari. Their reunion satisfies Fiammetta's day's theme in the clearest way: a reported death proves false, two separated people recognize each other, and material circumstances no longer bar the match.

It is tempting to hear the ending as a lesson that suffering is always rewarded. The story offers no such guarantee. Gostanza could not know that a storm would preserve her life, or that a stranger would protect her, or that Martuccio would rise in a foreign court. Nor could Martuccio reasonably predict the benefits of captivity. Good fortune operates precisely where the characters lack control. By contrast, hospitality and useful intelligence are human acts whose value can be described without pretending that fate is fair.

Emilia's romance allows a reader to feel relief without forgetting the risks that made relief necessary. Separation begins in the unequal power to decide a marriage; reunion depends on changes that neither lover could command. Their happiness is genuine within the tale. Its arrival is a combination of endurance, help, competence, and chance, not a proof that the world naturally delivers such endings.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 3: Pietro and Agnolella

In Rome, Pietro Boccamazza loves a young woman called Agnolella, whose family does not agree to their marriage. Pietro comes from a more distinguished family than hers; the objections are rooted in rank and in the arrangements relatives believe they have a right to enforce. They decide to flee together. Riding toward Anagni, where Pietro expects to find friends, they take a wrong turning. The change in direction brings them into a region troubled by armed bands.

A group of men emerges from a nearby stronghold. Agnolella sees the danger first and spurs her horse into a wood. Pietro is caught before he can follow. The men threaten him, recognize his family affiliation, and prepare to hang him as an enemy. Another band attacks at just that moment, and Pietro escapes in the confusion. Once free, he has no idea where Agnolella has gone. He wanders through the forest calling her name, unable to tell whether she has survived or fallen into worse hands.

Elissa tells the lovers' parallel stories. Pietro spends the night high in an oak tree to avoid animals and strangers. Agnolella, following a path, reaches an elderly couple's cottage. They warn her that raiders often visit the district and that their shelter cannot guarantee her safety. They do not turn her away. Their immediate difficulty is practical: how can they conceal a vulnerable guest when armed men may arrive? The elderly woman makes a space in a heap of hay, and Agnolella hides there while danger passes.

Pietro meanwhile finds help through another encounter. He meets people who know the region and eventually reaches the protection of a household connected to the same country castle where Agnolella has been brought. One by one, frightening accidents that seemed to separate the lovers instead place them among people capable of helping them. At the end they recognize each other, marry, and return to Rome with the advantage of allies whose intervention makes their union possible.

The story derives much of its suspense from geography. A missed turning is not an incidental mistake, because the roads outside a walled city are not interchangeable. The lovers have crossed into a world where allegiance, brigandage, forest paths, and a stranger's decision about lodging can determine whether one lives. Pietro's high social standing may make his forbidden romance possible in Rome; once a hostile band identifies his family, that same standing can endanger his life.

The shelters are more memorable than the marriage arrangements. An old couple allows a lost girl inside when admitting her may bring peril upon them. Helpers make passage between unsafe and safe spaces possible. Elissa's romantic conclusion depends on kindness and practical intelligence exercised by people outside the love story's central pair.

That is why this account of luck has a different emotional texture from a tale of pure conquest. Pietro and Agnolella decide to elope, but they cannot overcome the armed landscape by force or exceptional wisdom. They become separated; other people preserve them. The ending is not fate simply reversing its mood. It is a chain of human interventions that lets a bad turn in the road cease to be the last thing that happens.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 4: Caterina and the Nightingale

Messer Lizio da Valbona has a beloved daughter, Caterina, and a household run with the expectation that a respectable young woman will accept her parents' arrangements. Caterina loves Ricciardo Manardi. Their attraction is mutual, but they cannot openly spend the night together. Caterina devises a harmless-sounding request. The summer heat is unbearable indoors, she says; might she sleep on a bed made up on the balcony, where she can hear a nightingale sing?

Her mother resists and then seeks her father's consent. With a combination of persistence and apparent innocence, Caterina wins the right to sleep outside. She sends word to Ricciardo, who climbs up to her after the house is quiet. The lovers spend the night together. They fall asleep near dawn, and when Lizio comes out to see how his daughter has rested, he discovers Ricciardo beside her.

Filostrato turns the dangerous discovery into a series of jokes. Lizio brings his wife to look at the nightingale their daughter has caught. Caterina's invented interest in birds has become a double meaning that the adults cannot mistake. But a joke cannot by itself decide what will happen. The father could treat Ricciardo's presence as a mortal offense; Ricciardo knows that he has breached the family's trust and could be punished.

Instead Lizio calculates that the young man is of good family and can become a suitable husband. He requires Ricciardo to marry Caterina immediately. Ricciardo agrees, and the marriage is solemnized there with a borrowed ring. Later the couple has a public celebration. The nightingale joke continues into the tale's closing description of their married life, when the lovers are free to share the pleasure they had tried to conceal.

On Fiammetta's day of fortunate endings, the crisis is comparatively small: no shipwreck, no long imprisonment, no public execution. Yet its stakes are real within the household. The father controls the right to recognize a union, and the unmarried couple cannot assume that their private desire will be accepted. The speed of the solution depends partly on a fact that could have been otherwise: Ricciardo's family and status make him welcome as a son-in-law. The same discovery involving a servant or an unsuitable suitor might not have ended with laughter.

The comic achievement belongs in considerable part to Caterina. She invents the nightingale, persuades her parents, arranges the meeting, and helps turn a private relationship into a socially recognized one. Her ingenuity is not a magical defeat of patriarchy. It is exercised within limits that remain visible even after she succeeds.

Filostrato, responsible for the day's preceding tales of unhappy love, now gives his audience an unusually cheerful piece of domestic comedy. The difference is instructive. A secret meeting that could become disgrace becomes a marriage because those empowered to object decide to accept it. The nightingale is a memorable euphemism, but the decisive action is a parent's choice not to turn discovery into punishment.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 5: Guidotto’s Ward

Two old soldiers, Guidotto da Cremona and Giacomino da Pavia, have spent years traveling with armies and know how easily a settled household may be overturned. When Guidotto approaches death, he places a young girl in Giacomino's care, together with the property that supports her. She has lived with him since she was small. The circumstances of her birth are not fully understood by the man who now becomes her guardian.

Giacomino eventually settles in Faenza. The girl grows into a young woman admired by many, but two men are particularly determined to marry her: Giannole di Severino and Minghino di Mingole. Their families oppose the matches, and neither man accepts the obstruction. Each finds a helper in Giacomino's household. Giannole confides in the servant Crivello; Minghino works through the older woman who serves the household and who has carried messages between him and the girl.

The separate intrigues collide on a night when Giacomino is expected to dine elsewhere. Crivello plans to let Giannole in, while the woman servant expects Minghino. What each party believes will be a private meeting becomes a confused struggle at the house. Men gather, identities are mistaken, and the young woman's future seems about to be decided by whichever suitor can prevail in the disturbance. The confrontation is not a contest she has designed. Other people, persuaded of their own rights or desires, are moving around her as though the problem is which man can obtain her first.

At this crisis, the question of parentage comes unexpectedly to the fore. The surviving history of the girl is reconstructed. She is recognized as the long-lost sister of Giannole, who can therefore no longer be her suitor. A pursuit that looked like rivalry turns into a recognition scene. Minghino becomes her husband, with the impediment from the other claimant dissolved. Neifile ends on the return of a family connection that nobody knew they had lost.

The use of recognition belongs to an old tradition of romance and stage comedy. A child displaced by war, an inherited uncertainty, a distinguishing memory or witness, and the sudden recovery of kinship can transform the legal and emotional meaning of an action. The same two people standing in the same room become brother and sister rather than prospective spouses. An external fact changes the story more rapidly than argument or force could have done.

Boccaccio also places this turn inside the social world of guardianship. Giacomino has a duty to the child left with him. The young woman has a marriageable future that others discuss as though it were an estate to be claimed, while her own growing attachment receives less space than the suitors' schemes. What the tale calls a fortunate outcome includes the restoration of kinship and an approved marriage; it does not make the earlier rivalry admirable.

By letting the girl's background resolve the quarrel, Neifile chooses a kind of luck unlike the perilous crossings in the preceding stories. Fortune is not merely a storm that moves people from danger to safety. It is information that was already true but unknown. One truth, once established, forbids one union and makes room for another.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 6: Gianni and Restituta

On the island of Ischia, Gianni di Procida loves a young woman called Restituta. They have known each other and hope to remain together. Their plans are interrupted not by a father deciding against a marriage, but by men who arrive by sea, see Restituta alone, and carry her away. The raiders cannot agree who should claim her. They deliver her to King Frederic in Palermo, reasoning that her beauty will win royal favor. In an instant she is transferred from her own home to a ruler's pleasure, without being asked what she wants.

Gianni searches the coastline for news. At length he learns that the abductors went to Sicily and that Restituta has been lodged in a royal residence called the Cuba. He remains nearby and makes contact when she appears at a window. She gives him instructions for returning. He climbs into her room by night, and the two decide that he will prepare an escape. They are finally together again, but not yet safe. Exhausted, they fall asleep.

The king unexpectedly arrives to visit Restituta and finds the couple in bed. His first impulse is to kill them at once. Then he decides that punishment should be public. The lovers are bound and displayed in Palermo, with preparations for burning them at a stake. The king's fury is described as an injury to his own dignity, though he has never possessed a just claim to Restituta in the first place. Pampinea lets the threatened execution reach its most terrible stage before the reversal begins.

A nobleman named Ruggieri dell'Oria recognizes Gianni as a young man connected with an honorable family. The king learns who has been condemned. That recognition changes the calculations of the court. Frederic releases the lovers, makes amends with gifts, and arranges their marriage. Gianni and Restituta return to Ischia with the security and wealth that the ruler can bestow.

This is a story of rescue, but not a simple demonstration of royal mercy. It begins with kidnapping by men who treat a woman as property, then transfers her to a king who regards the arrival of her actual beloved as an affront. The decision to burn two sleeping lovers is an exercise of power almost impossible for them to contest. They survive because a member of the elite recognizes a family name and persuades the ruler that these people are not expendable.

Pampinea's narrative therefore exposes something about the political conditions of romantic happiness. In a courtly world, a recognized identity can save a life that love by itself cannot protect. The public spectacle intended to demonstrate the king's authority becomes an occasion for him to demonstrate generosity. The law, or what passes for law in the ruler's hands, changes because his sense of honor has been redirected.

The final wedding is exactly what Fiammetta requested: lovers survive danger and attain the future denied to them. Yet the plot also asks whether the greatest danger might come from the very office that claims to impose order. The ruler who gives presents is the same ruler who ordered a fire. The happy ending belongs most fully to Gianni and Restituta. The king's reputation, though repaired within the romance, remains a more difficult subject for the reader.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 7: Teodoro and Violante

In Trapani, a gentleman named Amerigo buys several boys from sea raiders who have taken captives in the eastern Mediterranean. One boy seems more accomplished than the others. His name is Teodoro, but because his new owner believes him to be Turkish, he is baptized and given the name Pietro. Teodoro grows up alongside the master's children, wins his confidence, is eventually freed, and becomes a trusted manager of the household. But the freedom granted to a formerly enslaved servant is not the same as social equality.

Amerigo's daughter Violante and Teodoro fall in love. They hesitate to speak; both understand what it would mean for the daughter of the house to be found in a relationship with the man once owned by her father. A storm gives them privacy. They take shelter in a damaged building, confess their affection, and become lovers. Later Violante realizes that she is pregnant. Teodoro fears that discovery will bring his death. She persuades him to stay, promising to conceal his part in the affair.

The secret fails. Violante gives birth, and her father unexpectedly hears the newborn's cries. He demands to know whose child it is. Under threat of death she names Teodoro. Amerigo has the young man arrested and tortured; a magistrate orders him scourged through the city and hanged. The father also sends a servant to Violante with a choice between poison and a sword and commands that her infant be killed. These are not exaggerated predictions by frightened lovers. They are orders within the narrated action.

As Teodoro is being marched toward execution, three Armenian ambassadors look down from an inn. An older man notices a distinctive mark on the condemned youth's chest. He calls out the name Teodoro in Armenian. The prisoner recognizes the language and tells what little he remembers of his childhood. The older man, Fineo, realizes that his son, abducted years earlier, stands before him. He intervenes to stop the execution.

The revelation transforms Amerigo's reasoning. The man he believed an inferior dependent is the child of an honored foreign nobleman. A marriage that would have disgraced the house becomes acceptable. Teodoro chooses Violante; she declares her wish to marry him. The family recovers the infant and celebrates the match. Lauretta carries them eventually to the home of Teodoro's recovered family, where they live in safety.

This is one of the day's sharpest exposures of rank. Nothing about Teodoro's conduct changes when Fineo recognizes him. He has the same knowledge, affection, and courage before the intervention. What changes is the information the authorities possess about his birth. At one moment they are prepared to torture and kill him. At the next they regard him as a fitting husband. The story's machinery of recognition makes the social contradiction visible.

There is also a difference between escape from punishment and justice. Amerigo's willingness to destroy his daughter and grandchild is not vindicated by the wedding. The reader can welcome their survival without calling the threatened deaths reasonable. The romance resolves its crisis by discovering an equality of status that was hidden, not by abolishing the rule that made the crisis possible.

Teodoro's two names tell the whole story in miniature. Pietro is the name bestowed by the household that purchased him. Teodoro is the name called by the father who thought him lost. The second name rescues him. It is a powerful conclusion, and an unsettling measure of the world he has survived.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 8: Nastagio and the Infernal Hunt

Nastagio degli Onesti, a wealthy young man in Ravenna, loves a woman of the Traversari family. He spends lavishly, entertains magnificent company, and tries to win her affection through displays of wealth. She refuses him. He responds not by accepting her choice but by spending more and more, until his relatives fear he will ruin the estate he inherited. They persuade him to leave Ravenna temporarily. He camps among the pine trees near Chiassi, still arranging fine meals and parties.

One Friday he walks alone in the wood. A naked woman runs toward him, crying for help. Two fierce dogs pursue her, followed by an armed rider. Nastagio tries to intervene. The rider stops him, explaining that both he and the fleeing woman are dead. He once loved her and killed himself after she rejected him; the woman died later. Now, he says, they are condemned to repeat a supernatural hunt. He must pursue her, kill her, open her body, and feed her organs to the dogs; she rises and flees again. Nastagio watches the horror unfold.

The rider presents the punishment as divine justice for the woman's cruelty. His account is not an independent explanation from an omniscient authority; it is a speech by a character inside a frightening vision. The story invites its audience to believe that the spectacle has moral significance, while also showing its ghastly violence. Nastagio is appalled. Then he realizes the hunt recurs in that place at a predictable time, and he devises a use for it.

He arranges a banquet in the pine grove for the following Friday. His invitations include the woman who refused him and her relatives. The guests are seated so that they can see what Nastagio saw. During the meal, the cries begin. The woman flees, the dogs seize her, the rider repeats his story, and the killing is performed before the assembled company. The guests are overwhelmed by fear. The young woman whom Nastagio wants imagines herself subjected to a similar fate if she continues to refuse him.

That night she sends word that she will accept Nastagio. He proposes marriage rather than a secret affair, and the families arrange the ceremony. The tale goes even further: women of Ravenna, we are told, become more accommodating toward men's desires after hearing of the frightful judgment. Filomena calls the outcome a cure for cruelty and a happy end for the lover.

The mechanism of persuasion must not be hidden under the word love. Nastagio has arranged a public act of terror to change a woman's decision. Whatever the vision means within the fiction, he knowingly uses her fear. The tale claims a moral order in which rejecting a suitor can earn torment even after death, and the warning is directed outward from the one woman to others. To describe this as consensual courtship without qualification would misrepresent the decisive scene.

The story is influential partly because it joins two incompatible kinds of narrative. The hunting vision belongs to the literature of the supernatural and exemplary punishment. The feast belongs to social comedy: a host stages an event to secure a marriage. Together they produce an ending that can be called successful while remaining profoundly disturbing. Later artists, notably Botticelli, painted scenes from the tale as parts of a wedding furnishing cycle; the subject's connection to marriage is part of what makes its violence important to interpret.

Nastagio's first impulse in the wood is to defend the pursued woman. His second is to turn her pain into a lesson for someone else. Between those choices lies the moral drama of the novella. Its happy ending is not a moral agreement between teller and every listener. It is an invitation to notice how confidently a culture can call coercion good fortune when a wedding follows it.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 9: Federigo and the Falcon

Federigo degli Alberighi has everything required of a splendid Florentine gentleman except the love of Monna Giovanna. He holds tournaments, entertains guests, and gives away wealth in an effort to be noticed by her. Giovanna neither solicits this expenditure nor rewards it with affection. Federigo impoverishes himself pursuing a woman who has given him no promise. At last he retires to a small country property, where he survives with difficulty. His remaining treasure is a magnificent falcon, both companion and means of sport.

Giovanna's life changes. Her husband dies, leaving wealth to their son and, should the boy die without heirs, to her. She moves to a country estate near Federigo's. The boy gets to know the impoverished gentleman and falls in love with the falcon. He becomes seriously ill. When his mother asks what might make him happier, he says he wants the bird, but he knows it is precious to Federigo and has not dared to demand it.

Giovanna faces a painful decision. The falcon is almost all Federigo has left. She knows he loved her and that she did not return his love; to appear now and ask for his best possession seems cruel. Yet she is desperate to help her child. She visits Federigo, accompanied by another woman, and asks to dine with him before explaining her request.

Federigo is overjoyed and ashamed. His visitor is someone he longs to honor, but his household has no food fit for a guest of such standing. He searches for a way to provide a meal. Nothing seems adequate except the falcon. Without knowing what Giovanna has come to ask, he kills the bird, has it cooked, and serves it. The women eat. Only afterward does Giovanna explain that she hopes to take the falcon home to her sick son.

Federigo breaks down. He has given away the very thing she needed, not because he refused her request, but because he had no opportunity to hear it before trying to show courtesy. He explains, and brings the bird's remains as proof. Giovanna first reproaches him for sacrificing such an excellent creature to provide a meal. Then she recognizes the generosity that poverty has not destroyed. She returns home without the falcon. Her son dies soon afterward; the narrative does not establish that obtaining the bird would have saved him.

Time passes, and Giovanna's brothers urge her to marry again. She would prefer to remain a widow, but their pressure persists. She declares that if she must marry, she chooses Federigo. Her brothers object that he is poor. She answers in favor of the value of a good person over mere possessions. She brings wealth to the marriage; Federigo, now responsible for a household again, lives with greater care in managing it.

There are two kinds of giving in this tale. Federigo's early spending is magnificent and ineffective, driven by hope that wealth will buy affection. His final gift is sincere but tragically misdirected. He kills his falcon because he believes a guest deserves the best of the house, never knowing that his guest has come specifically for the living bird. Generosity alone is not sufficient; it must meet another person's actual need. The story's poignancy depends on the failure to communicate before acting.

The ending, too, should not erase its losses. The child dies. The falcon dies. Giovanna's new marriage is made possible by widowhood, grief, kinship pressure, and a judgment she forms about Federigo's character. Federigo is rewarded in the romance, but not because every act he undertook was wise. Indeed the final line emphasizes his improved management of money, a quality absent from his earlier courtship.

Fiammetta tells this as the ninth tale of her own day. Compared with the terrifying spectacle that produced Nastagio's marriage, her story proposes a different measure of worth: conduct that remains generous after status and fortune have disappeared. The most cherished gift cannot be given twice. That irreversibility gives the novella its sorrow, even as its concluding union brings a form of happiness.

## The Decameron — Day Five, Tale 10: Pietro and the Hen-Coop

Dioneo ends a day of dangerous voyages and narrow escapes with a farce inside a prosperous home in Perugia. Pietro di Vinciolo has married a woman whose expectations of companionship are not met. The teller explicitly describes Pietro as attracted to men. He married, it seems, more for appearances than a shared desire for married life. His wife resents the arrangement: she thought she was entering a mutual relationship and finds herself neglected.

When Pietro goes to dine with a friend named Ercolano, his wife invites a young man into the house. The two have barely had time together when Pietro unexpectedly returns. A commotion at the friend's house has interrupted the meal. The wife conceals her visitor beneath a hen-coop and must listen as her husband explains why supper ended early.

At Ercolano's house, Pietro says, a young man was discovered hidden by Ercolano's wife. The revelation has produced a scandal. His own wife instantly joins in condemning the other woman. The audience knows the irony: her lover is only a few steps away. The contrast does not require an intricate conspiracy. It requires the familiar social rule that a person may condemn conduct in public while trying to keep the same conduct invisible at home.

The plan might have worked had an animal not wandered nearby. An ass steps on the hidden young man's fingers. He cries out, Pietro investigates, and the secret is exposed. It is almost impossible to devise an appropriately dignified reaction to a guest crawling out from beneath a poultry enclosure. Pietro has found a lover exactly where he least wished to find one, after listening to his wife denounce adultery at another man's table.

The ending is not a duel or a trial. Pietro decides to tolerate the situation. Dioneo makes the reconciliation depend on mutual imperfection: Pietro can hardly demand a perfect marriage while pursuing his own desires elsewhere. The wife does not gain a legal argument for a new kind of household, but she has exposed the gap between the institution they inhabit and what either spouse wants from it. The scene lets comic embarrassment do work that other stories assign to violence.

A modern listener need not accept the tale's invitation to make same-sex desire itself an object of ridicule. The plot's central contradiction is not that Pietro desires men. It is that his marriage is organized around concealment and an expectation his wife cannot satisfy. The anonymous young visitor, too, is placed at risk by an arrangement that leaves everyone hiding something. The ass is the unintentional truth-teller.

Dioneo's privilege as the final narrator permits him to interrupt the day's promised pattern. This couple has not crossed the sea, recovered an inheritance, or survived a public execution. Their narrow escape is from a household confrontation that might have ended much worse. It closes with laughter rather than an exemplary wedding, and the laughter leaves Fiammetta's theme quietly changed: perhaps good fortune sometimes means finding a way to live after the appearance of respectability has failed.
