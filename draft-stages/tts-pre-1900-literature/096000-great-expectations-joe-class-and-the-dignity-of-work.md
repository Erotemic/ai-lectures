# Great Expectations — Joe, Class, and the Dignity of Work

If Great Expectations were simply a warning that money corrupts, Joe Gargery would be the uncomplicated symbol of goodness, and Pip's final lesson would be to return to the forge. Dickens gives us a more demanding arrangement. Joe is a working blacksmith with skill, patience, and a capacity for affection; Pip's ambition is not evil merely because it might take him somewhere else. The problem is the system of judgment Pip learns, in which manual labor and imperfect speech make a person an embarrassment. Joe teaches us not that aspiration is wrong, but that rank cannot tell us whether aspiration is admirable.

When Pip first describes the forge, its rhythms are familiar. He knows tools and tasks, and the blacksmith's strength gives Joe a dignity that Pip cannot yet name. Joe is neither a polished conversationalist nor a master of institutions. He can be powerless before Mrs. Joe's anger and awkward when the demands of fashionable society are imposed on him. But he does not try to repair his discomfort by making a child feel inferior. He has learned, from his own father's violence, the cost of ruling a household through force. That history gives his gentleness a reason. It is an ethical practice rather than a harmless lack of sophistication.

Estella's words about hands and boots change Pip's perception of labor. The hands that make and repair objects begin to seem signs of a failed identity. This shift reveals how class judgment can work from inside. Nobody needs to seize Pip and forbid him the forge. He learns to police his own associations. He can still love Joe and feel ashamed of him, which makes the change more corrosive. Affection and snobbery coexist until affection is deprived of action. The grown narrator knows that wanting to move into a different social world did not require humiliating the one that had cared for him.

Joe's visit to London is the novel's decisive social experiment. Clothing that fits one environment becomes an obstacle in another; Joe does not know how to place his hat, and even ordinary speech seems exposed to mockery. Pip could translate the situation by welcoming a beloved guest, but instead tries to protect his new social persona. Joe detects that he is causing pain and withdraws. A scene that can be played for comic awkwardness becomes devastating because courtesy flows in only one direction. The person with less social power is the one protecting the feelings of the person who has more.

Biddy complicates the picture. She is intelligent, attentive, and willing to teach Pip while he is still a child. Unlike Pip's fantasy of Estella, her work and perception are visible to him. He can ask for guidance and receive it, but he does not turn that evidence into the kind of admiration he reserves for prestige. When he finally imagines marrying Biddy, he arrives too late. Biddy's marriage to Joe is not a reward left behind for Pip to claim after he has exhausted grander ambitions. Her decision shows that the people Pip neglected continued living and choosing while he narrated himself as the center of everyone's destiny.

The novel does not pretend that poverty is charming. Joe works hard; Mrs. Joe's anger is bound up with labor and constraint; Biddy lacks the avenues of advancement Pip takes for granted after his inheritance. Nor does the story imply that any person who works with hands will be morally good. Orlick is an abusive laborer; Compeyson is a refined fraud. Its argument is not a reversal in which all workers are saints and all gentlemen villains. It is a demand that judgments be supported by conduct rather than visual class markers.

After Magwitch's fortune is confiscated and Pip falls ill, Joe returns with practical, bodily care. He pays debts and tends the invalid. Those actions are not represented as grand charity from an extraordinary benefactor. They are extensions of the reliability we have seen since the forge. A reader might ask whether Pip's eventual employment with Herbert means he has rejected Joe's trade. It does not make him a blacksmith, but it begins to attach his own livelihood to work, cooperation, and accountability. The change is not a return to childhood; it is a departure from the idea that gentlemanliness consists of being free from earning a living.

Listen to two kinds of speech. Jaggers's precise commands and Wemmick's office calculations often carry institutional authority. Joe's hesitations and repetitions carry social vulnerability. Yet when Pip most needs a response, the vulnerable speaker finds the language of care. Dickens uses dialect and verbal habit for comic effect, and a modern reader can ask whether those effects always respect the people they represent. Still, within the moral argument of the novel, Joe's voice matters because he makes a home without being fluent in the language of power. Pip's education is measured by whether he finally learns to hear it.
