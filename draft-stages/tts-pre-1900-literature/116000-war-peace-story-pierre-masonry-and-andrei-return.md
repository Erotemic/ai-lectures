# War and Peace — Story: Pierre Searches for a Rule to Live By

After the duel and the breakdown of his marriage, Pierre Bezukhov becomes obsessed with the question of how to live without harming others. He is attracted to Freemasonry, whose language of moral regeneration offers the order his impulses have lacked. He joins with great sincerity. A system of charitable work, self-examination, ritual, and brotherhood appears to promise that even a flawed man might improve. Yet the promise encounters the conditions of Pierre's actual wealth. He has vast estates, stewards, and serfs, and the good he wishes them to receive depends on administrators whose interests are not his own.

Pierre tours his properties and hears grateful speeches from people who know how to satisfy a visiting landowner. Some improvements are real, others cosmetic, and he does not penetrate the system of exploitation that makes him wealthy. Tolstoy permits the ethical aspiration while exposing the ease with which a rich reformer mistakes an organized demonstration for evidence of change. Pierre is not a hypocrite who secretly wants the harm to continue. His difficulty is that sincerity without practical knowledge can leave a harmful arrangement nearly intact.

Andrei has withdrawn to country life and tries to govern his estates with greater competence. In one sense he is more effective than Pierre, but this competence does not make him happier. His grief for Lise and his contempt for public vanity have become a defensive habit. When Pierre visits, their conversation becomes a confrontation between two kinds of disappointment. Pierre speaks of moral renewal and the possibility of working for others. Andrei answers from the wreck of his previous ideals. Under the argument lies affection: each man wants the other to remain capable of living.

Tolstoy gives Andrei an image through which we can follow the change. He notices an old oak, apparently dry and stubborn among younger trees. It seems to confirm that spring cannot truly touch every life. Later, after seeing the Rostov girls and encountering Natasha's unguarded delight in a moonlit night, he sees the oak again with new leaves. The tree has not proved any theory. It has made visible a shift in his capacity for attention. He begins to think that his life need not have ended with his wife's death.

Andrei returns to public work and becomes involved with reform projects in Saint Petersburg, including those associated with Speransky. Their polished reason and administrative ambition initially appeal to him. But the vitality he has rediscovered is not identical to official usefulness. Once more, Tolstoy presents institutions that speak in the language of the good while remaining subject to performance, ambition, and exclusion. Andrei can identify these failures more sharply than Pierre, yet he is still susceptible to the pleasure of being thought important.

In the Rostov family, time has its own tests. Nikolai's military service and his love for Sonya coexist with financial calculations that cannot be banished by sincere declarations. The count's generosity has produced obligations he cannot easily pay. Natasha grows from a child into a young woman whose quick understanding of other people's feelings does not amount to a guarantee of prudence. She sees through pretension in one scene and falls under its power in another. She is capable of feeling music and life intensely; she is not yet able to protect herself from every person who will exploit that intensity.

Pierre's marriage continues to deteriorate. Helene returns to society with control over her own reputation that Pierre does not possess. Pierre is capable of forgiveness in principle, but his life alternates between hope and unregulated appetite. The lodge becomes another place where lofty public language can exist beside vanity and ambition. It would be easy to pronounce the whole project foolish. Tolstoy's greater interest is in why an imperfect person continues to need a framework for conscience, even after discovering how easily frameworks become costumes.

Austerlitz brought down Andrei's dream of glory by placing him under the open sky. These quieter chapters ask whether revelation can survive ordinary life. Pierre discovers that wanting to improve the world is not the same as changing it. Andrei discovers that grief need not be the final definition of his existence. The Rostovs continue to spend money and love one another without being able to calculate their future. The novel is preparing a marriage plot, but it is also preparing the reader for a war in which every confident calculation will be put under pressure.
