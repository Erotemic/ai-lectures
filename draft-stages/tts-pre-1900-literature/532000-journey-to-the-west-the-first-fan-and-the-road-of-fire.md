# Journey to the West: The first fan and the road of fire

## A red house in autumn

The travelers had left behind the terror of the two Monkey Kings. The genuine Wukong had returned to Xuanzang, and the stolen bundles were back on the horse. For a time the road gave them no great trouble. Summer passed, and the grasses and woods showed the signs of autumn. One morning, however, the air grew hotter as they went west. What should have been a cooling season began to feel like the inside of a furnace.

Xuanzang wiped his face and asked how autumn could burn like midsummer. Bajie had an explanation ready. Perhaps they were nearing a western country where the sun fell into the sea every evening and made the water boil. The local king, Bajie declared, had to drown the noise with horns and drums, or the roar would kill the children in the streets. Wukong laughed at him. The country at the end of the sky was nowhere near them, he said, and at their master's pace they would not reach it in three lifetimes. Sha suggested that the weather itself had gone wrong. They were still arguing when a house appeared beside the road.

The house seemed to have been built to match the heat: red tiles, red-brick walls, red-painted doors, red furniture visible within. An elderly man came out carrying a knotted bamboo staff. He startled at Wukong's face, then welcomed the pilgrims after hearing that they had come from the Tang empire to seek scriptures. He invited them indoors for tea and food.

The place, he explained, lay near the Flaming Mountains. There was no true spring or autumn here. The mountains occupied the western road for eight hundred li. Their fires had burned without stopping, and no living traveler, however strong, could march through them. Xuanzang heard this and fell silent. There was no alternate western road that the old man could offer.

Then a young man wheeled a cart of steaming cakes to the door. Wukong bought one with a coin made from a hair. The cake was so hot he juggled it from hand to hand. Why, he asked, would anyone bake food in a place where the stones were already baking their feet? How did the people grow grain at all?

The seller told him that the answer lay with an immortal known as Princess Iron Fan. She kept a plantain-leaf fan with power over the fires. A proper fanning would put out the flames, raise a wind, and bring rain; the villagers could then sow their fields, gather a crop, and live another year. To obtain that relief they had to approach the princess with gifts and petitions. The crops on which they depended were therefore tied to a power held by someone who did not live among them.

Wukong asked where the princess could be found. They called her the Rakshasi, said the old man, and her home was at Plantain Cave on Green Cloud Mountain. She was the wife of the Bull Demon King.

At that name Wukong's expression changed. He had once called the Bull Demon King his sworn elder brother. He had also recently fought the Bull King's child, Red Boy, who had tried to cook Xuanzang. Guanyin had subdued the child and taken him as her attendant. That outcome had saved the boy's life, but Wukong remembered the grief in a mother's position. Earlier, the boy's uncle had refused him the Water of Release for precisely this grievance. Now he needed a favor from the boy's mother.

The old man could not understand the monkey's sudden worry. Wukong put a pleasant face on it, instructed Bajie and Sha to guard their master, and went to request the fan.

## The brother who had saved a son

Green Cloud Mountain was cool and green where the fields around the Flaming Mountains were scorched. Its paths climbed through bamboo, trees, and damp stones to a quiet cave. Wukong found the door closed. He called politely for the Lady of the Plantain Fan. A young attendant carrying gardening tools opened the door and asked his name. When he said Sun Wukong, she went inside with the message.

The effect was immediate. Princess Iron Fan demanded her weapons and dressed for battle. She came out with two swords and called for the monkey to show himself. Wukong bowed as though arriving at a family feast.

"Elder sister-in-law," he said, "your younger brother has come to borrow a fan. My master cannot cross the fires. Lend it to me until we have gone through, and I will return it."

"Whose sister-in-law?" she demanded. "Who gave you leave to call me that?"

Wukong reminded her that the Bull Demon King had once sworn brotherhood with him. A brother's wife was therefore entitled to his respect. She was entitled to more than respect, the Rakshasi replied. Her son had been taken from her, and the monkey who had helped Guanyin take him now stood at her door asking for a household treasure.

Wukong tried a defense that he had used before. Red Boy was alive; indeed, he had become the bodhisattva's Child of Wealth. He no longer needed to terrorize passing travelers. Wukong could ask Guanyin to let his mother see him once the pilgrims were safely beyond the mountain. The lady heard only a man explaining that her separation from her child was a blessing. She had not been asked whether she wished it.

"Stretch out your head," she said. "Let me strike you with my swords first. If you can bear it, we will speak of a loan."

Wukong bent forward, hands folded, and let the swords fall. They rang against a head that could withstand the weapons of Heaven. After several strokes the Rakshasi retreated, frightened by his invulnerability, and Wukong demanded the fan again. She fought him among the cliffs until his iron staff began to press too hard. Then she brought out her plantain fan and swept it once through the air.

The ground vanished beneath Wukong. He was carried westward, rolling through clouds as though the sky itself had become a river. Even his somersault cloud could not immediately arrest the wind. At last he recovered his balance and came down on a strange mountain, far from the cave.

A bell sounded from a monastery. The monks recognized him as the traveler who had once summoned Lingji Bodhisattva to defeat the Yellow Wind Demon. Lingji welcomed him and listened to his complaint. The fan, Lingji explained, was no ordinary woven leaf. It was an ancient treasure of cooling power. Its wind could carry a being eighty-four thousand li before dying away. Wukong's command of the clouds had stopped him sooner, but the Lady's fan had done exactly what it was made to do.

Lingji had another ancient treasure: a wind-settling pill, given to him for an earlier task. He sewed it securely into Wukong's collar and told him to return to the cave. If the wind could not carry the monkey away, the Rakshasi would need another answer.

## Tea served in the cave

At Plantain Cave the young attendant heard the monkey knocking again. Her mistress could scarcely believe it. She had sent him into the sky and he was already at the door. The Rakshasi resolved to fan him three times and lose him forever.

The swords came out. Wukong stood his ground, asked for the loan again, and endured several attacks. The fan opened in her hands. Once, twice, then a third time she swept it toward him. This time he did not move. The princess backed into her cave and locked the doors.

A locked door was hardly a barrier to Wukong. He removed the pill from his collar, kept it safe in his mouth, and changed into a minute flying insect. He slipped through a crack. Inside, the Rakshasi called for tea. The servant poured a steaming cup. The tiny Wukong settled beneath the foam, and the princess swallowed him with the drink.

His voice came from inside her. "Sister-in-law, about that fan."

She called to the servants to check the doors. They were barred. Yet the monkey was speaking somewhere inside the room—or, as an attendant finally realized, inside their mistress herself. Wukong announced that he could see the chambers of her body and knew where to press. He stamped downward and pushed upward until she collapsed in pain. Her fury gave way to pleading.

"Uncle, spare me. You shall have the fan."

Wukong insisted on seeing it before leaving. The fan appeared beside her mouth, held by an attendant. He flew back out, landed upon it, grew to his usual size, and took it from the servant's hands. The Rakshasi was still recovering on the floor when he bowed in exaggerated thanks and departed.

At the red house the pilgrims welcomed his return. He told them where the wind had carried him, how Lingji's pill had protected him, and how he had acquired the fan. Xuanzang praised his effort. It was not a clean triumph, though. Wukong had used violence against the grieving mother of the child he had helped remove from her care. His new treasure had been given under pain. The others were too relieved to examine the method.

They thanked their host and set out. Near the Flaming Mountains the air thickened, their feet burned, and the horse quickened its pace in distress. Wukong ordered the others to retreat while he approached the flames with the fan.

He swept it once. The fire rose higher. A second sweep made the mountain flare with still greater force. A third sent flames leaping up like a wall. Wukong fled, two patches of hair singed from his legs because he had been concentrating on the fan rather than protecting his body from fire. He shouted for everyone to turn back.

Behind them the heat roared. They retreated eastward until they could breathe. Xuanzang asked how this could have happened. Wukong dropped the fan and admitted that the Rakshasi had deceived him with a false one.

Bajie proposed a wonderfully simple solution: go east, or north, or south, wherever there was no fire. Xuanzang asked in which direction the scriptures lay. "West," Bajie conceded. Then, said the monk, west was where they must go. Sha put the difficulty into plain words. In the direction without fire there were no scriptures; in the direction of the scriptures there was fire.

As they argued, an old spirit in formal dress came forward with food. He was the local Earth God, carrying a meal for the weary pilgrims. When Wukong showed him the fan, the god laughed: this was indeed a counterfeit. To obtain the real one, the monkey would have to seek the Great Strength King. The Earth God named him—the Bull Demon King, Red Boy's father, Princess Iron Fan's husband, and Wukong's old sworn brother.

Their path across the mountain had become a path back through the monkey's own past.
