# Crime and Punishment — Publication, Translation, and Close Reading

Crime and Punishment appeared in the Russian Messenger over the course of 1866. A separate book edition followed in 1867. Its structure comprises six parts and an epilogue. Remembering this arrangement helps a reader resist stopping with the police confession. The final section makes a moral claim about change while refusing to represent change as complete. Dostoevsky's achievement depends on both the criminal suspense and the unfinished inner life that remains after the law has acted.

For an English-speaking listener, translation is unavoidable unless the Russian text is available directly. Constance Garnett's public-domain version offers a complete named reading text. Later translators have made different choices about colloquial speech, the speed of argument, names, register, and wordplay. In Russian, a person may be addressed by first name, patronymic, surname, or diminutive. Raskolnikov is Rodion Romanovich; Dunya is Avdotya Romanovna; Sonya is Sofya Semyonovna. Readers may mistake these forms for separate people if no one explains them. A quotation-based argument should name the translator whose English words are being examined.

The University of Toronto's exhibition on the novel's global contexts documents its original periodical publication and its long afterlife in translation, art, stage, and film. A source record can establish where a work was published; it cannot decide on its own which interpretation of Raskolnikov's redemption is right. The novel's reception should be described as a history of arguments, not a tally that proves one reading.

For a first close-reading exercise, return to the conversation in the tavern with Marmeladov. Mark the places where he acknowledges causing harm and the places where his self-description risks becoming another form of indulgence. How does the narrative make his suffering audible without allowing him to escape responsibility for what he has done to his family? Compare this scene with Raskolnikov's later explanations of the murders. Both men can speak movingly about their distress. Neither speech is equivalent to a repair of the harm.

Second, examine the dream of the beaten horse before the crime. Who acts, who protests, and who watches? The child shows pity that adult Raskolnikov has not lost completely. Does the dream prove he never intended to kill, or does it show how a person may feel horror and proceed anyway? Keep the distinction between affect and action in view.

Third, study the first interview with Porfiry about Raskolnikov's article. Separate what the article claims, what Porfiry asks, and what Raskolnikov actually admits. How does an investigator make a person defend a position without stating an accusation? The exercise shows why dialogue, not a narrator's summary of philosophy, is the novel's most effective instrument of argument.

Fourth, compare Luzhin's accusation against Sonya with Nikolai's false confession. Both involve the form of evidence without the truth it is supposed to establish. In the first, a planted banknote is meant to produce a public verdict; in the second, spoken admission creates an apparent answer to a murder inquiry. Ask how witnesses, context, and knowledge beyond the immediate declaration alter what can reasonably be believed.

Fifth, read the final police confession and the epilogue as separate events. Raskolnikov acknowledges the crime before the law, but in Siberia he continues to interpret his failure through pride for a long time. Sonya's presence and the eventual emotional breakthrough are not a cancellation of the previous pages. Notice the final gesture toward a new story yet to be told. Dostoevsky ends the novel before anyone can certify that the long work of becoming different is finished.

The textual record also imposes limits. A modern digitized Garnett translation is not a facsimile of every Russian serial installment. Exact claims about variants require collating the appropriate Russian witnesses; claims about editorial intent require documentary evidence. These lectures use a public-domain reading text and selected bibliographic scholarship, not an exhaustive reconstruction of the author's revisions.

The book has endured because its central conflict cannot be contained in one slogan about guilt. It asks whether intelligence provides moral exemption, whether poverty explains violence, how courts distinguish knowledge from appearance, and how a person can begin to live truthfully after an irreversible act. The literary method makes those questions felt through particular voices and relationships. We have watched a theory fail; the novel leaves us at the beginning of a life that might acknowledge the failure.
