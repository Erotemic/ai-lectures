# The Decameron — Alibech and Rustico

For his final story, Dioneo turns the company's attention from elaborate Florentine intrigues to a young girl living near Gafsa in North Africa. Her name is Alibech. She has heard people speak of the Christian God and wants to learn how she might serve him. She leaves her family's home and travels into a remote region where religious hermits have withdrawn from ordinary society. Her motives, in the story, are sincere. Her knowledge of the language and customs of these ascetics is almost nonexistent.

The first hermits she encounters understand that a young visitor presents a temptation and a responsibility they do not want to undertake. They send her farther into the wilderness. Eventually she reaches a man named Rustico, who decides to receive her. He tells himself that this is an opportunity to test his spiritual strength. But he is not immune to the same desires that occupy so many characters in the book.

Rustico soon begins to manipulate Alibech's ignorance. He gives ordinary sexual desire an invented religious vocabulary, calling an intimate act a way of putting the devil into hell. Because she believes she is being taught a duty owed to God, Alibech takes his explanation at face value. Dioneo tells the story as a bawdy comedy in which the novice becomes so eager to practice the supposed discipline that Rustico can no longer keep up. The repeated phrase becomes a joke about a teacher who loses control of the lesson he invented.

Eventually news reaches the wilderness that Alibech's family has died and that she has inherited substantial property. A young man named Neerbale comes to find her, takes her back to town, and marries her. In conversation with other women she innocently repeats the vocabulary Rustico taught her. Those who understand its ordinary meaning laugh, and the language of the hermitage is exposed as a private euphemism, not a recognized practice of worship.

It is important to be clear about the characters' different knowledge. Alibech is a child or very young adolescent in the story. Rustico is an adult religious man who understands what the phrase conceals and exploits her lack of knowledge. We can analyze the comic mechanism without joining the teller in treating that exploitation as harmless. The girl has sought religious instruction, and the person she finds substitutes his own interests for the instruction she asks for.

The tale nonetheless offers one of Boccaccio's sharpest demonstrations of what words can do. A person who knows what an action is may rename it in the terms another person trusts. The hearer can then take the renamed action to be precisely the virtue she came to practice. This is the dangerous inverse of the third tale, where a woman used the vocabulary of complaint to convey a desired invitation. Here the deceived person is not directing the message; the supposed instructor controls the definition.

Dioneo is permitted to end a day with material that pushes against the common theme. He has found a story in which the acquisition of a longed-for good—service to God—gets mixed with a private, fraudulent lesson. The sequence of women and men gathered in a luxurious garden will laugh at the hermit's failed control and the girl's innocent descriptions. Their laughter is part of the literary evidence of what the novella expects from its audience. It need not dictate the judgment of everyone who reads it.

Day Three has offered us a full gallery of schemes. Some restore the innocent, some embarrass the powerful, some open a space for choice, and others take that space away. The technique is often the same: study another person's expectations and make them serve your plan. Dioneo closes not with a simple rule for conduct but with an account of how easily a powerful speaker can transform the vocabulary of virtue into an excuse for personal gratification.
