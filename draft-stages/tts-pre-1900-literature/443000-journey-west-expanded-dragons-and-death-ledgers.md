# The Dragon King's Treasure and the Registers of Death

Sun Wukong's monkeys had been rescued from the Demon King of Havoc, and Water-Curtain Cave was theirs again. The king could have resumed their old life of feasting beneath the waterfall. He did not. An army had attacked while he was away, and the next attack might come without warning. The monkey who had once sought immortality for himself now looked at the members of his troop and saw a need for defense.

He had the weapon taken from the slain demon, but his followers were less fortunate. They trained with stalks of bamboo and pieces of wood. Such toys were good for practicing a formation, not for facing an armed enemy. Wukong asked how they could obtain real weapons. Some of the older monkeys knew that a human kingdom lay across the sea from the mountain. Its towns had workshops and storehouses filled with metal tools. If the king could bring back swords, spears, bows, and armor, the whole troop could train.

He leaped into the air. The sea was quickly behind him, and below lay the city of Aolai. From above he could see the ordered streets and markets of a kingdom unlike the monkeys' hidden cave. People went about their business, unaware that a powerful visitor had arrived and was deciding how to acquire the contents of their armory.

He might have purchased a few weapons. He might have approached a ruler and asked for assistance. Instead he summoned a violent wind. Dust filled the streets. Roofs and banners shook. People abandoned their work and sought shelter. In the confusion Wukong entered the storehouses, pulled hairs from his body, and created a crowd of small monkeys who carried away weapons by the armful. He gathered the stolen arms in a supernatural wind and departed. The weapons had been intended to arm a human realm. Now they were piled before Water-Curtain Cave.

His followers rejoiced. They seized swords, knives, spears, axes, bows, and all the other treasures that had fallen into their laps. They practiced marching and attack, played at warfare, and rejoiced at being better armed than before. The king organized them into a force large enough to impress neighboring creatures. The story numbers the monkeys in the tens of thousands. Nearby animal and demon rulers came to recognize Wukong's power, and the many caves of the mountain region began to pay him respect and tribute.

The little kingdom behind the waterfall was turning into a military domain. Wukong was no longer simply the courageous discoverer of shelter. He had followers who called him lord, troops who could bear arms, neighbors who deferred to his power, and the beginning of the political expectations that accompany such authority. He was pleased. But he noticed a problem: the weapon he had taken from the Demon King of Havoc did not suit him. The monkey could hold and swing it, but it seemed clumsy and light for a being who had learned immortal arts.

The older monkeys proposed a journey beneath the sea. The water running below the bridge in Water-Curtain Cave led toward the eastern ocean, and the ocean contained the palace of a Dragon King. A ruler who controlled waters and possessed a great treasury might have something worthy of their sovereign. Wukong was delighted by the idea. He had crossed oceans and the heavens of thought; now he would go beneath the waves.

At the bridge he recited a water-dividing spell and entered the stream. Instead of drowning, he moved through an underwater world with roads and guardians, a place governed by powerful beings and its own ceremonies. A watchman challenged him, expecting a name and a reason for entry. Wukong declared himself the naturally born sage of Flower-Fruit Mountain and a neighbor of the Dragon King. The guard hurried to report this demanding visitor.

Ao Guang, ruler of the Eastern Sea, came to receive him with the proper honors. His palace was no open cave. It was a court surrounded by officers, family, servants, aquatic soldiers, and a treasury. The king offered his guest a seat and refreshments. Perhaps he hoped courtesy would move the visit along. When he asked what brought the monkey under the sea, Wukong explained that he needed a weapon to match his abilities.

The Dragon King ordered a blade brought out. Wukong handled it and was dissatisfied. He did not want an ordinary sword. A great fork followed, far heavier than anything a human soldier would carry. He tried it and set it aside. A painted halberd came next, heavier still. Wukong lifted and swung it without difficulty, then dismissed it as too light. With each rejected weapon the host's embarrassment grew. The visitor had asked for a gift, and no gift seemed large enough. The servants could see that the dragon ruler was dealing with someone who would not be easy to send away.

At last word reached Ao Guang of a tremendous iron object kept in the palace. It was not displayed as a weapon. It had long served as a measuring or stabilizing instrument associated with the waters, a treasure of ancient origin. Lately, the object had shown a strange light, as though awaiting a particular master. The Dragon King could hardly imagine that the monkey would be able to lift it, but the ordinary treasury had failed. He led Wukong to see it.

The object looked like a pillar. It was far too large for the hands of a monkey. Wukong walked around it, inspected its bands, and told it to become smaller. The pillar responded. It diminished enough for him to grasp. He tested its weight and balance. It was immense, yet exactly what he had wanted. He commanded it to shrink again and again until it took the form of a staff that he could spin in his hands. Markings identified it as a gold-banded rod of miraculous responsiveness, with a weight measured in thousands of catties, thirteen thousand five hundred in the familiar text.

The treasure had been waiting under the sea, and the visitor who had rejected all the weapons in the armory had found something that obeyed him. He enlarged the staff and whirled it until the palace trembled. Water creatures fled. The Dragon King saw the distress his guest could cause without even intending a full assault. Wukong rejoiced at his good fortune. The staff's ability to change size meant that it could be a colossal weapon in battle and a tiny object tucked behind his ear when not needed.

The monkey now wanted the clothing of a great ruler. Ao Guang had exhausted his patience and his armory, and he summoned his brothers, the other Dragon Kings, to help deal with the demand. They came bearing splendid objects: a golden cap with phoenix-like ornaments, bright chain mail, and boots that suited a supernatural traveler. Wukong accepted each gift. Armor and staff transformed his appearance. The monkey who had wandered in human clothes while seeking a teacher now stood in the Dragon King's court like a prince who expected deference.

The Dragon Kings were not pleased by the visit. Wukong had demanded gifts with the pressure of a superior fighter, treated their property as though it were available for his choosing, and disturbed their palace with demonstrations of the iron staff. He left in excellent humor. His hosts knew that they had been bullied. When he returned to Flower-Fruit Mountain, the dragons began considering how to complain to the higher government of Heaven.

Back at the cave, Wukong displayed the staff to his followers. He made it gigantic, then small. His troop could see that no ordinary invader would casually disarm their king. He joined his friends in feasting. Other powerful creatures came to visit him. The mountain was peaceful enough for drinking, games, and boasting, and the king had every reason to believe that his earlier worries had been solved.

Then two officials appeared in his sleep and took him away. Their work belonged not to the Dragon King's palace but to the underworld. The monkey discovered that the freedom he had won over living creatures did not protect him from the administration of the dead. He was being summoned to the realm of death, precisely the realm whose power had first driven him from his mountain in search of instruction.

He woke to the outrage of the situation. Was this why he had learned immortal arts? Was he to be dragged away by clerks with orders and ropes? He struggled, struck at the messengers, and demanded an explanation. The passage into the dark realm led him before its rulers. They were astonished to see an enraged visitor with a name and powers that did not fit an ordinary case. Wukong declared that he had escaped the lot of mortal creatures. Why, then, had their agents come for him?

The underworld officials pointed to their books. Their authority was written in registers. The kings might have great titles, but the decisive matter was the record in which a creature's birth and allotted death were entered. The monkey ordered them to bring out those ledgers. He examined entries belonging to different kinds of living beings until he found his own name and the span of years assigned to him. There it was in writing: the king of Flower-Fruit Mountain, who had thought himself beyond ordinary death, could still be summoned by an office that had never heard of his teacher's private instruction.

He did not merely argue that the record was wrong. He seized the brush and crossed out his entry. Then he struck out the records of the other monkeys as well. By erasing the names, he meant to erase the claim that death's officials held upon them. The underworld had summoned one monkey and discovered that he had taken the entire species' future into his own hands.

The officials could not readily arrest him. Wukong left the halls of death, crossed back toward the world of the living, and awoke among his companions. He told them of the strange journey and the names he had removed. The monkeys celebrated as though the story itself proved they would never have to die. For them the king's victory seemed absolute. No enemy had captured him; no ruler of the dead had stopped him; the books themselves had been changed.

But a ledger can be altered without settling the question of whether its owner has authority. In the novel this is not a modern dispute about bureaucracy. It is a scene in which registers, orders, and titles are part of the supernatural fabric of the world. Death has officials. The sea has a court. Heaven has a government able to hear petitions. Wukong has now offended more than one of those jurisdictions, and he has not tried to hide his name.

The Dragon Kings prepared a formal complaint. The rulers of the underworld did the same. Their memorials reached the Jade Emperor. A dangerous creature had entered the Eastern Sea, taken its extraordinary treasure, demanded finery, and disrupted the palace. The same creature had entered the world of the dead, intimidated its magistrates, and wiped out names from the registers. These were not small private quarrels. They challenged the order through which the divine rulers claimed to manage their universe.

There was an obvious response: send an army to suppress him. But the court contained officials capable of subtler advice. One was the Gold Star of Venus, an elder celestial figure who proposed bringing the monkey to Heaven and giving him a place in its service. An unruly being might be easier to manage inside an institution than beyond its reach. A title and a job could turn a threat into a subordinate, or at least provide a period of peace.

The Jade Emperor accepted the proposal. The Gold Star traveled down to Flower-Fruit Mountain bearing an invitation. He did not arrive with an army intending immediate battle. He approached Wukong as a messenger from the celestial sovereign. The king heard that Heaven was willing to welcome him and give him an office. He was delighted. No one had needed to defeat him, and his adventures had apparently earned him recognition from the ruler of the skies.

The offer seemed to confirm the monkey's entire career. He had found a home, escaped death, gained a master, learned transformations, defeated a raider, acquired an astonishing weapon, and frightened the rulers of the sea and underworld. Now the greatest court in existence was inviting him inside. In his eyes, the invitation was an honor paid to a being of exceptional ability.

In the celestial officials' eyes, it was a management strategy. Those two ways of understanding the same act would soon collide. A monkey who believed that rank should match prowess was about to encounter a government whose ranks, salaries, titles, and duties were decided by authorities who had never leaped through a waterfall.
