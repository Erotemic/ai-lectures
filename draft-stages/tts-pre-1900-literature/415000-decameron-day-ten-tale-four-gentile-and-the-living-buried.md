# The Decameron — The Woman in the Tomb

Messer Gentile of Bologna loves a woman who has married Niccoluccio Caccianimico. She does not return his love. Gentile leaves for a time to govern elsewhere, but he hears that the woman has died suddenly while pregnant and has been buried. Grief draws him back. He goes to the tomb, longing even to see the woman once more. When he opens the burial place, he discovers a faint sign of life. She has not died after all.

He carries her away and brings her to his mother's care. Together they restore her health. She has been placed in a grave after a condition mistaken for death, not brought back from a literal death. As she recovers, Gentile assures her that she will not be compelled to accept his love. He asks only that she stay with his household long enough for him to arrange a public restoration to her family. She agrees, though she longs to tell her relatives that she is alive. Before the arrangement is finished, she gives birth to the child she was carrying.

Gentile invites leading citizens to a banquet, including the husband who believes himself bereaved. He proposes a question: if one person abandons a servant believed beyond recovery, and another rescues and cares for that servant, who has the better claim afterward? The assembled guests agree that the rescuer has earned a claim. The husband himself endorses the conclusion. Gentile then brings in the restored woman and her child and reveals the analogy. The guests recognize her as someone they believed dead.

The dramatic unveiling temporarily gives Gentile enormous power. He can point to the care he provided and say that by the table's own judgment the rescued woman belongs to him. Instead he publicly renounces that claim and returns her to her husband. The family is reunited, and Gentile is honored for generosity. A situation that could have been described as a lover's victory ends with a lover's refusal to possess the person he rescued.

The praise is not difficult to understand. Gentile saves a life, nurses a mother and child, and resists using their vulnerability to press a forbidden desire. Yet the language of the banquet is unsettling. The woman's fate is argued through the example of a servant transferred between masters. The audience of men debates competing claims before she is allowed to be publicly recognized as a person in her own right.

Lauretta's story therefore makes an instructive case for the last day's theme. Gentile's magnanimity is a genuine relinquishment within the fiction: he gives up an opportunity for personal gratification, and the woman returns to the life she wants. At the same time, the theatrical form reveals how readily her body becomes the apparent object of male ownership. Reading the story well means recognizing both features, without forcing the tale either into unqualified moral perfection or simple cynicism.
