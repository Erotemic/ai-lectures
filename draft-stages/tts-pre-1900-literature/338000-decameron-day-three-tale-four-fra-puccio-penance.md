# The Decameron — Fra Puccio’s Penance

Fra Puccio is a devout Florentine householder with an interest in securing the salvation of his soul. His title does not mean that he is a cloistered monk; he has attached himself to religious practices while continuing to live at home with his wife, Isabetta. As his attention turns toward penance and instruction, her life becomes narrower. He is absorbed in exercises of piety while she is left alone with ordinary needs and a marriage that offers little companionship.

A priest called Dom Felice, recently returned from Paris, is received warmly by Puccio. Felice sees that Isabetta is attractive and that her husband will follow a confident religious teacher. He devises an exercise that sounds both solemn and demanding. Each evening Puccio is to stand in a prescribed posture, with his arms spread in the form of the cross, reciting prayers and maintaining his discipline for hours. His reward, according to Felice, will be a shortened road to blessedness.

Puccio is delighted. The ceremony gives his yearning for holiness a concrete task. It demands physical effort, regularity, and unquestioning obedience. He is so eager to follow it that he does not ask why his instructor should need such intimate knowledge of the household's evenings. While Puccio remains busy with the posture and the prayers, Dom Felice visits Isabetta and begins an affair with her. Each night the husband believes he is advancing toward paradise; the priest is using the same hours for his own pleasure.

The comedy depends on the terrible exactness with which the instructions work. A perfectly obedient pupil has become easier to deceive than a negligent one. Felice does not need to force his way past a locked door. He arranges for the person who might guard it to be occupied by an activity that looks virtuous. The husband's body is present in the house but mentally and physically committed elsewhere.

Isabetta, however, is not a mere object in the arrangement. She has desires and frustrations that are important to the story. Still, the opportunity is provided by a priest exploiting the religious trust of another person. The tale allows enjoyment of the trick while also letting us see the professional and emotional asymmetry on which it relies. Dom Felice presents himself as a guide to eternal happiness, but his competence is exercised chiefly in arranging a temporal affair.

Panfilo's narration joins several of the day's concerns. People seek something earnestly; the desire may be admirable in itself; the means chosen may defeat the goal. Puccio's wish to become a better Christian is not the joke. The joke is that he accepts an arbitrary routine as a guarantee of holiness and lets a more worldly man define its terms. He has confused the possession of a rule with the exercise of judgment.

For a listener encountering medieval religious life, it is worth retaining the difference between the tale's satire and actual theological teaching. Boccaccio has not documented a church-approved shortcut to sanctity. He has invented a credulous man and an opportunist who speaks in the vocabulary of spiritual discipline. The invented posture exposes the distance between authoritative-sounding advice and moral credibility. A person can follow every instruction and still have failed to ask who benefits from the instruction's design.
