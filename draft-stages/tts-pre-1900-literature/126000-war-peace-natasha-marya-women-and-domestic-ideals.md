# War and Peace — Natasha, Marya, and the Costs of Domestic Ideals

Natasha Rostov and Princess Marya Bolkonskaya begin from different positions in the novel. Natasha is young, expressive, rich in family attention, and increasingly attractive to society. Marya is older, pious, financially secure but constrained by a severe father who uses education as a form of discipline. Their lives intersect through Prince Andrei, but their friendship becomes more important than that one connection. Reading them together shows both what Tolstoy values in care and what he takes for granted about women's permitted futures.

Natasha's first ball offers a remarkable account of social exposure. She has entered a world in which a young woman must be seen in order to enter its system of marriage. Her longing for a dance is not frivolous. It is entangled with access to recognition, future security, and an adult self. Andrei's attention gives her happiness, but it also binds her future to decisions controlled by him and his father. The one-year delay highlights a difference in power disguised as proper procedure.

The affair with Anatole makes this imbalance more dangerous. Anatole is already married and conceals the fact. His family and friends are capable of arranging a girl's ruin as entertainment. Natasha responds recklessly to his attention, and the novel permits the consequences to be painful. Yet a criticism that simply calls her immoral would ignore the unequal information and social consequences. A man can leave the scene. Natasha must remain in the society that has witnessed her disgrace. Pierre's tenderness matters because it refuses to let the scandal exhaust her identity.

Marya's story raises a different question: what happens when a woman is trained to treat self-denial as her most reliable virtue? At Bald Hills she must care for a father who diminishes her and expects gratitude for the privilege of obeying him. Her religious convictions sustain her, but do not erase her anger or longing for affection. The moment she is endangered by the invasion, she is discovered and protected by Nikolai Rostov. It may seem like a romantic rescue. It is also an occasion to ask how much control Marya possessed before and after her protector arrived.

During Andrei's final illness, Natasha and Marya learn a practice of mutual care. One knows him as a former beloved, the other as a brother. They do not need to agree on a complete explanation of his dying to remain with him. The women are at their most consequential when they attend to someone who can give them neither status nor promised happiness. Tolstoy renders this attentiveness with seriousness that conventional histories of war often reserve for command and combat.

The epilogue is both moving and contentious. Natasha is absorbed in marriage and motherhood, and the narrator presents her domestic devotion as a culmination of her capacities. Some readers hear a profound depiction of changed desires; others hear the confinement of a woman who once sang, danced, and seemed to promise a broader life. The two readings need not cancel one another. A character can freely love her children and still inhabit a social imagination that restricts what kinds of fulfillment the novel recognizes for women.

Marya and Nikolai form a household marked by affection and disagreements over the treatment of people under their authority. Marya's moral discomfort can challenge Nikolai, but it does not turn the estate into a community of equals. Sonya's position makes another absence visible. She has loved Nikolai and helped raise the Rostov children, yet the marriage that might have given her a secure future does not happen. Her devotion is interpreted by others in ways that keep the family comfortable. A reader should listen for whose sacrifice becomes a background condition of somebody else's happiness.

Tolstoy's women are not reducible to symbols of virtue or temptation. Natasha acts unwisely and generously; Marya feels anger and love; Sonya is constrained by gratitude and dependence; Helene deploys social power in ways the novel often condemns. The novel's achievement lies in making many of these experiences vivid. Its limitation lies in the narrowness of some ideals through which the narrator judges them. Serious reading requires holding achievement and limitation together, rather than treating an exquisite scene of care as evidence that all questions of gender have been settled.
