# Journey to the West: The turtle's question and the wet scriptures

## Eighty is not eighty-one

The pilgrims were traveling east by a path they had never been able to follow on the way west. Eight heavenly guardians carried them on a wind, high above mountains and rivers that had once cost them weeks of fear and effort. They had obtained the scriptures: five thousand forty-eight written scrolls, packed into bundles that Xuanzang watched as carefully as he had watched the emperor's travel document. The purple-gold begging bowl had remained behind at the repository, surrendered in exchange for the texts. There was still time to bring the scriptures back to the Tang within the appointed reckoning.

Guanyin, however, was counting something else. The story of the pilgrimage had become a list of ordeals, each recorded by the guardian beings appointed to observe Xuanzang's progress. The names of the trials recalled the entire road: the tiger at the frontier, the fire at the monastery, the missing robe, the Yellow Wind demon, the River of Flowing Sand, the ginseng tree, the White Bone Demon and Wukong's dismissal, the king changed into a tiger, the cave of the Gold and Silver Horns, the struggle with Red Boy, the false Buddha at Thunderclap, the fires of Flaming Mountain, and all the many ways a man could lose the road while believing he had found it.

The list continued through lion kings, rhinoceros spirits, false brides, robbery and arrest. At the end came the passage at Cloud-Reaching Ford, where Xuanzang had left his mortal body behind. The count was eighty. But the ordained number for the journey's completion was eighty-one: nine times nine. One ordeal remained. The guardians had carried the travelers away with their scriptures, yet the full course of the journey had not been closed.

Guanyin sent word to the heavenly escorts. The wind stopped. Without warning, the four pilgrims, their horse and their bundles of scripture were set down on the earth. Bajie laughed at the abrupt change in fortune. They had been traveling faster than any horse and now were standing in the middle of nowhere. Sha looked about and listened. He heard a wide river. This was not the River of Flowing Sand, where he had once lived, but Heaven-Reaching River, the great water they had crossed years earlier with the aid of a white turtle.

Xuanzang recognized the place. On the eastern shore stood the village of the Chen family, whose children he and his companions had saved from a river spirit demanding sacrifices. On the western shore there was no village and no boat. They were on the wrong side to find their old hosts. Bajie complained that heavenly messengers were no better than earthly carriers if they could abandon passengers in such a place. Sha suggested that, since Xuanzang had now crossed the ford of release, they might simply fly him over. Wukong said no. He understood more than he chose to explain. There was another crossing to be made.

## An old friend in the water

As the companions walked beside the river, a voice called to them. It came from a large white head rising out of the water: the old turtle who had once carried the pilgrims across Heaven-Reaching River. He had waited many years for their return. Wukong laughed in recognition and greeted him. Xuanzang remembered his kindness, and the party welcomed the unexpected help.

The turtle climbed onto the bank so that they could settle their loads. The white horse stood on his shell; the disciples took positions beside it, and Xuanzang held to the horse near its neck. Wukong stood near the turtle's head, giving directions as if he were once again a ferryman's overseer. The great creature moved out into the water, his feet beating a path beneath the surface. His broad shell stayed steady in the current. He had carried them once before without payment or harm and seemed pleased to be entrusted with them again.

They traveled slowly enough for the river's changing light to show upon their packs. The western bank faded behind them. Xuanzang could see the eastern shore drawing nearer. He had walked a long way since he had first placed his feet upon this turtle's back. Then he had no scripture to protect and did not know whether he would ever see his homeland again. Now he had the scrolls and a body transformed by the final ford. Yet there was a request from his earlier life that he had not fulfilled.

As the shore came close, the turtle asked it at last.

"When you were before the Buddha, did you remember to ask about me? I wanted to know how many years of life I have left, and when I might be released from this form. Did you ask?"

Xuanzang looked down at the broad white shell below his feet. He had promised. He had remembered the promise while the mountain was still a dream and the road seemed limitless, but at Jade-True Monastery he had been occupied with the approach, at the bottomless ferry with his fear, and at Thunderclap with the audience, the scriptures, the blank pages, and the return for the written texts. The old turtle's question had never come to his lips.

He could have invented an answer. The turtle had no way to know what had been said before the Buddha, and a comforting falsehood might have carried them safely to shore. Xuanzang did not speak one. He hesitated. The silence lasted long enough for the turtle to understand.

The great creature shook his body. All four pilgrims, the white horse and the scripture packs slid into Heaven-Reaching River. There was a crash of water, a confusion of limbs, and the scrambling of people who had believed themselves almost home. Wukong hauled his master up and found the bank. Bajie and Sha knew how to move in water. The white horse was a dragon and did not drown. Xuanzang, who had shed his former mortal body, was no longer vulnerable as before. But the books had no such protection. The packs were soaked.

## A night beside the river

As soon as the pilgrims reached land, a storm gathered. Winds rose from the river. Thunder rolled, lightning struck through the clouds, and a heavy darkness covered the bank. Sha pressed himself over the bundles, Bajie held the horse, Xuanzang tried to shield the books with his body, and Wukong stood guard with the iron staff. The storm felt unlike ordinary weather. It was as if every remaining power that had opposed the transmission of the scriptures made one last attempt to take them.

All night the disciples remained at their posts. The monkey's staff moved through rain and wind. Xuanzang thought of the book bundles, of the begging bowl he had relinquished, and of the days when a demon's cave had seemed like the most dangerous place in the world. Now a piece of paper might be destroyed by water without any demon appearing at all. The night passed slowly. At sunrise the storm ended.

They brought the wet volumes onto flat stones in the morning light and spread them to dry. Pages lay open across the rock. Xuanzang inspected the writing, and Sha helped him separate the leaves. Bajie set out the garments and bedding. The pilgrims had reached a point where the supernatural transport of Heaven could move them across the sky, yet ordinary sunlight was needed to save their books.

As they gathered them again, a few sheets from the Sutra of the Buddha's Life stuck to the stone. Pulling them free tore part of the writing away. Xuanzang was dismayed. After all the losses and recoveries of the road, the scriptures themselves had suffered damage within sight of the eastern shore. Wukong told him that even the world was not made complete without defect; he was not to destroy himself over the broken edge of a few pages. The place came to be remembered as the stone where the scriptures were dried, and the tale links the damaged pages to that final mishap.

The old turtle had not returned to apologize. Nor had Xuanzang been able to supply the answer the creature needed. The last ordeal was not a clean victory over a villain. It began in the master's own neglect, in a promise made to someone who had helped when no other crossing was possible. That mattered even after the Buddha's scriptures were in his hands.

## The Chen children grown

Fishermen from the nearby village recognized the travelers. Word spread that the monks who had saved the Chen children had come back from the western mountain and were drying books beside the river. Members of the Chen family hurried to the shore and begged the pilgrims to visit them. Xuanzang wanted to remain with the drying scrolls and leave as soon as they were safe, but the invitations were too sincere to refuse without pain. The family helped carry the bundles into the village.

The children they had once rescued were there, no longer little victims waiting to be offered to a river monster. Chen Guannbao and the girl called A Weigh of Gold came forward to bow to their rescuers. Their lives had continued. The household had built a temple of thanksgiving called Life-Saving Temple and installed images of the four pilgrims, so that the memory of their protection would outlast those who had witnessed it. The old sacrificial shrine of the river spirit had been pulled down.

Xuanzang saw the statues and felt the embarrassment of a man looking at a painted version of himself made more dignified than he could ever appear after a journey. Wukong examined his likeness. Sha said that Bajie's figure was especially accurate. Bajie was delighted to find himself honored, although the sculptor had made the master's face much more handsome than any old traveler was likely to be. They were invited to inspect the temple, accept food, and hear the family tell how the village had prospered since the old sacrifice ended.

At the banquet, the companions found that their appetites had changed. Xuanzang had shed his mortal condition, Wukong was never dependent on ordinary cooked meals, and Sha took little interest in the feast. Even Bajie announced that he had less desire to eat than before. Wukong raised an eyebrow. After a few more invitations, Bajie managed several further dishes and a considerable number of buns, proving that the change of appetite had its limits.

The village insisted on one farewell after another. Every household wished to feed the pilgrims. Xuanzang was grateful, yet he had already lived through the generosity of Kou Hong and knew how easily a celebration could hold the travelers for weeks. The scriptures were ready, their pages dried as well as they could be. Late at night he spoke quietly to Wukong. They should leave while their hosts were sleeping; otherwise the road home might be delayed again by the kindness of people they did not want to disappoint.

The disciples gathered their packs and led the horse to the gate. It was locked. Wukong used a little spell to open it, and the four slipped into the dark road. They had barely gone beyond the buildings when a voice sounded from the sky. The eight heavenly guardians had returned.

"Travelers who try to escape us," they called, "come along."

A wind lifted the scriptures, the white horse and the companions. The old river fell away beneath them. The Chen family would wake to empty rooms and prayers offered before the images of men already moving beyond the reach of any ordinary road. The final ordeal was over. This time the heavenly escort carried them eastward without setting them down for another crossing.
