# Journey to the West: Yellow Wind Ridge, the injured disciple, and Lingji’s rescue

## Journey to the West — A House at Sundown and a Disciple Who Was Hungry

The road was warmer now. The company had left Pagoda Mountain behind, and the teaching that Crow-Nest Chan Master had given Xuanzang traveled with him as surely as his staff and robe. The monk repeated the Heart Sutra as he rode. Ahead and behind him, the two disciples were settling into habits that neither had quite chosen.

Wukong could go for hours without speaking of food. A mountain pass drew his attention, and a distant cloud gave him reason to look toward the sky. Bajie, who carried the luggage, measured the day differently. He noticed how far they had walked since breakfast, the weight on his shoulder, and the absence of houses where a traveler might beg for a meal. He had been a member of the party only a short while, and had already begun to learn that a promise to seek the scriptures could demand attention to very ordinary discomforts.

Evening came while they were still on the road. The sun dropped toward the mountains. Xuanzang saw a household beside the track and suggested that they ask for lodging. Bajie agreed so quickly that Wukong laughed. Was the new disciple homesick already? Had he left Gao Village only to spend the journey longing for a well-stocked kitchen?

Bajie protested. He was hungry; that was all. Wukong might live on wind and clouds, but a pig who carried bags needed a supper. He had hardly spoken of returning home. At the hint of complaint, Xuanzang grew stern. If the new disciple preferred the comforts of a settled household, he should say so rather than profess a vow he could not keep.

The remark frightened Bajie. He dropped to his knees and assured the monk that he had accepted Guanyin's direction freely. He intended to serve Xuanzang all the way to the western land. He was not withdrawing from his promise. Could a disciple not mention an empty stomach without being accused of abandoning the scriptures?

Xuanzang relented. Bajie sprang up, took the luggage onto his shoulder again, and walked toward the house, muttering about how easily an innocent request for dinner had become a trial of religious sincerity.

At the gate an old man rested on a bamboo couch and softly repeated the Buddha's name. Xuanzang approached before his companions, bowed, and asked permission for three monks and a horse to stay the night. He explained that he had been sent from Tang China to seek the sacred writings in the west. The old man listened politely until he heard their destination. Then he shook his head. The western road was no place for them. They would do better, he said, to go east.

Xuanzang stood at a loss. Guanyin had sent him west; the emperor had given him the mission; each day of hardship had been endured for that destination. An old farmer now advised the opposite, as though the direction itself were an error. Wukong was not intimidated. There would be obstacles, certainly. That was no reason to turn around. They had come too far to accept advice that contradicted the whole purpose of the journey.

Other members of the household returned from the fields: younger men, a woman, and children. They had been planting rice. Finding a white horse and three strangers by their door, they crowded forward to ask questions. Bajie turned his head to see what was happening, and the children saw his long snout and enormous ears. They fled in terror. Their elders staggered backward as well. The old man hastened to reassure them: the travelers meant no harm; their appearances were alarming, but they were religious men seeking lodging.

Xuanzang was distressed that his disciples had terrified a family willing to help them. Bajie, quite proud of the change he believed his conversion had made, declared that he was much better looking than he had been at Gao Village. There had been a time, he said, when the mere sight of him could have scattered a whole crowd.

Wukong offered a practical improvement. Bajie could fold his ears back and conceal his snout. Bajie tried, making a heroic effort to tuck away features that did not wish to be tucked. Xuanzang objected that no one could be blamed for the face he had been born with. Wukong smiled. He had not said Bajie should acquire another face; only that he might arrange this one a little less dramatically.

Inside, the family prepared rice. Xuanzang gave thanks before eating. Wukong took what he needed. Bajie began at once, and while the monk was still finishing his prayers, the new disciple had emptied several bowls. The host's sons brought more. Bajie continued, faster than anyone could count. At last the rice prepared for the household was gone and he declared, with a dissatisfied sigh, that he was only partly fed.

The old man did not send them away. There was no luxury in the farm, but there was room beneath the gatehouse, where the travelers spread their bedding. Xuanzang lay down with the white horse secured nearby. Wukong kept watch as he always did. Bajie was finally quiet, though he had been given considerably less food than he thought his service deserved.

By morning the host had arranged another small meal. The pilgrims thanked the family and prepared to leave. The old man repeated that they should return if the road became dangerous. Wukong answered that they did not intend to retrace their steps. Then the three set out westward.

The house fell behind. It had given them a night of shelter, a table, and a warning. Before half the day had passed, they would understand why the old man had been afraid of the way ahead.

## Journey to the West — The Wind That Wukong Could Smell

Yellow Wind Ridge rose before the pilgrims in steep, folded slopes. Pines clung to the cliffs, and the road narrowed where the mountain pressed against it. Xuanzang drew up the white horse and searched for a pass that would let them cross without danger. The peak looked formidable even in calm weather.

Then the wind began.

At first it was a restless stirring among the trees. Leaves turned their pale undersides to the light. Dust came out of cracks in the path. Soon whole branches bowed, and the forest sounded as though a vast company were rushing through it. The wind drove loose earth over the stones and filled the valleys with a roaring unlike the ordinary movement of air.

Bajie caught Wukong by the arm. They ought to find cover. He had heard, he said, that a wise traveler avoided a terrible wind as readily as an arrow. Wukong laughed at the proverb. Would Bajie hide every time the weather changed? What would he do when a demon showed himself in person?

This was no ordinary gust, Bajie insisted. Wukong had already grown suspicious. He moved to one side, waited for the strongest rush to pass, and reached into its trailing edge. He closed his hand as if grasping something solid, then lifted it to his nose.

Bajie stared. A person could not take hold of the wind; it would slip away through his fingers. Wukong had no wish to debate the matter. He smelled the air he had caught and frowned. There was a taint in it, the odor of something living and dangerous. Whatever this wind had passed over, it had not come from an empty sky. The mountain was inhabited by creatures that would not welcome travelers.

The monk urged caution. He had only lately been given a teaching about letting go of fear, but the teaching did not make him impervious to falling rock or hostile beings. He guided the white horse along the ridge while the two disciples watched the slopes.

A tiger sprang out from below the path.

Its striped body was large enough to fill the narrow space between the rocks. Xuanzang cried out and fell from his saddle. Bajie dropped the luggage and drew the nine-toothed rake from behind his shoulder. Before Wukong could reach the beast, Bajie charged, shouting for it to stand and fight.

The tiger did stand, but not in the way an ordinary animal would. It rose upright on its hind legs, seized the skin across its chest, and pulled. The striped hide came away as though it were a coat. Underneath stood a red, fierce creature, with fangs, bristling hair, and eyes bright with malice. The beast had been a disguise.

The creature shouted that he was Tiger Vanguard, the patrol commander of Yellow Wind King's mountain. He had been sent to seize human travelers for the king's table. Who were these monks to attack an officer on his own master's road?

Bajie answered with more courage than ceremony. They served Xuanzang, a monk sent to retrieve the Buddhist scriptures. Tiger Vanguard could clear the pass and live, or remain and suffer the consequences of the rake. Bajie had already begun to treat the pilgrim's dangers as his own, though he had not lost the habit of announcing his bravery at maximum volume.

Tiger Vanguard lunged. For a moment he fought with claw and tooth, attempting to close the distance before Bajie could swing the heavy weapon. Bajie gave ground, then struck back. The creature wheeled and fled toward a tumble of rocks. There he snatched up a pair of red-copper blades concealed among the stones and turned to fight again.

Steel rang against the nine teeth of the rake. Bajie advanced, retreated, and advanced again, his ears streaming in the fierce air. He had wrestled the Great Sage through an entire night at Cloud Pathway Cave, and one armed demon was not enough to break his nerve. But Tiger Vanguard knew the ground, and he used every ledge and boulder to break the rhythm of Bajie's strikes.

Behind them Xuanzang remained shaken. Wukong helped him sit safely by the road. The new disciple, the one who had asked for supper and complained of carrying the luggage, was fighting for the party without being ordered to do it. Wukong watched long enough to see that Bajie could hold his own, then took out the iron staff.

He warned Xuanzang to remain where he was while the two of them drove off the intruder. The monk nodded and began to recite the Heart Sutra, seeking steadiness in its familiar phrases. Wukong bounded down the slope, calling to Bajie that they would press the enemy together.

The tiger demon saw two opponents approaching and shifted his footing. The ground belonged to him, and so did a trick that would soon turn the pilgrims' strength against them.

## Journey to the West — The Skin Left on the Stone

With Wukong's staff coming toward him and Bajie's rake rising from below, Tiger Vanguard began to lose the fight. He gave way, stumbled over the broken ground, and fled downhill. Both disciples pursued. Neither intended to let a creature who hunted travelers escape into the mountain and ambush someone else.

Then the demon changed shape. He rolled across the earth and appeared as a great tiger again. Stripes flashed among the rocks. Wukong and Bajie chased the animal, making their way toward a narrow place where a boulder rested beside the path. The tiger turned, seemed to crouch upon it, and suddenly stopped moving.

Wukong struck. The iron staff hit something so hard that the shock ran back into his hands. Bajie brought down the rake and felt its teeth jar against stone. They looked closer. A tiger skin had been spread over the boulder. There was no living creature beneath it.

Wukong recognized the device. Tiger Vanguard had used the skin as a substitute for himself. He had shed his appearance to leave the pursuers a false quarry. While the brothers were spending their strength on the empty hide, the real demon had slipped away in a rush of wind.

And Xuanzang had been left alone.

The disciples raced uphill. They found the white horse, the luggage, and the place where their master had been sitting. The monk was gone. Bajie's anger dissolved into alarm. Where would they search? Which of the many folds in the mountain had swallowed him?

Wukong refused to let panic settle into despair. Xuanzang had vanished in the space of a few moments. It was unlikely that Tiger Vanguard had carried him beyond the entire mountain. They would find the lair, demand his release, and fight whatever stood between them and the monk. He told Bajie to bring the horse and bags and stay close.

In the same hours that the disciples searched the ridges, the captive was being borne toward a cave. Tiger Vanguard had returned to the road on the wind, found Xuanzang reciting the sutra, seized him, and carried him away. The monk had scarcely seen the danger arrive. One moment his companions were shouting somewhere below; the next the ground had disappeared beneath him.

At the entrance to a guarded cavern the demon resumed his ordinary monstrous form and instructed the sentries to announce that Tiger Vanguard had brought a prisoner. The cave belonged to Yellow Wind King. Its entrance was cut into rock, and behind it servants, warriors, and lesser animals moved under the command of the cave's ruler.

Tiger Vanguard presented Xuanzang with the satisfaction of a soldier who had fulfilled his orders. The monk was not simply any traveler, he explained. He was the religious brother of the Tang emperor, bound for the western scriptures. His disciples included a long-snouted warrior with a rake and the famous monkey named Sun Wukong. Tiger Vanguard had deceived them both. Here was the prize of his patrol.

Yellow Wind King was startled rather than delighted. Sun Wukong was not a name to be dismissed. News of that little monkey's impossible strength had traveled far beyond Flower-Fruit Mountain. The king wanted to know how his officer had managed to bring Xuanzang in while such a guardian stood beside him. Tiger Vanguard described his struggle, his feigned escape, and the trick with the skin.

The subordinate urged that the prisoner be cooked at once. Yellow Wind King refused. He had not captured Xuanzang himself, and he feared the noise that Wukong and Bajie would make if they reached the cave. Better to wait several days, he said, until the disciples gave up or departed. Then they could prepare the monk without interruption.

Orders were given. Attendants bound Xuanzang to a post in the rear garden, a place named for calming the wind. The ropes tightened around his body. There was no meal, no welcome, and no sign that he could persuade these captors to let him continue his journey. The only reason he had not yet been eaten was that the king feared the men outside.

Xuanzang called helplessly for his disciples. He remembered how readily Wukong fought, how much noise Bajie made, and how weak he himself had been when the tiger first appeared. He did not know that the two of them were moving through the mountain in search of him. He knew only that the next days would decide whether his pilgrimage ended in a demon's kitchen.

At last Wukong and Bajie came upon a cavern beneath the cliffs. It was set among trees and wild growth, too fortified and too full of strange activity to be the dwelling of ordinary shepherds. Wukong studied its entrance. The two brothers had been tricked once by rushing after their prey. This time they would divide their work. Bajie would watch the horse and keep the baggage out of sight; Wukong would challenge the guards and learn whether their master was inside.

Bajie did not object. The same strength that had carried him through the fight would now be needed to hold a position, and he had begun to understand that guarding the luggage and the white horse could be as necessary as striking a blow.

Wukong left him in a sheltered hollow and walked to the entrance with his iron staff. He had no intention of being fooled by a loose skin a second time.

## Journey to the West — Tiger Vanguard's Last Escape

Wukong stood before the entrance to Yellow Wind Cave and called for its master to send out the monk. The sentries began to shout. Deep inside, the noise reached the chambers where Tiger Vanguard had been congratulating himself on his capture.

The guard who hurried to him described a small, fierce monkey with a staff, demanding the release of the Tang monk and threatening to break the cave apart. Tiger Vanguard took up his copper blades. He had tricked Sun Wukong once. Surely a creature clever enough to seize the master could deal with the disciple at the door.

He went out with a company of lesser demons carrying flags and drums. The flags flapped in the mountain air; the drums beat against the stone. It was an attempt at a proper military display, but the sight of Wukong waiting alone seemed only to provoke him further. Tiger Vanguard demanded to know why an insignificant monk had the nerve to disturb his king's household.

Wukong answered that a bandit who abducted his master had lost the right to complain about bad manners. He knew perfectly well who stood before him. The two blades were the same ones that had met Bajie's rake. The skin was the same empty disguise that had drawn them from Xuanzang's side. The creature would return the monk or answer for the deceit.

Tiger Vanguard confirmed that he had taken the pilgrim and boasted that the monk would soon become a meal for his ruler. Wukong's anger sharpened. He lifted the staff and advanced.

This contest was shorter than the earlier fight. Tiger Vanguard's blades flashed in front of his face. Wukong pressed through the defenses and drove him backward. The demon threw up gusts and dust, but no imitation tiger could now persuade Wukong to forget the opponent in front of him. The staff struck against the copper swords again and again. Before long Tiger Vanguard's arms weakened and his steps became clumsy.

The creature fled. He did not turn back toward the cave, where he had already claimed an easy victory over the Great Sage. Instead he ran up the slope, hoping to vanish into the rocks beyond the fighting. Wukong followed, shouting threats over the clatter of the demon's retreat.

There was a chance here that neither warrior could see from the narrow path. Higher on the ridge Bajie had settled in a concealed hollow with the white horse. The bags lay close at hand, and he was waiting for word from his senior disciple-brother. His instructions had been to stay where he was. He had obeyed them.

Bajie heard the pursuit before he saw the figures. A rush of feet came over the stone, then the clang of a blade striking iron. Tiger Vanguard appeared around the bend, running so hard that he scarcely looked toward the hollow. Wukong followed him, staff raised.

Bajie recognized the fleeing demon at once. He set the horse safely aside, stepped out of concealment, and brought down the nine-toothed rake across the creature's path. Tiger Vanguard had escaped one attack by trading places with a tiger skin. He could not escape this one. The rake struck him, and the wound was fatal.

Wukong reached the body a moment later. He saw Bajie standing over their enemy, breathing heavily, the rake still in his hands. The Great Sage was delighted. If Bajie had not been there, the demon might have found some new way to slip away. Instead the two of them had caught him between pursuit and a guarded retreat.

Bajie wanted to be certain. Was this really the creature who had carried off Xuanzang? Wukong assured him it was. He added that the younger disciple deserved the credit for finishing the fight. It was a compliment Bajie enjoyed hearing from a brother who had mocked his appetite and ears at every opportunity.

They had, however, won only part of what mattered. Tiger Vanguard was dead, but the monk was still a prisoner. Wukong explained that the cave's ruler intended to eat him. The death of the patrol officer would not in itself undo the ropes in the rear garden.

Bajie agreed to continue guarding the horse and the baggage. He offered to intercept the king as he had intercepted the vanguard if Wukong could drive him back up the ridge. Wukong took hold of the dead tiger and dragged it toward the entrance as proof that his threats could not be dismissed.

Behind the gate, survivors of the escort scattered through the tunnels with torn flags and useless drums. They had gone out behind a confident commander and returned without him. The message they brought to Yellow Wind King was plain: Sun Wukong was alive, the first attack had failed, and the great monkey was standing outside the cave with the body of its best fighter.

The king now faced a different choice. He could stay hidden while the monkey broke his gates, or go out and fight. He called for his armor and his long three-pronged fork.

Wukong waited at the entrance. The dead tiger lay at his feet. Somewhere behind those walls Xuanzang was still tied to a post, wondering whether the voices outside belonged to the servants who would cook him or the disciples who might save him.

## Journey to the West — The King Whose Breath Darkened the Sky

The company of lesser demons had retreated into Yellow Wind Cave with their drums broken and their flags torn. They told the king that Tiger Vanguard was dead and that Sun Wukong had dragged his body to the entrance. The ruler of the cavern did not at first rise from his seat. The tiger had been his own officer, sent to patrol the mountain. Now the monkey was not content with demanding his master's release. He had killed the officer as well.

Yellow Wind King called for his armor. The attendants brought him a golden helmet, a breastplate, and a three-pronged weapon. He came out with the appearance of a commander who believed his rank should command respect. Wukong stood at the gate beside the fallen tiger, hardly reaching the demon's chest.

The king stared at him. Was this the Sun Wukong whose name had traveled through Heaven and earth? He had expected a creature of extraordinary size. In front of him stood a small monkey, lean and fierce, with a dark staff in his hands. He mocked Wukong's stature. The Great Sage answered that height could be changed whenever he wished. If the king needed a larger adversary to take him seriously, he could provide one.

Yellow Wind King raised his fork and struck toward the monkey's head. Wukong bent and lengthened his body until he towered over the weapon. The king stopped in surprise. Wukong laughed at him for being so easily impressed and invited him to fight properly.

They fought first on the ground, then in the air above the cave. The king's three sharp points drove toward Wukong's chest; the iron staff twisted aside and returned toward the helmet. Blow met blow. For thirty exchanges neither opponent forced the other to yield. This was no unarmed roadside tiger, and Wukong had reason to respect the skill in the king's grip.

But respect did not lessen his determination. Xuanzang was still bound inside. Wukong had been appointed to guard him, and every moment of combat left the monk in the hands of captors. The Great Sage pulled a handful of hair from his body, chewed it, blew it outward, and commanded it to change.

Small Wukongs sprang into being all around the king. Each carried an iron staff. They rushed him from every direction. To the demon below, the single monkey had become a ring of attackers. There was no room to concentrate on a single weapon; every turn exposed another side to a blow.

The king had a defense of his own. He turned toward the quarter from which the wind would rise, opened his mouth, and blew. The first rush of air struck the circling monkeys, sending them spinning. Then came the true Yellow Wind. Dust leapt off the ground. Pines bent toward breaking. Rocks shook along the slopes. The cloud of small Wukongs whirled like scraps caught above a furnace, their staffs useless in the air.

The storm spread far beyond the cave. In the novel's great vision of the gale, rivers are troubled, ships struggle against their ropes, travelers lose their way, and even heavenly beings are thrown into confusion. A sun that should have shone overhead darkened. Places that had seemed securely fixed in the world trembled before the blast.

Wukong recalled his hairs. Their borrowed bodies vanished back into his own. He pushed forward alone, staff in hand, determined not to surrender merely because an enemy had changed the weather. The king leaned toward him again and sent another mouthful of wind directly into his face.

This time the injury was intimate. Grit and force struck the eyes that had endured the Eight-Trigrams Furnace. Wukong's fiery golden gaze squeezed shut. Tears flowed from the burning lids. He could hear the enemy; he could not see well enough to place his blows. He withdrew from the cave, not beaten by the fork but disabled by the wind.

Bajie was hidden among the rocks with the horse and baggage. From there he had felt the mountain shake. He had flattened himself against the ground while the gale roared overhead, praying that it would leave something standing. When it cleared he looked toward the cave and saw no fighters. He did not know who had won.

At last Wukong called to him. Bajie ran out and asked how a storm could have made so much destruction. Wukong answered through aching eyes. The king fought well, but his power lay in the wind, and the wind had injured him. Bajie asked what could be done about their master. Wukong spoke of finding a doctor for his eyes before attempting another attack.

The younger disciple stared at the ridges around them. A doctor, here? They had scarcely found a road or a place to sleep. Wukong knew that much, but he would not let the absence of a visible house decide the matter. They would seek shelter for the night and return in daylight. The demon had delayed eating Xuanzang precisely because he feared the disciples. There was still a chance to save him.

Bajie took up the baggage. Wukong kept his staff near one hand and the other near his painful eyes. The two left the cave behind, not victorious and not abandoning their master. For the first time since Bajie joined him, the Great Sage needed his brother to help him through the dark.

## Journey to the West — The House That Wasn't There at Dawn

Wukong and Bajie made their way down from Yellow Wind Cave as the light faded. Wukong could scarcely open his eyes. The tears came even when he held still, and the pain grew whenever he tried to look into the wind. Bajie led the horse and carried the bags, stopping now and then to ask whether his senior brother could see the path.

The Great Sage found the question insulting. He was not blind, he insisted; he had been struck by a demonic gust. But the next stone was difficult to judge, and he had little choice but to listen when Bajie warned him of a drop beside the track.

They heard dogs barking below the southern slope. Among the trees shone a few lights. There was a farmstead with a gate, a yard, and people moving about within. It looked wonderfully ordinary after the roaring mountain and the demon's cavern. The disciples approached with care and called for admittance.

An old householder came out accompanied by young workers who carried farming tools. In those hills an unexpected knock could mean raiders, wild beasts, or demons, and the men had not come empty-handed. Wukong bowed and explained that they were disciples of a pilgrim monk. Their master had been seized by a monster; they had fought to recover him; darkness had overtaken them before they could succeed. Would the household permit them to sleep there?

The old man apologized for the armed welcome. This was dangerous country, he said. They were welcome to come inside. A place was found for the horse. The luggage was set down. The brothers were offered tea, followed by bowls of food made with sesame. For Bajie the meal was a relief. For Wukong the greater relief was a room where the wind no longer touched his burning face.

When preparations for sleeping began, Wukong asked if there were anyone nearby who sold medicine for the eyes. The host was puzzled. Which of the two monks was ill? Wukong admitted that the Yellow Wind King had blown into his face during a fight and that his eyes had been watering ever since.

The old man's manner changed. The wind of that demon, he said, was not a seasonal breeze or a mountain draft. It was a supernatural force capable of making the world dark and striking terror into gods and spirits. Ordinary men could scarcely survive it. Bajie began to name various ailments, hoping the old man would recognize an illness that could be cured. The host would not be distracted. Their enemy, he explained, possessed the samadhi divine wind.

Wukong answered that he was not an ordinary man. His eyes had survived fires stronger than those that cooked an ordinary meal, and his life was not easily ended. All the same, the pain was real. He needed something to soothe it.

The householder could help. He had once suffered from eyes that watered in the wind and had obtained a remedy from a wandering adept. From a small stone vessel he brought a medicated ointment. He touched a little to Wukong's eyes with a narrow implement and instructed him to keep them closed until morning. There would be no need to hunt for an eye doctor farther down the mountain.

Bajie prepared a bed for his senior brother and helped him settle onto it. Wukong, still proud and still irritable, reached about as though checking that the bedding was there. Bajie asked whether he would like a staff with which to feel his way around the room. Wukong snapped that he did not require the services of a guide for the blind. Bajie laughed quietly and lay down.

Through the night Wukong concentrated on his inner strength. He did not know the farmer's medicine or the identity of the person who had supplied it. He had accepted it because the pain was unbearable and because the host had offered it freely. Toward dawn the burning eased.

He opened his eyes. The world was bright, even clearer than before the storm. Wukong turned to thank the householder, and the entire house was gone.

There was no courtyard, no doorway, no beds or walls. Trees rose above the disciples, and they had been sleeping on grass. The horse stood tied beneath a branch, exactly where it had been left. The luggage lay beside Bajie's head. The mountain had not changed, but the place in which the brothers believed they had slept had disappeared.

Bajie woke and looked for the missing building. Perhaps, he suggested, the family had moved during the night to avoid tax collectors or local officials. But how could they have taken apart a whole farmstead without either of the sleeping travelers hearing a thing? The explanation only made him more confused.

Wukong found a written notice tied to a tree. It told them that their lodging had not belonged to ordinary householders. Guardian deities had transformed the place into a shelter, fed them, and supplied the medicine. They had done so to sustain the effort to rescue the pilgrim.

The Great Sage recognized the protectors who had been assigned to the pilgrimage. He grumbled that he had not called upon them recently and that now they were making a grand display of helpfulness. Bajie defended them. They had given the brothers food and medicine, and they had preserved the mission without demanding a reward. If this was the work of guardians who remained hidden by their orders, it was good work.

Wukong smiled. His eyes were healed. For all his talk about strength and rank, that fact mattered more than winning an argument over who should receive credit. He gave Bajie the horse and baggage to guard in the woods, then set off toward Yellow Wind Cave again. A day's defeat had taught him something useful: he could not solve every problem with a staff, and assistance might arrive from people he could not immediately see.

## Journey to the West — A Mosquito, a Bound Monk, and a Name Overheard

When Wukong reached Yellow Wind Cave on the second morning, he did not shout from the entrance. The guards had been warned to watch for him, and their king possessed a weapon that could overwhelm even his army of hair-made duplicates. He wanted first to see his master and learn how the demon intended to defend himself.

He changed into a small mosquito, slender-legged and almost invisible against the rock. A sentry slept near the entrance, snoring with his mouth open. Wukong landed on the fellow's face and bit him. The creature woke with a cry, swatted uselessly at the air, and complained of an enormous mosquito. By the time he stood and noticed the morning light, Wukong had slipped past him into the cave.

The entrance chambers were alive with preparations. Servants checked weapons. Officers carried messages. Yellow Wind King ordered his followers to be alert: if the monkey had survived yesterday's gust, he might return in person or bring powerful allies. The cave was a fortress expecting a siege.

The mosquito flew through the hallways and into the rear garden. There, tied firmly to the post, was Xuanzang. The monk had spent the night without knowing whether his disciples were alive. The ropes had left him exhausted, and he had scarcely any reason to believe his captors would release him voluntarily. His face was wet with tears.

Wukong landed on the monk's head and spoke softly. Xuanzang recognized the voice but could not locate its owner. Where was his disciple? Had he come alone? Was this another trick performed by the demons?

Wukong told him that he was sitting directly on his scalp in the shape of a tiny insect. He had come to make sure Xuanzang was still alive. Tiger Vanguard, the creature who had stolen him from the road, was dead by Bajie's rake. The ruler of the cave remained dangerous, however, and the wind had nearly overcome Wukong himself. They would not leave Xuanzang there; they needed to defeat the master of the cavern first.

Xuanzang asked when that might happen. He had already heard his captors discussing a meal they intended to make of him. Wukong did not promise that the ropes would fall away in an instant. Instead he asked the monk to keep his courage. He would find a means. He flew back through the door before the guards could notice anything unusual.

In the outer chamber the king was reviewing his officers. A scout hurried in carrying a command flag. He had seen the pig-faced disciple sitting in a grove nearby. He had not seen Wukong. Perhaps the Great Sage had been blown to death. Perhaps he had fled to seek help. The officers began to speculate. One hoped the problem was over. Another asked what would happen if the monkey brought a divine army.

Yellow Wind King dismissed the possibility of most rescuers defeating him. His wind was too strong. There was, he conceded, one being whose arrival he would fear: Lingji Bodhisattva. That name mattered enough for him to say it aloud in front of his servants.

Up near the roof beams the mosquito listened. He had come in search of the monk and learned the weakness of the captor. It was the kind of information a fighter could not obtain by striking the gate harder.

Wukong returned to the forest and appeared beside Bajie in his own shape. His brother had seen the scout and frightened him away but knew nothing else. Wukong described the bound monk and repeated the name he had overheard. They must find Lingji. The difficulty was that neither knew where the bodhisattva lived.

While they considered the problem, an old man approached along the road. He walked slowly but without a stick. His white hair and beard framed a face that had kept a surprising brightness of color. Bajie pointed him out. A traveler who had walked these roads might know the way to a nearby shrine; there was no harm in asking.

Wukong put away the staff and spoke respectfully. Did the elder know the abode of Lingji Bodhisattva? The old man pointed south. Far away, he said, stood Little Sumeru Mountain, where Lingji taught at a religious establishment. He described the narrow way that led toward it.

Wukong turned to study the path. When he looked back, the stranger had disappeared. A small message remained, identifying him as Taibai Jinxing, the celestial Gold Star, and mentioning the flying-dragon staff granted to Lingji.

Bajie bent low in gratitude. He remembered the star official who had once pleaded for his life in Heaven after his offense as Marshal Tianpeng. Without that intervention his story might have ended before he was ever born again as a pig. The appearance on the roadside was not simply helpful; it brought him face to face with someone to whom he owed a debt.

Wukong teased him for the sudden display of thanks, but he had also learned to recognize a gift. They had a direction, a name, and a sign of the power they needed. He instructed Bajie to keep the white horse and the baggage out of sight and not to become involved in trouble while he was away.

Bajie said he knew how to keep his head down when circumstances required it. Wukong took a leap into the air, rolled onto his cloud, and set off south toward Little Sumeru Mountain.

The monk in the cave had been promised that help was coming. Now Wukong had a plan worthy of the promise.

## Journey to the West — The Bodhisattva of Little Sumeru

Wukong's somersault cloud carried him over the ridges so quickly that the difficult land below became a blur of peaks and valleys. The old man's directions had named a place thousands of miles to the south. Such a distance would have been a grave expedition for a walking pilgrim. For the Great Sage it was a journey measured in a leap and a turn through the air.

He came to Little Sumeru Mountain, where clear air gathered around a religious retreat. A bell rang somewhere among the halls. The slopes were touched with incense smoke, and bright clouds hovered over the place where Lingji Bodhisattva taught. The mountain was quiet in a way Yellow Wind Ridge had not been. There were voices reciting scripture, but no clang of weapons or command to prepare a prisoner for the kitchen.

At the entrance Wukong met a religious attendant wearing beads. He greeted the man and asked whether Lingji was present. Yes, the attendant answered. Could he announce a visitor?

Wukong began to explain. He was Sun Wukong, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, disciple of Xuanzang, religious brother of the Tang emperor, on the western mission to receive the scriptures. His master had fallen into the hands of the Yellow Wind King, and he needed to see the bodhisattva at once.

The attendant interrupted gently. That was too many names and offices to remember. Would the visitor mind using a shorter introduction?

Wukong, whose pride had once filled the courts of Heaven, accepted a more modest title. Tell him, he said, that Sun Wukong, the disciple of the Tang monk, had come.

The message was carried inside. Lingji came out to receive him with courtesy, brought him into the hall, and offered refreshment. Wukong was in no mood for tea. His master was bound to a post in a demon's garden, he explained, and the captor's terrible wind had already injured his own eyes. The medicine that healed him had been a mercy, but not a victory. They needed a power capable of stopping the wind itself.

Lingji knew the demon. He had been assigned by the Buddha to restrain Yellow Wind King. The Buddha had equipped him with a wind-stilling pellet and a wondrous flying-dragon staff. The creature had been subdued before. On that earlier occasion Lingji had spared its life and allowed it to withdraw into the mountains, on the condition that it neither injured living beings nor committed fresh evil.

The bodhisattva listened as Wukong described the kidnapped monk, the slain Tiger Vanguard, and the storm that made ordinary combat impossible. It was clear that the previous arrangement had been violated. The king had not been living quietly; he had organized a predatory court and intended to devour a traveler. Lingji accepted the duty of bringing him to account.

Wukong hoped to leave immediately. The bodhisattva offered to feed him first, but the Great Sage declined. His master had waited through a night of captivity. A guest's meal in a peaceful sanctuary was a luxury he could not enjoy while the monk remained in danger.

Lingji took up the flying-dragon staff and went with him. As they approached Yellow Wind Ridge, he explained how the encounter should proceed. The demon feared him and might flee if he saw his captor coming. Wukong was to challenge the king openly, draw him from the cave, and keep his attention on the fight. Lingji would remain in the clouds until the decisive moment.

There was no shame for Wukong in following instructions. At Flower-Fruit Mountain he had once tried to win every contest by increasing the strength of his own blows. On this road he had already learned that Xuanzang's safety could require more than an individual victory. Guanyin had subdued the bear of Black Wind Mountain; the unseen guardians had healed Wukong's eyes; an old celestial official had shown him where to go. Now the same journey required a particular tool in the hands of a being entrusted to use it.

Wukong dropped from the cloud and landed at the cave entrance. He called for the Yellow Wind King to give up the captive. A guard appeared, saw the iron staff, and disappeared into the tunnels. The ruler took up his weapon again, furious that the monkey he thought he had defeated had come back to damage his gate.

Before the demon emerged, Wukong broke open the outer entrance with his staff. He wanted the king outside, where Lingji could see him clearly. The sound of the blow ran through the cavern. Inside, servants scrambled to report it, and the king's voice rose in anger.

Above them a cloud remained still. Lingji held the flying-dragon staff, waiting for Yellow Wind King to attempt the very power that had saved him the day before.

## Journey to the West — The Golden Dragon and the Yellow-Furred Marten

Yellow Wind King came out armed and angry. Wukong's staff had damaged his gate, and the monkey stood in the open with no visible allies. The king assumed that his earlier storm had failed only because he had not used enough force. This time he would blow the challenger from the mountain entirely.

He lunged with the three-pronged fork. Wukong turned aside, drove the iron staff toward his shoulder, and forced him to step back. For a few exchanges they fought exactly as they had before. The king still had the strength and skill to meet his enemy's blows. Wukong did not call out to the sky or reveal that Lingji was present.

The demon drew away from the staff and turned his head toward the direction from which he summoned his wind. His mouth opened. He was about to release the gust that had darkened the heavens, sent Wukong's doubles spinning, and wounded the Great Sage's eyes.

From above came the bodhisattva's staff.

As it fell, the weapon changed. The shape of polished wood became a golden dragon with eight claws. The dragon plunged through the air and caught the demon before the storm could form. Its claws closed around him. It lifted him, struck him against the rocks, and held him there until the commanding figure in armor was gone.

In his place lay a small yellow-furred marten, shuddering in the dragon's grasp.

Wukong advanced with the iron staff. He had crossed the mountain twice, watched his master vanish, fought the king and his officer, and suffered the wind that threatened to put out his sight. He was ready to finish the matter in a single blow.

Lingji stopped him. The creature would not be killed on the ridge. It was to be taken before the Buddha, who would judge the violation of the terms under which it had once been spared.

The bodhisattva explained the history. The marten had lived near the sacred mountain of Vulture Peak, where the Buddha taught. It had stolen clear oil from a glass lamp, dimming the light. Fearing punishment from the guardian warriors, it fled and acquired the powers of a demon. The Buddha had ordered Lingji to restrain it. The first capture had ended in mercy on condition that it cause no further harm. Now the animal had returned to predation and taken the scripture pilgrim prisoner.

The king had appeared before Wukong as a richly armored sovereign. He had commanded troops, accepted a captured human as food, and used a wind that made the whole world tremble. The small body now exposed by the dragon did not make his crimes smaller. It showed how much power a creature of modest origin had gathered, and how readily that power had been used against the weak.

Wukong lowered his staff. He thanked Lingji for the help. The bodhisattva departed with the captive, taking him westward to the place where judgment would be made. The great wind was gone. The cave remained, but its ruler would no longer answer from behind the gate.

Wukong went up the mountain to find Bajie. The younger disciple was still guarding the horse and baggage where he had been told to wait. He had seen the clouds move and heard the distant sound of combat, but he did not know its outcome. When he recognized his senior brother's voice, he hurried out to ask what had happened.

The Great Sage reported the capture. Lingji had used the flying-dragon staff, the feared king had been revealed as a yellow-furred marten, and the bodhisattva had carried him away. Bajie was delighted. For once the news was wholly good. Their enemy's power had been broken, and the way to their master stood open.

The two entered the cave together. They found its remaining inhabitants: rabbits, foxes, deer, and other creatures who had served the king's predatory household. Wukong and Bajie attacked them with staff and rake. The novel does not turn this into a quiet liberation of prisoners. The disciples kill the creatures within, then pass through the chambers toward the rear garden. Their rescue involves real violence, even after the principal demon has been taken away.

Xuanzang was still tied to the wind-stilling post. When he heard their voices, he raised his head. Bajie hurried to help free him while Wukong assured him that the king would not return. The monk wanted to know how they had succeeded when the power of the Yellow Wind had been so great. Wukong recounted the fight, the injury to his eyes, the unexpected medicine, the overheard name, and the visit to Lingji. His master listened with relief. He thanked the disciples whose persistence had brought him out alive.

The cave held food suitable for the monks. They ate before leaving, not because the danger had been forgotten but because Xuanzang needed strength after his confinement and the road offered no guarantee of a meal. Bajie, who had been thinking about supper when they first approached Yellow Wind Ridge, could finally eat without being accused of longing for Gao Village.

The horse was brought from its hiding place. The bags were repacked. Xuanzang mounted, and the brothers took their places, one scouting ahead and the other shouldering the burden. They left the broken entrance of Yellow Wind Cave behind and returned to the western road.

The three travelers had faced their first great danger together since Bajie's recruitment. The new disciple had killed the tiger who took their master and had guarded the horse while Wukong sought help. Wukong had fought, been injured, accepted medicine, gathered intelligence, and called upon an authority whose power matched the enemy's. Xuanzang had endured captivity without being able to free himself.

They were still far from the scriptures. But the road was open again. Ahead lay other mountains and, beyond them, the river monster whose coming had been foretold at Pagoda Mountain.
