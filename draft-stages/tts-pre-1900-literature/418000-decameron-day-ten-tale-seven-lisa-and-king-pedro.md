# The Decameron — The Love Song Reaching a King

Lisa is the daughter of Bernardo Puccini, a wealthy Florentine apothecary living in Palermo. She sees King Pedro of Aragon at a public celebration and is seized by love. She knows that her feelings cannot easily find a path to fulfillment. The king belongs to an incomparably higher rank, and she is not part of his court. She becomes so despondent that her family fears she will die. Neither money nor physicians' remedies bring her comfort.

Lisa asks for Minuccio d'Arezzo, a singer who is known to the king. Her father thinks she wants the consolation of music, but she has a specific purpose. In private she tells Minuccio her story and asks him to carry it to Pedro without exposing her to public shame. A song is composed to express what she cannot speak to the king herself. Minuccio performs it at court, and the sovereign understands that an unnamed young woman has put her life and hope into his hands.

Pedro does not respond by using the opportunity for an affair. He visits Lisa, comforts her and allows her to speak with dignity. The encounter helps her recover. He later returns with his queen and courtiers, honors Lisa publicly and announces that he will help settle her future. He chooses a young gentleman named Perdicone as her husband, provides the means for a distinguished marriage and offers his own continuing, ceremonial devotion as her knight. Lisa accepts the arrangement and obtains the queen's permission for the kiss the king offers as the only tribute he will ask of her affection.

The tale transforms private longing into a public relationship that avoids scandal. Lisa does not receive what her first passion imagined, but neither is she merely dismissed for having loved beyond her station. Her account is heard. Her feelings, carried through art, have become a reason for the king to honor rather than exploit a vulnerable subject.

The use of a song is essential. The court musician's performance makes an inward experience legible to someone whom Lisa cannot address directly. Boccaccio is writing a story about the power of crafted language within a larger work whose organizing premise is that stories can preserve human beings in a time of suffering. The song does not cure disease in a medical sense. It creates a social encounter, and the encounter alters the situation that had made her despair seem absolute.

Pampinea praises King Pedro's courtesy, but a reader can distinguish courtesy from perfect equality. Pedro remains the person who chooses Lisa's marriage, and the woman's words show how readily she submits her own will to his. The happy ending depends on his being humane and on others allowing her private feeling to become public without punishment. The story imagines generosity at the top of a hierarchy, and it asks what might happen when that hierarchy is governed by compassion rather than appetite.
