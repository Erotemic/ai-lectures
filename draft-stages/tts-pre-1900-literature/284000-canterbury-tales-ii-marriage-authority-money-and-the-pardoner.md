# Canterbury Tales II: marriage, authority, money, and the Pardoner

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Friar’s Tale, a Summoner and the Devil

The Wife of Bath has scarcely finished when the Friar promises to entertain the company with a story about a summoner. The real Summoner in the group protests. Officials of the church courts have the authority to summon people accused of religious or sexual offences, and their power can be a profitable opportunity for extortion. The Friar knows this, and he knows the Summoner cannot stop him from speaking. Their quarrel gives the story the feel of an attack made before the victim's eyes.

A summoner sets out on the road looking for a person from whom he can collect money. He encounters a young yeoman dressed in green. The stranger seems friendly and familiar with the pleasures of cheating the public. The two talk as professional colleagues. The summoner frankly confesses that he threatens people with court action and accepts payment for overlooking their offences. What matters to him is not whether someone has sinned but what can be extracted. He is delighted to discover that his new companion appears to follow the same trade.

The yeoman eventually admits that he is a devil. The summoner is startled, but not enough to break off their acquaintance. He asks how devils seize their victims and whether human curses have real power. The devil explains that words of anger are not always genuine gifts to Hell; intention matters. The point is illustrated when a carter curses his horses as they struggle to pull a wagon. The devil refuses to claim them, because the man does not sincerely wish their ruin. The summoner's attention does not improve. He sees a possible partner in professional exploitation, not a warning about his own soul.

They reach the house of a poor widow. The summoner demands payment and threatens to carry off her household possessions if she refuses. She protests that he has no rightful claim, that he has already taken money from her and that she has little left with which to live. He continues to press. Finally she curses him to Hell, with the money and goods he would steal. The devil asks whether she means it. She answers that she does, unless he will repent. The summoner refuses. The devil takes him away.

The tale is satisfying as a fantasy of justice: a corrupt official who uses religious authority to injure the vulnerable is delivered to the punishment he has effectively chosen. Its humor comes from the gap between the summoner's professional self-confidence and the audience's foreknowledge that his companion knows more about spiritual prosecution than he does. The widow's words are not powerful because she has occult knowledge. They become effective when a victim speaks truthfully against a man who has lost the capacity to repent.

But this is also an attack made by a Friar, not a neutral reformer. The real Summoner has every reason to believe that religious professionals can be just as greedy as secular officials. He is now entitled by the rules of the contest to answer. The road creates a rough kind of debate: no one speaker can establish the entire truth about church corruption, but each can make the concealed practices of the others audible. The Summoner's response will be more bodily and more insulting than the Friar's devil story.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Summoner’s Tale, Thomas’s Gift

The Summoner cannot answer the Friar with a sermon about a devil dragging off corrupt officials. He chooses humiliation instead. First he tells a brief joke about friars who are plentiful in Hell, hidden in the devil's enormous backside. Then he begins a story about a mendicant friar who visits the house of a sick man called Thomas, seeking a donation while claiming to care for his soul.

The friar makes a display of intimacy. He praises Thomas, invokes his suffering and assures him that prayers from the religious community will help. He recounts how the friars pray for their benefactors and how much more effective their intercession is than the spiritually clumsy prayers of other people. But Thomas knows that the friar has been collecting from many households and has already received gifts from him. The contrast between spiritual language and material demand becomes increasingly difficult to ignore.

Thomas's wife is grieving for a dead child and has little patience left. The friar offers elaborate condolences, then returns to the question of what the family can give. Thomas, angry at being approached as a purse while ill, promises him a precious gift if he will take it from beneath his body. The friar reaches down, expecting money or treasure. Thomas releases a fart into his hand. The visitor is outraged and declares that the insult must be avenged. He has spent the visit treating the household's pain as an occasion for income; now he claims that his wounded dignity is a matter of cosmic importance.

A lord hears the complaint and wonders how a single fart might be divided equally among a whole convent of friars. A young squire devises the answer: place the donor at the hub of a cartwheel and seat a friar at the end of each spoke. If the wind travels outward, all can receive an equal share. The lord enjoys the ingenuity. The friar's demand that others distribute their wealth to his order has been given a mathematical parody. It is as if the theology of collective prayer has been reduced to a literal problem of distributing air.

There is cruelty in the comedy as well as justice. The sick man's illness is real, the bereaved mother's grief is real, and the friar's corrupt solicitation exploits those facts. The tale makes the friar ludicrous by breaking the distance between sacred speech and the body he pretends to transcend. It is not an attack on devotion itself; the good Parson elsewhere shows a religious life of care rather than extraction. The Summoner claims that a particular kind of religious authority has turned charity into a transaction and cannot recognize its own indecency.

The Friar and Summoner have accused one another without discovering a court capable of handing down final judgment. Their tales are weapons, but they are also public descriptions of how institutions fail. The storytelling game remains comic precisely because no one can prohibit the opposing reply. The next speaker, a quiet Oxford Clerk, takes the quarrel over marriage in a different direction. He will tell of a woman praised for perfect patience and of a husband who tests that patience by making her suffer nearly everything.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Clerk’s Tale, Griselda and the Tests

The Clerk of Oxford introduces a story he attributes to the Italian poet Petrarch. A noble ruler named Walter is reluctant to marry. His people persuade him that a wife and heir will help secure the future of his land. Rather than choose among wealthy families, he selects Griselda, the daughter of a poor peasant, who has lived modestly and cared for her father. Before the wedding Walter makes her promise obedience to whatever he may require. She agrees. The marriage raises Griselda to the position of a marchioness, but the terms of the promise place her at the mercy of another person's will.

Their first child is a daughter. Griselda is delighted; Walter announces that his nobles resent a child of such low maternal birth. He pretends that the infant must be taken away and killed. Griselda does not resist, though her grief is apparent. The child is secretly sent to be raised elsewhere. Later a son is born, and Walter repeats the performance. Griselda is told that her second child must die. Again she submits. The narrative praises her constancy, but it does not disguise the astonishing brutality of a husband who creates grief to measure how patiently his wife will bear it.

Walter continues. He claims that the people will no longer tolerate their marriage and produces a supposed papal permission to set her aside. He orders Griselda to remove her fine clothing and return to her father's house in poverty. She asks only for a simple garment to cover her body. Years of obedience have earned her not security but repeated dispossession. Walter announces that he will marry a young woman brought to court. He commands Griselda to return and prepare the house for the new bride, as if the former wife were now only a servant.

Griselda obeys, welcomes the girl and even advises Walter to treat this young bride gently, unlike the woman he had tested before. At last he reveals that the supposed bride is their daughter, and the younger attendant is their son. Neither child was killed. Griselda is restored to her husband and children. The plot ends in reconciliation, but the relief depends upon a moral distinction that no apology can fully erase: the children were safe from death, yet their mother was deliberately made to believe they were dead.

The Clerk's narrator draws lessons about patience under trials. But he also closes with a lively envoy warning husbands not to expect their wives to be like Griselda and suggesting that women should resist humiliating treatment. This ending prevents us from treating the narrative as a simple manual for obedient wives. The Clerk has placed the Wife of Bath's argument about mastery under a severe test. She demanded the right to speak and control the resources of marriage. Griselda gives up everything, and it is her husband's conduct that becomes morally disturbing.

We can listen to the story as a parable about the relation between human beings and God, since the question of suffering without understanding has a religious resonance. That analogy must not excuse Walter as a real husband. He is not omniscient, and his desire to test another person springs from suspicion rather than divine knowledge. The tale asks what patience can mean when powerful people manufacture the wrongs they then require others to bear. The Merchant, whose own new marriage has made him bitter, will answer this troubling example with a very different account of a wealthy old husband and a young wife.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Merchant’s Tale, January and May

A Merchant rises after the Clerk's tale of the obedient Griselda and announces that marriage has brought him misery. His own wife, he says, has made his brief married life worse than years alone. His story turns not toward a patient poor woman but toward January, a wealthy knight of sixty who decides that marriage is the proper condition for his age. He wants a young and beautiful wife who will care for him, keep his household and provide an heir. His friends Placebo and Justinus debate the plan: one flatters every wish, the other cautions him against the dangers of marrying for self-indulgence.

January chooses May, a woman whose youth and attractiveness seem to him guarantees of pleasure. The wedding is sumptuous. He imagines his private enclosed garden as a kind of paradise and sets about enjoying the marriage for which he has made such elaborate provision. May's own desires receive little attention from him. A young squire named Damian falls in love with her and becomes ill with longing. May comes to care for him and the two secretly arrange an affair. The husband's attempts to possess her completely create a system of keys, rooms and surveillance, but not fidelity.

January loses his sight. He clings still more closely to May and seldom allows her to be outside his reach. She and Damian devise a plan in which the young man will enter the garden and wait in a pear tree. January and May go into the enclosed space together. While he sits beneath the tree, she invents a reason to climb it and meets Damian among the branches. The moment turns an image of cultivated Eden into a scene of secrecy, desire and comic exposure.

In the supernatural realm Pluto takes pity on January and restores his eyesight. He will be able to see, he says, what his wife has done. But Proserpina, offended by the assumptions about women's deceit that underpin his argument, grants May the verbal quickness needed to answer anything her husband may say. January looks up and sees May with Damian. He cries out in outrage. May does not deny the spectacle. She tells him that his restored eyes are confused, that she was struggling with a man in the tree as part of an effort to heal his blindness. Against the evidence of vision she places a clever explanation. January accepts it, or at least chooses the peace of belief.

The tale turns several cherished ideals into instruments of comedy. January imagines marriage as a remedy against sin, but chooses it largely to gratify his appetites. He longs for a private paradise, yet reproduces the conditions of suspicion that make it impossible. He acquires eyes only to lose the capacity to trust what he sees. May's verbal triumph gives her a form of agency, though it is constrained by marriage to a far older man who controls her household and money. The laughter does not settle whether deceiving him is justified.

Read beside the Clerk's story, the Merchant's tale unsettles the expectation that marriage is made safe by unconditional obedience. Read beside the Wife of Bath, it asks whether possessing a spouse is the same thing as loving one. It also makes an awkward point about storytelling itself: seeing an event does not guarantee agreement about what happened. The teller's bitterness, the narrator's pleasure in elaboration, and the characters' competing accounts all press upon the reader. When the Squire takes the floor, the quarrel over marriage will give way to a far more extravagant world of mechanical wonders, talking birds and an unfinished adventure.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Squire’s Tale, Canacee and the Falcon

The young Squire, son of the Knight, chooses a romance of wonders set in a distant court. A king named Cambyuskan rules in Tartary and celebrates his birthday with a magnificent feast. Into the hall rides a knight bearing gifts from a foreign ruler: a horse of brass that can carry its rider through the air at extraordinary speed, a mirror that reveals danger and deception, a sword whose wounds can be healed by touching them with the same blade, and a ring that permits its wearer to understand the speech of birds. The king and his court marvel at the gifts. Their meaning has not yet been disclosed by a complete adventure.

The brass horse stands so convincingly alive that people speculate about its making. Some appeal to mysterious sciences, others to engineering, and still others to sorcery. The messenger explains how to mount and direct it. The tale takes visible delight in the idea that nature might be imitated or extended by art. The gifts resemble objects from the romances the young Squire loves, but also raise questions about technology and knowledge: a machine can be understood as magic when no one in the audience can explain how it works.

Among the court is the princess Canacee. She puts on the ring, walks into the garden and suddenly hears birds speak. One falcon is bleeding and grieving, having wounded herself in distress. Canacee shelters the bird and asks for her story. The falcon speaks of a male falcon who vowed faithful love but then abandoned her for a kite. As she recounts the deception, the marvel of a talking bird turns into a familiar account of betrayed trust. A court can receive wonders from abroad and yet still fail in the ordinary duty to keep a promise.

Canacee treats the suffering falcon with care and arranges a chamber for her recovery. The Squire announces that other adventures lie ahead: those of the brass horse, the princess, her brothers and romantic obstacles. But the surviving poem stops before these promises are fulfilled. We do not learn the complete story of the falcon's healing, the horse's exploits or the remaining family adventures. A continuation here would be the work of a later writer, not the end Chaucer supplied.

The Franklin interrupts the Squire with lavish praise, saying that a young man with such eloquence would make a father proud. The compliment also cuts the narration short as the text comes down to us. This provides one of the great reversals of expectation in the collection. We are led to anticipate a sweeping romance of exotic technology and dynastic adventure, but the only emotionally completed movement is the grief of a wounded bird and a young woman's response. The promise of marvel has been redirected toward a small act of sympathy.

We should also recognize the conventions through which medieval English writers imagined Asian courts. 'Tartary' here is a literary setting, not an ethnographic account of a real population. The young Squire's distances are part of the romance's attraction. The next teller, the Franklin, will remain in a romantic mode, but he will make courtesy and freedom depend less upon miraculous objects than upon the decisions people make when their promises conflict.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Franklin’s Tale, Dorigen and the Rocks

A knight named Arveragus loves Dorigen in Brittany. After courtship she agrees to marry him. They make a promise that sounds different from many contracts in the Canterbury stories: he will not rule her with tyranny, and she will be faithful to him. Each yields something without becoming a servant of the other. Their marriage begins as an experiment in courtesy built upon restraint. But Arveragus travels abroad for a long period of military service, and Dorigen suffers intensely from their separation. Friends try to help her by walking with her along the coast.

From the cliffs Dorigen sees black rocks projecting into the sea. Ships may strike them, she fears, and her husband may die returning home. The rocks become a visible shape for anxiety. A young squire named Aurelius falls in love with her and eventually declares himself. Dorigen does not return his love. To put him off without an outright quarrel, she says that she will yield to him if he removes every dangerous black rock from the coast. The request is meant to be impossible. It is also a promise made in words that another person will insist on taking literally.

Aurelius despairs until his brother leads him to a learned clerk of Orleans, skilled in the kind of astronomical and magical arts that can produce wondrous appearances. The clerk arranges a spectacle in which the rocks seem to vanish. Aurelius pays dearly for the service and comes to Dorigen to claim her promise. She is appalled. The condition she believed impossible now appears to have been satisfied, yet fulfilling it would betray her marriage. In her grief she thinks about famous women whose courage took the form of death rather than dishonor.

Arveragus returns and hears what happened. He tells Dorigen that a promise must be kept, even when it brings sorrow to the people who made it. He sends her to Aurelius, warning that their private unhappiness must not become a public quarrel. This might look like extraordinary nobility. It also forces Dorigen toward a situation she never freely desired. The moral pressure of honor is still being placed upon her body. Aurelius, seeing her misery and the husband's willingness to sacrifice his own happiness, refuses to claim her. He releases her from the promise.

The clerk who made the rocks disappear then learns that Aurelius has given up his reward. He cancels the substantial debt owed to him. The tale ends by asking who has behaved most generously. The answer is not supplied. Arveragus yields to the authority of a promise; Aurelius yields to compassion; the clerk yields his payment. Dorigen, who feared a husband's death, has been pushed through a crisis in which every option seemed to cost her something essential.

The Franklin calls the story a Breton lay, a romance associated with marvels and ethical tests. Yet the most consequential wonders are not the vanished rocks but the decisions to refrain from exercising power. The tale belongs beside the Wife of Bath's concern with sovereignty and the Clerk's account of coercive obedience. It offers a vision of marriage in which dignity may depend on refusing to enforce a legal or verbal advantage. It also leaves us the difficult fact that other people's generosity arrived only after Dorigen was made afraid. Chaucer lets the question at the end continue to trouble the apparent happy ending.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Physician’s Tale, Virginia and the Judge

A father named Virginius has a daughter, Virginia, praised for beauty and chastity. The physician who tells the story presents her almost as an example in a moral treatise: she is careful, virtuous and protected from the temptations that ruin other young people. Her beauty attracts a powerful judge named Appius. He wants her, and because he holds public office he has the means to disguise private desire as a legal decision.

Appius recruits a corrupt man named Claudius. Claudius brings a fraudulent claim before the court: Virginia, he says, is not Virginius's daughter but an enslaved girl who belongs to him and was unlawfully taken from his household. Appius plays the part of an impartial judge while already knowing the accusation is invented. He orders the girl handed over. The court, which should distinguish a lawful claim from a criminal scheme, becomes the mechanism by which the girl is to be stolen.

Virginius returns home and tells Virginia what has happened. He frames her choice as dishonor at the judge's hands or death by her father's. Virginia is terrified; she asks whether there is no way to save her life and chastity together. He kills her and takes her head to court. The tale treats his action as an appalling response to threatened violation, and its moral language praises the preservation of honor. Yet the story gives Virginia no adequate escape from the men who claim to protect or possess her. A father has killed his own daughter rather than allowing the state to injure her.

The public sees the consequence of Appius's decision and rises against him. His scheme is exposed. He is imprisoned and takes his own life. Claudius is condemned but spared death through Virginius's intercession and sent into exile. The ending supplies the shape of a moral example: a wicked judge falls and a girl's virtue is vindicated. But the girl is dead. Political justice comes only after a killing that the narrative's honor code has made thinkable.

The tale is particularly difficult beside the Wife of Bath's story, where a knight's sexual violence is met by a judgment giving women the right to name what they desire. Virginia is not asked what she would choose from real possibilities. She is given a cruel question after her options have already been destroyed. The Physician's praise of virtue cannot restore her voice. Chaucer's company includes a physician, a man whose occupation should preserve bodies, telling a story in which the body of a young woman is destroyed to protect an abstract code of honor.

The Host reacts with anger and sorrow and calls for a lighter story. The Pardoner offers to speak. That transition is sharp: after the judge who turned law into predation comes a religious salesman who openly confesses that he turns moral warnings into money. In neither case can a public role be trusted to guarantee private goodness. The tale's lasting power lies not in its claim that Virginius found a correct solution, but in the discomfort created when an official's crime and a father's supposed virtue lead to the same loss of life.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Pardoner’s Tale, Death Under the Tree

The Pardoner earns his living by selling the promise of spiritual benefit. He carries documents of indulgence, a collection of objects he claims are holy relics, and a polished sermon against greed. He tells the pilgrims how the trick works. First he displays his supposed credentials. Then he shows his relics, which include material with no credible connection to the sacred figures he names. Finally he preaches against covetousness until his frightened audience pays. He says plainly that his concern is not their souls but his purse.

His sermon is built around a story of three young men drinking in a tavern. They hear a funeral bell and learn that a companion has died. In anger they swear to seek Death and kill him. On the road they meet a very old man, whom they insult for still being alive. He tells them that they will find Death under a tree farther along. They hurry toward the place and discover not a monster but a great heap of gold coins. The sight of treasure changes their plan at once. Rather than destroy Death, they begin imagining what the money will buy.

They cannot carry the gold through the streets by daylight without inviting suspicion. They draw lots, and the youngest goes to town to buy bread and wine while the others wait by the tree. The two older men agree to murder him when he returns so they can divide the gold between themselves. The youngest makes a calculation of his own. He buys poison and puts it into two of the wine bottles, intending to kill both companions and keep the whole treasure. When he comes back, the others stab him. Then they drink the poisoned wine. All three die beneath the tree. The old man's directions have proved perfectly accurate.

The Pardoner knows that his tale works. It has the compression of a moral trap: every step taken toward greed brings the characters closer to the death they promised to fight. But after the story the teller reverts instantly to business. He invites the pilgrims to approach him, kiss his relics and pay for remission. The Host recoils and replies with a humiliating insult. The Pardoner's performance collapses because he has shown the audience the mechanism of fraud and then assumes he can still operate it. The Knight intervenes to repair the quarrel so that the journey may continue.

What makes this more than a lesson about money is the speaker's divided intelligence. He understands the religious and psychological meaning of his story. He can preach a genuine warning while using that warning dishonestly. The distinction between true speech and virtuous speaker becomes central: an argument may expose an evil even when the person making the argument is guilty of it. The result challenges listeners who want a reliable moral authority attached to every good lesson. A skillful sermon is not evidence of a holy life.

There is also a quieter problem: the audience on the road has agreed to judge stories for pleasure and instruction. The Pardoner gives both and behaves terribly. Should Harry Bailey award him the prize for a tale that teaches exactly what it violates? The collection makes that question unavoidable. In the Shipman's story, another circuit of debt and pleasure will show money moving through a household in ways that no one can completely keep track of.

## The Canterbury Tales — Story: The Shipman’s Tale, a Hundred Francs

A rich merchant in Saint-Denis near Paris manages a prosperous household and spends much of his time counting money. He has a beautiful wife, a fine house and a familiar guest: a monk named Daun John, who calls himself a kinsman and enjoys the hospitality freely offered to him. The relationships appear secure because everyone knows the ordinary forms of courtesy. The merchant trusts the monk, the wife has access to household comforts, and the monk can pass between commercial and domestic spaces.

One day Daun John finds the wife alone. She complains that she needs a hundred francs to pay a debt, but cannot obtain them from her husband without difficulty. She promises the monk sexual favors if he will give her the money. He agrees. The transaction is unromantic in its accounting even if the language of affection is lavish. What the monk does next makes the plot possible: he asks the merchant to lend him a hundred francs, supposedly for his own temporary business. The merchant, about to leave on a trading trip, provides the money to the trusted guest.

Daun John gives the borrowed sum to the wife, and they sleep together. The merchant travels to Flanders, pursues his commercial affairs and returns expecting that the loan will be repaid. When he asks the monk for the money, Daun John says that he has already delivered it to the merchant's wife. The merchant, who cannot know the circumstances of that delivery, believes him. He asks his wife what became of the payment. She says she spent it on clothing and other things appropriate to a respectable merchant's wife, then offers to pay him back with affection in bed.

The merchant may think the exchange has been settled. The reader can follow every transfer: his own money financed the monk's liaison, the monk appears to have discharged a debt by handing the money to the woman who received the benefit, and the wife promises to repay the merchant with something he already expects from the marriage. If anyone has gained, it is not the man who supplied the funds. The humor arises from different kinds of value being treated as though they belong in the same ledger.

The story has the structure of a fabliau, a short comic narrative of sexual trickery, social appetite and embarrassment. Yet it is unusually precise about credit. The merchant has no idea what his money has purchased. The monk has used spiritual status and household intimacy to gain access to funds. The wife has solved a material problem without securing financial independence. Each participant has a different definition of payment, and the final agreement works only because the full accounts remain hidden.

The Shipman speaks as someone accustomed to risk, trade and bargains. His tale does not simply mock a cheated husband; it asks what trust means when friendship, hospitality, marriage and commerce overlap. When the Prioress takes the floor, the shift in tone will be severe. Her devotional tale offers the language of innocent childhood and heavenly intervention, yet contains a violent accusation against an entire religious minority. The pilgrim audience's enjoyment of stories cannot replace the need to judge what those stories do.
