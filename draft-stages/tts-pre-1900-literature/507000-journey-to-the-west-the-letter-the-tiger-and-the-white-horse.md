# Journey to the West: The princess's letter, the tiger, and the white horse

## A voice inside the cave

The fighting at Boyue Cave went on above the mountain. Bajie's rake met the demon's sword, Sha Wujing's staff struck from the other side, and still the Yellow-Robed Lord held his own. Cloud hid the fighters, then tore apart and revealed them again. The cave's little demons beat drums and shouted encouragement. To those servants it must have looked like sport. To the men fighting for Xuanzang it was the difference between getting their master back and leaving him to be eaten.

Xuanzang could hear the battle from the post where he had been bound.

He called for Bajie and Sha, although he knew they could not hear him. He had sent one out to look for food and the other to find his brother. He had then left the safe clearing himself, persuaded that the shining tower must stand above a temple. Now both his protectors had found the cave only because they had discovered he was missing.

Someone approached from the darkness beyond the post.

It was a woman, perhaps thirty years old, dressed neither like a servant of the cave nor like a pilgrim. She asked why the monk had been tied there, and he answered miserably that he had entered the dwelling by mistake. He expected its master to eat him. If she had come to ask questions before the meal, there was not much she needed to know.

She told him she was no eater of human beings. Her name was Baihuaxiu, the Princess Hundred-Flower-Shame. She was the third daughter of the ruler of Baoxiang Kingdom, whose capital lay along the western road, about three hundred li away. Thirteen years earlier, on the night of the mid-autumn moon celebration, a violent wind had seized her from the royal festivities. The creature now called the Yellow-Robed Lord had carried her to this mountain and kept her there as his wife. She had borne two sons in the cave. Her father and mother had received no word of her whereabouts.

She asked where the monk was traveling.

When Xuanzang said he was going west to obtain the Buddhist scriptures, she grew hopeful. Baoxiang lay on his route. If she secured his freedom, would he carry a letter to her parents? He need not remain to rescue her; he would do more than she had dared hope if he simply carried news that she was alive and could be found.

Xuanzang promised to deliver the letter. He warned her that her family might not recognize a message written after thirteen years. She said her father had no sons and only three daughters. She believed he would hear her.

She went away, wrote on paper, sealed the letter, and returned to untie him. She placed it in his hands, then stopped him as he moved toward the front entrance. The cave was full of armed demons. They might kill him without waiting for their master's instructions. He must slip out the back and hide among the thorns until the fighting ended. She would speak to her husband and try to make him let the monk go openly.

The princess then walked out to the cave's front and called sharply to the Yellow-Robed Lord.

He broke away from combat and came down from the air. He called her his wife and asked what she wanted.

She knew what would happen if she confessed to passing a secret message. Instead she described a dream. Long ago, she said, she had promised to honor the gods with offerings to monks if she were blessed with a good husband. In her sleep, a warrior in golden armor had appeared and demanded that she fulfill the vow. She had woken to find a monk bound in the cave. Would her husband not spare this one man so that she might make the charitable offering and settle the debt?

The demon had plenty of people he could eat, he said. One monk mattered little. He told the two fighting disciples to collect their master and leave, and warned them not to enter his territory again.

The followers on the mountain were astonished at their good fortune. Bajie and Sha hurried around to the rear entrance. There they found Xuanzang crouched in the thorns, a sealed letter held close to his robe. Sha helped him out, Bajie brought the horse, and they left the cave before anyone could change his mind.

It was a rescue achieved by the person who had been kept prisoner longest, not by the two warriors in the sky.

## The country that had lost a daughter

The road carried the pilgrims west. Their arguments began almost as soon as they were safely away. Bajie thought they had been lucky to escape. Sha considered the debt they owed the woman who had saved Xuanzang. The monk kept the letter protected and urged them forward. In time the mountains thinned, cultivated land appeared, and the distant towers of Baoxiang Kingdom rose ahead.

The kingdom had streets full of merchants, official gates, halls raised on broad foundations, and people going about ordinary business. To the travelers, after so much time among monsters and wilderness, its order seemed almost astonishing. At an official lodging house they put up the horse and left their baggage. Xuanzang went to court to have his travel passport examined and marked by the king.

The royal passport was more than a piece of paper to be carried in the monk's sleeve. It embodied the emperor's command that he seek scriptures in the west, and its stamps would attest that he had passed through foreign kingdoms along the way. The king treated a monk from the Tang as a distinguished visitor and applied the seal of Baoxiang.

Xuanzang thanked him. Then he said he had brought another document, a private letter from the king's third daughter.

The ruler could scarcely believe it. Thirteen years earlier the princess had disappeared from a palace celebration. Officials had searched, interrogated townspeople, and punished servants, sometimes fatally, for failing to explain her absence. The king had grown accustomed to hearing that no trace had been found. Now a traveler claimed not merely to have seen her but to have spoken with her.

He took the letter. Its first words told him his daughter was alive. His hands shook so much that he could not open it. An official scholar came forward to unfold the page and read it aloud while ministers, royal women, and attendants listened.

Baihuaxiu's letter named the night she had vanished and the creature who had carried her away. It described her years in a cave at Wanzi Mountain, her unwilling life as the Yellow-Robed Lord's wife, and the two children born there. She apologized for her absence, though she had not chosen it. She admitted her shame at sending such news and explained why she had done so: if she died in the mountains without telling anyone the truth, her family might never know what became of her. She had freed a pilgrim and trusted him to carry the message.

The hall filled with grief. The king wept for his daughter, whom he had imagined dead or lost to them forever. Her mother and the other women of the court wept too.

When he recovered enough to speak, the king asked which of his officers would lead a force to bring her home.

None volunteered. His generals were trained to defend roads, hold walls, and fight human armies. A monster who could move through the sky and change its form was not an opponent their weapons could reliably reach.

The ministers found a solution that required them to do no fighting. If the monk had come from the Tang to visit holy places and obtain sacred teaching, surely he possessed arts for overcoming demons. Why should they not ask him?

Xuanzang was aghast. He knew how to chant and how to keep the rules of his religious life. He did not know how to fight a demon. The king, who desperately wanted his daughter home, offered him a place at court, riches, even a brother's closeness, if he would help.

At last the monk admitted that he traveled with two powerful disciples, neither of whom looked suitable for a royal audience. The king insisted that any protector of such a pilgrim was welcome.

A summons went to the lodging house.

Bajie heard the invitation with delight. A king who had received a letter from a long-lost daughter would surely prepare an enormous meal for the messenger. Perhaps they would eat at court, rest, and resume the journey the following day. Sha suggested they might first learn why they were wanted.

When the two appeared in the palace, the courtiers recoiled at their strange faces. Bajie, with his tusks and great ears, stood beside Sha, who was towering, dark-faced, and no more familiar in appearance. They did not kneel as the officials expected. Bajie heard the murmurs and told the courtiers that people often found him alarming at first glance but became accustomed to his looks if they stared long enough.

The king was not reassured. He nearly fell from his seat.

Xuanzang hurriedly apologized for having brought such frightening companions into the hall. When the king's fear subsided, he demanded to know which of them could fight demons.

Bajie was already answering.

He had been Marshal Tianpeng in Heaven, he said, commander of water troops, and he knew transformations. The king asked for a demonstration. Bajie told him to choose any form. The king said he would like to see something large.

Bajie straightened, invoked his powers, and rose until his body towered above the court. Asked whether he could grow still taller, he answered that it depended on the wind. If a strong wind came from the south, perhaps he would poke a hole in the sky. The king begged him to return to his ordinary size before any such accident occurred.

Then came an embarrassing discussion of weapons. The king was surprised that so formidable a heavenly warrior carried something that resembled a farmer's rake. He offered swords, halberds, axes, and more respectable arms from the arsenal. Bajie indignantly described the ancient workmanship of the nine-toothed rake and the armies it had served. A court weapon might look grander, but it would not fit his hands nearly so well.

The king gave him wine to fortify him before the rescue. Bajie bowed toward his master, announced that a royal cup should naturally go first to Xuanzang, then drank it himself because a royal gift could not be refused. Xuanzang did not drink wine; Sha accepted the next cup.

Bajie flew off toward Boyue Cave.

Sha, remembering how hard they had struggled against the demon before, asked to join him. The monk agreed. The king, alarmed at the sight of men taking to the sky, seized Xuanzang's arm and implored him not to fly away as well. The monk assured him that there was little danger of that. He could not so much as leave the ground unaided.

## A fight without the hidden allies

Bajie and Sha struck the cave gate hard enough to break the stone. The Yellow-Robed Lord came out furious. Had he not released their master? Why had they returned?

Bajie announced that the king of Baoxiang had ordered the return of his daughter. The demon had kept her in the cave for thirteen years. He must surrender and submit to being bound.

The Yellow-Robed Lord drew his sword.

This time the fight turned quickly against the pilgrims. Earlier, invisible guardians had helped them defend their master while he was a prisoner in the cave. Now those guardians were in Baoxiang protecting Xuanzang. Bajie and Sha were left to meet the demon's strength on their own.

After a few exchanges Bajie could feel his arms tiring. He told Sha he needed to retire for a moment, as though nature had called him away. Then he plunged headlong into thorn bushes and hid there. Sha remained above the slope, alone against the monster's sword. The demon drove him back and seized him. The cave servants bound his limbs and carried him inside.

Bajie did not know this. He lay in the bushes, listening for battle and hoping not to be found. Eventually he fell asleep.

Inside the cave the Yellow-Robed Lord stared at his captive. Why had the monks come back after their master had been spared? The king could not have known of the princess without receiving a message. Who had sent it?

He went to Baihuaxiu.

The princess greeted him with practiced courtesy, but he accused her of deceit. He had clothed and fed her, given her jewels and a place at his table. Why did she still think of her parents? Had she written to them behind his back?

She denied it. When he seized her hair and threw her to the floor, she asked that the prisoner be questioned. If Sha testified that she had sent a letter, she would accept his judgment; if no letter existed, he must not kill her on suspicion.

The demon hauled Sha forward and demanded an answer.

Sha knew the truth. Baihuaxiu had freed Xuanzang, and she had entrusted him with a letter. To reveal either fact was to expose her to an enraged captor who was holding a sword over her. So Sha made a different choice.

He said the monks had learned of the princess at court. The king had shown his visitors an image of his missing daughter. Xuanzang remembered seeing someone who matched it in the cave. The king had sent his disciples to retrieve her. No letter was necessary to explain their arrival.

The demon questioned him further. Sha held to the story and declared that, if a life was demanded, his own should be taken instead of that of an innocent woman.

This was not a convenient lie for his own comfort. Sha was bound and defenseless. By concealing the princess's letter he protected the person who had saved his master, even if it cost him his life.

The Yellow-Robed Lord relented. He lifted Baihuaxiu from the floor, apologized for his roughness, and tried to soothe her. She used the moment to ask that the bindings on Sha be loosened. The demon did not free him, but he allowed the ropes to be removed and the prisoner held under guard instead.

Then the demon had another thought. If his wife's father ruled Baoxiang, he was the king's son-in-law. Why should he not go to the capital and introduce himself?

Baihuaxiu protested. Her father had lived a sheltered royal life. A monster with a blue face and glaring eyes would terrify him. The Yellow-Robed Lord answered that he could easily make himself handsome.

He stood up from the table and transformed into a graceful young nobleman, well dressed, courteous, and entirely suited to a court. Baihuaxiu could not stop him. He went to Baoxiang, leaving her, the two children, and the imprisoned Sha in the cave.

## The polite stranger

At the palace a handsome traveler asked to be admitted. He claimed to be the king's son-in-law, long married to the missing princess. The court, which had shrunk from Bajie's monstrous face, admired this visitor's refined manners. The king asked where he lived and how his daughter had come to marry him.

The demon told a carefully constructed story.

Thirteen years earlier, he said, he had been hunting near Wanzi Mountain when he saw a tiger carrying off a young woman. He shot the beast, rescued the girl, and brought her to his household. She never said she was a princess. Believing her a commoner's daughter, he married her, and they lived happily. He had intended to kill the tiger, but the girl urged him to spare it, saying the beast had brought them together. So it went back into the forest with an arrow wound.

The king listened. The story explained the princess's disappearance, the marriage, and the stranger's late arrival at court. It also gave the demon an opportunity to say what he had really come to say.

That tiger, he continued, had cultivated magical powers in the years since. It now moved about disguised as a monk, using stolen travel papers from the Tang. The supposed Buddhist pilgrim sitting in the palace was the tiger itself.

Xuanzang tried to protest, but the king wanted proof.

The stranger asked for a little clean water. He pronounced a spell, sprayed the water toward the monk, and cried out for the disguise to fall away.

To every human eye in the hall Xuanzang became a huge striped tiger. The monk's own consciousness remained, but he could neither speak nor move as a man. Courtiers screamed. Soldiers rushed in with blades and spears. Invisible guardian spirits kept their weapons from cutting him. After a long struggle they secured the creature with iron chains, put him in a cage, and carried him to a guarded room.

The king had received his daughter's letter, but he had chosen the charming voice of the man who had held her prisoner over the testimony of the captive who had carried it. The pattern had changed shape, yet it was painfully familiar: someone with a convincing appearance had been believed, while the truth became monstrous to those who saw it.

The king ordered a banquet for his new son-in-law.

Music played that night in a splendid hall. Young palace women sang and danced for the honored guest. He sat among wine and dishes, accepting the hospitality due to a noble bridegroom. But as he drank, he lost patience with the human costume he had put on. The handsome face vanished. The huge blue-faced monster returned, snatched one of the musicians, and began eating her. The other women fled through corridors and behind screens, too frightened even to raise the alarm at the king's chambers.

Soon rumors moved through the palace: the guest was a monster, but the monk was already confined as a tiger. No one seemed to know how to put the terrible story back in order.

## The horse who remembered he was a dragon

In the royal lodging house the white horse stood alone.

He had once been a dragon prince of the western sea, condemned for his offense against heavenly authority and given a chance to serve the pilgrimage by carrying Xuanzang. Most days he played the part of a horse so completely that his companions hardly spoke of his old life. A traveler tied his reins. A stable servant gave him fodder. The monk mounted and dismounted. He accepted the ordinary work that had become his penance.

Now he heard the report that his master was a man-eating tiger.

The horse knew Xuanzang was no tiger. Bajie and Sha had vanished, Wukong had been dismissed, and the monk had no one at his side. The dragon could wait for the missing disciples, or he could act.

He broke his tether, shook free of the saddle, and rose into the night in his true dragon shape. From above he saw the palace lights burning and the celebration continuing. He descended toward the banquet hall and found the Yellow-Robed Lord seated among wine and the remains of his victims.

The dragon changed into a palace dancer and entered with a graceful bow. He offered to pour wine.

The demon, still drinking, gave him a cup. The disguised dragon poured with a water-control spell, raising the liquid above the rim without spilling it. The wine stood in a shining tower, one layer rising upon another. The demon was amused and asked for more. The cup seemed to hold impossible quantities.

Could the dancer sing? A little, came the modest answer. A song followed. Could the dancer perform a sword dance? There was no sword at hand, the dragon answered, unless the honored guest would lend his.

The demon unfastened his own blade and gave it over.

At first the dance was graceful, slow enough to make every movement appear harmless. Then the dragon turned and struck at the monster's head.

The demon dodged. He snatched up one of the heavy iron candle stands and used it to catch the blow. The dancer shed the disguise and rose into the air as a dragon. Sword and iron stand rang together in the night above the palace.

The young dragon fought hard, but the Yellow-Robed Lord was stronger. He caught the sword when the dragon hurled it and struck the dragon's hind leg with the candle stand. Wounded, the dragon plunged into the palace watercourse and hid beneath the surface until the demon lost his trail.

Only then did he return to the lodging house. He became the white horse again, dripping water, with a great bruise on his leg. He crouched by the feeding trough, exhausted and in pain.

## A voice from the stable

Bajie woke in the thorns after midnight.

He remembered the battle, his retreat, and the fact that he had left Sha alone. Perhaps, he thought, he could go to the king and ask for soldiers to rescue his brother. There was no point in rushing back to a monster he had already failed to defeat.

He flew to the lodging house, found no monk, and saw only the white horse lying wet beside the trough. The horse had not traveled anywhere. Why was its coat soaked? Who had struck it?

Then the horse spoke.

Bajie recoiled so violently that he nearly fell over. He knew the creature had once been a dragon, but hearing the horse address him in words was something else. He begged it not to frighten him, particularly at such an hour. Talking horses were surely a sign of calamity.

The dragon told him what had happened: the nobleman at court, the false tiger in an iron cage, the palace banquet, the failed rescue, the injured leg. The monastery's scripture mission had not ended, but Xuanzang might die before sunrise if no one acted.

Bajie listened, then began arranging an escape. Sha had been captured. Wukong was gone. He himself had lost to the Yellow-Robed Lord. Why should they go on pretending to be a pilgrimage? The dragon could swim or fly home. Bajie could gather the baggage and return to Gao Village, where he had once hoped to live as a comfortable son-in-law.

The dragon caught his robe in his teeth and would not release it.

He pleaded with Bajie to show some perseverance. There was one person who could save Xuanzang: the elder disciple, Sun Wukong. Bajie must cross the eastern sea to Flower-Fruit Mountain and ask him to return.

Bajie grew nervous. At White Bone Mountain he had encouraged the monk's suspicions. Wukong knew it. The monkey could be vindictive, and the iron staff was heavy. What if Bajie went all that way only to be beaten for his trouble?

The dragon said Wukong would not abandon the master who had released him. If Bajie feared a refusal, he could begin by saying that Xuanzang missed him. Once Wukong saw the truth, he would come.

Bajie agreed, though he warned the horse that if Wukong refused he might not return at all.

He took up his rake and traveled east, the wind filling his enormous ears like sails as he crossed the sea.

By morning he was climbing Flower-Fruit Mountain. He heard shouts and found Wukong seated on a high rock, surrounded by a great assembly of monkeys. The mountain seemed green again. The king appeared entirely at home.

Bajie did not march forward to announce himself. He squeezed into a line of monkeys and tried to bow with them.

Wukong saw the large snout at once. What stranger had wandered into his court? Where were his credentials? What species of monkey was he claiming to be?

Bajie muttered that the king ought to recognize an old friend. Wukong made him lift his face. There was no hiding that snout.

The Monkey King laughed and named him. The visitor, now relieved to be recognized, hurried to explain that their master missed his eldest disciple terribly and had sent him to bring Wukong back.

Wukong did not believe it. The monk had written a dismissal and spoken an oath. Why would he reverse himself now? Bajie improvised: the monk had called for his disciples, and neither Bajie nor Sha had answered promptly. Then he had remembered how Wukong had always responded at once and understood whatever was needed. Surely he wanted the clever monkey at his side again.

Wukong softened enough to take Bajie around the restored mountain. He showed him the trees, the streams, the cave, the life returning to the slopes. Monkeys presented fruit, and Bajie ate readily, though he worried about how long they had been away from the captive monk. When he urged Wukong to set out, the Monkey King stopped at the edge of the path.

He was not going.

The mountain was his. No heavenly official had command over him here, and no human master could dismiss him one day and summon him the next. Bajie could return with the answer: once sent away, Sun Wukong would not come merely because somebody missed his services.

Bajie bowed and departed. He had traveled all the way across the sea only to obtain a refusal.

On the road down he could no longer contain his frustration. He cursed the obstinate monkey who would rather reign among demons than help his own master. Wukong had guessed this might happen and sent small monkeys to listen. They ran back to report every angry word.

Wukong ordered Bajie brought back.

The mountain monkeys swarmed over the traveler, took him by his ears and bristles, and dragged him toward the cave. Behind them the sea shone in the morning light, and far to the west a monk sat in an iron cage with a tiger's face.
