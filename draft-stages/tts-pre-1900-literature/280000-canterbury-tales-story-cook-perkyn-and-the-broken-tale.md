# The Canterbury Tales — The Cook’s Tale, Perkyn and the Broken Tale

After the Reeve's rough story, the Cook laughs with particular pleasure. He knows a good joke and promises to add one of his own. The Host teases him about his trade, because a cook who sells spoiled food and allows flies and dubious ingredients into his kitchen is already living in a comic story. The Cook replies in kind and begins his tale with a London apprentice named Perkyn Revelour, whose name suggests the revels that occupy too much of his time.

Perkyn is handsome, strong and lively. He is also unreliable in precisely the ways that trouble the people who employ him. He drinks, dances, joins street celebrations and spends more time seeking pleasure than serving his master. When he is sent to conduct an errand, there is always an opportunity for diversion. The other apprentices of his company begin to follow him into trouble. His master tries repeatedly to correct him, but discovers that warnings have no power against the next night's entertainment.

At last the master dismisses Perkyn, deciding that one bad apprentice will spoil the household's whole discipline. Perkyn finds a new home with an equally idle young man who lives on gambling and theft. The young man's wife, though she keeps a shop, also has a reputation for illicit activity. The surviving fragment stops at the point where this household might become the setting for the apprentice's next adventure. Chaucer does not tell us what Perkyn does next, how the friends fare, or whether the expected comic punishment arrives. We must not turn the end of the manuscript into an invented ending.

The brevity of the fragment is itself useful evidence of the larger project's unfinished state. The storytelling competition promises more tales than Chaucer completed. Here the Cook begins with an attractive setup, then the text breaks off. Later manuscripts sometimes attempted to fill such gaps with non-Chaucerian material. Those additions are historically interesting but should not be silently included as if Chaucer wrote them. The fragment stands on its own as an opening without an ending.

Even unfinished, the story adds a new London setting to the collection. The Knight's aristocratic Athens, the Miller's Oxford household and the Reeve's Cambridge mill are followed by a city of apprentices, trade, dancing and commercial discipline. The same question keeps shifting form: what is owed to the community by a person who wants pleasure or advantage? Perkyn wants to live as if every day is a holiday; his master needs work done and his household governed. The question is unresolved because the story itself is unresolved. On the pilgrimage road, however, the next surviving cluster will take us far beyond London, into the life of a woman repeatedly driven across the sea by the ambitions and fears of rulers.
