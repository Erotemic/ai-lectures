# A World of Exchange

Money in the *Decameron* does not simply make people greedy. It gives them a reason to travel, strangers a reason to trust each other, and clever people opportunities to exploit the trust that transactions require. The merchant characters inhabit a world that depends on messages, reputations, goods, receipts, and the uncertain arrival of ships. If an arrangement fails, its consequences may fall on a household thousands of miles from the place where the mistake occurred.

Boccaccio knew something of that world through his father's work and his own early exposure to banking in Naples. A merchant's education could include arithmetic and account keeping, but books were not enough. He also needed to decide which partners to believe and what to do when news arrived late. Commercial competence meant reading people as well as numbers. The *Decameron* makes that human judgment a recurring plot device. Many stories turn on an apparently plausible narrative that someone accepts too soon.

Landolfo Rufolo in Day Two, Tale Four enters trade hoping to increase his wealth. Disaster at sea transforms him from trader to pirate and then to a man cast into the water. The chest on which he survives turns out to contain valuables, and fortune reverses his apparent ruin. The episode is not a reliable probability model for maritime business. It offers a sequence in which calculation repeatedly loses control of events. Wealth can make risk possible and cannot guarantee its outcome. Chance can rescue a character in a manner no plan could have arranged.

Andreuccio in Day Two, Tale Five goes to Naples to buy horses. He has sufficient funds to attract a fraudster but little local knowledge to protect himself. A woman persuades him to believe a story about kinship, and he follows her into a sequence of dangers. The tale makes especially clear why trust is both necessary and dangerous. A traveler cannot personally verify every claim made in an unfamiliar city; to conduct business he must act on information that may be fabricated. Andreuccio's later survival requires a different, more defensive kind of ingenuity.

Day Eight, Tale Ten gives a more deliberate anatomy of fraud. Salabaetto is a merchant in Palermo who is deceived by a courtesan presenting affection as genuine. She obtains his money by persuading him that his interests and hers are aligned. Salabaetto's retaliation uses staged financial appearances and a borrowed sum to reverse the losses. The story admires his ability to counter a ruse with a ruse. But if we ask how an economy might function if every transaction were organized this way, the joke becomes uneasy. Tricks may settle a private account while reducing the very trust that makes commerce possible.

Commercial stories also connect with the older ideal of magnificence. In Day Ten, Tale One, Ruggieri's unlucky selection of a chest filled with earth rather than treasure places fortune beside a king's willingness to give. Alfonso's ultimate generosity corrects an outcome produced by chance. The underlying contrast is not simply merchant greed versus noble virtue. Wealth takes on different moral meanings depending on whether it is accumulated, risked, stolen, displayed, or given away. A successful merchant may be generous; a noble may be vain or cruel. Social labels do not determine which action the reader should admire.

Some tales reach beyond the merchant's own ledger to the people who sustain his business. Lisabetta's brothers in Day Four pursue what they consider family interest so fiercely that they murder the man she loves. Servants and dependents may carry the practical cost of a prosperous household's ambition. Markets are social institutions. Their victories are not enjoyed by every member of the household in the same way, and the agency of the person treated as a commercial asset is easily lost.

Modern readers sometimes praise Boccaccio for presenting a recognizably capitalist world. The observation catches the importance of banking, trade, and economic mobility but risks erasing the specific institutions and moral debates of fourteenth-century Italy. Religious objections to usury and profit, guild organization, noble honor, and family strategies remained powerful. The stories depict a society of overlapping obligations, not a modern market whose rules everyone has agreed to enter.

A way to listen for commerce is to follow each character's evidence. Who claims to know the value of something? Who can read a document, enter a house, or recognize a counterfeit? Who can survive waiting for another ship? Who has the power to force a settlement, and who must trust a promise? When those questions are audible, the *Decameron*'s adventures become a study of how people coordinate under uncertainty—and of how profitable it can be to make uncertainty worse for someone else.
