# Journey to the West — Eighty-One Ordeals and What Remains

## The difficulty of reaching an ending

At the end of *Journey to the West*, the pilgrims have endured so much that one expects arrival to settle everything. The Buddha's mountain has been reached. The scriptures have been acquired. Heaven has supplied guardians to carry them east. But the book refuses the satisfaction of a single triumphant arrival. First the pilgrims are given wordless scriptures. Then they return for written ones. Then the heavenly guardians discover that the recorded number of trials is one short. Then the old turtle overturns the travelers in a river because Xuanzang has forgotten a promise. Then the scriptures are soaked, and some pages stick to a stone while they are being dried. Only after all that do the books reach Chang'an and the companions return for their final honors.

Why so many endings? Is the novel reluctant to stop? Or has it saved one of its most serious ideas for the moment when readers think the adventure is complete?

The answer begins with the form of the entire novel. Its long middle is built from repeated journeys, captures, disguises, confrontations, rescues, and departures. What makes the repetition matter is not that every episode delivers a different exotic animal or weapon. It is that the story repeatedly changes the meaning of arrival, safety, knowledge, obligation, and success. The last ordeals allow us to see what the earlier patterns have been doing.

## Repetition with consequences

A first-time listener can be forgiven for predicting the sequence. The pilgrims approach a place. Xuanzang is put in danger. Wukong investigates, fights, and discovers that force will not suffice. He seeks a special helper, defeats or contains the demon, retrieves his master, and the company resumes its travel. Variations of this pattern recur so often that they become part of the book's comedy. The reader knows trouble is coming before the monk believes it.

But the story does not simply reset. Some episodes leave consequences that return across dozens of chapters. The Red Boy affair changes Wukong's later relationship with Princess Iron Fan and the Bull Demon King at the Flaming Mountains. The old turtle's kindness in the Heaven-Reaching River episodes creates a promise that reappears at the end. Wukong's early treatment by Heaven shapes the way he negotiates with celestial officials as a pilgrim. The initial tightening fillet remains both a practical instrument of command and an unresolved injury until the last pages.

Other repetitions become comparisons. The White Bone Demon creates apparent human victims whom Xuanzang believes Wukong has murdered. Later, real human violence is part of the crisis that sends Wukong away again. The two dismissals resemble each other enough for us to remember the first, but they do not have the same moral facts. The counterfeit Little Thunderclap monastery invites the pilgrims to mistake a false arrival for the real one; the actual scripture repository offers scrolls whose outward legitimacy does not guarantee the contents the travelers expect. Across the book, a similar form returns under altered conditions and asks the reader to judge again.

A long episodic novel also changes the significance of the mundane. At the start, obtaining supplies and travel papers may seem like delay before the real adventure. After many kingdoms, the petition to a ruler, the lodging request, the unpaid kindness, and the accusation against a stranger have proved as consequential as a fight. Repetition gives us enough experience to notice the recurring human institutions inside the magic.

## The tools that promise easy answers

Each great magical object seems for a moment to offer a shortcut. Wukong's staff can become enormous or tiny. The gold and silver horn kings possess gourds, a rope, a fan, and other treasures capable of turning the hero's own power against him. The Blue Ox's ring removes the weapons of heaven's best fighters. Princess Iron Fan's fan could extinguish the Flaming Mountains, but the first fan Wukong obtains is false. At the scripture repository the visible shape of a scroll proves an equally treacherous sign of success.

The objects create sharply defined problems. They allow the reader to understand a danger without needing every rule of a magical universe explained. If a gourd captures anyone who answers to a name, we watch Wukong search for a linguistic way around it. If a ring takes the weapon, a hero must change strategies. If the fan is counterfeit, possession is no longer the same as victory. These are narrative machines for producing ingenuity, jokes, reversals, and revelations about the characters.

The forms of Wukong's cleverness are especially varied. He may use hair to make a body, change his size to enter a demon, borrow a face, imitate an official, acquire a document, or trick an opponent into stating a rule aloud. His power is not simply superior force. Often it is superior attention to how a game has been arranged. The reader enjoys the contest because the hero must learn the terms before he can exploit them.

That is also why some of his failures are structurally necessary. If the staff ended every contest immediately, the journey would require no allies. If he could recognize every impostor, the double-Monkey episode would have little force. If the pilgrims never mistook a counterfeit destination for a true one, the end would not carry the same warning about confusing the appearance of a sacred object with understanding.

## The narrator who enjoys a complication

The storytelling voice is central to this effect. Chapter headings use balanced phrases and promise surprising events. The prose can offer a quiet landscape or formal welcome, then tip into insults, slapstick, threats, and transformations. Verses can stop time to describe a landscape or imply a religious pattern; action sequences can accelerate through rapid reversals; court scenes can give a comic delay the form of ceremonial procedure.

The narrator frequently allows the audience more knowledge than Xuanzang possesses. We may know a demon's disguise while the monk still sees a victim or a benefactor. We may know a promised meal will become a trap before Bajie has finished praising it. This produces dramatic irony, but also an ethical problem for us. Being safely informed as readers can make Xuanzang's caution look foolish even when the evidence available to him would not justify Wukong's certainty. A narrative technique has shaped our judgment before we consciously choose a moral position.

The novel also knows when to withhold information. The two-Monkey-Kings dispute is disturbing because the usual mechanisms of recognition fail. The apparent sacred place at Little Thunderclap produces danger by fulfilling expectations too convincingly. The reader must periodically surrender the comfortable superiority that was supplied by earlier scenes. Sometimes we know more than the pilgrims; sometimes we are being fooled alongside them.

The quiet episodes help keep that variation alive. The moonlight poetry at Baolin Temple and the tree spirits' literary gathering on Thorny Ridge slow the rhythm and make language itself an event. These moments remind us that the book is made not only from combat encounters but from changes of register: solemnity against mockery, learning against fraud, cultivated verse against the raw needs of travel. To hear the full novel is to hear those registers interrupt and question one another.

## Why eighty-one?

The story famously describes the required ordeals as eighty-one, a number associated with a complete sequence of nine times nine. But the hundred chapters are not a list in which each chapter equals one ordeal. Episodes overlap chapters, and the challenges vary from spectacular battles to failures of judgment or incomplete obligations. Toward the end, the heavenly overseers inspect the record of what the pilgrims have undergone and discover that one trial is still needed.

A writer could have added another monster. Instead, the book returns to an old friendship. The turtle of Heaven-Reaching River once carried the company across the water. He asked Xuanzang to inquire about his own future when the monk reached the Buddha. Xuanzang promised, and then forgot. On the way back, the turtle asks for his answer. The monk has none. The turtle throws the travelers into the river.

This is a remarkable final ordeal because the source of the trouble is not an enemy newly encountered. It is a debt the travelers have incurred themselves. Their official mission has succeeded; their personal obligation has not. The last danger arises from a failure of attention by a religious figure who has sought to bring wisdom to others. The journey can be counted complete in Heaven's ledger only after an incident that reveals the incompleteness of the pilgrim's own conduct.

The turtle's act is not necessarily proportionate or admirable. Throwing people and precious scriptures into water because of an unanswered request is severe. The episode invites judgment of both sides. The turtle has been neglected after doing a real service; Xuanzang has not honored his word; the retaliation endangers more than the forgetful monk. If we insist that the final ordeal is simply deserved punishment, we miss the conflict. If we treat it only as a malicious prank, we miss the broken promise that makes it possible.

## Wordless scriptures and visible writing

Before the turtle, there is the first shock of the repository. The scrolls appear legitimate, but they contain no words. It is possible to read this as a joke about officials who withhold the goods until properly compensated. The guardians request an offering, and the travelers ultimately surrender the purple-gold bowl in exchange for scriptures that can be carried home and read.

Yet the Buddha also gives the wordless texts a spiritual explanation. The idea that the highest truth may outrun ordinary language is not a ridiculous one in the context of Buddhist thought. What is ridiculous, or at least troubling, is the mismatch between that insight and the travelers' public mission. Xuanzang must return to a community that expects teachings in a transmissible form. An empty scroll may provoke insight in a sage; it cannot be copied as a body of written instruction for the Tang court's readers.

A strong reading can allow both meanings. The scene questions whether sacred knowledge can be reduced to an object. It also questions gatekeeping, rewards, and the practical means by which teachings move through institutions. To declare that the blank scriptures were the only true scriptures would remove the novel's insistence that the pilgrims obtain written texts. To declare that the blank scriptures were nothing but fraud would flatten the story's own religious ambiguity.

The two collections—wordless and written—expose a tension between understanding and transmission. A book cannot make a reader wise merely by being possessed. But if teachings are meant to be conveyed across languages and communities, their physical forms matter. The scriptures must exist as scrolls that can be read, dried, copied, transported, and damaged. The novel has made an argument about text by turning text into a material adventure.

## The torn page

When the scriptures are wet after the turtle's intervention, the pilgrims dry them on a stone. Part of a page sticks and tears. This is a peculiar detail to place so close to the end of a triumphant journey. It makes the sacred collection vulnerable to an ordinary accident. The texts are not floating above nature; they are made of surfaces and marks that water and stone can damage.

One can read the damage as a reminder that the transmission of teaching is never completely protected from the world. Another can connect it to imperfection in the pilgrims' achievement. The words are obtained, the vow to the turtle was forgotten, and not every page survives its return unchanged. The novel does not need to announce a philosophical theorem for the scene to register the stubborn reality of incompleteness.

There is an irony here for readers of *Journey to the West* itself. The novel has a textual history full of editions, revisions, abridgments, commentaries, retellings, and translations. No modern listener holds the original performance of every story that contributed to its making. We encounter what has been transmitted, and we must identify our witness. The damaged scripture is not a literal explanation of that publishing history. It is an image that encourages us to think about what transmission preserves and what it changes.

## Who gets to say the journey is complete?

In Chang'an, the pilgrims present the scriptures, receive imperial attention, and arrange for the texts' dissemination. The mission has a public meaning beyond the personal advancement of its participants. Only then do they return to receive their final appointments from the Buddha. The ending therefore has at least two audiences. The human kingdom needs the scripture collection. The celestial assembly recognizes the transformed travelers. Neither ceremony wholly replaces the other.

The appointments are differentiated. Xuanzang and Wukong receive titles marking Buddhahood. Sha is honored as an arhat. The dragon-horse resumes his dragon form and receives his own station. Bajie is assigned a role connected with offerings and remains visibly dissatisfied. The final scene could have made every flaw vanish under the brightness of spiritual success. Instead it keeps comic personality alive at the edge of solemnity.

Then Wukong asks about the fillet. He is thinking about the history of pain and control that has accompanied the journey. The golden circlet, it turns out, has already vanished. Here the novel's most conspicuous instrument of enforced obedience is absent precisely when the pilgrim receives ultimate recognition.

One reading says that inner discipline has replaced outer coercion. Another sees trust supplanting enforced command. Another questions whether the punishment Wukong suffered has been fully accounted for. These readings can be compared by returning to the first chapters, where the monkey rejected the ranks of Heaven, and to chapter fourteen, where his new master received the power to hurt him. The ending is not detached from the beginning. It is constructed to be read against it.

## The story's final claim

The novel finishes with an extraordinary degree of closure. The scriptures have reached their intended public. The last ordeal is counted. The travelers receive names that mark their changed places in the cosmos. In that sense, the story is finished, and it would be perverse to deny the satisfaction the ending offers.

But it does not end by making every moral account perfectly clear. The turtle's complaint was real. The heavenly owners of dangerous creatures have not all been required to answer for their negligence. Bajie has not ceased to want a better portion. Wukong's vanished fillet raises a final question about what growth has cost. And the written scriptures themselves have proved fragile, subject to barter, water, and stone.

The book's art lies in allowing an ending to be complete without claiming that completion means the eradication of every contradiction. The journey has converted a monkey who wanted to escape death into a protector who can receive a spiritual title, and a solitary scripture-seeker into the member of a complicated fellowship. It has carried the reader through institutions that deserve ridicule, religious mysteries that resist simple decoding, and a long series of encounters that change the meaning of a single goal.

A poor summary would tell us that the travelers passed eighty-one tests and were rewarded. A fuller reading hears what the tests do to one another. The monkey who could not escape the Buddha's palm finally ceases to need a ring. The monk who carries sacred texts discovers that an obligation to an ordinary turtle is still binding. A collection of scrolls may be empty, then full, then torn, and still capable of being carried home.

The last image is not just a victory. It is an invitation to remember the road with better questions than the ones with which we began. What is the difference between getting what you sought and understanding why it mattered? When does restraint become freedom? What do we owe the companions, strangers, and enemies who helped make our journey possible? And how can a book teach us to ask those questions while making us laugh at the traveler who has, once again, been fooled by an impressive-looking piece of paper?
