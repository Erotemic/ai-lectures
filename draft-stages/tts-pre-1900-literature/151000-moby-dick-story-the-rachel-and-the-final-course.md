# Moby-Dick — Story: The Rachel and the Final Course

Near the end of the voyage, Ahab's language becomes more absolute while the sea continues to furnish reasons for caution. He orders the blacksmith Perth to forge a special harpoon. The act resembles a dark ceremony as much as skilled metalwork: material, blood, and oath are gathered into a weapon that Ahab wants to make the instrument of his will. But a sharpened point remains a tool, not a guarantee. The captain asks for an object that can turn an impossible certainty into an actual event.

Starbuck's resistance takes several forms. He speaks openly about the wrongness of pursuing one whale at the expense of the owners and the crew. During a dangerous episode involving the oil casks, he urges the captain to attend to the practical business of the voyage. Ahab sometimes relents in small matters. Those moments are important because they show he can still recognize an argument. He is not in a trance that makes him wholly incapable of choosing; he is a commander who repeatedly chooses to return to his obsession.

A great storm brings lightning to the ship, and the mastheads glow with the electrical phenomenon sailors call Saint Elmo's fire. Men read the spectacle as a warning or a blessing. Starbuck urges a change of course; Ahab imagines confirmation of his power to stand against the heavens. The same event produces incompatible interpretations. In an ordinary voyage such readings might be private fears or curiosities. Here the captain's interpretation can determine who lives, because it is tied to authority over the ship.

Ahab's compass becomes unreliable after the storm, and he makes a substitute to orient the vessel. The episode reveals an irony. He boasts about control over natural forces, yet that control depends on ordinary instruments, the knowledge of sailors, and a magnetic world he cannot command. Later a log line for measuring speed fails as well. Again the ship loses access to dependable information. The physical equipment that should keep a ship on a safe course is treated as secondary to an imagined course that exists mainly in Ahab's mind.

The meeting with the Rachel offers the novel's clearest alternative. Her captain, Gardiner, asks the Pequod to help search for a missing whaleboat; his young son is among those lost. He begs Ahab to join the search for a short time. There is no puzzle about the claim on the Pequod: a child may be alive in the water, and nearby sailors could help. Ahab refuses because he fears losing the trail of Moby Dick. It is not necessary to decide whether the whale embodies evil to evaluate this action. A human being asks for immediate assistance, and the captain chooses his private hunt instead.

Ahab's refusal is more devastating because he is himself a father. His own child, left at home, enters his thoughts at moments when he remembers the life he might have chosen. On the deck he later speaks intimately with Starbuck about years of absence, misery, and the cost of his pursuit. He can imagine a home, a wife, and the meaning of a life spent at sea. Starbuck answers with the possibility of return. Ahab's recognition of loss is genuine, but it does not become a change of course. The tragedy is not that no one has ever shown him another path; it is that he sees it and continues.

Another encounter sharpens the contrast: the Delight, a ship that has lost men in an encounter with the whale, passes with the marks of mourning. Ahab receives yet another reminder that the chase can destroy sailors. The evidence does not dissuade him. News from other captains no longer enters an open deliberation. He selects details that support the plan already made and dismisses the rest.

Fedallah's cryptic assurances persist. Ahab expects conditions so extraordinary that they seem impossible: a strange form of hearse, a death preceded by certain signs, a final relation to hemp. To the captain these are assurances that the ordinary laws of death have somehow been suspended. To a cautious reader they are warnings against treating figurative language as an insurance policy. A prophecy interpreted by the person who most wants to believe it is weak evidence for that person's safety.

The narrative moves toward the whale itself, but the decisive ethical action has already occurred. The captain could have searched for the Rachel's missing boy. He could have accepted Starbuck's arguments and returned to Nantucket with a crew that had completed a difficult occupation. He could have recognized in his conversation about home a reason to stop. Melville does not require us to solve every symbol before recognizing these possibilities. Moby Dick is still at large, while Ahab's command has nearly completed the work of destroying the Pequod.
