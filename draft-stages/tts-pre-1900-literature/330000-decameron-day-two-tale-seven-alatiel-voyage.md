# The Decameron — Alatiel’s Long Voyage

Alatiel is the daughter of a powerful ruler whom the story calls the Soldan of Babylon. Her father sends her by sea to marry a king in the western Mediterranean. The marriage has been planned as a public alliance. But ships do not obey court plans. A storm breaks the voyage, and Alatiel is cast into a succession of foreign households. She travels across regions where she cannot easily communicate and where the men who take charge of her often treat her beauty as something to possess.

The ship runs onto the shore of Majorca. The sailors who try to escape by boat drown; Alatiel and some attendants remain aboard and survive. A nobleman named Pericone takes her in. They cannot speak one another's language, and her refusal of his advances is clear even without common words. Pericone serves wine to a woman unused to drinking it and takes advantage of her intoxication. His brother Marato then kills him and carries Alatiel onto a ship. Two Genoese captains murder Marato by throwing him overboard and quarrel violently over her themselves. One dies and the other is badly wounded. A succession of men who claim to protect her prove unable, or unwilling, to distinguish care from possession.

The survivors reach the eastern Mediterranean. Alatiel is taken into the household of a prince in the Morea, then becomes the object of the Duke of Athens's desire. The duke murders the prince and abducts her. When war threatens and the duke must defend himself, Constantine, a relative of the duke's wife, visits Alatiel, becomes consumed by the same passion, and secretly carries her away by sea. She reaches Chios and later Cyprus. Political rivalries and household intrigues repeatedly change who controls her movements. The names and ports matter less than the accumulating pattern: a royal woman has become the prize in a series of private betrayals, and the violence does not stop because a new captor claims to love her.

At Cyprus, Antigono, a man who once served her father, sees her and recognizes her. For the first time in years Alatiel meets someone who knows her earlier identity without having obtained it through possession. She entrusts him with the history she has concealed from strangers. He arranges her return, but he also teaches her the account she must give the royal households on the journey home. Boccaccio's tale moves with the energy of romance and adventure, but that movement is not the same thing as freedom for the woman at its center.

Four years pass before a means of returning to her father's court emerges. The woman who left home as a royal bride has lived through a history that an official marriage negotiation would never willingly acknowledge. She faces a further dilemma. The arrangements that govern her father's household assume a sexual history she no longer has. A trusted adviser helps her produce a version of events that can be accepted. She claims to have remained untouched through her travels. The tale's final reversal is therefore not simply a safe arrival: it is the successful substitution of a persuasive account for an experience that would expose her to rejection.

Her father believes the explanation and renews the old marriage plan. Alatiel goes to the intended husband, with the public appearance of the bride originally promised. The narrator presents the outcome as fortunate. The ironic distance between what the audience knows and what the father believes is the source of much of the tale's humor. But the humor is built upon lost autonomy, coercion, sexual double standards, and the capacity of public reputation to erase a complicated private life.

Panfilo's story is one of the longest and most uncomfortable examples of Fortune's reach. Its sheer geographical movement can make Alatiel seem like a precious object shipped from one owner to another. In the world of dynastic marriage she is already the subject of an agreement made before the wreck. The men she meets often intensify that condition rather than free her from it. To call every encounter a romantic adventure would conceal the injury present in the plot; to say the heroine possesses no resourcefulness would conceal her struggle to survive.

The ending matters because a story saves her a second time. The first rescue takes place through travel and shelter; the second through the account of herself she presents to her father. The listener, knowing far more than the king, must decide what kind of happy ending this is. It is safety within a social order that has not changed its demands. Boccaccio allows the company to laugh, but a reader can also hear the cost of making a woman acceptable to a world whose categories leave little room for what she has endured.
