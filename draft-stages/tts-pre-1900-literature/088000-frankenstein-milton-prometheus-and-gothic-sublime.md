# Frankenstein — Prometheus, Paradise Lost, and the Gothic Sublime

Frankenstein carries the subtitle The Modern Prometheus. Its 1818 title page also places a line from Milton's Paradise Lost before the story, asking why a maker brought a creature into existence without its consent. These references invite comparison, but neither supplies a code that unlocks every scene. The novel repeatedly changes who resembles creator, who resembles creation, and who experiences punishment. Victor makes a life and abandons it. The creature begins like an Adam without guidance, then feels like Satan cast out from the company of others. The analogies matter precisely because none is complete.

In Paradise Lost, Adam belongs to a created order in which he can address his maker and imagine a relationship with him. Shelley's creature receives neither a place in a community nor a stable account of why it exists. When it reads Milton, it finds language for the injustice it feels. It imagines that it should be loved as a creation but experiences itself as an outcast. We should remember that this is the creature's reading, narrated as part of its education. It is not a definitive declaration that Victor is literally God or that the creature is literally Satan. An interpretation becomes more useful when we ask what it illuminates and where it fails.

Prometheus also offers multiple possibilities. Classical stories associate him with cunning, rebellion, and the gift of fire. A maker who transforms the boundary between living and nonliving matter may seem a modern fire-bringer. Yet Victor never shares his discovery as a public gift; he hides its method and fears its success. The title therefore places grandeur beside irony. An act that might sound heroic in a scientific autobiography becomes catastrophic because it is followed by refusal of ordinary duties. The measure of a creative power is not simply whether it breaks a boundary, but what the maker can answer for afterward.

The novel is Gothic in its pursuit of dread, secrecy, violent interruption, and the uncanny return of a thing that should have stayed concealed. Victor's nightmare after animation joins the hoped-for bride with the decaying body of his mother. The creature appears at moments of vulnerability, often as if a fear in Victor's mind had acquired independent movement. Yet it is a living speaker with its own history, not merely the physical embodiment of his unconscious. Treating it as a symbol alone would erase the moral conflict that gives the Gothic scenes their force.

Mountain and polar landscapes offer what critics call the sublime: encounters with scale, power, beauty, and danger beyond ordinary human control. Victor seeks peace among Alpine peaks, but the creature finds him there and demands a hearing. The Orkney shore is bleak and isolated; it mirrors the narrowed life Victor has chosen. Walton's Arctic is at once an imagined realm of discovery and a threat to his crew's survival. These places do more than reflect emotion. They confront the characters with environments that cannot be commanded into serving a personal project.

There is an important contrast between elevated language and small necessities. The creature speaks about Adam, Satan, and universal injustice, but also needs food, fire, shelter, companionship, and a safe place to sleep. Victor describes transcendent ambition, yet fails to arrange the simplest care for the life he made. Gothic grandeur can tempt a reader to admire powerful feeling rather than count its consequences. Shelley keeps bringing the narrative back to people who are hungry, frightened, abandoned, or dead.

As a listening practice, choose one allusion and one landscape. Ask how the allusion changes when spoken by Victor rather than the creature; ask what the landscape makes possible and what it makes impossible. Then return to the first animation scene. No mythic analogy relieves Victor of the task of staying to greet the life before him. That gap between sublime ambition and ordinary responsibility is where much of the novel's enduring force resides.
