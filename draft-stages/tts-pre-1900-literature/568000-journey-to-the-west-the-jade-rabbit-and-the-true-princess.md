# Journey to the West: The jade rabbit and the true princess

## The bee at the wedding feast

Xuanzang entered the women's court believing himself trapped at his own wedding. His three disciples had been formally dismissed from Tianzhu, and the palace celebrated as if nothing remained but the bride's appearance. The bridegroom, in his monk's cap and ceremonial robe, was led between painted screens and columns wrapped in silk. He lowered his eyes before the queens and attendants. He knew that Wukong was with him, but the monkey had made himself into a bee, and there was little comfort in looking for one's rescuer among flowers and hair ornaments.

The royal women assembled. The princess came forward among them, adorned like a flower that had been gathered for a festival. She bowed to the king and to the queen. Wukong, balanced at the top of Xuanzang's hat, watched through eyes that could see what court dress could conceal. A small trace of uncanny breath rose above her head. It was not the violent darkness of a mountain tyrant or a creature that had eaten a village, but it did not belong to the king's human daughter.

The bee moved down to Xuanzang's ear.

"That one is false," Wukong whispered. "The princess has been replaced."

Xuanzang felt both relief and fresh terror. At last there was an answer to the prisoner's story. But the women around the king were not combatants, and the palace was full of people who would panic if a demon fought among them. He asked Wukong to wait until the king and his household could be moved away. In that much the disciple and the master agreed: a rescue could become another disaster if it treated the innocent as furniture to be broken.

Wukong had never been easy to persuade to wait. He sprang back into his ordinary form, shouted that the princess was an impostor, and seized her before the household had understood what was happening. Curtains flew back; musicians scattered; the queen's ladies ran in several directions at once. Xuanzang caught the frightened king and told him that the violence was meant to identify the false princess, not to harm the royal family.

The supposed bride slipped free. She tore away the wedding ornaments and ran to a small shrine in the palace garden. From it she took a short heavy weapon, broad at one end, as though made for pounding something in a mortar. She struck at Wukong. He met her with his iron staff, and the two fought through the garden amid the broken order of the feast.

It was a sight the palace had never intended to provide its guests: the bride in combat with a monkey who should have been far along the road to the Buddha. Their fight rose into the sky. From the courtyards below, the people of Tianzhu watched the clouds move around two flashing weapons and tried to understand why a wedding procession had turned into a heavenly battle.

## The pestle from the moon

The impostor was strong enough to resist Wukong for a long time. He multiplied his staff, sending a forest of iron rods against her. She could not hold the whole attack and fled as a gust toward the heavenly gate. Wukong went after her, shouting to the guards to stop the escaping spirit. The celestial officers barred the way, and she turned to fight him again.

He saw the weapon clearly. It was a pounding pestle of rare substance, not an ordinary court staff. The false princess declared its origin with pride: it came from the moon palace, where medicine was ground. She had carried it down from the world above. She was no roadside fox who had found a convenient dress and stolen a title. She belonged to a realm Wukong knew to be full of beings whose beauty could conceal old grudges.

She called him by the heavenly office he most despised, the old title of horse keeper. The words struck where no ordinary weapon could. Wukong, who had endured years under a mountain for resisting the court of Heaven, attacked with fresh anger. The false princess retreated again. At last she fled toward a mountain in the south and disappeared into a cave. Wukong searched but did not find her. He turned back to the palace because a fleeing enemy might return by another route while Xuanzang remained among the terrified courtiers.

The king had not left Xuanzang's side. He asked Wukong a question that mattered more than the struggle above the palace.

"If she was not my daughter, where is my daughter?"

Wukong told him that the truth was close to the story of the girl confined at Gold-Spreading Temple. First he would catch or expose the creature who had taken her place; then they would bring the missing princess home. He asked that Xuanzang be kept safe on the throne-room side of the palace, with Bajie and Sha recalled to guard him. Then he set off to look for the fugitive again.

A brighter figure now crossed his path. The Lady of the Moon, Taiyin, came down with attendants and called out that the fleeing creature was her Jade Rabbit. The rabbit had disappeared from the lunar palace and taken the form of a young woman on earth. Its pestle was the implement by which the moon-dweller worked, and its borrowed human form had carried a grievance far older than the wedding ceremony.

The moon goddess asked Wukong to spare the creature. He would gladly have struck down a demon that had imprisoned an innocent girl, nearly trapped Xuanzang in marriage, and brought violence into a king's household. But the celestial mistress claimed responsibility for her runaway servant. Wukong had seen too many occasions when an earthly monster turned out to belong to someone of rank above. He did not mistake celestial ownership for innocence. Yet he allowed Taiyin to reclaim the rabbit, and the creature's disguise was stripped away.

## The grievance of the rabbit

Taiyin explained the older conflict. The real princess of Tianzhu had a connection to a celestial woman named Su'e, who had once lived in the moon palace. There had been an injury, a blow given to the Jade Rabbit. Years later the rabbit saw its chance for revenge. It slipped into the human world, seized the reborn Su'e from the garden, and took her place in the king's family. When it learned that Xuanzang was approaching, the rabbit wanted more than revenge. It wanted to draw him into marriage and take the spiritual vitality of the pilgrim for itself.

To the court, such a history explained what human officials could not have guessed. To the young woman locked away at the monastery, it would not make a year of confinement less real. Xuanzang had heard her cries. The king would have to hear them too.

Wukong returned with the news. The false princess had been the moon's rabbit, and the real princess was alive. The queen and courtiers stared at one another. During all the days when they had dressed a stranger as their daughter, the person to whom the clothes and name belonged had been imprisoned a short journey away. The king wept. He was ashamed, bewildered, and eager to make the matter right.

The following morning, after the first shock had passed, Xuanzang told the story as carefully as he could. At Gold-Spreading Temple an old monk had found a girl carried in by a night wind. The girl claimed to be the king's daughter. When the monk came to court and found that the princess was said to be present, he feared the stranger might be a spirit. Rather than release her, he had locked her in a room. The girl had spoken wildly by day to keep men away from her and wept for her parents at night. The old monk had finally entrusted the matter to the travelers.

The king gathered an escort and went with his queen toward the temple, which lay some distance from the capital. Wukong flew ahead and told the monks to prepare to receive the royal family. The old monk came to him with the same anxious question he had asked before.

"Was she truly a princess?"

"Your king is coming to answer that himself," Wukong said. "Open the room when he arrives."

## The door unlocked

The royal party reached the monastery with attendants, music, and a confusion of pilgrims and officials. The old monk led them to the locked chamber at the rear. Inside, the captive girl still spoke in a manner meant to drive unwelcome visitors away. She had been surviving on food passed through a narrow opening and whatever privacy she could contrive in confinement. She did not look like a person who had lived among silk, servants, and festivals.

The king ordered the lock opened. The queen entered with him. They recognized their daughter beneath the dirt and disordered clothing. They embraced her without waiting for attendants to wash her or provide new garments. She stopped her deliberate nonsense when she heard their voices. For a time none of them could speak. A royal family that had believed itself complete was complete again only after acknowledging how badly it had mistaken a presence for a person.

Warm water and clean clothes were brought. The princess was returned to the capital. The old monk's fear had protected her from some dangers while exposing her to others; no solemn statement could make that whole year disappear. But the king thanked the monastery for keeping her alive and recognized that the travelers' questions had finally broken the deception.

Wukong asked for something beyond gratitude. The road through Hundred-Leg Mountain was still troubled by venomous creatures, especially centipedes that preyed on travelers. Since roosters were their natural enemy in the story's logic, he suggested releasing many strong cocks on the mountain and making the road safer. He also asked the king to give the temple a new name, support its restoration, and reward the old abbot. The king agreed, renaming the hill Treasure-Flower Mountain and giving the monastery royal patronage.

The pilgrims were not permitted to leave at once. Pain had become celebration, and the king wanted the people who had restored his daughter to share it. Painters were summoned to preserve their likenesses. The princess herself thanked Xuanzang and the three disciples. For several days the palace offered banquets. Bajie did not object to that portion of the royal gratitude. Xuanzang did. He had been the object of one marriage ceremony already and had no wish to become a permanent guest of the court for another kind of entertainment.

At last the king presented silver, gold, and precious goods. The monks refused them. He signed the travel documents and escorted the company out of the city, surrounded by people who wished to see the rescuers one more time. The crowd followed so far that Wukong finally sent a wind across their eyes, confusing the path just long enough for the pilgrims to slip away.

They went west again: Xuanzang on the white horse, Wukong moving ahead with his staff, Bajie thinking about the banquets left behind, and Sha bearing the baggage. They had saved a princess and escaped marriage, but the final mountain was still ahead. The road had not ended merely because the distant country was called Tianzhu.
