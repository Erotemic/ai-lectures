# Journey to the West: The false Thunderclap temple

## The words above the gate

Beyond the thorn ridge, the pilgrims met another mountain, tall enough to tempt Xuanzang into thinking it might be one of the last before the western shrine. It was spring. New leaves trembled beside the path, birds were active among the rocks, and a distant building glimmered through colored mist. Bells carried over the valley. From far off the place appeared to be a great religious foundation, with raised roofs, red pillars, and a brightness that promised holiness.

Wukong shaded his eyes and studied it. There was something wrong. The outlines suggested the Thunderclap monastery where the Buddha dwelled, but the road was not one he knew. A thin current of hostile vapor stirred among the auspicious clouds. He said to his master that a handsome temple need not be safe. They should inspect it without assuming that the sight of a building made it holy.

Xuanzang had longed to see Thunderclap so fiercely that even a resemblance made him impatient. Perhaps they were closer than Wukong believed. Perhaps the west contained many blessed residences, each worthy of reverence. Bajie thought that anyone living in so grand a building would be good enough to offer a bed and a meal. Sha suggested they need not settle the question from a distance: the path led past the gate, and they could read what was written on it.

The first look almost settled the matter against Wukong. Xuanzang saw the name Thunderclap Temple and fell from his horse in haste to bow. He scolded his disciple for doubting the place. Wukong pointed to the sign again. There were four characters, not three. The first was *small*. This was Little Thunderclap Temple, not the great monastery they sought. Wukong insisted he knew the real road. Xuanzang answered that a sanctuary of the Buddha did not cease to deserve reverence because it was a lesser sanctuary. He was vowed to bow where he found Buddhist images. What would become of such a vow if fear could excuse him from entering a temple?

That was how the travelers passed through a gate Wukong had warned them against. The people who had laid the trap did not have to drag Xuanzang in. His own determination brought him to their hall.

Inside, a voice called out as if the Buddha himself had been waiting for the party. Xuanzang changed into his formal robe and approached the main shrine. Monks, arhats, guardians, bodhisattvas, and attendants appeared arranged in ranks, too numerous to count. The central figure sat high upon a lotus throne. Incense hung in the air. Xuanzang bowed step by step, with Bajie and Sha following. Only Wukong stood still.

The voice from the throne demanded that the monkey bow too. Wukong had come close enough to see through the decoration. He seized his iron staff and shouted that the entire assembly was a company of monsters pretending to be holy beings. To assume a sacred name was bad enough; to use it as bait against a traveler seeking the scriptures was an offense he would not overlook. He raised his weapon toward the figure on the throne.

A clang fell from above. Two enormous golden cymbals shut around him, sealing him in darkness from head to foot. Bajie reached for his rake and Sha for his staff, but the false monks rushed them from every direction. They were bound before they could reach their leader. Xuanzang was seized with them. The splendid temple emptied of its holy appearance. The figure on the lotus seat showed himself as Yellow Brows, a demon king who had arranged the hall to resemble the Buddha's court. He laughed at the success of his disguise.

The demon carried his prisoners to the rear buildings. He planned to leave Wukong inside the sealed cymbals for three days until the monkey was destroyed, and afterward to cook the other pilgrims. He did not intend to debate theology with any of them. All the pageantry had one purpose: to make Xuanzang kneel long enough to be captured.

## A prison that answered every spell

Within the gold, Wukong could hear his own breathing and the faintest sounds of movement outside. He beat against the walls with his staff. Nothing shifted. He enlarged himself, growing as he had grown during his rebellion in Heaven. The cymbals enlarged with him. He shrank to the size of a seed. The prison shrank too. Not a hairline of light appeared.

He turned the staff into a great prop and tried to pry the halves apart. He made drills from his hairs and attacked the seam. The metal rang, but did not yield. He was a master of forms and sizes, and the object answered every change by maintaining its grip. In other stories a prison fails when the prisoner discovers that it is merely large or merely strong. Here the treasure seemed to know his movements before he made them.

Wukong summoned the lesser guardian spirits who watched over the pilgrimage. The Five-Face Revealers, the six Ding and six Jia spirits, and the protectors of the Buddhist law gathered outside. They promised that their attention had not left Xuanzang. But no number of shoulders would lift the shining trap. Wukong, sweating and furious, told them to bring help that could.

A Revealer flew to the Jade Emperor and reported that a demon had installed a counterfeit Buddha in a temple, trapped the scripture-seeking monk, and locked Sun Wukong inside golden cymbals that none of the ordinary protectors could open. The Emperor ordered the Twenty-Eight Lunar Mansions down from Heaven. The star officers came at night, when the monster's soldiers were celebrating their capture and sleeping after the feast.

The celestial visitors did not strike at once. They feared that an enormous blow on the golden metal would wake the entire hall and cause the demon to harm the prisoners. They tried lifting, twisting, and prying with swords and spears. Every grip failed. Wukong shouted from within that they should stop being delicate and break the thing. Their commander answered that they needed a better opening.

Kang Jinlong, the Horned Golden Dragon star, proposed a painful trick. If he pressed his horn through the meeting seam, perhaps the monkey could shrink and travel along it. He reduced himself and eased the tip into the gold. When his horn enlarged, the cymbals stretched tightly around it as though the metal were living flesh. There was no passage beside it.

Wukong felt the horn under his hands. He asked Kang to endure a little more. With a small drill he bored a narrow hole through the horn itself, then shrank until he could hide in the hole. The star officer pulled with all his strength, dragging his own horn back through the golden grip. Wukong emerged at the end, battered but free. He stood upon the floor again and smashed the empty cymbals with his staff. Gold fragments scattered across the hall. That sound did wake everyone.

The demon called his troops together. Wukong and the star officers withdrew into the sky, where they could meet Yellow Brows in an open fight. The monster came outside carrying a short spiked mace. He boasted that Little Thunderclap was his lawful realm. He had acquired holiness by his own cultivation, he said, and had arranged the great sanctuary to draw the pilgrims into a wager. If Wukong beat him, they might go on; if the demon won, he would kill them and go to India to collect the scriptures himself. Wukong told him to stop performing holiness and begin fighting.

## The bag

The first fifty exchanges did not settle the contest. The monster's mace was short and supple, but it caught and deflected the iron staff. The star officers and the Revealer spirits joined in, weapons flashing around Yellow Brows. He still did not flee. He reached to his waist for a shabby-looking white cloth bag and tossed it into the air.

The bag opened with a snap and swallowed the fighters—Wukong, the Twenty-Eight Mansions, and the protectors—as though a company of armored gods weighed less than a handful of grain. The demon threw the bag over his shoulder, walked back to his temple, and emptied its contents onto the floor. His servants bound the prisoners and dragged them to the rear chambers. The great golden cymbals had taken one warrior. The cloth bag took almost everyone who came to save him.

In the night Wukong heard someone weeping. It was Xuanzang. The master had tried to conceal his despair from the guards, but he could not stop blaming himself. He had insisted that reverence required entering the temple. Now his protectors were bound because he had mistaken imitation for sanctity.

The monkey listened in silence. He was not pleased to have been proved right. His master had remembered him in the middle of suffering, and that mattered. Wukong shrank, slipped out of his cords, and made his way through the dark to the other captives. He freed Xuanzang, then Bajie and Sha, then the stars and Revealer spirits. They gathered the horse and began to flee the compound.

But the bundles were missing. In them were the travel papers, the scripture-seeking monk's precious robe, the begging bowl, and the other possessions they had carried from kingdom to kingdom. Kang Jinlong told Wukong to be sensible. People mattered more than luggage. The monkey answered that those belongings were also the visible proof of the pilgrimage, and without them the rescued company could be stopped at the next court. The others went to a safe place below the slope while Wukong returned to look.

He became a bat and slipped beneath a tiled roof. Through the rooms he followed a glimmer. The jeweled robe had been crammed into a bundle and was shining through the fabric. Wukong took it up. In his haste, one end dropped onto the floorboards. The crash woke the demon. Guards shouted that the prisoners had escaped. Wukong abandoned stealth and vaulted through a window with what he could carry.

At dawn Yellow Brows pursued the fugitives. The star officers, the protectors, and the two younger disciples turned to meet him, leaving Xuanzang and the horse behind. Their army was larger than it had been the previous day. The demon waited until the fighting drew close, raised the cloth bag, and gathered them all up again. This time Wukong understood the warning signs and escaped on a cloud before the mouth of the bag reached him.

Alone on a hill, Wukong watched the monster return to the temple. He had broken one treasure, freed the prisoners, and recovered their belongings. None of it had secured their escape. Xuanzang was again in the demon's hands, and the friends who had come from Heaven were imprisoned beside him. He was ashamed of how many rescuers had suffered because he had asked for help.

He sat looking toward the counterfeit sanctuary, his anger giving way to grief. The wrong question, he realized, was how many soldiers could defeat a demon who did not need to defeat them in battle. The bag would swallow the next army as easily as the last. He remembered another power: the Northern Lord of True Martial, the demon-quelling master on Mount Wudang. If anyone could help against this creature, perhaps that lord could. Wukong rose and turned his cloud toward the south.

The temple still shone below him, decorated as if it belonged to the Buddha. Its gates stood over men and gods in ropes. The real Thunderclap remained farther west.
