# Othello — Story: The Night of the Deaths

The final night in Cyprus contains two assaults that are meant to confirm each other. Roderigo attacks Cassio in the street and is wounded. Hidden nearby, Iago stabs Cassio and slips away from the apparent position of attacker. Othello hears Cassio's cries and assumes that Iago has fulfilled his promise to kill the supposedly unfaithful officer. The inference is wrong: Cassio survives. But Othello takes the sounds as permission to complete his own crime. Once again, he treats a fragment of evidence as a complete account because it matches the story he has chosen to believe.

Iago emerges in the street acting as a helpful rescuer. He kills the wounded Roderigo, whose testimony could expose his dealings, and works to direct suspicion toward Bianca. Cassio is alive, but confused and injured. The conspirator continues to arrange appearances even while his plan is becoming unstable. The stage moves between public confusion in the street and a private bedroom where there is nobody left to protect Desdemona.

Othello enters as Desdemona sleeps. He has decided to kill her as punishment for a sexual betrayal that did not occur. He imagines the act as justice and speaks as if he is saving others from future deception. These words are not the play's moral verdict. They are the murderer's attempt to make violence seem necessary. Desdemona wakes and denies the accusation. She begs for time and for her life. Othello refuses. He smothers her in the bed that had been associated with their new marriage.

Emilia knocks and comes in with news from the street. Cassio is alive, and Roderigo has died. Desdemona makes a final effort to speak, affirming her innocence and, in some versions of the dialogue, trying to shield her husband from responsibility. Othello acknowledges that he killed her, and he repeats the evidence of the handkerchief. Emilia hears the story and realizes what her husband asked her to do with the cloth. She had picked it up and handed it to Iago, but had not joined his design to frame Desdemona. She now refuses to keep quiet, even when Iago orders her to hold her tongue.

The exposure is collective. Emilia tells what she knows; others arrive; the account of the handkerchief is reconstructed; documents found on Roderigo and surviving testimony help reveal more of Iago's schemes. Iago kills Emilia to silence her, then is captured. Othello, finally confronting the manufactured nature of his belief, wounds Iago but does not kill him. Iago refuses to give a complete explanation of his motives. The refusal is one of the play's hardest endings: the person who controlled everyone else's interpretations with words now withdraws his own words.

Othello speaks about himself, remembering service to Venice and asking others to recount his deeds. He also attempts to describe his enormous error. This final speech can be heard as confession, self-defense, or a mixture; it cannot restore Desdemona or make the murder an accident. He kills himself beside her. Cassio survives and is left in command, while Iago is taken away for punishment. The play does not show the eventual sentence carried out on him.

The tragedy ends with no successful love plot hidden behind the violence. Desdemona's innocence was present all along. Cassio did not have the affair of which he was accused. Bianca's possession of the handkerchief was part of a route she did not design. Emilia learned the meaning of her own small act and risked her life to tell the truth. Othello could have listened, delayed, tested the testimony, or refused to turn imagined injury into a death sentence. He did not. Shakespeare has constructed a world in which the audience possesses the missing evidence and watches a person with power reject every opportunity to reconsider. That is what gives the ending its cruelty: not simply that the truth comes late, but that the person murdered had been telling it throughout.
