# The Decameron · Day 8: frame and the day’s complete tales

## The Decameron — Day Eight: Lauretta and Tricks Without Boundaries

The seventh day's storytelling ends in the Valley of the Ladies. Dioneo has presided over a series of wives' stratagems, heard among trees and beside clear water. As the afternoon passes, he gives the crown to Lauretta. She might have retaliated by ordering stories in which husbands fool wives. Instead she chooses a wider field: tricks women play on men, men on women, and one man on another. The enlargement matters because the tricks of the seventh day were never only a question of gender. They were about access to knowledge, the management of appearances, and the power to decide what another person must believe.

The company spends the remaining hours singing and walking in the valley. In the evening they return to their home. Early the next day, after walking in the meadows, they attend service at a nearby church, eat together, sing and dance, and rest through the hotter part of the day. Their religious observance and their very worldly fiction remain close together. They do not live in a fantasy where all duties have vanished; they have built a schedule in which duty and chosen amusement can coexist. Once the sun has passed noon, Lauretta gathers the company again at the fountain and asks Neifile to begin.

The tales she hears range far beyond the jealous-marriage plots of the preceding day. Two opening stories turn sexual bargaining into disputed accounts of debt and payment. Others humiliate people who believe their authority entitles them to special knowledge. A judge's public dignity can be undone by a prank from below the courtroom bench. A physician imagines he is being initiated into a society of secret pleasures. A scholar, made to suffer humiliation by a widow, answers with retaliation so severe that the comedy becomes difficult to accept on the terms of an ordinary practical joke.

And now a trio enters the collection whose names are worth remembering. Calandrino is a gullible Florentine painter with a great appetite for profit and marvels. Bruno and Buffalmacco, also painters within the fiction, are skilled at noticing what he hopes to believe. In one story they send him hunting for a stone that makes a person invisible. In another they steal his pig and arrange for him to be accused of the theft. The two tricksters will return, and Calandrino will be made a recurring comic object. Their repeated appearances make the city of Florence feel less like a series of isolated anecdotes and more like a network of acquaintances and grudges.

A viewer or listener must still keep the levels of the text apart. Painters with these names appear in later historical and artistic discussion, but a lively episode in Boccaccio is not proof that the recorded person actually endured it. The fiction draws energy from recognizable trades and places. It does not thereby become a court deposition or a reliable biography.

Lauretta's theme lets us compare kinds of deceit that produce quite different results. There is the trick that merely exposes pretension, the trick that takes wealth, the trick that beats or terrorizes another person, and the trick that recovers something already stolen. The ten tellers do not speak with one moral voice. Their stories instead ask what changes when the laughing audience knows facts withheld from the character on whom the joke is played. As Day Eight opens, that gap between reader and victim becomes the day's main instrument.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 1: Gulfardo and the Debt

Gulfardo is a soldier of fortune who wants an affair with the wife of Guasparruolo, a wealthy merchant. She is willing to meet him, but asks for a substantial payment: two hundred florins. The request turns what Gulfardo hoped would be an intimate bargain into an explicit price. He agrees, though he is unhappy about the terms. Neifile's narrator, herself one of the women in the country-house circle, begins the new day by telling a story about a man who intends to outmaneuver a woman who believes she controls the exchange.

Gulfardo already owes money to Guasparruolo. Instead of looking for an entirely new sum, he borrows two hundred florins from the merchant and brings the money to the merchant's wife as the price she demanded. He spends time with her. At this stage the arrangement looks complete from her perspective: he has received what he asked for, and she has been paid. But the money has been borrowed from her own husband, and Gulfardo has arranged the affair so that its financial record can be retold.

Later, in the wife's presence, Gulfardo tells Guasparruolo that he has repaid the loan by handing the money to the merchant's wife. She cannot deny receiving the sum without contradicting what the men can establish. Her husband expects the money to be turned over as a loan repayment. She hands it to him. Gulfardo's debt vanishes, and the money she believed was hers has returned to the household from which it came. The same payment has been counted twice, in two incompatible private agreements.

The merchant sees an ordinary transaction. His wife knows another reason the money changed hands. Gulfardo knows both explanations and chooses the one he can safely announce in public. The trick depends on the asymmetry. The woman cannot state her understanding of the money's purpose without publicly admitting the affair. It is not that the husband is incapable of arithmetic; he has no reason to know which account is missing from his books.

Neifile presents the woman's demand for payment as deserving a rebuke. That is the moral position of the fictional storyteller, not a rule we must adopt. The man also arranged to deceive someone about the agreed price of sex, and he remains protected by her fear of exposure. Their power is not equal simply because both enter the bargain. The story's design makes private conduct costly to reveal, then allows the better-informed party to exploit that secrecy.

The opening tale of Day Eight is therefore a financial comedy as much as a sexual one. Credit, cash and reputation are made to interact. One transaction can be narrated as a loan settlement, another as a sexual fee. The person who speaks first in the accepted public language determines what the payment means. Boccaccio has begun Lauretta's day by making accounting itself a form of storytelling.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 2: Belcolore and the Priest

In the village of Varlungo, near Florence, a parish priest has his eye on Monna Belcolore, a married countrywoman with wit, energy and an understanding of what her attention might be worth. He courts her with gifts and talk. She does not think his status as priest should exempt him from ordinary bargaining. When the chance comes for a private encounter, she asks for money that she needs, five lire. He has no cash with him. Instead he leaves a valuable outer garment as security, promising to recover it when he brings the sum.

Belcolore has the garment, and the priest has enjoyed the encounter. The material guarantee appears to place the woman in control of the bargain. But when the priest returns to his affairs, he begins to regret giving up the pledge. He is not eager to pay what was agreed, and he does not like the idea that another person can hold a valued possession against his word. He devises a way to bring the garment home without making the payment.

He asks for a household mortar and pestle, borrowing them through a pretext that seems innocent. When it is time for the things to be returned, he arranges for the transaction to be made known in such a way that Belcolore will have to send back his garment. The cloak and the kitchen tools become pieces in a contest over obligation. What was a promise between two people can be reclassified as a temporary exchange of objects rather than the price of the earlier meeting.

Belcolore understands that she has been cheated, and her final refusal is memorable. Whatever the priest may want in the future, she tells him in earthy language that he will no longer have access to what she once offered. The sexual double meaning of mortar and pestle makes her speech both an insult and an assertion that the encounter is finished. The priest recovers his garment, but he loses the easy welcome that brought him to her in the first place.

Panfilo's tale follows immediately after Gulfardo's account of a payment that returns to the buyer. The two stories rhyme. In each, a woman demands a price and a man finds a way to recover his money or property while concealing the reason it was transferred. Yet the details differ. Belcolore is a recognizable figure in a rural community; the priest's standing is not simply social prestige but religious office. His willingness to bargain for sex while seeking the privileges of respectability makes the fraud sharper.

The tale does not require us to treat the woman as a victim without agency. Belcolore speaks, negotiates, and finally refuses. Nor does it require us to applaud the priest merely because he has the cleverer trick. His maneuver gives a story of desire the language of debt and return, exposing the gap between religious status and personal conduct. Her closing words puncture that status with a joke that uses the very kitchen equipment through which she was deceived.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 3: Calandrino and the Heliotrope

Calandrino is a painter in Florence, easily fascinated by promises of wealth and hidden powers. His acquaintances Bruno and Buffalmacco know this and often amuse themselves at his expense. One day another wit, Maso del Saggio, allows Calandrino to overhear what sounds like a learned discussion of wondrous stones. The talk passes through fanciful lands and marvelous properties until it reaches a prize Calandrino cannot resist: the heliotrope, a stone said to make its possessor invisible.

The supposed source of the stone is conveniently local. It can, Maso says, be found among the pebbles of the Mugnone, a stream outside Florence. Calandrino rushes to tell Bruno and Buffalmacco. They pretend to be excited and make plans for the hunt. They also begin privately planning a much more practical marvel: convincing their companion that he has acquired the power and can no longer be seen.

The three go to the stream, where Calandrino gathers dark stones until his clothing is heavy with them. Bruno and Buffalmacco move a little apart. They speak as though their friend has disappeared and loudly wonder where he has gone. Calandrino hears everything. Because they do not address or look toward him, he reasons that one of his stones must have succeeded. The companions then throw stones at him as they continue pretending to see no one. Rather than recognizing an assault by his friends, he treats the blows as accidental and keeps quiet, fearful that speaking will dissolve his miraculous condition.

He hurries home, delighted at the chance to use invisibility for profit. His wife, Tessa, naturally sees him and asks why he has come carrying such a ridiculous load. To Calandrino this proves that a woman has broken the spell. He turns his anger against her and beats her. Bruno and Buffalmacco arrive and hear his furious explanation. They know precisely why the magic failed: it never existed. But they allow his mistaken account to stand, reinforcing the misogynistic superstition that makes the wife responsible for what his friends have done.

The narrator makes Calandrino ridiculous, but the joke has a dark edge. Three men manufacture a false belief, two of them inflict painful blows, and a woman who did not participate becomes the target of the fool's anger. Calandrino is gullible not because he is simply ignorant of stone lore, but because he urgently wants a power over the people around him. That wish makes him believe the performance of invisibility even as he feels the stones hitting his body.

The sequence inaugurates a group of recurring comic characters. Bruno and Buffalmacco will return in further stories, and Calandrino will again mistake staged evidence for a discovery. Their world is not populated by abstract tricksters. It is a small network of working Florentines with knowledge of one another's habits. We need not assume any encounter happened to historical painters of the same names. The literary characters are complete in this scene: Maso supplies the legend, the friends supply false witnesses, and Calandrino supplies the longing that makes the lie work.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 4: The Rector of Fiesole

A widow in Florence is being pursued by the rector of Fiesole, a cleric whose persistence has become unwelcome. She has made it clear that she does not want him. He continues anyway, relying on office, proximity and repeated requests. What he takes as courtship she experiences as pressure. Having found direct refusal ineffective, she works out a way to expose him before someone whose judgment he cannot easily disregard.

The widow pretends to yield. She tells the rector that he may come to her bed in darkness, where they can meet without scandal. The priest, excited by the apparent change, fails to imagine that the setting might have been designed for an audience. The woman arranges for a maid, described by the narrator as unattractive and old, to take her place. The maid is paid for the role. The substitution depends on darkness, silence, and the priest's certainty that he is entitled to the encounter he believes he has finally won.

At the appointed time the rector enters the darkened room and takes the substitute for his hostess. Then the widow's brothers bring the bishop to witness the scene. The rector's powerful religious superior sees the priest in circumstances that contradict the decorum of his office. The affair is exposed not by an argument, but by directing authority to an already staged piece of evidence. The cleric who ignored a woman's refusal is made answerable to the person who can discipline him.

The bishop rebukes and punishes the rector, and his humiliation circulates as a local joke. For the widow, the result is release from harassment. She has not persuaded the priest by moral reasoning. She has used the social mechanism he respects—ecclesiastical supervision—to stop his pursuit. Emilia's narrator invites us to take pleasure in a person of power being trapped by his own appetite.

Yet the maid's role is also worth examining. She is not merely a neutral item of scenery but a working woman recruited, for pay, into the most physically intimate part of the plot. The widow secures an outcome she needs by directing risk toward someone below her in the household hierarchy. The narration treats the substitute's age and appearance as a joke at the priest's expense. A listener can see the critique of a predatory cleric without repeating that contempt toward the servant.

The story is one of several in which Boccaccio turns a would-be investigator into the person being investigated. The priest thinks he has secured a private encounter, but his privacy is theatrical. The bishop's arrival makes the private public. Unlike the clever confessor episode of the preceding day, a religious official does not successfully impersonate authority here. He is caught by it. The widow's victory does not depend on proving that all priests behave similarly. It depends on making one persistent man unable to escape the meaning of his own actions.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 5: The Judge from the Marches

A magistrate arrives in Florence from the Marches to administer justice. His appearance and manners do not impress the young men of the city. He presents the signs of judicial dignity but has little of the judgment that might justify them. His robes and place on the bench demand respect, yet to the pranksters who watch him these objects seem almost to invite a test. Filostrato chooses a tale in which authority is not argued down by a speech. It is physically dismantled in front of the people who are supposed to obey it.

Three Florentine men decide to make the judge ridiculous. Rather than confront him with an accusation about any specific case, they arrange access to the space near and beneath his bench while he sits in court. During a hearing, with attention ostensibly fixed on the business of justice, one of them works close enough to the judge's clothing to pull down his breeches. The magistrate's lower body is exposed before the onlookers. The court turns from a place of solemn decision into a scene of public laughter.

The action is quick, and that is part of its violence. A judge depends on the cooperation of everyone in the room in treating his body and clothing as signs of office. Once those signs can be handled as comic objects by a stranger beneath the bench, the impression of unassailable authority disappears. No elaborate lies are required. The prank exploits a practical weakness in the stage on which rank is displayed.

Filostrato's fictional Florentines are satisfied. The judge's pompous assumptions have been punctured, and the story treats the humiliation as a deserved correction. But its meaning is more limited than a judicial reform. We are not told that the procedure protects a litigant, changes a law, or improves the quality of verdicts. The prank only gives spectators a powerful visual contrast between the splendid clothing of office and the ordinary human body underneath it.

The tale belongs beside the Sixth Day's witty answers to nobles and officials. There, a speaker could expose pretension with a phrase that left the social order formally intact. Here, the correction is a bodily insult, and the audience's laughter takes the place of reasoned public criticism. Boccaccio may revel in the collapse, but a reader need not confuse embarrassment with accountability.

The shortest stories in the collection can have complicated implications because they focus a whole social situation into one motion. The bench is supposed to raise the judge above the crowd. The trick begins below it. A man's clothing is supposed to certify his authority. The prank makes those same clothes reveal his vulnerability. Day Eight's theme allows us to ask why spectators are eager to treat a stranger's embarrassment as proof that an official deserves no respect, and whether that judgment is supported by anything more than the joke itself.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 6: Calandrino and the Stolen Pig

Calandrino, the painter who believed he had become invisible by collecting stones from the Mugnone, has saved a pig for his household. Bruno and Buffalmacco, his companions in the earlier joke, learn about it and decide they would prefer to possess the animal themselves. They steal and slaughter the pig. Then, instead of letting Calandrino endure an unexplained loss, they return to offer their expertise in discovering the thief. His trust in these two men has survived the affair of the heliotrope.

Calandrino is distraught. The loss has obvious practical cost: the meat of a pig is a household resource, not an ornamental trinket. Bruno and Buffalmacco advise a test they present as capable of identifying a thief. Each suspect will take a specially prepared morsel of sweet food, and the guilty person will be unable to swallow it. It sounds like a procedure in which truth reveals itself involuntarily through the body. The tricksters take responsibility for supplying and distributing the morsels.

They control the test completely. Calandrino's portion is made bitter and almost impossible to eat, while the other portions are harmless. When Calandrino struggles, the false experiment seems to have produced a decisive result. His own companions now accuse him of stealing his own pig, perhaps to conceal it from his wife and use it elsewhere. They threaten to expose him at home unless he pays for their silence. Calandrino gives them capons, suffering a second loss on top of the first.

The trick has a particularly cruel arrangement. The thieves are the investigators. They plant both the evidence and its interpretation, then charge the victim for relief from an accusation they know to be false. Calandrino is made a suspect in the loss he came to them to explain. As in the heliotrope story, the fraud succeeds because he wants to believe that a simple external test can solve a confusing problem. This time the imagined marvel is not invisibility but a machine for reading guilt.

Filomena's audience is invited to laugh, yet the plot raises a question that the laughter cannot settle: what could Calandrino have done differently? He has chosen unreliable friends, but the false proof is imposed on him by people who know the truth and control the procedure. He lacks the knowledge necessary to test the test. The reader can see the invention before the victim does; that is why the joke makes sense to us and not to him.

The repeated characters give the day continuity. Calandrino does not become wiser after the first humiliation, while Bruno and Buffalmacco refine their method. Their cruelty is inventive, and Boccaccio's fictional Florence offers no guaranteed correction for it. In this story, unlike a witty retort that resolves a misunderstanding, cleverness is the deliberate creation of an injustice. The pig is stolen twice in a sense: first as meat, then as the occasion for extracting still more food from the person who lost it.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 7: Rinieri, Elena, and the Seasons

A scholar named Rinieri returns to Florence after years of study in Paris. He notices a beautiful, wealthy widow named Elena and falls in love. She is already attached to another man. Instead of refusing the scholar directly and leaving it there, she decides with her lover to make sport of him. She gives Rinieri hope of a secret meeting, and he believes he has been admitted to the intimacy he desires. The night chosen is bitterly cold.

Rinieri comes as instructed and waits outside while Elena remains indoors with her preferred lover. She prolongs the waiting through false promises until the scholar spends the freezing night exposed to wind and snow. He fears he may die. At dawn he manages to leave and recover. The scene is an act of calculated humiliation rather than a casual change of mind. Elena has made his longing and trust into instruments against him, and the physical suffering is part of what she intended.

Time passes. Elena's lover abandons her, and in her distress she turns to the scholar whom she mocked. He has learned enough of her situation to see an opportunity for revenge. Rinieri claims knowledge of an elaborate ritual that can restore the man's affection. The ceremony, he says, must be performed in secret at an isolated tower. Elena submits to his instructions because her desire makes the promises plausible, much as his own desire earlier made him accept her invitation.

The scholar orders her to go onto the tower exposed to the summer heat, to remain unclothed and to endure a long wait. When she realizes that she has been deceived, he does not relent. He reproaches her for the night in the snow, leaves her to the sun and insects, and prolongs the punishment. Elena is nearly destroyed by the ordeal. She survives through suffering and assistance, but the recovery is slow. The second humiliation is not merely an equivalent exchange of tricks. Rinieri has converted an injury he received into a system of retaliation that endangers another person's life.

Pampinea introduces her story as a warning against humiliating someone who may retaliate. That may describe a prudential lesson, but it is not a sufficient account of responsibility. Elena's cruelty is real. Rinieri's answer is also deliberate cruelty. The scholar's supposed learning gives him no special moral clarity. He knows how to make an explanation convincing, and he uses that knowledge to extract punishment rather than reconciliation. His intellectual advantages matter in the plot precisely because they do not restrain him.

The story is long for a day of pranks, and its length serves the escalation. The first half makes us watch a man trapped by an invitation he wants to believe; the second makes us watch a woman trapped by an invented cure she wants to believe. Winter and summer are not decorative opposites. Their forces pass through the body, measuring the disproportion between a joke told by its perpetrator and suffering experienced by its victim. Boccaccio's narrator may call this revenge just. The events themselves allow us to ask whether returning injury with a still greater injury makes anyone wiser.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 8: Zeppa and Spinelloccio

Two men in Siena, Zeppa and Spinelloccio, are close companions who spend much time in one another's houses. Their wives, too, know one another. The familiarity seems to remove the need for vigilance. Spinelloccio takes advantage of that trust to begin an affair with Zeppa's wife. At length Zeppa discovers what is happening. He does not immediately announce the betrayal. He decides that the most satisfying revenge will be one that Spinelloccio is forced to witness but cannot prevent.

Zeppa confronts his wife and requires her cooperation. When Spinelloccio comes again for a secret meeting, she will receive him as usual, then help imprison him in a chest. The plan goes forward. Spinelloccio is shut inside, able to hear the household but unable to act. Zeppa then visits Spinelloccio's wife, explains the affair, and proposes his own counter-encounter. He presents himself as a wronged husband and invites her to participate in retaliation against the man who has deceived them both.

The arrangement culminates in an encounter above the chest in which Spinelloccio is trapped. He hears what is happening and knows that the scene is being arranged for his humiliation. Zeppa has converted the secrecy of the first affair into an enforced public knowledge within a tiny room. When Spinelloccio is finally released, the men negotiate a new understanding. The narrator presents the conclusion as a friendly agreement in which the couples accept a continuing sexual arrangement among themselves.

Fiammetta tells the story as a comedy of retaliation and settlement. It has the mechanical symmetry of a wager: one man betrays another through sexual access, and the injured man answers by achieving the same sort of access in return. But the fiction's supposed balance is incomplete. Zeppa's wife has little opportunity to determine the terms once her husband begins threatening exposure. Spinelloccio's wife is approached under the emotional pressure of a newly disclosed betrayal. A pact between the men does not automatically describe what each woman freely desires.

The chest matters because it transforms an absent witness into a captive audience. Throughout the Decameron a cupboard, a bed, a barrel or a locked door can separate what one character knows from what another performs. Here the concealment is punitive. It is designed to make someone hear his own humiliation, not simply to hide a lover. The revenge succeeds partly because Zeppa controls when Spinelloccio can speak.

The closing reconciliation may delight the storyteller, but it does not make the earlier injuries unreal. Friendship, marriage and retaliation have been treated as negotiable transactions, and the narrator treats a new agreement as though it could settle all accounts. We can understand how the plot reaches its comic settlement without assuming that symmetrical deception always produces justice. The story's real subject is what happens when intimacy is taken as property that one injured person may exchange for another.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 9: Master Simone and the Secret Society

Master Simone has returned from Bologna with the costume and title of a learned physician. His dress makes a claim about his competence, but he is more eager for admiration than capable of careful judgment. In Florence he makes the acquaintance of Bruno and Buffalmacco, the two painters who have already deceived Calandrino. They quickly recognize a different weakness. Simone wants not an invisible stone or a test for thieves, but admission to a world in which important men enjoy secret feasts and erotic pleasures beyond the reach of ordinary neighbors.

Bruno and Buffalmacco speak of an exclusive company that gathers at night for fantastical adventures. They give hints about magnificent tables, beautiful women, rapid journeys and special privileges. Simone supplies the credulity needed to complete the performance. The painters do not need to prove that the group exists. Each new detail increases the doctor's desire to be invited, and his response becomes their evidence that they can demand still more from him.

Simone begins giving presents and entertaining his supposed sponsors. He hopes that gifts will buy intimacy with a select brotherhood. The painters accept what he offers and prolong the initiation. Their invention grows more extravagant, decorated with language and ceremonious conditions. Finally they tell the doctor that he may attend a meeting and instruct him how to behave when he reaches the appointed place. He must be obedient and must not question the strange transport by which he is supposed to join the company.

At night, in a place chosen by the tricksters, the supposed adventure ends in a filthy ditch or cesspit. Simone is left covered in refuse rather than introduced to any secret society. The men arrange their exit and later provide an account that allows him to preserve some dignity without discovering how thoroughly he has been fooled. He can explain away the failure as the hazards of an occult initiation rather than admit that his acquaintances tricked him into their practical joke.

Lauretta's tale is more expansive and verbal than the Calandrino episodes. Bruno and Buffalmacco create an imagined institution with ranks, privileges and hidden customs; it is not enough merely to tell Simone one lie. They build a vocabulary in which every ordinary doubt appears to be proof that he has not yet earned the secrets. The physician's professional title makes the joke pointed. A man presumed to know the body and its ailments cannot diagnose the motives of two people he has allowed to manage his expectations.

We should resist turning the joke into a historical verdict on all medieval medicine or on a documented physician. This is a fictional Simone, exaggerated for comic effect, just as the painters are fictional characters with names associated with Florentine artists. What the tale observes is a recurrent human weakness: people who crave membership in a privileged circle may surrender the ordinary skepticism they apply elsewhere. The stranger and more exclusive the promise, the more flattering it can seem to believe it. The foul ditch is the unromantic fact at the end of a carefully narrated fantasy.

## The Decameron — Day Eight, Tale 10: Salabaetto and the Sicilian Woman

A young Florentine merchant, Salabaetto, travels to Palermo to sell woolen goods. His business requires him to turn stock into money and send the proceeds home. In the city a woman known as Jancofiore, or Biancafiore in some translations, notices him and devises a more profitable affair than a simple courtship. She surrounds him with signs of wealth, attention and refinement. Salabaetto takes the performance for proof of sincere affection and gradually allows the commercial judgment that brought him to Sicily to be governed by the desire she has encouraged.

After their meetings have made him confident of her love, Jancofiore invents an urgent need. She must have five hundred florins, she says, and only a man devoted to her can help. The sum is effectively the money Salabaetto has received for the merchandise entrusted to him. He lends it. She delays repayment, then becomes difficult to find. He discovers that the expensive-looking household and its apparent emotion were arranged to convince him to part with his funds. Losing the money threatens both his pride and his obligations as a merchant.

He leaves Palermo for Naples, where an older acquaintance, Pietro Canigiano, helps him think through the fraud. Pietro's counsel is not to appeal to the woman's pity or to try to recover the money through public shame. Salabaetto must create a better prospect of profit than the one she has already taken. He returns to Palermo and lets it be known that he has access to a far larger shipment, worth thousands of florins. The woman hears of the opportunity and renews her friendship. To gain his trust, she returns the original five hundred.

Salabaetto now presents a fresh difficulty. He needs another thousand florins to release his goods from a supposed emergency. As security he offers access to a warehouse believed to contain the valuable shipment. Jancofiore, judging the apparent merchandise more valuable than the loan, advances the money. But the warehouse is filled with goods of little value, arranged to look like the expected cargo. When she discovers the truth, Salabaetto has escaped with the cash and recovered more than she once took from him.

Dioneo's long concluding story is a fitting answer to the brief financial trick that opened the day. Gulfardo made a payment count as the repayment of a debt. Salabaetto, instructed by Pietro, makes worthless stock count as security for a loan. In each case the decisive object is not merely money but an interpretation of money: what a transfer proves, what inventory represents, what a promise can make another party imagine it owns.

The story also gives a different form to the anxieties that run beneath the country-house comedy. Merchants depend on information, credit, strangers and distant transactions. A handsome face and a staged household can become part of a counterfeit balance sheet. Salabaetto must first learn how his judgment was captured before he can exploit Jancofiore's desire for a larger gain. The revenge is clever; it also teaches the same deception rather than establishing an open standard of fair dealing. Two frauds end with one fraudster triumphant.

After the story, Lauretta hands the crown to Emilia. Emilia will allow a new change of pace: the ninth day's company may tell stories without a prescribed common subject. The movement from Day Eight to Day Nine is therefore also a release from the strict rhythm of moral themes. With eighty tales told, the company has practiced almost every manner of disguise and reversal. Yet every teller still depends upon the same basic tool: a listener willing to inhabit a world that words have made.
