# The Decameron — Tedaldo Returns as a Pilgrim

Tedaldo degli Elisei is a young Florentine whose relationship with a married woman, Ermellina, has come to an abrupt end. She has withdrawn her favor and refuses even to hear him. A friar's counsel has helped her resolve to abandon the affair. Tedaldo cannot persuade her otherwise. He leaves Florence and spends years away, carrying his disappointment from place to place. When he finally comes home, he does not arrive as the familiar man his friends remember. He wears the clothing of a pilgrim and moves through the city without announcing his name.

He discovers a bewildering piece of news. The people of Florence believe that Tedaldo has been murdered. A body resembling his has been found, and because the dead man's identity was misunderstood, Ermellina's husband Aldobrandino has been accused of the killing. The man's own brothers have helped bring the charge. Aldobrandino faces death for a crime he did not commit, while the supposed victim is walking the streets alive.

Tedaldo might simply reveal his identity and put an end to the mistake. But the story gives him a more elaborate task. His return creates an opportunity to repair the relationships he left behind, win Ermellina's affection again, and halt the prosecution before it becomes irreversible. Retaining his pilgrim's disguise, he gains access to Ermellina. He speaks as though he were a religious guide with knowledge of her suffering and makes her recount how she once dismissed Tedaldo.

The conversation becomes a long reproach. He attacks the friar whose advice separated the lovers and argues that her rejection was unjust. The account is remarkable because the man who claims spiritual authority is in fact the man asking for renewed intimacy. His words are not neutral counsel. Tedaldo presents his interpretation of the past as a judgment she ought to accept. Ermellina, grieving and afraid for her husband, promises that if Tedaldo were alive she would welcome him again. Only then does the pilgrim show her a ring she once gave him and remove his disguise.

Their reunion cannot be allowed to distract from the prison. Tedaldo goes to Aldobrandino, reassures him, and asks that he forgive the dead man's brothers for pursuing a charge they sincerely believed to be true. He then discovers the real circumstances of the killing and brings forward the evidence needed to save the innocent husband. Florence's mistaken story is corrected. A man is spared an execution, the brothers cease their hostility, and the household can come together without an unresolved murder accusation dividing it.

Emilia's novella is one of the day's longest and most intricate. It moves through exile, disguise, erroneous identification, judicial danger, recognition, and reconciliation. The first question is a classic romance question: how will a rejected lover regain access to someone he desires? But the story enlarges that question until it concerns the lives of other people. Tedaldo's presence is necessary not only to restore a romance but to prevent the law from punishing an innocent man for the wrong man's death.

Notice how much weight the tale places on testimony. The same city that can mistake a dead stranger for Tedaldo is prepared to convert its mistake into a criminal conviction. Sight, hearsay, old quarrels, and the apparent plausibility of an accusation are not sufficient. Tedaldo's return forces the evidence to be revisited. Yet his own method of recovering Ermellina's favor involves another claim to authority: the pilgrim's disguise allows him to speak with a borrowed moral status. He is right about the identity of the dead, but that does not make every reproach he directs at Ermellina an impartial moral truth.

The tale ends with social peace and a discreet continuation of the affair. Boccaccio has composed a success story in which a lover can solve a judicial crisis and emerge better placed than when he departed. The complexity of the plot keeps us from treating desire alone as a sufficient explanation. Tedaldo's wish starts the story, but the life of Aldobrandino gives it urgency beyond his wish. The success worth recognizing is not simply that he gets the woman back; it is that a false story, once accepted by a community, is made answerable to what actually happened.
