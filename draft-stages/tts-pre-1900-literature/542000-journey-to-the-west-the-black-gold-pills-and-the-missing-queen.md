# Journey to the West: The black-gold pills and the missing queen

## A pulse taken through silk

Wukong went into the palace holding three threads made from his own tail hairs. The king of Zhuzi did not have to look directly at the monkey. Attendants placed the threads according to Wukong's instructions, running from the patient's wrists to the physician waiting beyond his bed. The king had been unable to tolerate unfamiliar faces since his illness began; now his strange visitor could attend him without standing over him.

For a while the court was quiet. Wukong handled the threads and made a show of examining the pulse, then announced that the case was not simply one of heat or cold, fullness or weakness. It was a disease of grief and fear, the sickness of a pair of birds separated by a storm. The physicians looked puzzled. Wukong explained that when a male and female bird had always flown together, and a violent wind had driven them apart, each might pine for the other. Such separation could produce a deep illness that ordinary prescriptions had failed to reach.

The court admired the poetic diagnosis even before it understood the facts behind it. Wukong did not yet ask which person the king missed. He merely said that he could prepare a remedy. The medical officers wished to know the prescription. He answered that a skilled healer did not always begin by fixing a formula and forcing a patient to fit it. Bring the medicines that were available, he said, and he would choose.

Zhuzi's doctors gathered every substance they could find. They arranged for eight hundred and eight medicinal ingredients, three pounds of each, to be carried to the envoys' guesthouse with mortars, pestles, sieves, and vessels. The caravan was so large that the officials could hardly believe one patient would require such a quantity. Wukong had deliberately asked for everything to prevent the physicians from understanding what he actually intended to use.

Xuanzang asked to return to the guesthouse and oversee his disciple. The king kept him in the palace instead, with a promise that he would be rewarded and his travel document stamped when the cure succeeded. Xuanzang understood the more dangerous meaning of that arrangement. If Wukong's treatment failed, the court could hold the master in place as security for his disciple's promise. He begged the monkey to stop treating the affair as a joke.

Wukong promised that the king would recover. The master had heard such promises before, sometimes followed by a surprising victory and sometimes by another disaster. He remained at court, sleeping under the eyes of officials while the night preparations began across the city.

## Eight hundred medicines, three pills

At the guesthouse Bajie examined the deliveries with amazement. So many boxes and sacks! He told Wukong that the master was evidently giving up the pilgrimage and establishing a pharmacy. A person might sell medicines for years and never exhaust such a store. Why else would the monkey collect such a quantity for a single king?

Wukong said that he intended to use only a few ingredients. He had ordered the rest to confuse the court's physicians, who would otherwise insist upon discussing his technique. Bajie did not find this an encouraging admission. But the three disciples were already committed, and the officials were waiting for a miracle.

At midnight Wukong began. He ordered Sha to grind a little rhubarb and told Bajie to prepare a little croton seed. Sha recognized the traditional properties attributed to the rhubarb and warned that a weakened patient might not tolerate such a strong purgative. Bajie spoke just as seriously about croton, which was notoriously harsh. They were not merely cooks fooling with strange powders; they knew enough to object when the ingredients appeared dangerous. Wukong brushed aside their concern, promising that the medicine would move what had long been stopped inside the king.

Then he asked for soot scraped from the underside of the cooking pot. Bajie fetched it. The monkey called it by its medicinal name, Hundred-Grass Frost, and told the brothers to grind it fine. Bajie surveyed the piles of expensive ingredients and looked at the cheap dark soot. A kingdom had spent a fortune to watch the physician scrape a stove.

The fourth substance was stranger. Wukong demanded urine from the white horse. Sha protested that such a thing could not possibly make a respectable pill. The monkey explained that this was no ordinary horse. It was the dragon prince of the Western Sea, transformed and assigned to carry Xuanzang. A drop from such a being might have powers inaccessible to common medicine.

Bajie went into the stable, pushed a bowl under the horse, and waited. Nothing happened. He returned complaining that the animal was so dried up it needed treatment of its own. Wukong brought him back. The horse raised its head and spoke, as it rarely did. Did his elder brother not understand? His body was a dragon's, and its fluids were powerful. If he relieved himself in a stream, fish might become dragons; on land, plants might take on the properties of wondrous herbs. He was reluctant to waste such a thing in an ordinary stable.

Wukong reminded the dragon horse that the king was suffering and that the whole pilgrimage might be delayed if the party could not cure him. The horse finally consented, producing only a small amount. Bajie complained that he could surely manage more. Wukong said what they had was enough.

The powders, soot, and liquid were mixed and rolled into three round pills. They were called Black-Gold Pills, a grand name that delighted Bajie because the color came largely from pot soot. The brothers went to sleep. Xuanzang spent the night in a royal residence, afraid that the next day would determine whether he traveled west or remained a hostage in Zhuzi.

## Rain from a dragon's mouth

In the morning officials arrived to collect the medicine. They asked its name and how it should be taken. Wukong presented the three pills and first offered an impossible list of ingredients for the liquid that should accompany them: things no ordinary person could procure from birds, dragons, royal garments, and divine palaces. The bewildered officials begged for another method. The monkey answered that rainwater not yet fallen on the ground would do.

The courtiers thought this easy. They could take water from a well or river into a clean bowl, they said, and bring it without setting it down. Wukong rejected the suggestion. Such water had a source. He wanted water descended directly from Heaven. The court sent messengers to find someone who could call down rain.

Meanwhile Wukong summoned the Dragon King of the Eastern Sea. Ao Guang appeared over the palace, asking what task was required. When he heard that a sick ruler needed only a little rain to swallow medicine, he protested that he had come without the clouds, thunder, wind, and equipment for a proper storm. Wukong told him that none of those instruments mattered. A few drops would be enough.

Ao Guang offered a practical solution in the manner of a dragon: he would spit. He lowered his cloud, concealed himself over the palace, and sent a fine spray down into the waiting bowls. The court saw droplets falling from the sky and praised Heaven for answering its prayers. Officials, concubines, and servants raised cups and saucers everywhere. Many caught nothing; others captured a few drops. Collected together, the water filled three drinking cups.

The king took one Black-Gold Pill with each cup. Soon his belly began to churn. Attendants hurried for vessels as the medicine drove a violent purging through him. The court observed that a heavy lump of partly digested rice had passed out with the discharge. The king had eaten ceremonial sticky rice on the day his troubles began, and in the story it had remained within him through the long illness. He drank some rice liquid, rested, and found his chest easier.

Before long he could sit up without pain, stand without attendants, and walk across his chamber. He dressed for court and summoned Xuanzang. The master had spent the night imagining punishment; now the king bowed to him in gratitude. Royal messengers went to fetch Wukong, Bajie, and Sha for a feast. The king's court had received a cure, even though the ingredients and the reasoning behind it were hardly what the physicians expected.

## A secret disclosed over the banquet

At the royal banquet Xuanzang refused wine in keeping with his monastic discipline. The king turned to Wukong. The monkey accepted cup after cup while Bajie watched with growing indignation. He had helped make the medicine, yet the best wine was passing him by. At last he began to say something about the horse. Wukong pushed a cup into his hand before he could reveal what had gone into the pills. When the king asked what Bajie meant, Wukong said that his brother had been referring to a herb whose name began with the word *horse*. The court doctors accepted the explanation.

The king was now strong enough to laugh. Yet as the feast went on, his smile faltered. Wukong asked about the sadness he had detected in the palace. The king admitted that the diagnosis of the separated birds had struck the truth. For three years he had lived without his principal queen, known as the Golden Sage. The two other queens remained with him, but he had lost the woman whose company mattered most.

It had happened during the festival of the fifth day of the fifth month. The palace gardens were full of food, wine, fragrant plants, and the pleasures of the season. The king and his wives were watching celebrations when a violent wind swept across the garden. A demon appeared above them, claiming to be the creature called Sai Taisui, the Rival of Grand Age. His dwelling, he said, lay in Xiezhi Cave on Qilin Mountain. He had heard of the Golden Sage's beauty and wanted her as his wife. If the king would not yield her at once, he would devour ruler, officials, and people.

The king had faced a choice between protecting one person and exposing an entire city to slaughter. He had given the queen to the demon. The creature carried her away through the wind. Fear, shame, and endless mourning had followed. Food stopped passing normally through the king's body; he dwelt upon the loss through day and night. A few months later the demon returned demanding palace women to attend the captive queen. Again and again he sent for more attendants, and the king sent them because he feared for the palace and the city.

Wukong listened without mocking the grief. The king had been unable to rescue his wife, and his terror had cost other women their freedom as well. The monkey asked whether the king wished the Golden Sage restored. The king was astonished. Was there any doubt? He would surrender his kingdom, he said, if only Wukong would bring her home. Bajie laughed at a ruler prepared to abandon his throne for love. Wukong told the king he wanted no kingdom. Let him see the creature and learn what power he possessed.

## The refuge below the stones

The king led the guests to the private grounds behind the palace. He spoke of a tower built to shelter the royal household whenever Sai Taisui returned. Wukong expected a tall structure. Instead two eunuchs lifted a stone slab from the ground. Underneath lay chambers dug deep below the garden, supplied with lamps and oil. Whenever a storm wind announced the monster, the court descended there and pulled the heavy slab into place.

Wukong laughed at the defense. A demon who wished to destroy the palace would not be stopped by a few feet of earth. The king answered that the refuge was all he had been able to build. As they spoke, a fierce wind rose. Dust surged across the gardens. The king and the officials fled down the opening. Xuanzang hurried after them.

Bajie and Sha wanted to do the same. Wukong caught them before they could enter. They had just seen the suffering the demon caused. Now was the time to look at him, not to hide underground with everyone else. The pig protested that he had never promised to fight a creature on account of an injured king's wife. Wukong held his arm and made him wait.

Out of the blowing dust appeared a huge, hard-featured figure with staring eyes, protruding teeth, red hair and blue-dark limbs, carrying a spear. Wukong looked up at him and asked his brothers whether they recognized the visitor. Sha had never met him. Bajie, despite knowing nothing, made an argument that the creature was not an ordinary ghost, because ghosts did not usually ride the wind in bright daylight. Perhaps this was Sai Taisui himself.

Wukong considered the shape. The monster had not yet attacked. The king and Xuanzang were below the stones; the palace stood almost empty; the captive queen remained somewhere beyond Qilin Mountain. Wukong told Bajie and Sha to guard the entrance, took his staff, and flew upward to demand the intruder's name.

The king had risen from his sickbed, but he had not yet recovered the Golden Sage. Wukong steadied his staff, looking at the dark figure in the air ahead. He did not yet know who the visitor was or what message it carried. Beneath him the palace waited. At last the trouble that had haunted Zhuzi for three years was coming to meet him.
