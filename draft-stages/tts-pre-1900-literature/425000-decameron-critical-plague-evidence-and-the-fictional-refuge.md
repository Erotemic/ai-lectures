# Reading the Plague at the Beginning of a Book of Stories

The *Decameron* does not begin with a joke. It begins with burial, contagion, fear, and an account of ordinary human bonds coming apart. The date is 1348. A deadly plague has reached Florence. Remedies and public efforts fail to bring the catastrophe under control. Familiar forms of care become uncertain. People alter their habits not because some new theory of morality has persuaded them but because they are afraid to die.

Boccaccio gives more than a list of symptoms. He describes swollen glands and dark marks on the body, but also the conduct of relatives, friends, and attendants. Some people shut themselves away and regulate their diet. Others behave as though drinking and pleasure might make tomorrow irrelevant. Others wander with flowers or fragrant things, hoping to make the corrupted air less dangerous. The contrast is part of the scene's intellectual force. Many people are confronting the same event, yet they have radically different accounts of how to respond to it.

The causes of the plague were not understood as they are by modern epidemiology. Boccaccio mentions possible celestial influence and divine judgment without demonstrating either. Today the Black Death is studied in connection with plague caused by *Yersinia pestis*, but the historical description of epidemic experience cannot be translated directly into a laboratory diagnosis for every person it mentions. Historians combine the literary testimony with archaeological, documentary, and scientific evidence. Boccaccio's words tell us particularly well what catastrophe felt like to a writer considering the failure of social expectation.

Notice which details he selects. The loss of customary funeral processions matters because funerals are a form of public recognition. Families ordinarily help make a death socially intelligible. Under extreme mortality, bodies may be hurried to burial or placed in communal graves. A dying person can be abandoned by those who would once have promised care. In these passages, what collapses is not just health but the ability to count on another person's participation in the practices that give life shape.

From that scene the ten young people step forward. Seven women meet in the church of Santa Maria Novella, and three men join them. They leave for the country. They do not flee to an untouched part of Europe, and the story never proves that their estates are medically safe. Their advantage is wealth, mobility, food, accommodation, and attendants who continue working. They are able to make a small protected world because other people preserve the means of its existence. Their retreat is an imaginative response to fear, not a public-health plan applicable to everyone in Florence.

Within the retreat the company creates forms of time that the plague has disrupted outside it. Meals arrive; flowers and songs mark the hours; one member presides; everyone receives a turn to speak. A declared theme gives the day shape. A story ends, another begins, and tomorrow can be anticipated without certainty that tomorrow will be safe. This regularity can be understood as an answer to the city's formless loss. Yet the narrators do not abolish mortality by refusing to discuss it. Day Four's unhappy loves, the beheadings, poisonings, and deaths inside other tales allow suffering to return under the conditions of art.

The book offers no tidy proof that storytelling cures epidemic fear. The characters enjoy leisure, and at the end they choose to go back to Florence while the wider crisis has not been retrospectively solved by the narration. Nor is the opening a statistically complete history of Florence. Its figures for mortality and its dramatic examples serve a literary argument, and any claim about the number who died must be checked against independent historical work. A historian may find evidence for an enormous demographic catastrophe without taking every sentence as a literal survey.

There is a productive tension between two forms of testimony. Boccaccio describes what people did when obligations became difficult, and he invents a company who undertake obligations voluntarily: to take turns, to respect an agreed program, to hear voices unlike their own. The first scene presents social relations under maximum stress. The second asks whether a small deliberate practice can help people remain human under that stress.

A listener can hear the book's hundred tales as entertainment and still remember why they are being told. They are not told in a world after the plague. They are told in a world where the plague has made continuing ordinary life hard to imagine. The achievement is therefore limited but important: a practice of attention is kept alive, and the company eventually goes home. They have not defeated death. They have remembered forms of living while death is close.
