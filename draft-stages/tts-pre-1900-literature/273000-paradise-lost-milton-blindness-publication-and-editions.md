# Paradise Lost — Milton's Life, Dictation, and the 1667 and 1674 Editions

Paradise Lost emerged from a life that had included several careers, political upheaval, and the loss of sight. John Milton was born in 1608 and educated at Christ's College, Cambridge. He studied languages, classical literature, biblical writing, and theological controversy. Before his great epic, he wrote shorter poetry and some of the most consequential political prose of the seventeenth century. It is difficult to understand the ambition of Paradise Lost without recognizing that its author had already devoted years to arguments about how human beings should govern and worship.

Milton supported the republican regime established after Charles the First was executed in 1649. He served as a Latin secretary and wrote in defense of the government and of his own controversial positions. His work involved the intellectual and political disputes of Europe, not a sheltered lifetime of solitary verse. After the monarchy was restored in 1660, he faced personal and legal danger. His political project had failed, and he was no longer a state official. The epic was composed and published in this changed world.

Milton had become completely blind before Paradise Lost appeared. Scholars distinguish the evidence of his impaired and lost sight from confident guesses about the precise medical cause. He composed through memory, speech, and the work of people who wrote down what he dictated. Dictation did not make the resulting poetry less crafted. It required a different set of habits for holding verse in mind and revising it. A surviving manuscript fragment in a helper's hand provides material evidence for this manner of production; it is not a full surviving draft of all twelve books.

The first edition appeared in 1667 with the work divided into ten books. That statement is easy to repeat but worth understanding bibliographically. Copies from the initial printing circulated with different dated title pages and with changes in their preliminary materials. The explanatory prose arguments and Milton's defense of unrhymed verse became part of the early publication history. A title page dated 1668 is not automatically evidence of a new edition; some issues reuse sheets from the first printing.

In 1674 a second edition reorganized the poem into twelve books, the arrangement followed by most later readers and the one used in this lecture sequence. The revision mainly divided two large books from the ten-book arrangement, with relatively modest connecting changes, rather than composing two wholly new books. The new number strengthened the poem's visible relation to classical twelve-book epic, especially Virgil's Aeneid, although scholars need not assume that imitation of Virgil was Milton's only reason.

The first and second editions should not be described as interchangeable in every detail. Editorial spelling, punctuation, capitalization, numbering, and lineation may also differ among modern reading texts. A reader who cites Book Eight in a twelve-book edition may be pointing to material that belonged to a differently numbered book in 1667. When precision matters, name the edition, book, and lines rather than treating an unqualified line number as sufficient.

The economics of publication also belonged to ordinary life. Cambridge University Library preserves evidence of Milton's arrangement with Samuel Simmons, including the initial payment and further payment tied to sales. This was an authored work brought into print through a commercial relationship, not a poem descending without mediators into the public world. The fact that Milton was blind also complicates our view of the physical text, because the surviving printed forms were made through assistants, printers, and an author with exacting poetic purposes.

An accurate historical account should not romanticize blindness as a source of supernatural vision, or deduce the author's private thoughts from the immense voice of the Muse. Milton's invocation of inward light draws power from his actual condition, but it remains a poetic act. Likewise, the poem's portrayal of defeated rebellion has obvious resonance with his politics without being a direct coded memoir of 1660.

The study of editions gives a practical reward: the poem becomes an event in history rather than an untouchable monument. It was spoken, written down, printed, amended, reissued, and argued over. Readers have inherited not only Milton's story of Paradise but also a long sequence of decisions about what text should be placed before them. A responsible close reading begins by knowing which form of that text is on the page.
