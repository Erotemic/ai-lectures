# War and Peace — Serfs, Soldiers, and the Social Order Beneath the Families

The principal families in War and Peace are landowning nobles. Their salons, music, estates, military commissions, and marriage prospects depend upon a social order in which many other people labor without comparable freedom. Russia's serfdom had not yet been abolished during the years the novel depicts. A reading focused only on the moral education of Pierre or the romance of Natasha can forget the people whose work gives those central characters their rooms, horses, meals, and leisure.

Pierre's attempt to improve his estates makes the system visible precisely because he wants to do good. He expects his orders to mean that the peasants' lives will improve. Estate managers can comply in appearance while protecting revenue or privilege. Grateful peasants can perform what the visiting owner expects, whether through politeness, fear, strategic calculation, or genuine benefit. The scene is not proof that all reform from above is worthless. It shows that a reformer's power to issue an order is not the same as knowledge of its consequences, and that people subject to the order may have little freedom to correct him.

At Bald Hills, the old Prince Bolkonsky exercises control over both his daughter and the peasants. During the invasion, the peasants hesitate to permit Marya's departure. Their actions arise from fear and uncertainty as much as from a political program. Nikolai Rostov intervenes as a noble officer and suppresses resistance. In the conventional heroic rhythm, a distressed woman is rescued. In the social rhythm, aristocratic command is restored. Tolstoy lets us observe both. We should not pretend that the second disappears because Marya and Nikolai will become sympathetic characters.

Count Rostov's endless hospitality is another form of inequality. His openness is loving; his accounts are disastrous. When debts accumulate, the costs do not fall only on the man who invited too many guests. They pressure his children into advantageous marriage and endanger the lives of dependents. Nikolai's later labor as estate manager can look like earned maturity, but the objects of management are not simply fields and animals. They include households whose members cannot negotiate with him as equals.

The novel's soldiers complicate social hierarchy in a different way. Men of different rank march, eat, and die within one army while remaining subject to distinct obligations and risks. Tolstoy repeatedly moves attention toward the labor of ordinary soldiers: carrying supplies, holding positions, mending equipment, enduring cold. This attention challenges histories that make campaigns entirely about generals. Yet visibility in a literary scene is not identical to political equality. Being admired for endurance is not the same as being able to refuse service or control resources.

Platon Karataev becomes central to Pierre's reorientation because he offers a form of equanimity Pierre could not purchase through wealth. But Karataev is not a simple mouthpiece for an entire peasant class. His sayings and habits arrive through Pierre's desperate gratitude. He dies in forced retreat, another reminder that a person's moral influence need not be rewarded by survival. We can learn from what Pierre learns while refusing to make a sentimental portrait stand in for the history of serfdom, conscription, and rural poverty.

The French-speaking nobility also inhabits a multilingual empire. Language can signal education, fashion, class, and distance from other Russians. The invasion heightens political suspicion of French conversation, but it does not make the aristocracy suddenly separate from its own habits. Tolstoy's movement between French and Russian invites the reader to hear how identities are performed. An English translation that leaves some French passages visible will create a different experience from one that translates everything into a single language.

A productive question is not simply whether Tolstoy approves of aristocracy. He depicts generous nobles, cruel nobles, wasteful nobles, and nobles capable of learning. The sharper question is whether the moral vocabulary of the novel reaches all who sustain the households. Watch for moments when the narration allows a servant or soldier a vivid existence, and moments when such a person is absorbed into a noble character's development. The tension between sympathetic observation and unequal narrative attention is part of what a modern reader must confront.
