# Journey to the West: The kingdom that vowed to kill monks

## A warning under the willow trees

After escaping Bottomless Cave, the pilgrims reached a country in the warmth of early summer. Green shade lay along the roads; birds called from the branches; water ran in little channels beside the fields. It was a relief to ride through a landscape in which the trees were simply trees and the women carrying water did not belong to a demon's household. Xuanzang's spirits rose. He watched the road turn west through a stand of tall willows and hoped that the worst of this stretch lay behind them.

From the shade an elderly woman stepped forward holding a child by the hand. She called for the travelers to stop. If they followed the road into the next kingdom, she said, they would die. Xuanzang dismounted and asked why. The woman pointed to a city not far ahead. Its ruler had sworn to kill ten thousand monks. He had already had nine thousand nine hundred ninety-six put to death. Four more would complete his vow. Four monks had just arrived from the east.

Bajie looked from the woman to his companions and counted again, in case the number might somehow be improved. Wukong asked if there was another road. The country could not be crossed without passing the city, she replied, unless one could fly. Bajie announced at once that flying presented no problem; all three disciples had means of traveling through the air. Xuanzang, who could not simply fly across a kingdom with horse, baggage, and travel credentials, remained frightened.

Wukong had been watching the woman and her child rather than the road. His eyes showed him what Xuanzang could not see: the old woman was Guanyin, and the child was her attendant Shancai, once the fearsome Red Boy. He knelt. The others followed his example as the disguises fell away and the holy pair rose in a cloud. Guanyin had not appeared to solve the danger for them. She had come to make sure they knew it existed. The rest was left to the pilgrims.

"We cannot ask the people we pass to make themselves a new king," Xuanzang said when they were alone. "Nor can we turn back to Chang'an. How are we to enter a city where our robes are a sentence of death?" Wukong replied that a ruler who slaughtered men for the shape of their heads was dangerous indeed, but he was still a man, not a demon with a bottomless kingdom beneath the earth. A human court could be tricked, challenged, or persuaded. First they had to know its gates and customs.

He left the party in a hollow by the roadside and went alone toward the city as the lamps were being lit.

## The moth at Wang Xiaoer's inn

From a cloud above the roofs Wukong saw an orderly town. Markets were closing, guards were patrolling, and comfortable households were preparing for sleep. Nothing in the lit streets announced the terrible count of executed monks. That made the danger more disturbing. People could tend shops, make supper, and go quietly home while their ruler continued a vow that had already taken thousands of lives.

Wukong changed into a moth and flew through the streets, reading signs and listening to the talk of travelers. At one inn, kept by a man named Wang Xiaoer, merchants were laying aside their clothes and head coverings. If four travelers entered dressed like these men, they might look less like monks. Wukong waited for an opportunity to borrow clothing without being seen. But the innkeeper, mindful of thieves, advised the merchants to place their bundles in his keeping. The garments disappeared into his private room.

The household would not sleep. Children cried, a woman mended an old garment, and a lamp burned on. Wukong flew into the flame and extinguished it. Then he changed into a mouse and made a commotion among the stored clothing. The innkeeper's wife cried that a supernatural rat was stealing the guests' clothes. Wukong, annoyed at being mistaken for a common thief, spoke out of the darkness. He was the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, he declared. He meant to borrow the garments to carry the Tang monk safely through a wicked kingdom. When the travelers had passed, the clothing would be returned.

Wang Xiaoer was too frightened to inspect the speaker carefully. He groped for his trousers, found a shirt instead, and struggled to dress while the voice moved about the room. By the time he could hold a light steady the mysterious borrower was gone. Wukong returned to the hollow with coats, head coverings, and enough miscellaneous garments to disguise his companions. Bajie stared at the narrow caps and shook his large head. He was not made for ordinary men's hats. Wukong cut and sewed two together and forced the result onto his brother's ears.

Xuanzang removed his monastic robe and replaced it with a merchant's clothing. Sha dressed in the same fashion. Wukong took a set for himself. For the time being, he said, they must stop calling one another master and disciple. Xuanzang would be the elder merchant Tang; Wukong would be the second brother Sun; Bajie would be the third, Zhu; Sha would be the fourth, Sha. They were horse traders from the north. Six additional brothers were following with a larger drove. The white horse would be their sample. Wukong would do the talking.

Bajie admired this new family arrangement but wondered which brother would be allowed to eat first. Wukong said he could eat after they got inside. They entered the city at evening, passed Wang Xiaoer's inn while its guests were still complaining about missing hats, and chose a lodging house across the street.

## Four horse merchants and a widow

The new inn belonged to a widow named Zhao. She welcomed travelers, but she was also an experienced businesswoman and wanted to know how they meant to pay. There were three levels of hospitality. The finest offered abundant dishes, wine, and singing companions; the middle offered simpler refreshment; the lowest meant a place to sleep and whatever food was already in the kitchen. Wukong announced that merchants of their standing could afford the finest. Bajie immediately urged him to reconsider. A man could be very happy beside a kitchen pot, he said, and the cost was less.

Widow Zhao was delighted by the prospect of generous guests and began ordering poultry and meat prepared. Xuanzang leaned toward Wukong in alarm. They had changed their clothes, not their vows. Their host was about to slaughter animals for a meal they could not eat. Wukong called her back and explained that the party was observing a special fast on this day. They required vegetarian food now; the richer dishes could wait until the six brothers arrived tomorrow. The widow adjusted her orders. Tofu, mushrooms, bamboo shoots, grains, and greens took the place of the meat.

The meal was good, and the disguise appeared to work. Wukong told a cheerful story about the large number of horses they expected to sell. Zhao's household listened carefully. Xuanzang kept his eyes lowered and spoke as little as possible. Sha was no more eager for talk. Bajie had difficulty remembering that he was a merchant brother and not a hungry monk, but the food gave him something to attend to.

When the meal was finished the question of sleeping arose. Their monk's caps could slip; their bare heads might show. An upper room with moonlight and windows would expose them. Wukong asked for a dark place without a draft. Widow Zhao sighed. One brother feared light, another disliked cold air, and another had complaints about his shoulders. She was losing the ordinary profits of a fine lodging while satisfying the needs of a party of troublesome guests.

Her daughter suggested an old storage chest that had belonged to Zhao's husband. It was broad enough for several people to lie in and shut out the light completely. Wukong accepted at once. The chest was carried to the courtyard. He asked that the horse be tied beside it. Then he settled Xuanzang and the brothers inside with the baggage, climbed in after them, and asked the widow to fasten the lid securely. It was a remarkable arrangement: four supposed horse dealers paying for a fine room and choosing to sleep together in a locked box.

Inside the chest, the air grew close. The hats came off, the coats were loosened, and Bajie complained of the heat. Xuanzang tried to rest. Sha shifted a bundle beneath his head. Wukong alone remained wakeful, amused by the absurdity of their hiding place. He had got them past the gates and into the city without a single guard recognizing a monk. The kingdom's killing vow might be defeated by nothing more heroic than an inconvenient night's lodging.

Then he began to speak in his sleep, loudly enough for anyone outside to hear, about large sums of silver from their horse trading. Thousands of taels, he murmured; more profit still to come. Bajie sleepily told him to be quiet. But other listeners had heard.

## The chest that went traveling

Some servants at the inn had dealings with local thieves. They carried Wukong's boasts to a gang, who came after midnight with torches and weapons. Widow Zhao and her daughter shut themselves away in fright. The intruders searched for rich horse merchants, found no one in the upstairs room, and finally noticed the heavy chest in the courtyard. A white horse stood tethered to it. The chest was locked. What merchant would go to such trouble, the thieves reasoned, unless the box contained money?

They lifted the chest onto carrying poles and took the horse along. Inside, Bajie felt himself moving and demanded to know who was shaking his bed. Wukong told him not to make a sound. If the thieves happened to carry them westward, it would save a day's walking. Xuanzang was horrified. They had hidden to avoid the king's officers; now they were being abducted by criminals in the middle of the night. Wukong found this development almost convenient.

Unfortunately the thieves took the road east, not west. They killed a gate guard to get out of the city, and the alarm spread through the streets. Soldiers pursued them with lamps and weapons. The thieves abandoned the chest and horse and escaped into the darkness. The officers recovered what they believed was stolen property. By morning the sealed chest stood under guard in a government compound, with the magnificent white horse beside it. Officials planned to report their capture to the king.

Xuanzang lay inside listening to boots and voices. Now, he said, he was being delivered to the very ruler who had ordered the death of monks. Wukong assured him that the king would not kill them. He had another plan. The master asked what it was, but the monkey would not explain, saying only that they should be patient until the lid was opened in the royal court.

Near the third watch Wukong made his staff into a drill and bored a hole in the base of the chest. He changed into a tiny ant, crept through the opening, and flew to the palace. There he divided his magic into many forms. From one arm he produced little copies of himself; from the other he produced insects that put people to sleep. He sent the insects through the chambers of the king, the queens, the attendants, and the officials. When everyone lay motionless, he transformed his staff into countless razors and placed one in the hands of each little monkey.

The razors went to work. Across the royal palaces and the offices of state, heads were shaved. Wukong did not ask who had joined the persecution, who had only obeyed an order, and who had wished it would stop. His enchantment fell on the whole governing household. He then reclaimed the copies and the razors, crawled back through the hole as an ant, resumed his place inside the chest, and waited for morning.

The sun had not yet risen when servants in the palace began crying out. Women who had gone to sleep with carefully arranged hair woke bald. Attendants, courtiers, and officials were the same. The queens hurried to the king, who reached for his own head and felt bare skin. He looked upon the faces of his household and remembered the monks he had ordered killed. He began to fear that the vow he had treated as righteous had made a monastery of his own palace.

Outside, officers were preparing to present a chest captured from robbers, unaware of the people concealed inside. The king called his court together. The ministers arrived with uncovered heads and prepared written reports about the catastrophe. The monks had not yet been discovered; the chest had not yet been opened. That encounter would take place in the next chapter.
