# Journey to the West: the captive daughter and the unwanted husband of Gao Village

## Journey to the West — The Robe Returned and a Servant Stopped on the Road

The monks of Guanyin Monastery had spent a terrible night among burned beams and fallen tiles. They had survived the fire, but the embroidered robe that had brought the trouble remained missing, and the old abbot who had wanted it was dead. The visitors could not depart. Xuanzang had been entrusted with that robe for the journey to India, and he would not abandon it in the ashes.

Sun Wukong, meanwhile, had just taken leave of Guanyin and the black bear who was now under her discipline. Before going back to his master, Wukong made one further visit to Black Wind Mountain. The little demons had scattered when they saw their lord subdued. The cave was empty, but its many doors still stood. Wukong heaped dry wood against them and set them alight. Black Wind Cave, the story tells us, became Red Wind Cave. It was the kind of finishing touch Wukong considered satisfactory: the stolen robe recovered, the enemy recruited away, and his dwelling reduced to cinders.

Only then did he carry the robe northward to the monastery.

Xuanzang had begun to fear that his disciple might never return. Perhaps Guanyin had refused to help. Perhaps Wukong had grown tired of guarding a frail monk and had used the errand as a chance to escape. The monk was caught between hope and suspicion when colored mist shone in the sky. Wukong dropped before the steps, knelt, and held out the recovered garment.

The relief was immediate. Xuanzang took the robe in both hands. The surviving monks cried out that their lives were safe at last: no thief or accuser could demand from them a treasure they did not possess. Xuanzang wanted the baggage tied up and the white horse saddled without further delay. Wukong looked at the sinking sun and asked what sort of traveling hour this was. He had been away all day, he said, and the road would be waiting in the morning.

The monks agreed fervently. They had made vows during the danger; they wished to offer what food and incense they still possessed in thanksgiving for surviving it. The wealth of their monastery had mostly gone into the flames. What remained could still make a vegetarian supper for guests and neighbors. Fires burned again that evening, but these were small, watched, and used to warm food and light the rites of gratitude.

At daybreak the pilgrims left. The monks escorted them beyond the ruined gates, then turned back to the labor of putting a community together from its remains. Wukong led the white horse by the bridle. Xuanzang rode with the robe safely packed away. Neither spoke as if the detour had been a simple triumph. An entire monastery had been destroyed over a garment whose religious significance had done nothing to make its admirers less acquisitive.

Spring had softened the road. Grasses bent under the horse's hooves; willow branches showed new leaves; flowering trees had grown bright across the hills. After days of burnt timber and a wind-whipped mountain, the green of the countryside almost made traveling feel easy. They walked for five or seven days through lonely stretches, asking for no lodging where none could be found.

One evening Xuanzang saw a settlement ahead. Roofs gathered under the slope of a hill, and a street ran toward a cluster of large courtyards. They could seek shelter there, he proposed, rather than spend the night beside the road. Wukong narrowed his golden eyes and examined the village before answering. It appeared to be an ordinary place inhabited by ordinary people. He saw no reason not to go.

They had scarcely entered when a young man came hurrying out, wearing a blue jacket, carrying a bag and an umbrella, and walking as though he were already late for something unpleasant. Wukong caught him by the arm.

“What village is this?” the monkey asked.

“Ask someone else,” said the young man. “I have business.”

Wukong did not let go. He smiled and urged the fellow to be helpful. The stranger twisted left and right, trying to pull himself free. Wukong's hand held him as securely as iron tongs. The young man abandoned his bag and umbrella and began striking at the arm that held him. Wukong could stop every blow with one hand while steadying the pilgrims' baggage with the other.

Xuanzang disapproved. Plenty of villagers were walking nearby; why hold one man against his will when the same question could be asked politely of another? Wukong insisted that this man, precisely this man, had something worth knowing.

The servant was too furious to be frightened. He had already been cursed by his employer that morning, he said, and now a bald-headed stranger had caught him like a thief. It was an outrage. If he could not get away, he would have to speak.

The place was Gao Village, named for the many households bearing that surname. His own master was Old Squire Gao. And he, the unhappy traveler, was called Gao Cai. He had been ordered to find another specialist in demons, the latest in a succession of monks and Daoist priests who had failed to solve the family's trouble.

“A demon?” Wukong repeated, delighted. He loosened his grip just enough for Gao Cai to gesture. Here was an errand that seemed to have been arranged for him.

Three years earlier, the squire's youngest daughter had acquired an extraordinary husband. The creature had worked about the farm, eaten in the household, and refused to leave. For half a year he had kept the daughter locked in the rear rooms so that her own relatives could no longer see her. Gao Cai had been sent out with travel money to find someone capable of driving the husband away. Several practitioners had tried and failed. The squire had blamed the servant for every failure, then sent him out again.

Wukong told him he need not squander another coin. These two travelers, he declared, were not ordinary mendicant monks. The elder was a holy pilgrim from the Tang court, and the younger was exceptionally skilled at taking hold of monsters.

Gao Cai looked from the mild-faced monk on the horse to the small, hairy figure gripping his wrist. He was not convinced. Another disappointment would bring another beating of words from his master, and perhaps worse. But he could not get free, and Wukong had already taken up his bundle and turned him back toward home.

“Show us the gate,” said Wukong. “You can decide whether I know my trade after you have seen me work.”

The servant retrieved his umbrella. He had set out to search the country for help. Before he had left his own village, the help had seized him by the arm.

## Journey to the West — Old Squire Gao and the Husband Who Changed His Face

Gao Cai left the two pilgrims at the entrance to his master's house and hurried inside to report. The report was not immediately reassuring. The visitors were strangers from the eastern empire; one was dignified and plainly a monk, but his companion had a face like a creature from a mountain tale. It was difficult to see how this pair could succeed where professional exorcists had already failed.

Squire Gao came out to see for himself. He greeted Xuanzang with respect and stared openly at Wukong. Wukong returned the stare without embarrassment. If the old man wished to know whether his daughter's husband could be dealt with, there was no time to waste on appearances. They had come to ask for a night's lodging; driving out a demon would be something to do along the way.

The squire had asked many people for help. This was the first prospective deliverer to make his family's misery sound like a casual diversion. Still, the strange disciple carried himself with enough confidence to demand an explanation.

Gao had three daughters. The two older girls, Xianglan and Yulan, had married men of the village. The youngest, Cuilan, was supposed to remain near her parents. The squire had no son, and he wanted a husband for her who would live in his household, tend his land, and support him in old age. Such an arrangement had looked practical. A man without many relatives of his own would be especially welcome.

Then, three years earlier, a stranger had arrived from Mount Fuling. He called himself Zhu. He had neither parents nor brothers to bring into the bargain and offered to become a resident son-in-law. The man's appearance was acceptable; his willingness to work was splendid. Old Gao had accepted him.

At first the new son-in-law seemed almost too good to be true. He plowed the fields without an ox, broke the soil without a plow, and gathered crops without needing the usual tools. Work that exhausted farmhands took him little time. He did not complain of the hard labor. A household needing strength had found a worker who could do the work of many.

Xuanzang listened and asked what had gone wrong. A useful man with an appetite might be a blessing to an old farmer, not a calamity.

“That is how he began,” replied the squire.

The son-in-law had changed. His face grew broader and darker, his ears lengthened, and his mouth thrust forward in a snout. Bristles appeared behind his head. His body took on the coarseness and bulk of a pig. He could still work, but the creature living beneath Gao's roof was no longer a human son-in-law who could be introduced proudly to relatives. And he ate. A meal might require enormous measures of rice; even breakfast might use up heaps of cakes before his hunger quieted. It was fortunate, the squire said, that he claimed to eat vegetarian food. If he had also demanded meat and strong drink, the household would not have lasted.

Xuanzang observed that a man who did the work of several men might also eat accordingly. The squire agreed that food was not the heart of the trouble. It was the supernatural power, the gusting wind, the clouds into which his son-in-law vanished, the terror of the neighbors, and above all the imprisonment of Cuilan. For six months, the family had scarcely heard her voice. The old man could no longer be certain she was safe.

Wukong asked whether there was truly only one demon in the family. It was his manner of keeping the conversation just short of politeness. Gao declared that one such son-in-law was a lifetime's supply. He would gladly have the marriage ended and every link to the creature severed. Wukong offered to make the husband sign a paper renouncing the marriage. The old man wanted more: he wanted the source of the trouble taken away so that it would not return.

By now the courtyard had begun to fill with servants preparing a vegetarian meal for the guests. Xuanzang sat down to eat. Gao asked whether his young companion would need weapons, helpers, torches, or a company of men to confront the monster after dark.

Wukong showed him the staff that usually traveled concealed as a needle behind his ear. It grew in his hand until the squire found himself looking at an iron pillar thick enough to break gates. He suggested no further weapons. Wukong did not want a crowd of frightened villagers following him into the rear quarters. He asked instead that Gao invite several elderly relatives to keep Xuanzang company while he worked. They could hear the outcome together, whether it was a confession, a promise to depart, or some stronger means of removal.

The guests arrived and sat in the hall. They were not warriors. They were the sort of neighbors who knew family histories, marriage arrangements, gossip, and old grievances, and who might be called to witness a settlement. Xuanzang could pass an evening among them in conversation about the past. It was a better task for him than chasing clouds across a roof.

When dinner was over, Wukong rose and asked for the entrance to the rooms where Cuilan was kept. Old Gao led him into the rear of the property. The festive noise of the front hall grew faint; the passage narrowed. At the last door they found a lock that had been filled or sealed with copper. It was not intended to be opened by the simple turn of a key.

Wukong told Gao to fetch a key anyway, then laughed at the old man's incredulous look. He was playing. He stepped forward, felt the metal, and raised his staff.

The old man's daughter had been out of sight for half a year. Until that moment his plans had consisted of hiring strangers, offering money, and hoping someone else could undo the trouble. Now a single blow was going to make it impossible to pretend the rear house was an ordinary part of the home.

Wukong brought the staff down. The door burst inward. Beyond it lay a dark room, and somewhere inside was a woman's voice that had almost ceased to expect an answer.

## Journey to the West — Cuilan Behind the Door and the Visitor in the Wind

The darkness beyond the shattered door held still. Old Gao dared not rush into it. He called the household name by which he knew his youngest daughter. At first there was no reply. Then a weak voice answered from within.

“Father. I'm here.”

Wukong's eyes needed no lamp. He could see the young woman in the back of the room. Her hair was disordered, her face pale, her strength diminished by confinement and anxiety. When Cuilan recognized her father, she crossed the room and clung to him. They wept together. For the first time in months, the squire was not describing his daughter to other people; he was holding her alive in his arms.

Wukong did not have a great deal of patience for prolonged reunions. He needed to know how the husband came and went. Cuilan explained that the creature left at dawn and returned at night. He traveled in winds and clouds, and she did not know where he went. He had grown suspicious that her father meant to drive him away. That suspicion was why the rear rooms had been sealed and why she was kept from her family.

Wukong had heard enough. He asked the squire to take Cuilan into the front house and give her food and time to rest. He would wait where she had been imprisoned. If the demon did not return, there would be no encounter that night. If he came, he would find someone ready for him.

The old man took his daughter away. Wukong looked around the empty room. The door was broken; the scent of damp walls and a closed chamber remained. It was a poor place for an ambush unless the enemy believed nothing had changed. Wukong shook himself and assumed Cuilan's form. His small, hairy body vanished; in its place sat the young woman who had just left, with her face and manner well enough copied to fool a husband returning in the dark.

He waited on the bed. The hour deepened, and the courtyard fell quiet. Then a noise gathered beyond the windows. At first it seemed like wind in the trees. It became a roar that shook tiles from the roofs and flung dust down the lanes. Branches bent. Loose objects struck the walls. The violence of the arrival was as recognizable as a shouted name.

Through the whirling darkness came the son-in-law. He was not wearing the handsome appearance in which he had first presented himself to the family. His face was dark, with short bristles, a protruding muzzle, and broad ears. His clothing hung awkwardly on his body, neither the dress of a dignified scholar nor the costume of a tidy laborer. He had the air of a creature who had stopped caring whether people looked at him.

He entered the room and found a figure lying as though unwell. Thinking it was Cuilan, he leaned down and tried to embrace and kiss her. Wukong waited until the long snout came close, then caught it in his hand and pitched the visitor bodily away from the bed.

The demon hit the floor with a crash. He sprang up, astonished. Why was his wife behaving so strangely? Had he been late returning? Was she angry with him?

“Not angry,” said Wukong in Cuilan's voice. He wanted the creature relaxed and talking. He told him that he felt ill and did not wish to be crowded. Ordinarily, he said, he might have opened the door and greeted him sooner. Tonight the demon should get undressed and lie down first.

The creature accepted this explanation with touching lack of suspicion. He began to take off his clothes. Wukong moved away from the bed to a place where he could watch him. When the demon reached for the woman again, the space beside him was empty. Where had she gone? The disguised Wukong answered that he had only stepped aside for a moment and that the visitor could settle himself.

The scene had become a farce, though there was nothing funny about Cuilan's imprisonment. Wukong, who could kill with one blow, chose instead to let the monster reveal his habits. A creature who thought he had locked a powerless woman away now found himself continually outmaneuvered by someone who appeared to be that same woman. The guest at the bedside was a husband only in his own account; Cuilan had not consented to be held behind the door, and no clever disguise could erase the harm already done.

The demon relaxed again. He was back in the house, had not been attacked, and appeared to have persuaded his supposed wife to forgive him. Wukong drew him into conversation about the household. Her father, he said, was upset, and men had been sent to bring an exorcist. What would her husband do if someone truly dangerous came to the gate?

The monster stretched out comfortably. Ordinary priests and ritual specialists were no concern of his. He possessed transformations of his own and carried a marvelous nine-toothed rake. The old man could hire as many practitioners as he liked. They would run away when they understood whom they faced.

Wukong listened. The more the fellow boasted, the more useful his answers became. This was not an ignorant beast living on scraps. He knew the names of gods, magic weapons, and the offices of Heaven. Someone had once taught him to speak in the language of immortals.

Outside the back windows, the wind subsided. The man on the bed believed himself alone with Cuilan. The disguised visitor was patiently testing whether there was a name that would make his confidence fail.

## Journey to the West — The Name That Sent the Son-in-Law Running

The son-in-law was not embarrassed to explain why Gao's household ought to be grateful for him. He had done the work of oxen and field hands. He had moved bricks, built walls, dredged ditches, planted rice, and harvested grain. The household had better clothes and a fuller storehouse than when he entered it. Was he to be turned out, he asked, because of his face and appetite?

Wukong, wearing Cuilan's likeness, gave a little sigh. The demon wanted to know what troubled her. Her father and mother had spent the day quarrelling, she answered, throwing things and insulting her. They said their son-in-law was a disgrace because nobody could visit his relatives, nobody knew his family, and his face changed whenever he pleased. They did not even know where he went in his nightly winds.

The creature was annoyed by the complaint, but it worked on his vanity exactly as Wukong intended. Why had they accepted him three years before if they now objected to the arrangement? His looks could be changed whenever necessary. And if the family insisted on knowing his origins, they could be told plainly.

He lived on Mount Fuling, in a place called Cloud Pathway Cave. His surname was Zhu, which sounded the same as the Chinese word for pig, and his personal name was Ganglie. He had given them work and a home full of food. He did not see why an old man's anxiety about reputation should count against all this.

Wukong repeated the names in his mind. Mount Fuling. Cloud Pathway Cave. Zhu Ganglie. A sorcerer who had wanted to hide had just supplied a home address. A creature who had boasted of having no family had told the supposed wife everything an adversary needed to pursue him.

But Wukong had another question. What if Gao sent for someone who knew how to handle demons?

“Let him,” said the creature. He knew transformations, and his nine-toothed rake could break the confidence of any ordinary priest. The squire might invoke high heavenly protectors; the son-in-law had once moved among them himself. He was not going to tremble at the sight of a few paper charms.

Wukong decided to try one last name. Suppose the squire had found the Sun who had overturned Heaven five hundred years before. Suppose the notorious Great Sage Equal to Heaven were on his way.

The pig-faced man sat bolt upright. There were gods to whom he could speak proudly and monks he could terrify, but that troublesome monkey belonged to a different class of danger. He had heard what happened in Heaven when the Great Sage chose not to obey. The heavenly post Wukong had once held, keeper of horses, could be used as a sneer, but mocking the office did not make its former occupant any less formidable.

If that was the visitor, the son-in-law said, he had better leave. A quarrel between husband and wife could be repaired; defeat in a contest with that monkey would ruin him. He began gathering up his clothing in a hurry. The generous confidence of a moment earlier vanished. He had been quite prepared to frighten the Gao household. He was not prepared to test whether its newest guest could split open Heaven again.

Wukong asked sweetly why he was in such haste. The creature explained that he could not afford to lose face in a fight he might lose. In fact, if the Great Sage had arrived, perhaps the marriage should simply end here. He had no wish to stay and be beaten before the whole village.

That was enough confession for Wukong. As the creature reached the doorway, the apparent Cuilan sprang up and caught him by the clothing. With a swift motion Wukong wiped the borrowed appearance from his face. The woman's features dissolved. In their place were golden eyes, a short snout, a hairy face, and the grin of the very Great Sage whose name had emptied the demon of courage.

“Look again,” Wukong said. “Who do you think has been talking to you?”

The son-in-law recoiled so violently that his clothes tore in Wukong's grasp. He changed into a rush of wind and escaped through the doorway. Wukong lunged after him, brought out the iron staff, and struck at the fleeing air. The wind seemed to break apart into streaks of fire. Those lights fled toward the distant mountains.

The village's quiet night was suddenly crossed by two supernatural travelers, one escaping and the other pursuing. Wukong rose on his cloud, shouting that distance would be of no use. If the creature flew to the heavenly palaces, Wukong would follow him there. If he hid among the dead, Wukong would go down into the prisons of the underworld after him.

Behind them the rear house stood open, and Cuilan was with her parents instead of behind a sealed door. The old squire had found his daughter and learned where the unwanted son-in-law lived, but he could not yet know whether the visitor's promises would hold. The demon was not captured. He had been frightened into flight, not brought to account.

Wukong pursued the last fiery traces into the night. At the mountain ahead, the fugitive was about to stop running and bring out a weapon that had once belonged to an officer of Heaven.
