# Don Quixote: Fame and Deception

At the start of the second part of Don Quixote, the world has changed in a way no ordinary adventure hero is prepared for. A book about Don Quixote's earlier travels has been published. People have read it. They have laughed at the windmills, discussed Sancho's behavior and formed opinions of the knight. A character who entered the road because he had read too much must now travel through a society that has read about him. The reader's problem has become the hero's problem as well.

A young university graduate, Sansón Carrasco, arrives with news of the printed book. Sancho is delighted by the idea of fame, but both men are disturbed by what the text may have got wrong. The knight tries to distinguish the nobility of his intention from the embarrassment of his recorded acts. He still wants to continue his career. Sancho still hopes for a governorship. They set out together on a third expedition, no longer quite private individuals who can invent themselves unseen.

One of the first challenges concerns Dulcinea. Don Quixote wants to visit her, but Sancho has never met the exalted lady his master imagines. He has previously misrepresented a mission to her. Now he points out an ordinary peasant woman and claims she is Dulcinea under an enchantment. Don Quixote sees what is in front of him. Instead of acknowledging that his fictional lady may differ from the woman he has named, he accepts the explanation that a hostile magician has hidden her beauty. Sancho, often presented as the voice of everyday fact, has learned to use fantasy as a tool. His lie becomes one of the book's recurring burdens.

Carrasco also attempts to bring the knight home by entering the fiction rather than arguing against it. Disguised as the Knight of the Mirrors, he challenges Don Quixote and expects to win a duel that will compel him to abandon wandering. The plan fails. Don Quixote defeats him and takes the victory as further evidence that his own vocation is real. An attempt to cure a man through performance has simply given him another triumph within the performance.

The pair witness the wedding preparations surrounding Quiteria, a beautiful young woman whose marriage to a wealthy man, Camacho, appears settled despite the devotion of a poorer suitor, Basilio. Basilio stages a desperate act, and the resulting crisis changes the apparent rules of the marriage. The scene is constructed around performance, consent, wealth and public pressure. It also demonstrates that people who are not Don Quixote can manipulate an audience by controlling what it thinks it has seen.

Don Quixote then descends into the Cave of Montesinos and recounts a series of extraordinary visions involving figures from romance and myth. Sancho doubts the account. The narrator offers a story without granting the reader a stable place from which to certify whether the visions happened, were dreamed or were invented. Earlier windmills stood visibly in the landscape and supplied the easy comparison. Here a reader cannot simply replace a fantastic description with a fully documented ordinary event.

They encounter a puppet show run by a performer called Master Pedro. Its story, based on heroic romance, becomes too urgent for Don Quixote to endure as theatre. He attacks the puppets as though they were real people in danger. The destruction is comical but expensive. Once again the desire to defend imaginary victims damages the possessions of living people. The scene rhymes with the prince in Hamlet watching actors to discover truth, but Cervantes reverses the direction: his hero cannot bear to let the theatrical world remain separate from actual action.

Soon Don Quixote and Sancho come into contact with a duke and duchess who know them through the printed first part. Unlike earlier strangers, these aristocrats do not need to be convinced that the knight interprets reality through romances. They can construct adventures specifically calibrated to that weakness. Their house becomes a theatre in which the guests are the subjects of the performance and the nobles its directors. Much of the comedy is extravagant; much of the pleasure depends on social inequality. The ducal household has the money, servants and physical security to stage intricate tricks. The travelers pay with embarrassment, exhaustion and hope.

A staged prophecy proposes that Dulcinea's enchantment can be undone only if Sancho whips himself a large number of times. Sancho, who created the supposed enchantment to escape an awkward moment, is now assigned painful labor to remedy it. His invented explanation has acquired the force of an obligation. The joke reveals how narratives escape the control of their makers, especially when someone powerful decides to enforce them.

The duke and duchess announce that Sancho will finally receive a governorship. The island of Barataria is not what he imagines; it is a jurisdiction prepared as part of their deception. Before he goes, Don Quixote gives him advice about humility, justice, and resisting corruption. The counsel is often practical and perceptive. An observer who concluded that the knight is incapable of all judgment because he mistakes windmills for giants would now have to account for the quality of his moral reasoning.

This stretch of the novel ends with Sancho about to govern and Don Quixote still convinced that he belongs to an exalted mission. But the greatest change is the audience around them. In the first part a ridiculous belief collided with an unsuspecting world. In the second, people who know the belief begin designing realities around it. The question is no longer only whether Don Quixote can see clearly. It is what readers, institutions and spectators are entitled to do with a person whose vulnerability has become a public entertainment.
