# The Buddha’s Scriptures and the Westward Mission

For five hundred years the Mountain of Five Elements stood over Sun Wukong. Its stone was crowded with the weight of the Buddha's hand, and a strip bearing a sacred formula held the mountain fast. Below it, the former Great Sage Equal to Heaven could look out and speak. He could not get up. What he had wanted most in the world was freedom to go wherever his somersault cloud could carry him, and now he could not turn his body beneath the rock.

Far away in the western paradise, the Buddha returned from settling the trouble in Heaven. The assembly at Vulture Peak greeted him with banners and flowers. He told them what had happened: a monkey born from stone had collected marvelous powers, stolen from Heaven, survived heavenly weapons and the furnace, and finally accepted a contest whose size he did not understand. The Buddha had left him beneath the mountain. The gods were safe again. Time resumed the unhurried rhythm of the western realm.

Centuries went by. Then, at a festival, the Buddha gathered the holy company and ordered a banquet of flowers and fruit. Ananda and Kasyapa brought the offerings before the assembly. After the celebration came teaching. The Buddha spoke of beings in the four great continents, of cruelty and kindness, of the difficult passage from ignorance toward understanding. He looked toward the eastern continent of human beings and described the violence, greed, and quarrels that clouded the lives of its inhabitants.

He possessed scriptures that could be carried to them. There were three great collections: one concerned the teachings of the law, one reflection and reasoning, and one the sacred texts that could guide souls. The novel gives the collection an enormous number of scrolls. Yet possessing the scriptures in the west was not enough. They could not simply be dropped like rain on people who did not know their value. Someone would have to come and seek them.

The Buddha asked for a messenger. Such a messenger must travel east, find a suitable pilgrim, and convince that person to journey westward over mountains and rivers. The traveler would have to endure exhaustion, temptation, hunger, fear, and the dangers of a road populated by creatures whose powers belonged to more than the human world. At the end, the pilgrim could receive the true scriptures and take them back to the Tang lands. The undertaking would require both courage and a mind capable of learning.

Guanyin stepped forward. She was the bodhisattva associated with compassion, the one whom the distressed called upon for rescue. If someone had to search for such a pilgrim, she would go. The Buddha was pleased. She would be accompanied by the guardian Hui'an, also called Muzha, whose strength and obedience made him a fitting escort. But he gave Guanyin an instruction that was strange for a being who could cross the sky. She was not to fly high above the earth and arrive in Chang'an without seeing what lay between. She must travel low enough to examine rivers, mountains, dangers, and distances. How else could she prepare the person who would have to pass that way with ordinary feet?

He gave her gifts for the future traveler: a magnificent embroidered robe and a staff with nine rings. The robe was not a piece of court clothing, though it shone with jewels and costly threads. It was an emblem of the faith and a protection for one who deserved to wear it. The staff could support the traveler through a country where an imperial order would mean nothing to monsters on a mountain path.

Then the Buddha gave her three circlets. They were small objects, easy to hide in a sleeve. Their use was not gentle. Each had a corresponding spell; when placed upon a disobedient supernatural creature, a circlet would bind itself to the head, and the spell could inflict a terrible pain. These were not decorations for the pilgrim. They were instruments for bringing powerful and unruly companions into a discipline that they might not accept by persuasion alone.

Guanyin accepted the robe, the staff, and the three circlets. She bowed and set out with Hui'an. The west was luminous behind them. Ahead lay the road that the scriptures themselves had never taken, a succession of places with different inhabitants and different laws. Guanyin had to recognize whom she met, not merely defeat an obstacle and fly onward.

As she traveled, she could see how the journey would be made. Rivers could rise so high that a boat was useless. Mountains sheltered monsters whose hunger had no human limit. Some of those monsters were not born evil; they had once held places in Heaven, had broken its rules, and were still suffering the consequences. A person born to the human world could not pass them by simply believing that a journey was good.

But Guanyin had a different way of looking at these scattered disasters. The pilgrim's problem might become a disgraced spirit's opportunity. A criminal could be given work and judged by what he did afterward. A creature that had spent years attacking travelers might one day protect one. Even a monkey pinned under a mountain might possess the very strength that a quiet scholar would lack.

At first there was no pilgrim in sight. There was only a destination in the west, a search in the east, and the road between them. This was the beginning of the other great movement of the story. The Monkey King's rebellion had been about rising against every authority he encountered. The undertaking now being prepared would ask whether enormous power could serve something beyond its owner's pride.

Guanyin and Hui'an descended toward the first great obstacle. A terrible river lay across their way, and something living in it was already moving toward them.
