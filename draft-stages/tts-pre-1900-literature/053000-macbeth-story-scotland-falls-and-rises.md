# Macbeth — Story: Scotland’s Defeat of the Tyrant

In England, Macduff asks Malcolm to return and rescue Scotland. Malcolm is cautious: the man before him might be an agent sent by Macbeth. To test him, Malcolm pretends that he himself would be a disastrous king, full of uncontrollable appetites and greed. Macduff tries at first to make allowances, but eventually he says that Scotland cannot safely be ruled by such a person. Malcolm then withdraws the claims. The test has allowed Macduff to show commitment to the country rather than automatic loyalty to anyone who might occupy its throne. Whatever we think of Malcolm's method, he has made judgment of rule a public responsibility.

Ross arrives with news from Scotland and finally reports the murder of Macduff's wife and children. Macduff demands to know whether all are dead. His grief cannot be repaired by Malcolm's instruction to transform it into revenge. He will fight Macbeth, but the loss remains loss. That is an important difference from the earlier scenes of Macbeth and Lady Macbeth: violence here is attached to bereavement, and the play does not promise that an appropriate killing will restore the dead.

In Scotland, Macbeth's hold on power weakens. Lords desert him; Malcolm advances with an army aided by English forces. A doctor and a gentlewoman watch Lady Macbeth sleepwalk. She rubs her hands as if washing away a stain and relives fragments of the night Duncan died and the other killings. Earlier she had treated blood as a mark that water could erase. Now the action of washing repeats without accomplishing anything. The doctor recognizes that the disturbance is beyond his practical art. We see a person whose confidence was once part of the murder's machinery, and whose mind now circles the consequences it cannot command.

Macbeth remains defiant at Dunsinane. He trusts the apparitions, boasts that no ordinary human opponent can harm him, and mocks the idea that a forest might move. Yet the words he speaks about life are stripped of the triumph he once expected. Reports of Lady Macbeth's death arrive without a scene of her dying. The play does not explicitly establish the manner of her death, though later lines can support interpretations. Macbeth hears that she is dead and responds with a bleak meditation on time: days move forward, and a life that seemed urgent comes to seem like brief noise without durable meaning. His language reveals exhaustion even while he resolves to fight.

Meanwhile, Malcolm orders his soldiers to cut branches from Birnam Wood and carry them as cover while approaching the castle. A messenger reports that the wood appears to be moving toward Dunsinane. This is the first reversal of Macbeth's confidence. The words of the apparition are coming true in a manner Macbeth did not imagine. The military operation remains human, practical, and visible; nothing requires us to believe trees have uprooted themselves.

Battle closes around Macbeth. He kills the young Siward and continues to rely on the promise about being born of woman. Macduff finds him and rejects the boast: his own birth, he says, came by an untimely cutting from his mother's womb. Macbeth understands that he has mistaken a carefully limited formula for universal protection. He fights rather than surrender. Macduff kills him and brings the news of his death. Malcolm is hailed as king. The play ends with the restoration of an order that Macbeth's murder had ruptured, but the restoration is costly. Duncan, Banquo, Lady Macduff, her child, the guards, Lady Macbeth, and others cannot return.

The outcome does not remove the question of the witches. They predicted things that occurred, but they also framed those things in words that invited disastrous confidence. Macbeth took their riddles as permissions. The final force that defeats him is made of human choices: Malcolm's coalition, Macduff's search, the army's camouflage, and Macbeth's decision to fight. In the opening scenes a loyal soldier imagined that kingship would complete his honor. At the end, the soldier is dead, the country has survived him, and the crown has become a lesson in the danger of trying to make a forecast substitute for moral judgment.
