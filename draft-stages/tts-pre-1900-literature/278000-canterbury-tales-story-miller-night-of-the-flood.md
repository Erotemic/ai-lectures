# The Canterbury Tales — The Miller’s Tale, a Night of the Flood

The Host wants the next speaker to be someone respectable, but the Miller will not wait. He has drunk too much, boasts that he will tell a wonderful story and refuses to defer to the social order that made the Knight first. The Reeve, himself once a carpenter, objects when he hears the story will make a carpenter ridiculous. Chaucer the pilgrim warns his audience that this will be coarse material and offers readers the chance to turn the page. Then the tale begins and overturns the Knight's world at once.

In Oxford lives John, an aging carpenter with a young wife named Alison. John is possessive and afraid she will betray him. He rents a room to Nicholas, a clever student of astrology who has an eye for Alison. Nicholas courts her boldly. She agrees to an affair if they can evade her husband's watch. A parish clerk named Absolon also desires Alison. He sings beneath her window and brings gifts, but she has no interest in his rehearsed devotion.

Nicholas devises a plan from the learned astronomy that gives him authority in John's eyes. He locks himself away and pretends to have discovered a terrible secret in the stars: another flood, like Noah's, will drown the world that very night. John panics. Nicholas advises him to hang three large tubs from the roof, one for himself, one for Alison and one for Nicholas, stocked with food. At the appointed hour they will cut the ropes and float above the waters. John makes the preparations. When night comes, he climbs into his tub and falls asleep, certain that he is the one person in Oxford prepared for catastrophe.

Nicholas and Alison slip into bed. Absolon comes to the window and begs Alison for a kiss. She leans out in the darkness but offers him not her lips but her backside. He kisses it before realizing what has happened, and rushes away in disgust. Returning with a hot iron borrowed from a blacksmith, he asks for another kiss. Nicholas now sticks his own backside out the window and breaks wind; Absolon brands him. Nicholas shouts for water to cool the burn. From high above, John hears the cry, assumes the flood has come, cuts his tub loose and crashes to the floor.

The neighbors gather. John tries to explain the flood, while Nicholas and Alison declare him mad. His injuries are real, his fear is made ridiculous, and the audience within the tale believes the conspirators. The laughter at the end is both liberating and cruel. A pompous guardian has been deceived, but a frightened man has also been hurt and publicly humiliated. A student who boasted of his knowledge has been burned, and the elegant lover has lost his dignity in a very literal fashion.

The Miller has not simply told a dirty story after a noble one. He has seized the same concerns and turned them into their bodily opposites. In the Knight's Tale two men watched a woman through a high window; here lovers meet at a low one. In the romance, celestial powers arrange a tragic fall; here a student invents celestial knowledge and causes a carpenter to fall. The noble tournament has become a contest of practical jokes, and the confident boundary between learned wisdom and fraud has broken down. The Reeve, hearing his former trade mocked, insists on answering. The storytelling game has become personal.
