# Ophelia, Gertrude, and the State in Hamlet

An account of Hamlet told only from the prince's thoughts can make the people around him look like symbols placed there to explain his pain. The play resists that convenience. Ophelia and Gertrude are both denied important information, both subjected to other people's judgments, and both participate in a court where private life is political. Their stories show what the avenger's perspective excludes.

Ophelia first appears in a family that wishes to instruct her. Laertes warns against Hamlet's courtship because the prince's rank limits the freedom of his choice. Polonius rejects her account of Hamlet's affection and orders her to cut contact. When she later returns letters and gifts, she is acting within a plan devised by two men watching in secret. Whatever the truth of Hamlet's feelings, she is not given a fair opportunity to negotiate them. Her father translates her emotional life into evidence for his theory of the prince.

Hamlet's language to Ophelia has competing possible motives. He might wish to protect her from marriage and from the corrupt court. He might suspect the observers and attack her as their agent. He might simply be cruel in anger and confusion. These possibilities need not be mutually exclusive. But it would be a mistake to describe the scene only in terms of his strategy. The words land on a woman who has already been made responsible for his behavior. She cannot control the experiment of which she is part.

After Polonius's death, Ophelia returns singing fragments that range across love, sexuality, betrayal and burial. The scene gives her speech a prominence she has rarely been allowed when others assigned her lines and duties. That does not mean her distress secretly frees her; she is suffering and vulnerable, and her songs are not a clear manifesto. Their fractured form nevertheless makes the court listen to material it has previously suppressed. She speaks about bodies, promises and loss in ways the official language of the palace cannot easily absorb.

Her drowning happens offstage. Gertrude describes a branch breaking and Ophelia entering the water. The narration is delicate and strange: she continues to sing until her clothes fill and pull her down. This is a reported event, and the later argument over the funeral shows the question of intention has not been settled. If we say simply that she chose suicide, we assert more than the visible action establishes. If we insist on an accident, we make the opposite leap. The play creates an ethical demand precisely because Ophelia's final perspective is unavailable.

Gertrude has a different kind of power and a different lack of it. She is queen, but her marriage is one of the foundations of Claudius's claim to continuity. Her desire and her public position cannot be separated. Hamlet often speaks as though her remarriage were evidence of a general failure in women. Yet the text does not give the audience direct certainty about what she knew of the old king's murder. Some productions imagine her as actively complicit, others as unaware. The printed scene in which Hamlet confronts her supplies strong evidence that she is disturbed by his accusations; it does not give a private confession of murder.

The bedroom confrontation also forces a question about moral coercion. Hamlet arrives to instruct his mother about the superiority of his dead father and the ugliness of her new marriage. His outrage is comprehensible as grief, but the rhetorical pressure is severe. When he kills Polonius behind the hanging, the scene changes from accusation into violence. Gertrude's fear is not an unreasonable misunderstanding of an entirely innocent son. She has just witnessed him stab a hidden man. When he sees the ghost and she does not, she has grounds to question whether his account of what is present can be trusted.

At the fencing match Gertrude drinks from the poisoned cup. Claudius knows its contents and tries to prevent her, but cannot publicly explain why. Whether a particular staging gives her an inkling of the conspiracy is a directorial question. Her death belongs to the logic of a court where a king's schemes are concealed even from those nearest him. The attempt to remove one danger destroys his wife as well.

Race, religion and nation are less prominent as explicit categories in this plot than the institutions of kinship, succession and marriage, but the state remains present throughout. Denmark's foreign policy runs alongside its domestic violence. The army of Fortinbras approaches while the royal family disintegrates. Claudius turns a diplomatic letter into a death warrant. Ophelia's grief helps provoke Laertes's revolt. A private injury repeatedly becomes a crisis of government.

An instructive way to study the play is to write a brief account of each scene without mentioning Hamlet by name. What does Ophelia do, and who prevents her from acting? What can Gertrude know? What does Laertes want before Claudius guides him? What does Horatio witness? Such a reading does not eliminate the protagonist. It restores the human costs that his story, told from inside his suffering, can make hard to see.
