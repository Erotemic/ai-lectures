# The Canterbury Tales — The Franklin’s Tale, Dorigen and the Rocks

A knight named Arveragus loves Dorigen in Brittany. After courtship she agrees to marry him. They make a promise that sounds different from many contracts in the Canterbury stories: he will not rule her with tyranny, and she will be faithful to him. Each yields something without becoming a servant of the other. Their marriage begins as an experiment in courtesy built upon restraint. But Arveragus travels abroad for a long period of military service, and Dorigen suffers intensely from their separation. Friends try to help her by walking with her along the coast.

From the cliffs Dorigen sees black rocks projecting into the sea. Ships may strike them, she fears, and her husband may die returning home. The rocks become a visible shape for anxiety. A young squire named Aurelius falls in love with her and eventually declares himself. Dorigen does not return his love. To put him off without an outright quarrel, she says that she will yield to him if he removes every dangerous black rock from the coast. The request is meant to be impossible. It is also a promise made in words that another person will insist on taking literally.

Aurelius despairs until his brother leads him to a learned clerk of Orleans, skilled in the kind of astronomical and magical arts that can produce wondrous appearances. The clerk arranges a spectacle in which the rocks seem to vanish. Aurelius pays dearly for the service and comes to Dorigen to claim her promise. She is appalled. The condition she believed impossible now appears to have been satisfied, yet fulfilling it would betray her marriage. In her grief she thinks about famous women whose courage took the form of death rather than dishonor.

Arveragus returns and hears what happened. He tells Dorigen that a promise must be kept, even when it brings sorrow to the people who made it. He sends her to Aurelius, warning that their private unhappiness must not become a public quarrel. This might look like extraordinary nobility. It also forces Dorigen toward a situation she never freely desired. The moral pressure of honor is still being placed upon her body. Aurelius, seeing her misery and the husband's willingness to sacrifice his own happiness, refuses to claim her. He releases her from the promise.

The clerk who made the rocks disappear then learns that Aurelius has given up his reward. He cancels the substantial debt owed to him. The tale ends by asking who has behaved most generously. The answer is not supplied. Arveragus yields to the authority of a promise; Aurelius yields to compassion; the clerk yields his payment. Dorigen, who feared a husband's death, has been pushed through a crisis in which every option seemed to cost her something essential.

The Franklin calls the story a Breton lay, a romance associated with marvels and ethical tests. Yet the most consequential wonders are not the vanished rocks but the decisions to refrain from exercising power. The tale belongs beside the Wife of Bath's concern with sovereignty and the Clerk's account of coercive obedience. It offers a vision of marriage in which dignity may depend on refusing to enforce a legal or verbal advantage. It also leaves us the difficult fact that other people's generosity arrived only after Dorigen was made afraid. Chaucer lets the question at the end continue to trouble the apparent happy ending.
