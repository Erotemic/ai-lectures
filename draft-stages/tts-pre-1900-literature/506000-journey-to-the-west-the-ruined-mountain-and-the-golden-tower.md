# Journey to the West: The ruined mountain and the golden tower

## Across the eastern sea

The last bow had been made. Tang Sanzang had refused to take back his dismissal, even when white bones lay on the road where the supposed old man had stood. Zhu Bajie had argued that a monkey capable of changing his own shape could change a corpse as well. Sha Wujing had been left to watch their master. And Sun Wukong, who had once seemed powerful enough to pull the world apart, could not force one human monk to believe him.

He leaped into the sky and turned east.

For a while he could still picture the party moving westward without him. He thought of the blind paths and hungry valleys ahead, of the absent-minded master who would leave his horse to look at a flower, of Bajie proposing to stop for dinner whenever the road grew difficult. The journey had been assigned to him by Guanyin. He had been released from five hundred years under a mountain so that he might protect this monk. Now the monk had written the dismissal with his own hand.

Beyond the mainland the sea opened under him. He recognized the waters that separated his old home from the human world. He had not taken this route home for five centuries. Wind pressed against his face, the waves shone and fell, and somewhere beyond the spray rose Flower-Fruit Mountain, the place where the stone monkey had first discovered that he could be a king.

He crossed the water in one bound.

What he saw from the mountain crest stopped him.

The trees had been burned. There were black trunks where there should have been orchards and green bowers, broken rocks where paths had once led to streams, slopes bare of grass. The Water-Curtain Cave still existed, but the landscape around it no longer resembled the home he remembered. In the old days he had marched out beneath banners with tens of thousands of monkeys and other creatures following him. Now he heard little beyond the wind.

He remembered the battle with Erlang, the heavenly general who had chased him through one transformation after another. While Wukong was captured and carried off to Heaven, Erlang and his companions had set fire to the mountain. From above, at the time, the Monkey King had imagined that his home would outlast the campaign against him. Standing amid the damage, he could no longer pretend that a war ended when its hero left the battlefield.

He grieved for the mountain.

Then movement stirred among the ruins. A few little monkeys came out cautiously, as if they did not quite trust what they had heard. One peered around a tree stump. Another took two steps forward and dropped to his knees.

Their king had returned.

More monkeys appeared, crying out to him. They had been hiding in holes, under rock ledges, and around the bridge to the cave. They were thinner than he remembered. Their greetings became complaints: the great fire had killed many of them. Many survivors had left when there was nothing left to eat. Hunters had been coming up the slopes with nets, arrows, dogs, and trained birds. The hunters killed some monkeys for meat or skins and carried others away alive to make them perform tricks in markets.

Wukong asked how many of his people remained.

A thousand or so, they said. They spoke of the forty-seven thousand who had once been gathered here, then counted the dead, the dispersed, the hungry, the captured, until the old number seemed to belong to another age.

The surviving officers still held their posts as well as they could: Marshals Ma and Liu, Generals Beng and Ba. They came out to receive him, bowing and asking a question that had traveled ahead of his return. Had he not been serving the monk on the pilgrimage to the western scriptures? Why had he come back?

Wukong answered with the outrage he had been holding since the White Bone Mountain. He had fought monsters and saved the monk again and again, and in return his master had called him wicked. The monk had written a dismissal and sworn never to employ him again. What sort of arrangement was that?

The monkeys cheered. If the monk did not want their king, they did. Let the monk find his scriptures without him. Let Wukong stay home, drink coconut wine, and make the mountain lively again.

Wukong did not reach for the wine. He asked when the hunters were next expected.

Every day, his officers told him. They might arrive at any moment.

## A king's answer

Wukong ordered the monkeys to collect the broken stones scattered by the old fire and pile them on the hillside. The stones had lain there for years. Under his direction they became ammunition. The monkeys scrambled, shouldered rocks larger than themselves, and heaped them wherever he pointed. Then he sent his people into shelter.

The hunters came with dogs and hawks, bows across their backs, men walking in ranks over the hills. Their noise carried up the slope. They expected frightened animals and a day's sport. They did not expect the mountain itself to move against them.

Wukong drew a breath from the direction of the wind and released it with a spell. The air thickened, then roared. Dust rose in clouds. Loose stones flew like a storm of iron pellets. Dogs, horses, men, and weapons tumbled together down the hillside. The hunters could not see where to run. The wind hurled rocks into their ranks until the expedition was destroyed.

When it ended, the mountain was quiet again.

This was no narrow act of chasing intruders away. Wukong had killed the hunters in great numbers. The same monkey who had just argued that he struck only dangerous demons had now answered the suffering of his people by slaughtering human beings without distinction. Bodies lay on the mountainside. In distant villages, families would wait for men who could not return.

Wukong clapped his hands and laughed. The monk had always taught him that many days of good deeds might scarcely outweigh one day of evil. The monk had scolded him for killing demons. Here, with no monk to restrain him, he had killed a multitude. He tossed the monk's teaching back at the empty air, half in anger and half in triumph.

He told the monkeys to gather the hunters' clothes, weapons, flags, and horses. Their garments could be cleaned and worn against the cold. Their weapons could be used in training. The dead were thrown into a deep pool, the horses butchered for meat. Wukong was restoring a kingdom by the methods of a warrior, not by the standards of the Buddhist teacher who had sent him away.

He made a new banner: Flower-Fruit Mountain rebuilt, Water-Curtain Cave restored, the Great Sage Equal to Heaven returned to his throne.

He summoned creatures from the neighboring heights. He called on the dragon kings to bring rain. There was water for the scorched ground, and then planting: elms and willows, pines and flowering trees, orchards that would take time to yield fruit. The mountain began to recover. Monkeys who had survived by hiding could come out into the sun and find work worth doing.

But rebuilding did not erase what had happened, nor did it settle the matter of the pilgrim far away. Wukong was king again. It had once been everything he wanted. Yet the road west had become part of him, and the old title sat differently on his shoulders now.

## The missing forager

Far to the west, the traveling party had one fewer protector and no improvement in its appetite.

Bajie walked in front of the horse with his rake. Sha Wujing carried the luggage. Xuanzang rode between them, watching the road. They passed beyond the White Bone Mountain and entered a pine forest threaded with climbing vines. It was the sort of place where a path could disappear within a few paces of the trees.

Xuanzang warned the disciples to take care. They had already met danger in mountains that looked peaceful. Bajie promised to lead, although the promise grew less cheerful as the road lengthened. Eventually the monk said he was hungry. They would need to find vegetarian food before night.

Bajie took the begging bowl and set off.

A mile passed. Then several more. He saw no fields, smoke, cottage, shrine, or traveler. There were no houses from which to ask for rice. He complained to himself that when Wukong had been with them food had appeared as soon as the master requested it. Now he understood what it cost to provision an expedition. No one truly learned the price of firewood and rice, he reflected, until the responsibility became his own.

He grew sleepy. If he returned immediately, his master would say he had hardly tried. Better to be absent long enough to make the search convincing. He found a patch of grass, put his head down, and fell asleep with the particular efficiency of someone who had meant to close his eyes only for a moment.

Back in the forest, Xuanzang waited. The sun moved. Bajie did not return.

The monk called Sha Wujing. His second disciple had been gone too long. Perhaps he had found food and was enjoying it without thinking of those waiting in the trees. Sha agreed that this was possible. At any rate they needed somewhere safe to spend the night. He would look for Bajie and bring him back, food or no food.

Sha took his staff and disappeared along the western path.

The monk was alone except for the white horse.

Xuanzang gathered the luggage, secured the horse to a tree, arranged his hat and traveling staff, and waited. He had little to do but listen to birds settling in the branches. The longer he sat, the more uncomfortable the silence became. Perhaps the disciples would find him more easily if he walked a little. Perhaps a dwelling was hidden just beyond the next stand of pines.

He set off on foot.

The paths were confusing. His disciples had gone west, along the proper road. Xuanzang, distracted by the forest and by worry, turned south without noticing. Through a gap in the trees he saw a dazzling flash of gold.

A tower stood above the slope. Its top caught the sinking sun and shone like polished metal.

Xuanzang stopped. He had made a vow to honor sacred places encountered along the route: to bow before images of the Buddha, to offer incense where there was a temple, to sweep the grounds around a religious tower. Surely the shining tower belonged to a monastery. There might be monks there, a place to sleep, and food for the party. His disciples had missed an opportunity by taking the other path.

He remembered the horse and luggage tied in the forest but told himself that no one would trouble them in such a quiet place. He could find the monastery and return to lead the others to it.

The closer he came, the more impressive the tower seemed. Rock walls rose above it, trees clustered about its entrance, and a bamboo curtain hung across a doorway. Yet there was something wrong with the stillness. No novice swept the path. No chant rose from the building. There was no sound of a bell.

Xuanzang lifted the curtain and stepped inside.

## The Yellow-Robed Lord

On a stone couch lay a creature with a deep blue face, enormous teeth, red tangled hair, and hands like bowls. A yellow robe draped across its body. Near it lay a sword that caught the light.

For one instant the monk stood frozen in the doorway. Then he tried to retreat.

The creature opened its eyes.

It called to its followers. There was a visitor at the door, a monk with delicate skin and the look of someone who had traveled from far away. Bring him in.

The little demons rushed forward. Xuanzang stumbled on the uneven ground; his legs would scarcely support him. They lifted him and carried him into the chamber.

The monster demanded to know where he had come from and where he was going. Xuanzang told the truth. He was a monk from the Great Tang, traveling west by imperial command to obtain scriptures. He had seen the golden tower and believed it was a sacred place. He hoped he had not offended its master by entering.

The creature laughed. Far from being offended, it was delighted. Here was the famous Tang monk, walking willingly into a place where a demon hoped one day to feast upon him. What better fortune could the demon ask for?

He ordered Xuanzang tied to a post deep inside the cave.

The monster then asked how many companions he had brought. Xuanzang, believing honesty required a full answer, described Bajie, Sha Wujing, the white horse, and the luggage in the forest. The monster counted them as additional portions for his table. He told his servants not to chase them. When the disciples discovered their master's absence, they would come looking for him. It was more convenient to let dinner come to the door.

Meanwhile Sha had found Bajie exactly where the monk's suspicions suggested he might be. The pig lay in the grass asleep, muttering through a dream. Sha pushed aside the stalks with his staff and seized an ear.

Bajie started awake. Had dinner arrived?

No, Sha told him. Their master had sent them to find lodging. Bajie had been given the begging bowl to seek food, not a bed in the weeds. The sun was going down. They must get back.

Bajie scrambled up, protesting that there was nowhere to beg and that a sensible creature required rest. They returned through the pine forest and found the horse and luggage, but no master.

Sha searched the clearing. Xuanzang's things were there. There was no sign of a struggle. That did not reassure him.

Bajie suggested that their master had simply wandered off to admire the view. He was forever stopping to look at things. Sha did not like the explanation, but they had no other lead. They took the horse and baggage and searched until they saw the same golden reflection that had drawn Xuanzang from the path.

Bajie was pleased. A tower with a golden crown must surely mean a wealthy monastery. Perhaps the master was already eating while they exhausted themselves outside. They hurried toward it.

At the gate they found words carved on white stone: Wanzi Mountain, Boyue Cave.

Sha read them and grew cold. It was not a monastery. They had arrived at a demon's dwelling.

Bajie pounded on the gate with his rake and shouted for the master of the house to come out. Inside, a gatekeeper rushed to report two fearsome monks at the door, one with a long snout and great ears, the other dark-faced and carrying a staff. The Yellow-Robed Lord rose, put on his armor, and took up his sword.

He came out smiling.

Bajie demanded that the monk from the Great Tang be released. The demon admitted the monk was inside and invited the brothers to join him for buns made with human flesh.

Bajie nearly took the invitation at face value. Sha caught his arm. Did he really wish to eat the very thing that threatened their master?

The rake rose; the demon's sword flashed. They sprang into the air to fight, with Sha coming alongside Bajie. The Yellow-Robed Lord was strong and practiced. Their blows rang across the mountainside, cloud rolled around the three combatants, and neither disciple could win a clear advantage. Below them the cave waited, its prisoner bound to a post, while the setting sun vanished from the golden tower.

The monks had found their master. Finding him was not the same as getting him out.
