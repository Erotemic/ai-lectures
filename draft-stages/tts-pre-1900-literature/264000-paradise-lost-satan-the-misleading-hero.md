# Paradise Lost — Satan, Charisma, and the Misleading Hero

One of the most consequential facts about Paradise Lost is that Satan can be compelling. A reader may feel the appeal of his refusal to surrender before noticing how easily he turns that refusal into a campaign against people who have never injured him. Milton does not simply put the villain on stage and require us to hiss. He allows the fallen angel to sound magnificent, and then makes us examine what that magnificence does.

Consider the scene after the rebels' defeat. Satan rises from a lake of fire and addresses the ruined host. Military courage, loyalty to companions, refusal to despair: these are qualities the traditions of epic poetry often praise. Satan's speech borrows their shape. But the speech does not begin with a frank account of why an army is here, what the fallen now owe to one another, or whether their original cause was just. It moves rapidly toward the preservation of his own status. He promises continuing struggle while refusing to ask whether the struggle is worth its cost.

The infernal council makes the problem harder to ignore. He appears to invite independent discussion but has already arranged for Beelzebub to offer his preferred proposal. He then volunteers for the dangerous mission which his own framing has made seem necessary. To the room he looks sacrificial. To the reader, who knows the mission is directed at innocent human beings, the gesture is more ambiguous. Courage does not cease to be courage when spent on a cruel project. It ceases to establish moral authority.

Satan's most revealing moments may be those when he is not performing for an audience. On Mount Niphates he admits that God was good to him and that pride caused his rebellion. He sees the possibility of repentance but recoils from the admission it would require. The speech does not sound like the settled conviction of a freedom fighter. It sounds like a person trapped by the story he has told about himself. When he spies Adam and Eve, sympathy briefly interrupts his rage. That interval matters because Milton makes him capable of understanding their innocence. The harm he plots cannot be excused as a consequence of ignorance.

Watch the verbs and comparisons by which Satan's stature changes. Early in the poem he is associated with vast creatures and immense landscapes. Later he creeps beside Eve's sleeping ear as a toad and then inhabits a serpent. His strategy becomes more successful as his apparent grandeur diminishes. In Book Ten, the applause he expects turns into a hiss. The form of his triumph is a judgment upon the instrument by which he obtained it.

This problem has divided generations of readers. William Blake's famous remark that Milton was of the Devil's party without knowing it challenged the poem's declared moral structure. Shelley too found moral force in Satan's resistance. Other critics argue that the poem deliberately attracts us to Satanic ideals of solitary greatness before revealing their price. Stanley Fish's reader-centered approach makes the reader's misjudgment a central part of the experience; other scholars resist making every attraction to Satan proof of sinful reading. These positions are not interchangeable, and their disagreements cannot be resolved by claiming that Milton secretly meant the opposite of what he says.

Try reading three scenes side by side: the speech on the burning lake, the solitary confession on Mount Niphates, and the speech Satan makes as a serpent. In the first he recasts defeat as strength. In the second he nearly recognizes self-deception. In the third he converts a false piece of evidence, the talking snake, into a promise of enlightenment. All three depend upon a gift for persuasion. What changes is the person he hopes to persuade and the falsehood he needs that person to accept.

The poem's challenge is not that eloquence is evil. Milton himself writes some of the grandest eloquence in English. The danger lies in mistaking eloquence for proof that the speaker is right. A reader who feels Satan's appeal has not failed to read the poem. That reader has reached the point at which the poem can ask a more searching question: what, exactly, have we been moved to admire?
