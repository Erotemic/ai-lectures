# The Decameron — Lisabetta and the Pot of Basil

In Messina, three wealthy merchant brothers live with their sister Lisabetta. A young man named Lorenzo works in their business. Lisabetta and Lorenzo fall in love and begin a secret relationship. The brothers discover it. They do not respond by listening to her or examining Lorenzo's character. They fear the shame that a liaison with a dependent employee might bring upon their family and trade connections.

They ask Lorenzo to accompany them on a journey outside the city. There they murder him and conceal his body. When they return, they explain that he has been sent away on business. Lisabetta asks repeatedly where he has gone. The brothers give evasive answers and eventually rebuke her for asking too much. Her grief is joined to uncertainty: she has no proof that the man she loves is dead, and the people who know have taken control of the account she receives.

Then Lorenzo appears to her in a dream. He speaks of his murder and shows where his body was hidden. Lisabetta goes to the place with a trusted woman. They uncover the remains. Unable to carry the whole body away, she takes his head and brings it home. She places it in an earthenware pot, covers it with soil, and plants basil above it. The plant grows under her constant care and tears. She visits it, waters it, and remains beside it, expressing a grief that her household has refused to recognize openly.

The brothers notice the attachment and take away the pot. When they discover what it contains, they are frightened of exposure and flee. Lisabetta, deprived even of the last object through which she could remain connected to Lorenzo, declines and dies. Her mourning cannot be managed into silence without destroying what is left of her life.

Filomena has given us a story of great economy. There is no long pursuit, mistaken court judgment, or series of elaborate disguises. The brothers' crime happens quickly. Most of the tale unfolds afterward, as a hidden killing is translated into the daily labor of tending a plant. The pot of basil is at once an ordinary household object and an improvised memorial. Nothing in its outward appearance tells strangers what it holds, but for Lisabetta every leaf belongs to the person she has lost.

The dream is equally important. Boccaccio does not invite us to reconstruct a modern forensic investigation. In the literary world of the novella, Lorenzo's appearance supplies the knowledge the brothers have suppressed. Dreams in the *Decameron* do not always have the same meaning or reliability. Here the dream gives grief a place to search and makes the concealed crime accessible to the victim's beloved.

The brothers' motives connect love to commerce and rank. Their sister's sexuality is treated as something that might diminish the standing of a merchant household, while Lorenzo's position as an employee counts against him. The brothers are able to travel and bury a body because they possess the practical authority and resources of employers. Their secrecy is not a private feeling; it is enforced by the means available to prosperous men.

Later readers often remember the basil plant more vividly than the murder. That is part of the novella's achievement. Violence is converted into an image of patient nurture; the hidden head becomes the root of an almost sacred attachment. But the tenderness is not consoling. Unlike the successful reversals of Day Two, no fortune will reunite these lovers. Unlike Ghismonda, Lisabetta does not deliver a public argument against family pride. She makes a private space of mourning, and even that is taken from her. Her story ends by showing what happens when the living are denied permission to remember the dead on their own terms.
