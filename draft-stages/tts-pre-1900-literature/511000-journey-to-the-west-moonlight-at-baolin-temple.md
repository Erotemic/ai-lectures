# Journey to the West: Moonlight at Baolin Temple

## The world's front door

After the fighting at Lotus Cave, the travelers were tired in a way that sleep could only partly repair. Xuanzang had been captured, threatened with death, and rescued from a household whose weapons belonged to Heaven itself. Every hill seemed to conceal another ordeal. The farther west he went, the farther the west appeared to be.

They rode through gentler country. The high mountain paths had given way to woods, and among the trees the light had grown warmer. For a little while nobody was screaming, fighting, or asking a demon king to put his master down.

Xuanzang looked across the hills and spoke of the distance. How many years had they already spent on the road? He remembered the Tang court, the departure ceremony, the emperor's instructions, and the faces of people he had promised to honor by bringing home the scriptures. The journey was not a small errand that could be abandoned when it became uncomfortable. Yet he could not tell where they were in relation to the holy mountain.

'Are we even halfway?' he asked.

Wukong laughed. They had hardly passed the front door of the world, he said. Why speak of halfway before one had properly entered the house?

Xuanzang asked what kind of house he meant.

The monkey opened his arms to the country around them. The earth was a dwelling so immense that mountains were its beams and ridges its rafters. The sky was the roof. The sun and moon were its windows. If one thought on that scale, a few years of walking had carried them scarcely over the threshold.

'In that case,' Bajie said, 'we ought to turn around before we get any farther inside. A house this large will have no shortage of places to get lost.'

Wukong told him that he could turn around if he wished, though he might find the road back no friendlier than the road west. Bajie said he had merely been suggesting an economical solution to their master's worry.

Sha Wujing kept his place by the baggage, listening while the others argued. When the talk had run its course, he pointed out that the daylight was fading. However far away India might be, they would need a roof before night.

At the end of a wooded stretch the road approached a large monastery. Its buildings shone in the last light of the day. There were gates, halls, towers, and an orderly series of courts extending behind the walls. The name above the entrance declared that it was Baolin Temple, the Precious Grove, established under imperial authority.

Xuanzang felt an immediate relief. These were Buddhist monks, devoted to worship and study. Surely they would not refuse a pilgrim making a journey on behalf of his emperor.

Wukong looked at the imposing gate and said it would be a comfortable place to stay. Bajie wanted to know whether the kitchens would be as impressive as the roof. Xuanzang asked his disciples to wait outside while he spoke to the monks alone. Their faces, he admitted, sometimes frightened people before any explanation could be offered.

Bajie was offended. He had done nothing at all. Not yet, Wukong agreed.

## A monastery that would not open

Xuanzang arranged his robe, took his staff, and entered the front court with proper greetings. The halls were broad and handsome. In one stood the Four Heavenly Kings. Elsewhere he saw images of Guanyin and assemblies of Buddhist saints. The place seemed to have everything a pilgrim would want in a night's lodging: space, quiet, learning, and people whose vows ought to make them hospitable.

A senior monk came forward. Xuanzang bowed and explained that he was a monk from the Great Tang, traveling to the west in search of Buddhist scriptures. He had come at evening with three disciples and a horse and wished only for a place to sleep.

The senior monk did not welcome him.

The abbey had received travelers before, he said, and kindness had been repaid badly. Some wandering monks had once claimed the hospitality of the community, then stayed on for years. Seven or eight years of demands, disorder, broken furnishings, and stolen provisions had taught the residents to be wary. The monastery had not been built to feed every stranger who came to its doors.

Xuanzang replied that a monastery was a home for those who followed the Buddha's teaching. Travelers who had no family nearby must depend on the compassion of those who did. He was not asking for property or privilege. One night under a roof was enough.

The other monk grew sharper. A beggar could use the words of religion as easily as a man of learning. Who could verify the visitor's story? Who could say he would not stay until the grain bins were empty?

Xuanzang tried to make him understand the scale of the pilgrimage. He spoke of his emperor's commission and the difficulty of crossing mountains and deserts. The senior monk heard only another account intended to win free lodging.

The Tang monk went quiet. He had expected a place of refuge and been made to feel like a nuisance. His eyes filled with tears. He returned slowly toward the gate.

Wukong saw his face before he spoke.

'No room?' the monkey asked.

Xuanzang shook his head. He did not wish for a quarrel. Perhaps they could find a stable or shelter among the trees. The evening was not yet completely dark.

Wukong went through the gate.

The monks who met him were less ready to argue than the senior abbot had been. His golden eyes and bristling face made them recoil. He announced that he was a disciple of the Tang monk and that the temple must find lodging for the whole company.

When someone protested, Wukong took the iron staff from his ear and enlarged it until it was thick as a great basin. He raised it above the court. The monks shrank back, imagining their doors and statues splintering beneath it.

Instead he brought it down on a stone lion beside the entrance.

The lion broke apart.

Pieces flew across the paving. Dust settled on the hems of the monks' robes. Wukong told them that the stone lion was only an example. If they preferred to offer a room, there would be no need to strike anything else.

The monastery had hundreds of resident monks and a great many chambers, yet Wukong demanded a thousand rooms for his master and three disciples. The number was impossible, and that was part of the threat. He would be satisfied with hospitality, but he would not be satisfied with being turned away again.

Monks hurried through the courts to call the others. Doors opened, sandals slapped against the stones, and figures in ceremonial robes came from the halls. Some had robes in poor repair; some carried the signs of hurried meals or interrupted prayers. They looked at the broken lion and decided that the safest course was to escort the travelers in with considerable respect.

Xuanzang entered behind Wukong, distressed at what his disciple had done.

'You must not injure anyone,' he whispered.

'I have injured a lion,' Wukong replied. 'A stone one. It will not complain.'

The senior monks bowed and offered the visitors lodging. The change of heart was complete enough that Bajie began making inquiries about the grain stores. There were many hungry people in the monastery, he observed, but there was also a very hungry pig. Perhaps they could set out a generous quantity of rice.

Wukong told him to ask for less than an entire storehouse. Bajie muttered that he had only wanted the meal to match the size of the establishment.

Food and rooms were prepared. Their horse was led to safety. Xuanzang settled for the night, troubled by the manner of his welcome but grateful to have his disciples beneath one roof.

## The moon above the courtyard

The monastery grew quiet. Lamps glowed in the corridors, and above the dark roofs the moon rose high. Xuanzang came out into the courtyard. The clouds had cleared, and light lay across the trees and paving. He stood looking at it, and his thoughts crossed the distance that his body could not.

He remembered the homeland he had left. The moon above the monastery was the same moon that shone over the Tang capital. Yet between this place and his home lay years of travel, dangers that sounded impossible when told aloud, and more miles than he could count. He spoke a verse of longing, measuring absence by the clear light overhead.

Wukong came to stand beside him. The monkey had a different way of looking at the sky. He spoke of the changing moon as teachers of inner cultivation spoke of it: the filling and emptying disk, the dark and light phases, the cycles of concealment and return. An adept who watched carefully, he said, could learn about his own heart from the course of the moon. A full moon did not remain full. A new moon did not remain dark. The task was to understand change without being dragged about by every change one saw.

Xuanzang listened. The explanation was strange to him in its alchemical language, but it was offered as consolation. He asked a question; Wukong followed the answer into further images of inner refinement, fire and water, and the discipline needed to keep a mind steady.

Sha Wujing approached and added his own thoughts. He had spent many years in exile on the Flowing-Sand River before Guanyin gave him a place on this pilgrimage. For him, the moonlit conversation called up the language of harmonizing what seemed opposed: water and fire, softness and strength, what descends and what rises. He spoke without the monkey's boasting, in the manner of someone who had learned to carry difficult things patiently.

Bajie had been listening long enough.

He contributed a verse of his own. He could meditate, he insisted, just as well as anyone. If a large belly was a sign of failure, that merely proved how little the others understood. The food he ate sustained the work. The sleep he took restored his energies. He could speak about refinement until sunrise, if they wanted, although it would be much wiser to go to bed.

Wukong laughed. Xuanzang could not help smiling. Even on a journey that exposed their failings, the four had begun to know one another's voices so well that they could tell what a brother would say before he opened his mouth.

Bajie went to sleep. Sha Wujing returned to his watch over the baggage. Wukong stayed only a little longer. Xuanzang looked up once more at the moon, then went inside and set a lamp beside his books.

His disciples had told him to rest, but the monastery had reminded him of his own early study. The scriptures he already knew were not the scriptures he had been sent to acquire. Yet he did not wish to forget the teaching that had brought him this far. He opened an old text and read the lines under his breath.

Wukong asked why he was studying the old books when his entire purpose was to bring new ones home.

'Learning does not remain in the mind if it is neglected,' Xuanzang replied. 'What I have received, I must preserve. What I have not received, I must seek.'

Wukong could find no simple objection. He left his master to the lamp.

Later, when the monastery was silent, the wind moved across the roofs and stirred the paper in the open book. Somewhere beyond the courtyard a sound rose and faded, as soft as a person sobbing into a sleeve.

Xuanzang lifted his head.

The night at Baolin Temple was not finished with him.
