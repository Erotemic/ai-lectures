# Journey to the West: The iron steamer and the roc's wings

## A sky too small for escape

The fight above Lion-Camel City had begun among clouds and weapons, but its outcome was settled by speed. Bajie was the first to falter. His great ears flapped about his face as the light failed. The blue lion drove him back, caught him, and had him tied in the palace. Sha Wujing tried to withdraw with his staff, but the elephant stretched out its trunk, seized him, and handed him to the waiting servants. Only Sun Wukong was still free.

For a time his iron staff seemed everywhere at once. He struck aside the lion's blade and the elephant's lance, kept the roc at arm's length, and watched for a passage through the three bodies closing around him. But a fight against one powerful enemy could be won by turning that enemy's strength aside. Three attackers, working together, left too little space. Wukong made his decision. He rose into the air and threw himself into a somersault cloud.

He had once believed that no creature could follow him. In a single leap his cloud could carry him a hundred and eight thousand li. In Heaven, whole armies had lost him against the breadth of the sky. But the great roc belonged to the air in a different way. A single spread of his wings covered an immense distance; a second carried him beyond the monkey's flight. He overtook Wukong, closed his talons around him, and returned with the last defender struggling in his grasp.

In the palace the three disciples were bound together. Xuanzang saw Wukong on the floor and came to him in tears. Usually, when a demon took the monk, his eldest disciple remained outside and could go to the sea, a mountain, or Heaven in search of help. What rescue could come now? Bajie complained that the rope cut into his thick arms. Wukong smiled faintly. Rope, even rope as broad as a cable, could not truly hold him. Wait until the monsters went to sleep, he said, and they would leave.

The demon kings were planning dinner. Their kitchens brought out a great iron steaming apparatus. The servants arranged the prisoners at different levels, with Xuanzang above the others, and piled fuel beneath. Wukong slipped his true body free and left a hair-transformed likeness in the bonds. From a cloud overhead he watched his master brought to the steamer. He knew the bodies of Bajie and Sha could endure more punishment than a human monk. Xuanzang would not survive the first proper rolling boil.

He called upon Ao Shun, Dragon King of the Northern Sea. The dragon came and asked how he might serve. Wukong did not ask for a flood or a mighty storm that would advertise his presence. He wanted the fire disabled without alerting the cooks. Ao Shun entered as a cold current of air beneath the iron vessel, keeping the water from reaching its deadly heat. The demons saw flames but did not get the meal they expected.

Wukong had one more precaution. He summoned sleep upon the attendants who were feeding the fire. Their heads fell forward. The three demon kings retired to their rooms, confident that by dawn the prisoners would be ready to season. When the palace was quiet, the monkey descended and looked into the steamer. For an instant he feared he had waited too long. Nobody called to him. Then a voice came through the dark. Xuanzang was alive, begging to be released.

## The escape that failed at the wall

The iron lid came away. Wukong lifted his master out, then Sha and Bajie. He gathered back the hair that had been tied in his place. Bajie was desperate to run immediately. His elder brother insisted on recovering the horse and luggage. The journey was not a game in which people could simply fly out of an enemy palace and call the danger over. The white horse carried a human master who could not rise on clouds like his disciples; the written travel credentials must pass through the kingdoms ahead. Without the baggage and passage documents, their pilgrimage would be crippled even if they escaped alive.

They crept into the stable. The dragon horse recognized Wukong. A strange handler would have met a kick and a warning neigh, but this was the man who had once held office in Heaven as superintendent of the horses and had cared for him along the journey. He stood still as the saddle was fitted. In the palace the brocade robe glimmered faintly with the light of its precious ornament, allowing Wukong to find the load where the servants had thrown it. Sha resumed the burden. Bajie took the horse's bridle. The party advanced toward the gate.

The city had been prepared for precisely this flight. Its great doors were locked and sealed. Bells and watchmen guarded each opening. The second gate was no better. If Xuanzang could fly, the disciples might simply have taken him into the clouds. He could not. Bajie proposed lifting him over a section of wall where the guards appeared thin. Wukong complained that their future reputations would be ruined if people heard that the sacred pilgrims had climbed a wall like burglars. Bajie, more concerned with survival than reputation, pointed out that a reputation was no use to a steamed monk.

They tried the wall. The alarm rose almost at once. The demon kings had awakened, found the empty steamer, and set the palace roaring with orders. Torches turned the night nearly to daylight. Xuanzang grew dizzy halfway over and fell. The lion seized him. The elephant recaptured Sha. The roc took Bajie. Wukong alone escaped by cloud. The careful plan, the cold dragon wind, the recovered horse and baggage—all had been undone at the last barrier.

Bajie, dragged back into the palace, accused his brother of rescuing them only far enough to put them in the same danger again. But the kings did not return immediately to their cooking. The roc proposed a crueller method. Hide Xuanzang in an iron chest inside the Brocade-Fragrance Pavilion. Spread the rumor that the monk has already been devoured. Wukong will hear it, lose hope, and go away. Once the monkey stops interfering, the kings can eat at leisure.

The eldest demon liked this plan. Xuanzang was shut inside the chest. Bajie and Sha were tied to pillars in different parts of the palace. Messengers and servants began repeating the story in every street. The monk from the East had been eaten without being cooked. There was nothing for his disciples to save.

## The news in every street

Wukong returned at dawn. Rage carried him first to the mountain cave, where he scattered its remaining demons with his staff. But the city still held his master, and this was no ordinary fight he could win by attacking a gate. He changed into a lesser demon and walked among the inhabitants, listening.

In one street he heard that Xuanzang had been eaten. In another, a cook gave the same account. Guards outside the palace spoke of the meal as a completed act. Wukong entered the royal precincts in the uniform of a palace servant and found Bajie tied to a pillar. He asked where their master was. Bajie said the demons had eaten him alive. The monkey began to weep. Bajie hurried to explain that he had not seen it; he had heard the same story as everyone else. Perhaps there was still a chance.

Wukong found Sha Wujing at the back of the hall. Sha gave the same answer. Tears ran down his face as he told his elder brother that Xuanzang was gone. Wukong could no longer treat the rumor as a clever trap or an uncertain report. Two trusted companions believed it; the whole city repeated it. He left them tied where they were and flew out beyond the walls to grieve alone.

He had once been imprisoned beneath a mountain for defying Heaven. The human monk had released him. He had then accepted a strange obedience: not simply to fight but to accompany, restrain himself, and protect a man whose compassion sometimes endangered them both. It had been a long and difficult apprenticeship. Now, Wukong thought, he had failed at the only obligation that gave all those battles meaning.

His grief turned into anger. If the scriptures were meant to benefit the people of the Eastern Lands, why had the Buddha not simply sent them there? Why insist that vulnerable pilgrims cross so many mountains and endure so much death? Wukong decided to go to the Buddha's own mountain and demand an answer. If the scriptures could still be carried east, let the Buddha arrange it. If not, let him remove the tightening circlet from Wukong's head, release him from his vow, and send him home to Flower-Fruit Mountain.

At Spirit Mountain the gate guardians rebuked him for coming in such disorder. The lecture hall received him differently. The Buddha summoned him, and Wukong knelt, weeping, to report the fall of Lion-Camel City and the death he believed had overtaken Xuanzang.

## Kinship among the birds

The Buddha knew the three monsters. The lion and elephant were the mounts of Manjushri and Samantabhadra. He sent for the two bodhisattvas. The roc, however, required an explanation. According to the novel's mythic genealogy, the phoenix had given rise to two great birds, the peacock and the golden-winged roc. In an earlier age the peacock had swallowed the Buddha, who emerged by piercing its back; the assembled Buddhas prevented him from killing it and gave it the title Peacock Wisdom King, honored as a Buddha Mother. The roc belonged to the same family. Wukong calculated the relationship with mischievous delight: by that account, the Buddha was rather like the roc's nephew.

This was not the time for a family joke, but Wukong could not resist one. He was also angry that creatures with so much sacred kinship had been allowed to destroy a kingdom. The Buddha did not deny the peril. He said that he himself would have to go to retrieve the roc. Manjushri and Samantabhadra arrived, and the company descended through the clouds toward the demon city.

Wukong went down first, under orders to fight but not to win. He challenged the kings, exchanged a few blows, and pretended to flee. The three demons followed him straight into the Buddha's radiance. The great gathering of bodhisattvas, arhats, and sacred guardians closed around them. The lion and elephant saw their masters and were compelled to take their animal forms. The bodhisattvas mounted them and brought them back to obedience.

The roc still resisted. He wheeled upward, trying to tear Wukong away from the Buddha's light. The Buddha used a manifestation to lure him within reach and fixed him there, denying his wings the sky. The bird protested that life among fasting holy beings would offer none of the pleasures of his man-eating kingdom. The Buddha bound him to service. The roc could no longer flee to his former table.

Wukong bowed in relief, then remembered the reason he had come. All this divine intervention was too late to bring back Xuanzang. The roc, furious at being captured, snapped that the monk had never been eaten. He had been shut in a chest in the Brocade-Fragrance Pavilion all along. The lie had done precisely what the demon wanted: it had broken Wukong's heart.

## The chest in the pavilion

Wukong fell back into Lion-Camel City. Without its rulers, the former army had dissolved. Guards and ministers had fled; the palace seemed suddenly emptied of the power that had occupied it. He cut Bajie and Sha loose, found the baggage and white horse, and led them to the pavilion. An iron chest stood inside. From within came a faint sound of weeping.

Sha struck the lock with his staff. The lid opened. Xuanzang looked up at the faces he had feared never to see again. He was alive, frightened and hungry, but not harmed beyond recovery. Wukong explained the battle, the cold wind beneath the steamer, the failed flight over the wall, the terrible false rumor, and the gathering of the Buddha's company. He could hardly recount his own tears without embarrassment; Bajie had plenty to say about the monkey's earlier certainty that the city was conquered. For once the talk mattered less than the sight of everyone together.

They found rice and food in the deserted palace, ate, prepared the horse, and left by the western road. The people who had once lived in Lion-Camel City were not restored by the surrender of its rulers. The old kingdom remained a place of destruction. What had been saved was the pilgrimage, and the possibility that the four travelers might carry it past the place where their enemies had seemed most powerful.

Wukong walked ahead again. The journey had brought him before the Buddha not in triumph, but in helpless grief, and he had learned that even his most certain news could be a trap. Behind him Xuanzang rode west, alive, the iron chest abandoned in a palace that would never again be mistaken for a house of hospitality.
