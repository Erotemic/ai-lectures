# The Hundred Tales Keep Changing

Boccaccio's hundred tales were never confined to the household of their imaginary narrators. Readers copied them, translated them, rearranged their lessons, and made new works from their plots. The process did not always preserve the frame, the tone, or even the ethical emphasis of a particular story. Yet the transformations are part of the *Decameron*'s afterlife. A work with many voices and many forms can lend individual inventions to writers with very different purposes.

Consider Day Three, Tale Nine, the story of Giletta, or Gillette, of Narbonne. She cures a king and asks to marry Beltramo, who resists and imposes conditions for reconciliation. Through persistence and a sexual impersonation, she eventually satisfies those conditions. Shakespeare's *All's Well That Ends Well* develops its principal action from this story. The Folger Shakespeare Library identifies William Painter's *Palace of Pleasure*, an English adaptation of Boccaccio, as a major channel for Shakespeare's access. The resulting play is not a mere staged transcript. Its expanded characters, theatrical situations, and difficult conclusion create new questions about fidelity, social position, and the difference between achieving marriage and being loved.

Day Two, Tale Nine gives another example: a wager on a wife's fidelity, a deceiver's fabricated proof, and the grave consequences of a husband's belief. Shakespeare's *Cymbeline* develops related material. The Folger's source scholarship includes Boccaccio among the relevant works while distinguishing sources from other similar narratives and possible intermediaries. That distinction is important. A resemblance can be strong evidence of a relationship, but it does not automatically tell us which exact book or language a dramatist read.

The wider history of reception includes editions produced for readers with very different expectations about proper language and behavior. The *Decameron*'s erotic jokes and criticism of religious figures have repeatedly generated enthusiasm as well as objections. Some readers find in the collection a defense of human pleasure against oppressive convention; others emphasize its participation in religious and moral traditions, or its treatment of women as both speaking subjects and objects of male control. The book is capable of sustaining these disagreements because it puts genuinely conflicting material into conversation rather than giving every story the same verdict.

The experience of reading a full hundred tales is different from reading the ten most famous. An anthology can make Boccaccio seem to write only tricks, only romances, or only daring female characters. The complete work has short verbal repartees, long adventures, cruel family decisions, religious arguments, impossible marvels, and stories whose endings resist easy praise. A reader who reaches Griselda after a hundred variations on wit, fortune, love, and authority has a different preparation from someone who meets her as a detached moral example.

The *Decameron* has also become a cultural image of the plague. Whenever societies confront widespread illness, a group of people retreating to tell stories offers an immediately recognizable symbol. But treating the book as a prescription to withdraw into entertainment misses the opening's account of suffering and the ending's return to the city. The frame does not abolish the world; it suspends certain pressures in order to make reflection and companionship possible. Its privilege is part of the story, not a reason to forget the people who cannot escape.

Boccaccio's authorial epilogue gives readers a way to approach the mixture. He anticipates critics who object to indecency, length, and the stories' choice of topics. He argues that readers may select what benefits them, refuse what displeases them, and judge material in light of its setting. We should not turn that defense into the claim that stories have no moral consequences. An active reader is not a passive consumer who can never be misled. Selection brings responsibility: to understand how a story persuades and to notice whom its pleasure requires us not to see.

There is another form of choice involved in listening. A narration can make a difficult tale intelligible, but it inevitably selects details and supplies a voice. A scholarly edition offers different kinds of fidelity. The original Italian contains wordplay that can be diminished or transformed in translation; manuscripts preserve evidence of the author's revisions and later readers' interventions. If a claim turns on the precise language of the work, the way forward is to consult that evidence, not to treat a summary as a quotation from Boccaccio himself.

The durable achievement of the *Decameron* is neither that it invented every kind of short fiction nor that it settled the ethics of love, religion, commerce, or power. It combined inherited forms with an extraordinary design for people to hear different tales in sequence. Its ten storytellers live by rules, disagree by implication, entertain one another, and eventually go home. Their author gives the reader a comparable task: to compare stories, revise judgments, enjoy skill without mistaking it for goodness, and recognize generosity without surrendering the ability to question authority.

When the final tale ends, the city is still there. The plague's devastation has not been erased. A woman can remain constrained by family power; a merchant can still be deceived; a generous gesture can still shelter a claim of ownership. But a reader has heard those arrangements described from many angles, with reasons to argue about them. Boccaccio's book remains available to later writers because it does not reduce human conduct to a single answer. Its lasting gift is a crowded space in which telling and judging stories become forms of thought.
