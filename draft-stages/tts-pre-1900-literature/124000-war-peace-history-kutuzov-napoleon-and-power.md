# War and Peace — Napoleon, Kutuzov, and the Problem of Historical Causes

War and Peace is not merely set amid historical events. It actively disputes the way historical events are explained. Tolstoy is skeptical of narratives in which Napoleon wills an invasion and millions of people therefore move, or in which Kutuzov makes one wise decision and an entire national fate follows. He asks what it would mean to explain the movement of armies without treating command as a magical force. The novel's historical essays can feel like interruptions until we realize that this dispute has already been enacted by nearly every important scene.

At Austerlitz, officers arrive with a plan so intricate that its presentation becomes an event in its own right. A plan gives the impression of an all-seeing intelligence. The ensuing battle supplies confusion, delay, misheard instructions, and people acting on assumptions they cannot verify. Tolstoy is not claiming that plans never matter. He is arguing that a plan cannot encompass the reactions of the people and the conditions through which it must operate.

Napoleon is repeatedly shown issuing commands after events have acquired a momentum beyond his effective reach. That portrayal is a deliberate artistic and philosophical intervention. It disputes the image of the sovereign military genius as a singular origin of history. But the intervention must not be treated as a neutral record of Napoleon's actual capacities. Historians can study administrative resources, orders, communications, contingencies, and strategic decisions in greater detail than Tolstoy's dramatic argument allows. The novel asks a sharp question about explanations; it does not end historical research.

Kutuzov is the counterfigure. He often seems passive to characters who crave decisive action. Tolstoy values his ability to recognize a collective movement already under way and to resist the vanity of controlling it by force of personality. After Borodino, the choice to yield Moscow becomes an example. Tolstoy invests Kutuzov with sympathy because he can accept a painful necessity without claiming to have manufactured the nation's courage. This should not be reduced to the proposition that old men are wise and young men rash. The distinction is between sensitivity to circumstances and worship of one's own intention.

Pierre supplies a smaller experiment. He resolves to kill Napoleon, believing that a single act by a single man could change the direction of history. Yet his actual experiences in Moscow are saving a child, helping a woman, being captured, and learning from prisoners. The contrast between imagined world-historical agency and concrete ethical action is precise. A grand intention may be morally dramatic while practically empty; an action whose effects are local may possess the substance that the grand intention lacks.

Tolstoy often compares history to phenomena that require attention to extremely many elements. His later philosophical discussion presses toward a view in which collective action cannot be reduced to the simple sum of famous intentions. It would be a mistake to translate that insight mechanically into modern mathematical models as though the novel supplied equations that have been scientifically validated. Its mathematical language is part of an argument about scale and explanation. It invites thought, not the uncritical adoption of a new technical theory.

We can also question Tolstoy from within the world he depicts. His serfs do not have the same latitude as landlords. Generals and emperors can command resources and impose harms no single soldier can. A theory that corrects the exaggeration of great men's intentions might still underestimate structured inequalities of power. Recognizing that no ruler can determine every outcome does not imply that all participants wield equal influence. Tolstoy's material often makes this distinction clearer than his most sweeping formulations.

A close reader should compare an authoritative passage in the second epilogue with a scene of people laboring on the ground. Ask what the scene adds to the abstract proposition: uncertainty, resistance, physical conditions, local incentives, courage, terror. Then ask what the abstract proposition makes newly visible in the scene. The best way to read Tolstoy's philosophy is neither to skip it as an unfortunate appendage nor to worship it as proven history. It is an ambitious attempt to make the scale of lived experience count in an account of collective change.
