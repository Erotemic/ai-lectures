# Journey to the West: The poets of the thorn ridge

## A road that would not let anyone through

The people of Jisai did not want their benefactors to leave. The king offered gold, which Xuanzang refused; instead he supplied fresh clothes, shoes, food, and the necessary seal upon the pilgrims' travel papers. The monks of the restored Dragon-Taming Monastery followed them out of the city. Some begged to join the company. Some offered to become their servants. Even after the king and his officials turned back, the monks continued walking beside the four travelers, speaking of gratitude and the wish to reach the western land themselves.

Wukong finally had to make them stop. He plucked a handful of hairs, blew upon them, and sent striped tigers leaping across the road. The monks scattered in terror. When the pilgrims were safely away, Wukong recalled his hairs, and the monks wept at a distance, believing that their chance of going west had gone with them. The animals had been imaginary, but the disappointment was real.

Winter yielded to spring. The travelers had passed through courts, mountain caves, and a dragon's underwater palace. Now they found a different kind of obstacle. Ahead stretched an enormous green ridge, all thorns and tangled creepers. There was a path beneath the growth, but the branches met above it like the teeth of a trap. Even a person crawling close to the ground would have been scratched. A man on horseback could not have entered at all.

Xuanzang looked at the narrow opening and asked how the white horse was supposed to pass. Wukong sprang into the sky to survey the obstacle. He returned with discouraging news. The thickets seemed to run for hundreds of miles, as far as his eyes could reach. Sha Wujing proposed setting the whole ridge alight. Bajie objected that they had come in the wrong season. In dry autumn, he said, a fire might run through dead brush. In spring, green growth full of moisture was another matter; and even if it burned, a huge fire would be dangerous to anything beyond it.

Then Bajie smiled. Here, he said, was a task fit for him. He uttered his spell and grew tall enough to look over the treetops. He made the handle of his nine-toothed rake longer still, planted his feet, and pulled the branches aside as a farmer clears a bed of weeds. He opened a passage wide enough for a horse. The first miles were slow. The rack of thorns kept catching and closing behind him; the rake had to rise, scoop, drag, and rise again. But he went on. Xuanzang rode in his wake; Sha followed with the luggage; Wukong used his staff to hold the edges of the new path apart.

By evening they reached a stone marker. It named the place Thorn Ridge and said that its thorny stretch ran for eight hundred li, though an ancient road lay underneath. Bajie, delighted by a task that showed off his strength, proposed adding another inscription: from this day onward, Pig Bajie had cut through the way, and the road to the west would be open. The master urged him to stop and rest. Bajie, who usually wanted a meal and a bed at the earliest opportunity, argued for continuing while there was light and courage enough to do the work.

He labored through the night and into another day. The companions heard birds and wind among the boughs, but no human voice. At last they reached a clearing and a weathered temple standing among pine, bamboo, and flowers. To Xuanzang it looked like a place where monks might once have sought solitude. To Wukong it looked like a place where a traveler should be cautious.

## The invitation

While they stood near the ruined shrine, a gray-haired man appeared with a staff and plain clothes. A servant followed him: red-bodied, green-faced, tusked, and carrying a tray of steamed cakes. The old man bowed and said he was the local earth god. There were no villages nearby, he explained, but he wished to offer food to holy travelers crossing his territory. Bajie was ready to accept on the spot.

Wukong watched the two strangers and reached for his staff. He had seen earth gods before. The polite old man and his fierce servant did not satisfy him. He called out that the stranger was lying and moved to strike. Before his staff could land, the old man turned into a gust of dark wind. The wind lifted Xuanzang from the ground, swept him past the bushes, and vanished with him.

For a moment the three disciples stared at one another. The white horse tossed its head. Wukong had anticipated a trick and still had not prevented it. Now he had no scent to follow, no visible opponent, and a maze of thorns that hid every possible path. He, Bajie, and Sha spread out with the horse and luggage, calling for their master.

Xuanzang, meanwhile, found himself set down beside a stone dwelling softened by moonlight. His captor supported him by the arm and spoke with the courtesy of a host. He was not, he said, an evil spirit planning to eat the monk. He was known as Eighteen-Gong, or the Venerable of the Eighteen, and had brought Xuanzang to meet friends. The night was clear; the air was cool; they wished to exchange poems and talk about the path to enlightenment. There was no cause for alarm.

The master did not believe that a kidnapping could become an invitation simply because the kidnapper had good manners. But the place was quiet, no weapon was raised, and he had no way to return. Three further elderly gentlemen emerged. One was stern and upright, another green and vigorous, and the third was lean, poised, and graceful. They named themselves Solitary-Upright, Sky-Towering, and Cloud-Brushing. With Eighteen-Gong, whose other name was Stout-Joint, they presented themselves as a company of ancient recluses.

They asked the age of the monk they had captured. Xuanzang replied in verse, recalling the danger at his birth, the river that had carried him away, his rescue at the monastery, his long study, and the imperial charge that had sent him to the west. They answered with verses about their own thousand-year lives and their endurance through frost, wind, and rain. Their words were beautiful. Their eyes held the pleasure of people who had waited a very long time for a visitor capable of answering them.

## The pleasure and peril of learned company

The old men invited Xuanzang to teach them something of Buddhist practice. He spoke of cleansing the mind, allowing attachments to fall away, and recognizing wisdom that could not be reached by proud recitation alone. They listened with real interest. Then Cloud-Brushing offered a challenge. Why travel thousands of miles in search of teachings born elsewhere, he asked, when a person might cultivate the Way here among mountains and living trees? What good was a doctrine if it became a set of impressive words, carried farther than the speaker understood?

The monk recognized the force of the question. He had passed enough miles to know that a pilgrim could mistake movement for progress. Yet he also knew why he had undertaken the road. The scriptures were not a fashionable object to bring back for praise. His master in China had sent him to gather a teaching others needed. The old men had the luxury of dwelling in one place for a thousand years. He did not.

That difference might have produced a serious debate. Instead Sky-Towering laughed gently and proposed a poetry gathering. The elders led their guest into a stone chamber marked Wood-Immortal Hermitage. A red-skinned attendant brought fragrant tea and cakes made from the root called poria. Xuanzang hesitated over the food, inspected the room, then took a little when he saw his hosts eat. Under the lamplight the walls looked as clean and clear as a mountain spring.

The monk, who usually worried about where the road would lead next, relaxed. He offered a line comparing the meditative mind to a moon free of dust. Eighteen-Gong answered with a line about fresh poetic inspiration. Each elder contributed to the growing poem. They passed a final word from one couplet to the next, made allusions to ancient poets, and praised the balance of an image or the unexpected turn of a phrase. Xuanzang tried to retreat into modesty, but the elders refused to let him begin a poem without finishing it. He completed the verse, and they pressed him for another.

There was kindness in the exchange, or something that imitated kindness convincingly. For once, the monk was valued not as miraculous meat, not as a prize for a monster, and not as a foreign envoy whose papers needed stamping. His hosts admired his language. They admired the books he carried in his head. As the hours passed, the stolen night began to seem like a holiday from fear.

Then an exquisite woman entered with two attendants bearing red lanterns. She held a flowering sprig of apricot and was introduced as Apricot Immortal. She had heard that a learned guest was composing verse and asked to participate. Tea was poured again. The elders recited Xuanzang's poems for her, and she responded with a graceful composition of her own, describing blossoms, fragrance, the passing seasons, and the subtle sadness of ripening fruit.

The company admired her work. She in turn praised the monk. She came closer, addressed him more intimately, and suggested that such a beautiful night should not be wasted on talk alone. Xuanzang drew back. The elders encouraged her. One proposed that a marriage would be entirely proper if reputable friends acted as matchmakers. The red-bodied servant grew impatient with the monk's reluctance and hinted that a refusal might be answered with force.

The room had not changed. The cup, the moonlight, the polished phrases were all still there. Yet the invitation had become a trap. Xuanzang rose and protested that they had taken him against his will and were now using their poetry to press him into breaking his vows. If their conversation was truly about wisdom, how could it end in coercion? He refused. He refused again when they argued that his life was short and pleasure fleeting. He refused when the woman offered to wipe his tears and asked him to follow her.

Through the remainder of the night the hosts alternately pleaded, praised, and restrained him. Xuanzang called for his disciples. He was no longer interested in proving that he could debate four ancient sages. He wanted to get out of the hermitage.

## What stood in the clearing

At dawn a shout came from beyond the trees: "Master! Where are you?" The disciples had searched without rest, crossed the rest of Thorn Ridge, and heard his voice. Xuanzang called back. The elders, the servants, and Apricot Immortal disappeared before Wukong reached the stone chamber.

The monk recounted the night. He did not conceal that he had enjoyed the discussion. These people had exchanged clever and sometimes searching poems; one had debated religious doctrine with him as a serious equal. But at the end, he said, they had tried to force him to take Apricot Immortal as a wife. His rejection had brought threats.

Wukong asked their names and inspected the clearing. The hermitage stood among old trees. There was a pine, a cypress, a juniper, a bamboo, a red maple, and an ancient apricot, with smaller flowering trees nearby. The Great Sage identified the poets by their bodies. Eighteen-Gong was the pine, Solitary-Upright the cypress, Sky-Towering the juniper, Cloud-Brushing the bamboo. The red servant was the maple. The elegant visitor was the apricot. Her lantern bearers were among the flowering trees. Their fine human faces had been another form of the forest.

Bajie did not stop to ask whether they could be persuaded to behave better. He raised his rake and uprooted the smaller trees. Red liquid ran from the roots. Xuanzang seized his arm and begged him not to kill them. They had behaved badly, he said, but they had not injured him. Why not leave them and take the road?

Wukong was not persuaded. A spirit that could abduct a monk with a wind and then pretend to be a gentleman might grow into a greater danger to the next traveler. Bajie struck again. The pine, cypress, juniper, and bamboo fell with the rest. The poets' thousand-year lives ended under the teeth of the rake that had opened the road to them.

The pilgrims continued west. Behind them the clearing was no longer a hermitage full of exquisite conversation. It was a place of cut trunks and bleeding roots. Xuanzang had been wrong to trust the invitation. He was not certain that destroying every tree had been the only answer. The story leaves those two facts beside each other. In a book full of demons who devour people, these spirits had wanted poems, company, and finally the monk's consent to a marriage he would not accept. Their end came with the same rough force that had pushed an eight-hundred-li path through the thorns.
