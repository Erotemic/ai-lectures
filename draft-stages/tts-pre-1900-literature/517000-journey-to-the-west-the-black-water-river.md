# Journey to the West: The Black Water River

## A child takes the road south

At Mount Hao, Red Boy lay on the ground beneath the spell of the golden rings. He had tried disguises, spear work, and the Samadhi True Fire. He had pretended to accept Guanyin's teaching, only to seize his spear once he was released. Now the five rings around his head and limbs tightened whenever the bodhisattva recited their charm.

At last she stopped. Red Boy pulled at the metal, but the rings had become part of him. No amount of struggling would remove them. Wukong watched him with the impatience of someone who knew what it meant to be disciplined by a headband. He could make jokes about the child's new jewelry, but he could not claim ignorance of the pain.

Red Boy made one more rush toward his enemies. Guanyin dipped her willow branch in the pure water and flicked a drop toward him. His hands came together at his breast and could not be drawn apart. The gesture of reverence that he had refused became the posture in which he would learn obedience. The child bowed.

Guanyin called him by his new name, Good Wealth Boy. She explained that he would go to her mountain under instruction. He must make a bow at each stage of the journey, all the way to Mount Luojia. This was not a punishment completed in a moment. The boy would have to cross the distance slowly, through repeated acts of submission.

She gathered her conjured sea back into the vase until the mountain ground lay dry again. Then she turned to Wukong. The demon had been subdued, she said. He must go and recover his master immediately; there was no time to stand watching a young convert walk away.

Wukong bowed in thanks and hurried to the pines where Sha Wujing waited beside the white horse and baggage. Sha had spent too much time imagining what might be happening at the cave. He was relieved to see Wukong returning in haste, alive and cheerful, with word that Guanyin had taken Red Boy into her service.

The two disciples crossed the stone bridge and forced their way into Fire Cloud Cave. The remaining demons had no protector now. Wukong and Sha Wujing scattered them, took down the leather bag from its beam, and untied the mouth. Out came Bajie, furious and delighted to be free, demanding to know where he could find Red Boy so that he might repay the humiliation with his rake.

'First find our master,' Wukong said.

They reached the rear courtyard. Xuanzang was still bound, stripped of his clothing, and waiting in fear for the steamer that had been promised him. Sha Wujing undid the ropes while Wukong found the monk's garments. The three disciples knelt before him and apologized for the suffering he had endured.

Xuanzang asked what had happened. When Wukong told him of Guanyin's intervention, the monk bowed toward the south in gratitude. The rescue had cost Guanyin much trouble, he said.

Wukong replied that she had gained a disciple. If anyone had profited from the matter, it was Guanyin. Bajie, listening to this calculation, seemed inclined to agree, provided someone would feed him before the party traveled again.

They found the stores of food in the cave, prepared a meal, recovered useful supplies, and gathered their belongings. Then, with Xuanzang safely mounted, they left Mount Hao behind.

## The monk hears a river

More than a month passed. The road led them over ridges and through narrow defiles until Xuanzang heard a deep, continuous sound ahead. He drew rein.

'What is that noise?'

Wukong listened. It was water, nothing more. Yet the monk's alarm irritated him. Xuanzang had learned the Heart Sutra from the master at Crow's Nest Mountain and could recite its teaching. Why was his mind still so easily seized by each noise or shadow on the road? Wukong reminded him of the senses and the habits that tied a person to fear: the eye demanding forms, the ear seizing sounds, the tongue craving taste, the mind running ahead into imagined dangers.

Xuanzang replied quietly that he remembered the words. He remembered all of them. What he could not banish was the longing to complete the mission and return home. The road had been so long that even the sound of water made him wonder whether another year of hardship lay between him and India.

Wukong laughed, then softened his response. A task came to completion through effort. There was no benefit in measuring each footstep against an imagined end.

Bajie was less encouraging. At this rate, with a new monster in every mountain, they might walk for a thousand years. Sha Wujing told him to keep his strength for the luggage. It was more useful than calculating how many years remained.

They came out of the trees and saw the river.

The water was black from shore to shore. Not blue-dark as a deep lake can be, but opaque as ink, with foaming streaks like coal dust. The banks were green, the sky was clear, and the current looked as though the earth itself had poured a vast basin of soot between the travelers and their road.

Bajie suggested that someone upstream had emptied a dye vat. Sha Wujing thought of an enormous inkstone being washed. Wukong ignored the jokes and asked how they might cross.

Bajie and Sha Wujing could ride the clouds or work through the water; Wukong could leap the distance with a turn of his heels. None of them could simply carry Xuanzang across in flight. A mortal body did not become light merely because a powerful disciple held it. Bajie protested that, with such a passenger, he would hardly rise above the ground before dropping back into the river.

Then a small boat appeared upstream. Its owner had cut it from a single tree trunk, leaving room for only two passengers at a time. The boatman said he did not operate a ferry, but Xuanzang's disciples appealed to him for help. They were travelers on a sacred errand, they said, and would be grateful for a crossing.

The man agreed. Bajie suggested that he and Xuanzang go first; the other disciples could wait with the baggage and horse and take a later passage. This gave Bajie the comfortable place in the boat and left the heavy luggage to someone else.

Wukong allowed it. He watched the little craft push into the black current.

Halfway across, wind burst up from the surface. The water folded over itself; the boat vanished in a whirl of spray. When the river settled, no boat, monk, or pig could be seen.

## The owner of a stolen palace

Sha Wujing plunged into the river to look for them. He could move beneath water with ease, an ability learned in his former life, and he followed the disturbance down into a complex of buildings hidden below the current. Over the gate stood a name claiming the place as the palace of the Black Water River God.

Inside, a monster was instructing his servants to prepare a steamer for two prisoners. He expected to serve their flesh to an honored guest. Sha Wujing struck the gate with his staff and demanded the return of Xuanzang and Bajie.

The monster came out in armor, carrying a heavy segmented whip. His face and movements had something of the great water reptiles about them. He was a tuo-dragon, an alligator-like spirit, accustomed to battles beneath the surface.

They fought for thirty rounds. Sha Wujing held his ground. The creature could not drive him off, but neither could he force an entry. At last Sha pretended to lose his balance and fled toward the surface, hoping to draw the monster within reach of Wukong's iron staff. The creature would not follow. He had a feast to arrange and an invitation to send.

Sha returned to the bank, angry at the failed lure. He told Wukong what he had seen and heard. A water monster held both prisoners and intended to feed them to his uncle.

An old man appeared downstream, kneeling in the mud. He declared himself the true god of the Black Water River. He had been robbed of his home. The monster had arrived from the Western Sea, defeated the river god, driven him from his palace, and taken the place as his own. The river god had sought justice from the Dragon King of the Western Sea, but the dragon king was the monster's uncle. The petition had been refused. The dispossessed god was too minor to secure a hearing at the higher courts of Heaven.

Wukong's temper flared. The monster had not merely captured two members of the pilgrimage. He had occupied a public office by force and relied upon family connections to protect him. This was the sort of heavenly or earthly administration that the Monkey King had always found intolerable.

He told the river god to remain with Sha Wujing and flew toward the Western Sea.

Underwater, on the way to the dragon palace, he met a black fish hurrying along with a golden message case. Wukong struck the messenger dead and opened the letter. It was an invitation to the dragon king: a nephew named Tuo Jie had captured two extraordinary eastern travelers, including the Tang monk, and wished to offer their flesh at a banquet in honor of his elder's birthday.

Wukong took the letter to the dragon king.

## Family obligations

Ao Shun, Dragon King of the Western Sea, received the Monkey King respectfully. Their acquaintance was old, and neither had forgotten how easily Wukong could overturn the peace of a palace. The dragon king offered tea. Wukong replied that Ao Shun had apparently been invited to drink something considerably worse at a feast.

He produced the letter.

Ao Shun read it and fell to his knees. The monster was the ninth son of his sister. That sister had married the dragon king whose fate had intersected the pilgrimage near its beginning: a ruler punished by Heaven for disobeying a decree about rainfall, beheaded by the mortal official Wei Zheng while the official slept. After the death, Ao Shun had taken responsibility for the widow and her children. His sister later died, and her youngest son had been allowed to live at the Black Water River until a more suitable office could be found for him.

The arrangement, Ao Shun admitted, had gone terribly wrong.

Wukong asked about the other children. Ao Shun named rivers, bells, temple roofs, and heavenly structures where the elder sons now served. Their younger brother, left without a proper appointment, had chosen to become a tyrant. Wukong listened with the impatience of a man who understood that an official explanation could be accurate and still fail to answer for a crime.

Ao Shun offered to dispatch his own son, Prince Mo'ang, with sea troops to arrest the offender. Wukong could punish him if he wished; the dragon family would accept responsibility for returning the prisoners.

Wukong agreed to let the prince try. He traveled with the soldiers through the water and emerged on the riverbank to wait with Sha Wujing and the displaced river god.

Prince Mo'ang approached the stolen palace under banners bright with scales and shells. Tuo Jie came out thinking his cousin had arrived for the banquet. He was puzzled by the soldiers. Mo'ang demanded to know whom he had captured. The young monster boasted about the rare flesh he had taken and the pig he would cook alongside the monk.

Mo'ang told him who served that monk: the Great Sage Equal to Heaven, whose anger had already been carried to the Western Sea. Tuo Jie had embroiled the whole family in a crime.

The monster protested. Were they not cousins? Shouldn't kinship matter more than the threats of a monkey from a distant land? He would not surrender a meal merely because his cousin was frightened.

Mo'ang invited him to settle the disagreement by combat.

The waters shook under the fighting. River troops and sea troops clashed among the weeds and temple roofs. Tuo Jie struck fiercely with his whip, but the prince was a disciplined fighter. He feigned an opening, drew his cousin forward, struck his arm, and knocked him from his footing. Soldiers rushed him, bound his hands, and fastened an iron chain through the bones of his shoulders.

Mo'ang brought the captive to the shore before Wukong. The monster begged for mercy and offered to fetch Xuanzang himself if he were unbound. The prince warned that he would escape as soon as the ropes were loosened.

Sha Wujing went instead. With the restored river god he descended into the captured palace, found Xuanzang and Bajie stripped and tied, and freed them. Each rescuer carried one prisoner back to the bank.

Bajie saw the chained monster and ran at him with his rake. He had come very close to being cooked twice in a short span of the journey and wished to settle matters in his own way. Wukong restrained him. The dragon family had provided the rescue; the culprit would be taken home to answer for his conduct. Wukong spared his life but did not call him innocent.

Prince Mo'ang led his cousin away under guard. The river god thanked the pilgrims for restoring his home. Xuanzang mounted the white horse, then looked across the black current again.

They were still on the eastern bank. How could they cross without falling into another trap?

The river god promised a safe passage. He stopped the upstream flow, drew the lower water away, and opened a dry road across the riverbed. Bajie led the horse, Sha Wujing carried the baggage, and Wukong walked close beside his master. With the black water held on either side, they crossed to the west.

Behind them the river resumed its course. Ahead lay another country, and somewhere beyond it the road to the scriptures.
