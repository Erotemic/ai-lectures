# The City Behind the Stories

An old social picture divides medieval people into three groups: those who pray, those who fight, and those who work the land. In Boccaccio's Florence that picture could no longer describe all the people who mattered. A banker might influence government. A craftsman could belong to a politically important guild. A merchant who owned no ancestral title could send goods across the sea and live in a house that impressed the nobility. The *Decameron* continually measures older forms of honor against such newer urban sources of power.

Florence was not a modern democratic city. Status, citizenship, occupation, gender, family ties, and wealth all affected a person's options. Guild organization helped structure political life, and access to office was unequal. The story world includes nobility and royal courts, but also merchants, artisans, servants, monks, lawyers, students, artists, and people living through petty transactions. The title of a tale does not tell you where its moral center will lie. A baker can have more dignity than a grand customer; a wealthy suitor can behave less generously than a poor laborer.

Consider Cisti the baker in Day Six, Tale Two. His excellent wine attracts the attention of Geri Spina and his companions. Cisti does not simply thrust himself into their company. He must negotiate the difference between possessing something worthy of admiration and being permitted to offer it to a man of higher standing. His tact allows Geri to recognize the baker's worth without pretending that social ranks have vanished. The episode makes politeness a kind of social intelligence. It also reveals why that intelligence is necessary: dignity does not automatically carry the same permissions for everyone.

Now place beside it Andreuccio of Perugia in Day Two, Tale Five. A young horse trader comes to Naples with money and limited knowledge of local networks. He is deceived, stripped of his confidence, and drawn into the dangers of the city's night. The story is an education in urban vulnerability. Money lets him travel and trade, but it also makes him a target. To survive he must learn a practical intelligence not supplied by his possession of capital. Naples, as Boccaccio knew it, is not merely an exotic stage; it is a system of houses, streets, official power, and criminal opportunity.

Lisabetta's story in Day Four, Tale Five gives a darker view of merchant family interests. Her brothers object to her attachment to Lorenzo, a young man in their service. Their calculations about family respectability become an act of murder. The brothers' concern with status is not abstract. In a world where marriage can join or damage commercial alliances, a sister's choice appears to them as a threat to the family enterprise. The story allows the listener to experience what such reasoning costs a person who is treated as part of the household's collective property.

Service is everywhere, and it should not become invisible merely because the narrators are well-off. The young company who leave Florence depend on cooks and attendants. Husbands and wives in the tales rely on maids and messengers to make secrets possible. Someone must open the gate, carry a note, prepare a bed, or assist a disguise. In stories of aristocratic generosity, gifts are sometimes impressive because a wealthy character can command resources acquired through the labor of others. The social world of the *Decameron* is never composed entirely of people with equal freedom to act.

The importance of trade does not mean that Boccaccio simply praises merchants. Salabaetto's dealings in Palermo in Day Eight, Tale Ten show deceit and counterdeceit; money gives leverage to fraud. Other stories expose avarice, pride, and the habit of calculating what everyone is worth. Still others admire commercial quickness, reliability, or the ability to recover from a loss. Boccaccio's characters live in a society where reputation is capital and capital can buy reputation, but neither is sufficient to establish virtue.

It is easy to imagine the Renaissance as a clean break with medieval hierarchy. The details suggest a more interesting overlap. Noble magnificence and merchant prudence coexist; a servant knows things a master does not; women are praised for resourcefulness while constrained by marital arrangements; a court and a market may each determine someone's fate. The stories do not form a census, and they cannot replace archival study of Florentine households or labor. They give those structures fictional consequences that can be heard and debated.

Read Cisti, Andreuccio, and Lisabetta together. One finds a narrow route through status by courtesy. One learns caution through a dangerous trip to a larger city. One is crushed by the financial reasoning of her own family. Their differences prevent a sentimental claim that cleverness always wins or that urban commerce steadily produces freedom. The *Decameron* brings the city's social world into narrative focus by asking who can speak, travel, marry, transact, and refuse—and what happens to those who cannot.
