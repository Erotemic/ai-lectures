# The Canterbury Tales — Marriage Debated Across the Tales

The marriage stories of the Canterbury pilgrims form a debate without a chairperson. The Wife of Bath argues that husbands and wives struggle over mastery, drawing evidence from her own five marriages. The Clerk tells of Griselda, a wife who obeys a husband who tests her cruelly. The Merchant describes an aging husband who wants a young wife as a possession and is deceived in his own garden. The Franklin offers a marriage founded upon mutual courtesy, then subjects it to a promise that appears impossible to fulfill. None of these stories answers the others completely.

Begin with the Wife's authority. She believes that experience gives her the right to challenge antifeminist books. Her first husbands control wealth and status; she learns to bargain with words, accusation and desire. The violence in the marriage to Jankyn shows what is at stake when learned men claim the right to define women's conduct. Her Arthurian tale imagines a knight who must surrender authority over a wife, although its happy ending remains entangled with beauty and an earlier sexual crime.

The Clerk's Griselda makes the structure of power visible by refusing to resist. Walter's right to test his wife allows him to claim he is measuring patience, but the measurements are performed through acts that would be cruelty in any ordinary household. The tale rewards Griselda with restoration, yet the Clerk's envoy ridicules the idea that real husbands should expect such submission. Whether the story endorses or exposes the husband depends partly upon how we hear that double ending.

The Merchant changes the angle. January is convinced that marriage will secure his comfort and salvation. He chooses a young wife without taking her wishes seriously. May's deception exposes his weakness, but does not provide an uncomplicated model of mutual respect. January's regained sight leads not to knowledge but to acceptance of an explanation that protects his desired domestic peace. The story mocks the fantasy that marriage gives one person reliable possession of another's mind or body.

The Franklin appears to offer something better. Arveragus and Dorigen promise not to rule each other tyrannically. But Dorigen's reckless promise to Aurelius produces a moral crisis, and her husband insists she honor it. Aurelius and the magician surrender their own claims only after she has suffered. The question of which person is most generous cannot be answered without also asking which person had the power to choose the terms of that generosity.

The Shipman's tale adds money and secrecy. A merchant's own loan pays for his wife's affair with a monk; the wife then promises to repay her husband in the currency of marital affection. An orderly account book cannot determine what has happened to the relationships. In the Knight's Tale, Emily's desire to remain unmarried is overridden by men's contest and divine arrangements. Across the work, the social meaning of marriage depends upon property, age, speech, violence, vows and the unequal power to refuse.

It is tempting to call this a single argument progressing toward an enlightened answer. The surviving text does not require that interpretation. The tales differ in genre and in the specific arrangements of their manuscripts. What Chaucer unquestionably provides is the opportunity to compare accounts in which the same institution can be a source of financial survival, intimacy, humiliation, domination or mercy. A good reader does not choose the story with the nicest conclusion and discard the others. The achievement lies in being made to test each conclusion against the people it leaves unheard.
