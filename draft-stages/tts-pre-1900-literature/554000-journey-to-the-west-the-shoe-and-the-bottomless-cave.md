# Journey to the West: The shoe and the Bottomless Cave

## Three nights in Zhenhai Temple

At Zhenhai Monastery, the guests had been treated with care. There were beds for the travelers, fodder for the horse, and enough food to make the long journey seem, for one evening, like an ordinary pilgrimage again. The young woman whom Xuanzang had found tied to a tree was fed and given a place to sleep behind the image of a Heavenly King. She thanked the monks who had sheltered her. She had spoken like a victim of robbers, and they had answered like people whose religious calling required them to offer help.

By morning Xuanzang was sick. His head ached, his body burned, and he could not rise to continue the journey. Wukong stayed beside him; Bajie found it difficult to be patient; Sha Wujing attended to the horse and luggage. The monks brought food and medicine as best they could. The master who had insisted that the woman be rescued now lay unable to travel. He had accepted responsibility for her, but he had not yet seen what that responsibility might cost others.

A day passed, and another. On the third day his fever still held him in bed. He asked whether the rescued woman had been properly fed. She was a stranger in a strange place, he reminded his disciples. Her misfortune must not be forgotten merely because he had fallen ill. The question drew a painful silence from Wukong. The Monkey King knew what he had seen in the forest: the black vapor, the deception, the way the woman had made compassion serve her passage through the monastery gate. But he would not abandon his sick master in order to quarrel with him once more.

Xuanzang grew despondent. What if he never recovered? The emperor in the east had entrusted him with a task that had taken him across wildernesses and kingdoms. His companions were strong enough to go on, but the promised scriptures were not merely a bundle to be carried by whoever remained standing. He had come as an appointed monk. Could a letter be sent back to the Tang court, asking the emperor to send another pilgrim to finish the work?

Bajie looked troubled at the suggestion. A new envoy would mean years lost, and every demon in the mountains would have another chance at them. Wukong refused to entertain the idea. His master had survived fire, water, imprisonment, and transformations more frightening than fever. They had not rescued him from Lion-Camel City only to let a headache end the pilgrimage. Xuanzang, he said, must stop arranging a replacement while he was still alive.

To lift his spirits Wukong offered a teasing explanation. In his former life as the Golden Cicada, perhaps Xuanzang had wasted a grain of rice given as an offering. A debt that small could still call for repayment. Bajie seized on the joke and made it worse, calculating how many ages a person might suffer for spoiling food. Xuanzang did not find the accounting reassuring. Still, the disciples had begun to speak to him as a living man rather than someone already beyond help.

## The kitchen's hidden grief

Wukong went to fetch cool water for his master's hot mouth. In the temple kitchen he found monks who should have been preparing the day's food. Instead they were wiping their eyes, whispering, and looking toward the rear of the monastery. When they noticed the monkey, they tried to hide their distress. He was a formidable guest with a fierce face, and they were not certain how much they should tell him.

Wukong asked what had happened. Had the kitchen run out of grain? Had someone stolen the offerings? At last one of the monks confessed that something dreadful had occurred. For three nights in succession, young novices had disappeared. Six of them were gone. In the rear garden the surviving monks had found the signs of terrible deaths: clothing and bones where boys who had been serving in the monastery should have been. They had feared to speak plainly while travelers were lodged there, but the deaths were real and could not be covered up by prayers.

The Monkey King did not need long to understand. He had warned that the woman was no helpless captive, and now the monks who sheltered her were paying with their lives. His anger was not directed at the men in the kitchen. They had done what Xuanzang had asked of them. Their failure, if it was a failure, was the same one his master had made: they had been given an appearance of suffering and had responded to it.

Wukong told them he would find the killer. They stared at the staff in his hand and dared to hope he meant what he said. But first he carried the water to Xuanzang. The monk drank, and the coolness gave him relief. Wukong told him what the brothers had found behind their temple and asked permission to investigate. Xuanzang was distressed by the deaths. He had brought the woman inside to save her. Six resident monks had died while she was under their roof. He could no longer dismiss Wukong's fears as mere suspicion.

He asked only that Wukong act with care and return. He still felt too weak to travel, and without the monkey the party had little defense against a foe able to enter a monastery unseen. Wukong promised to protect him and went out into the evening, leaving Bajie and Sha beside the bed.

## A novice who did not sleep

The night was quiet in the temple courts. Lamps burned before the images. The resident monks had gone to their rooms, and the travelers' horse stood under shelter. Somewhere among the statues was the woman whose tears had brought her out of the Black Pine Forest. Wukong decided that it was time to let her find someone who would not be deceived.

He pulled a hair from his body, worked his magic, and took the appearance of a young novice. His clothes were simple. He made himself look slight and harmless, the sort of boy who might sit awake repeating prayers because a teacher had ordered him to do so. He went into the temple hall and began his recitation. Then he waited where the shadows and candlelight met.

Before long the young woman appeared. She did not look like a prisoner now. She came forward with the easy confidence of someone who believed the hall held only a lonely novice. Why was he awake when all the others were asleep? Was he cold? Was he afraid? Wukong answered in the awkward manner of a boy surprised by attention. She moved closer, praised his looks, and invited him away from the temple. Her voice promised company and pleasure, a reward for leaving his prayers behind.

Wukong let himself be led. She kissed him and took his hand. To anyone watching, the novice would have appeared persuaded. They went toward the rear garden, the same place where the clothing and bones of the six dead monks had been found. The garden lay dark behind the buildings. Plants rustled in the night wind. Wukong waited until the woman believed she had brought him beyond the reach of help.

Then the novice vanished. The Monkey King stood before her, staff raised, his face no longer young or frightened. The woman sprang back. Her pleas and caresses were gone. She drew her weapons, and the two fought among the trees. Metal rang against iron. She had hoped for a victim who would not fight; instead she had brought the most dangerous of Xuanzang's protectors into the place where she hunted.

Wukong struck at her. The woman slipped away, turned, and seemed to remain before him in the form he had followed from the hall. He swung again. She fell, and he moved to finish the battle. But the body before him shrank into a single embroidered shoe. It lay on the ground with the senseless neatness of an object that had never breathed or spoken. The woman had used the shoe as a substitute for herself and had escaped while Wukong attacked the false body.

He picked it up and cursed the trick. The six novices would not be restored by catching a shoe. The demon had learned that the travelers knew her nature, and now she had another chance to strike at the person she had pursued from the forest.

## The master vanishes

Wukong hurried back toward the guest quarters. He expected to find Bajie arguing about the hour and Sha keeping his usual quiet watch. Instead he found confusion. Xuanzang was gone. The woman had returned to the place where the sick monk lay, and while Wukong had been occupied with her false double, she had carried her intended husband away.

For a moment Wukong's temper overcame everything else. He had warned them at the tree. He had warned them on the road. He had watched the woman enter the monastery. Now the master was missing and six monks were dead. He blamed Bajie for letting his attention wander and Sha for not preventing the abduction. Bajie protested that they had not invited the demon into the temple. Xuanzang himself had insisted on rescuing her, and Wukong had just chased her into the garden. There were too many people to blame and only one person to rescue.

Sha Wujing stepped between the quarrel and its consequences. Whatever had happened, he said, they were bound to one another as fellow disciples. Quarreling would not tell them where the master had gone. The whole purpose of their journey was to carry one fragile human being across a world in which strength and cleverness were never quite enough. If they began striking at one another, they would do the demon's work for her.

Wukong listened. His anger was real, but so was the sense in Sha's words. He put the embroidered shoe away as proof of the trick and turned his attention from blame to pursuit. They could not follow the road west as though Xuanzang had merely fallen behind. They had to find the cave into which the demon had carried him.

At daybreak the monks of Zhenhai Temple learned that their guest was missing. They had already mourned six novices. Now the visiting holy man, too, had vanished from the very rooms they had prepared for him. Wukong explained that the stranger they had sheltered was a demon. The monks remembered her face and her gratitude, and some found it hard to believe she could have been the killer. Wukong did not ask them to settle the question by arguing about her appearance. The empty bed, the bones, and the shoe were enough.

## Back through the Black Pine Forest

The three disciples retraced the way they had come. They led the white horse and carried the baggage eastward into the Black Pine Forest, back toward the place where a woman had been tied to a tree and where Xuanzang had first mistaken a snare for a rescue. The forest was no clearer by daylight than it had been before. The tall pines made the road dark, and every path seemed to turn among the same trunks. The demon had fled by magic; ordinary tracks would not be enough.

Wukong looked at the mountains and called for the local spirits to show themselves. When the earth gods did not come quickly, he brandished his staff and made his summons impossible to ignore. The guardians emerged frightened and apologetic. They were responsible for the hills and roots and paths, not for the deeds of every powerful creature that passed above them. Wukong was in no mood for distinctions. He described the woman, her magic, and the stolen monk. Where had she gone?

One of the spirits knew. The creature had flown south toward a mountain known as Trap-Sky Mountain, and beneath that mountain lay the Bottomless Cave. It was a distant place, roughly a thousand li from their present position. The name alone suggested that a traveler could walk down into it and never find the way out. The earth gods could tell Wukong the direction, but they could not promise what lay at the bottom.

Wukong did not ask them to fight for him. He had wanted a trail and now he had one. He thanked—or, rather, dismissed—the frightened spirits and called his brothers together. The horse, baggage, and three disciples must go south. Sha took charge of the load. Bajie complained about the distance, then prepared to travel. The promise they had made to Xuanzang had not been canceled by the fact that he could no longer speak to them.

They rose by cloud with the horse and packs and crossed the long stretch of land the local spirits had described. The forest fell away. Hills and valleys passed below. At last they came to the mountain and the place where the earth seemed to open into darkness. Its entrance did not reveal the cave's extent. There were shadows within shadows and air moving up from below. A demon strong enough to steal Xuanzang from Zhenhai Temple would have chosen a place that made rescue difficult.

Bajie had often been quickest to suggest retreat when trouble appeared. This time Wukong put him forward as scout. The pig had the strength to descend and the wit, when he used it, to observe an enemy's defenses. Wukong told him to examine the entrance, discover the cave's arrangements, and come back with what he learned. Bajie objected to being sent first into a place named Bottomless Cave. Wukong answered that the name was no reason for a rescuer to stay outside.

Bajie stepped toward the darkness. Wukong and Sha waited with the horse and luggage above. Far below them, somewhere beyond the reach of daylight, Xuanzang was in the hands of the woman who had entered his life as a prisoner tied to a tree. The shoe had been a diversion; the illness, the deaths, and the abduction had all unfolded under a monastery's roof. Now the next part of their pilgrimage led not west along a road, but down into the earth.
