# Journey to the West: The scorpion at Pipa Cave

## A different invitation

For a little while it had seemed that the dangerous country of Western Liang could be left behind. Its queen had wanted Xuanzang to remain as her husband. Wukong had persuaded the court to seal the pilgrims' travel document, then conducted the monk out through the west gate and announced that the journey would continue. The queen's disappointment was painful, but her ministers could not bring back a man who had already escaped the city.

Then a second woman stepped forward, promising pleasures of her own. She did not wait for the monk's answer. A violent wind rose from the road. Dust swallowed the horse, and when Sha Wujing reached for its rider, Xuanzang was gone.

Wukong leaped into the air. Bajie and Sha followed with the belongings. Behind them the people of Western Liang knelt in astonishment, realizing that the visitors were not ordinary men from a distant kingdom. The queen returned to her city. The three disciples followed the departing whirlwind toward a high mountain, determined to find its source.

The wind died before a wall of stone. Around the side of the rock stood a carved entrance. The words named it Pipa Cave, on the Mountain of Poisonous Foes. Bajie wanted to break the doors at once. Wukong stopped him. The path of the wind pointed here, but it was still possible that the cave belonged to another being who would take their attack as a declaration of war. He would go in first, small enough not to be seen.

Sha praised the decision. Wukong had many times been the first person to rush into a fight, and now he was thinking before he struck. The monkey became a bee and passed through the entrance while his brothers waited by the stone screen.

## A table set for a captive

Inside he found a courtyard and a pavilion. Servants in bright clothing moved about a woman who sat as though she owned a peaceful household. It was a picture of domestic comfort. The servants brought two trays of steamed buns, one prepared with a meat filling, the other with a sweet vegetarian filling. The meat, however, was human flesh. They were not feeding an ordinary guest in an ordinary house.

The woman ordered Xuanzang brought out. He appeared pale and terrified, but alive. She spoke to him softly. The court of Western Liang had been magnificent, she admitted, and her cave could not rival its luxuries; nevertheless, they might be companions here, reading scriptures and spending their lives together. She offered the monk something to eat and asked him to forget his fear.

Xuanzang feared both speaking and remaining silent. The queen had been a human ruler who worked through court rituals. This woman had transported him by force to an isolated mountain. She might kill him for refusing to eat, or interpret any courtesy as agreement to become her husband. He tried to preserve the narrowest possible distance. When she explained the two kinds of buns, he chose the vegetarian one. When she offered the meat bun, he passed it back without breaking it, saying that a monk did not tear apart meat.

The woman teased him in riddles. She knew of their adventure at the Mother-and-Child River, and her jokes about water, food, and the monk's vows had meanings he did not wish to entertain. Wukong, listening as a bee, heard enough. His master had already been trapped by a marriage proposal, and now the same pressure returned in a place from which there was no polite passage out.

He resumed his own shape and sprang into the pavilion with the iron staff. The woman breathed out a cloud of smoke, ordered her servants to take the monk away, and seized a three-pronged spear. She fought Wukong through the courtyard and out of the entrance. Bajie joined the battle, and Sha remained with the horse and luggage.

The stranger was astonishingly strong. She claimed to recognize Wukong, and she warned him that even the Buddha himself had reason to fear her. This was not a bluff invented for a monk who knew nothing of supernatural affairs. As Wukong drove her back with the staff, she used a power that reached where his weapon did not. Something stabbed his head, a sudden penetrating pain that no defense he had learned could stop.

Wukong cried out and withdrew. Bajie followed, unable to understand why his brother had abandoned a fight they seemed to be winning. The demon retreated into her cave and barred the entrance.

## The wound without a cut

Wukong crouched by the roadside with his hands on his head. Sha asked whether the flesh had been pierced. He could find no wound. Bajie joked that the monkey whose skull had survived execution and Laozi's furnace had been undone by an ailment of the scalp. Wukong could barely endure the teasing. He explained that the woman's sudden movement had brought a sting he could not see. There was no swelling to dress and no cut to bandage. Yet the pain made it impossible to continue fighting.

The monkey, for all his power, had never been free of bodily weakness. The diamond ring had made him helpless without his staff; now a creature had hurt him without leaving a visible mark. Neither disaster could be solved by boasting. Sha urged him to rest until dawn. Bajie would have preferred to keep the enemy awake all night, but he could not know what another sting might do to them.

Meanwhile the woman arranged her sleeping chamber. She brought Xuanzang inside and renewed her demands. She described the pleasures of marriage, the finery of the room, and the companionship she wanted. Xuanzang did not agree. He kept his clothes on and avoided her embrace. She argued, pleaded, and touched him, and he refused her through the night.

The scene is a dangerous struggle rather than a comic test of whether a man finds a woman attractive. Xuanzang is a prisoner, faced with a captor who insists on a sexual relationship he will not enter. His religious vows are central to his refusal, but consent would matter even if he were not a monk. The woman's frustration rose until affection gave way to punishment. She had her servants tie him up and put him under the corridor. He passed the rest of the night bound and waiting for his disciples.

At dawn Wukong's pain had diminished to an itch. He could move again, though he still dreaded the unseen weapon. Bajie resumed his jokes. Sha urged them to stop bickering and rescue their master.

## What the bee learned in the morning

Wukong entered the cave once more in the shape of a bee. The attendants were asleep, and their mistress lay exhausted after the night. In the rear corridor he found Xuanzang tied hand and foot. The monk whispered that he had refused the woman and that she had bound him because he would not submit. He begged his disciple to take him away.

Wukong, relieved to hear that Xuanzang was alive and had resisted, flew back to Bajie. They prepared a more direct assault. Bajie smashed the stonework of the entrance with his rake, making enough noise to wake the household. Their enemy came out armed again. Wukong and Bajie attacked together, but this time the sting caught Bajie on the lip. He fled with his hand over his mouth, groaning through swollen pain. Wukong did not risk remaining under the demon's weapon.

Sha found them both defeated, though neither had suffered an injury that could be seen in an ordinary way. They had entered with a staff and a rake and returned with a sore head and a sore mouth. It was at that moment that an elderly woman came near, offering guidance. Wukong recognized something divine beneath her appearance. The visitor revealed herself as Guanyin.

She explained why the monster could wound even a Buddha. The three-pronged weapon was the creature's own pair of claws. Its secret attack came from its tail: a terrible sting, called the Reverse-Horse Poison. The woman who had seized Xuanzang was a scorpion spirit. At Vulture Peak she had once heard the Buddha teach; when he pushed her away, she stung his finger and caused him severe pain. She had escaped his officers and now hidden herself here.

Guanyin did not claim that she could simply seize the demon. The sting was too dangerous to approach. Instead she named the one celestial official who could defeat the scorpion: the star lord called the Pleiades Sun Officer, whose true form was a great rooster.

Wukong thanked her and went to find him. Bajie, miserable with his injured lip, asked that his brother bring back medicine. Wukong laughed at the request. His own head had begun to recover after a night. But he knew that neither of them was eager to endure another sting if relief could be had.

## The star who crowed

Wukong went to the eastern heavenly gate and asked for the official. The star lord had gone to inspect the celestial observation platform but soon returned. When he learned that Guanyin had recommended him, he did not delay over formalities. He descended with Wukong to the Mountain of Poisonous Foes.

Before going into battle, he examined the injured disciples. He passed a hand over Bajie's lip and blew upon it. The pain vanished. He did the same for Wukong's head, removing the lingering irritation. Bajie was immediately eager for revenge, and Wukong was no less pleased to be whole again.

The plan was not to compete in weapon strokes. The celestial officer would wait outside while Wukong and Bajie drew the demon into view. The two disciples broke through the entrance and challenged her. She emerged with her spear, angry that they had returned again. They fought just long enough to draw her past the stone screen, then withdrew toward the waiting star lord.

On the hillside stood a rooster as tall as a person, with a splendid double comb. It raised its head and crowed once. The scorpion spirit lost her human disguise. She became a great scorpion, shaped like the musical instrument whose name the cave bore. The rooster crowed a second time. The scorpion collapsed, powerless, and died. Bajie struck its body repeatedly with his rake until little remained of the thing that had tormented him.

The star lord resumed his celestial form and departed. Wukong, Bajie, and Sha entered the cave and found Xuanzang waiting, weeping. He thanked his disciples and heard how Guanyin had identified the danger. They also found the women who had served the scorpion. These were not all monsters. Several said that they were women kidnapped from Western Liang and forced into the household. Wukong examined them and recognized that they lacked the signs of demons. He released them and showed them the path home.

The companions ate the simple vegetarian food they could recover, gathered their luggage, and burned the lair before returning to the western road. There had been no marriage. There had been no romantic surrender that made the imprisonment harmless. The monk had escaped an enemy who pursued him against his will, and the women taken from their homes had been set free as well.

The pilgrims left the Mountain of Poisonous Foes behind. Beyond it waited a danger of a very different kind: human robbers, whose lives Xuanzang believed were no less worthy of protection than those of the people they attacked.
