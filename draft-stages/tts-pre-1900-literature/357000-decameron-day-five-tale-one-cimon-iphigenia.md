# The Decameron — Cimon and Iphigenia

On the island of Cyprus lives a wealthy gentleman, Aristippus, whose son has disappointed every plan for his education. Called Cimon by those who judge him coarse and incapable, the young man takes little interest in books or the civilized accomplishments expected of his birth. His father sends him to the countryside, where rough labor seems better suited to him. One spring day Cimon walks through a grove and sees a young woman sleeping near a fountain, with attendants nearby. Her name is Iphigenia.

He is astonished by her beauty. When she wakes, she knows him and is alarmed by his stare. She asks him to keep away. He follows her nonetheless, then returns to his father's household with a determination his family has never seen. He wants fine clothes, instruction, conversation, music, riding, and every accomplishment associated with an educated gentleman. For years he applies himself until the youth dismissed as incapable becomes distinguished in the arts of peace and war. Panfilo presents this transformation as the power of love to unlock capacities that instruction alone could not release.

But Cimon's education does not create a reciprocal love. Iphigenia is promised to another man, and her family intends her to marry abroad. Cimon resolves not to yield. When the ship carrying her toward Rhodes sets sail, he and armed companions intercept it at sea. Iphigenia is taken by force. At this point the story of cultivated virtue has become an adventure of maritime violence. A storm drives the captors toward Rhodes, where Cimon is arrested and imprisoned, while Iphigenia is returned to the household waiting for her marriage.

Another man in Rhodes has his own grievance. Lysimachus, a powerful official, loves Cassandra, who is also about to marry someone else. He can release Cimon and gain an experienced partner for an assault on the wedding celebrations. The two agree to combine their aims. At the appointed moment, they enter with armed followers, seize Cassandra and Iphigenia, and escape by ship. They reach Crete, where they marry the women and eventually obtain a settlement allowing their return to their homes.

The finished tale calls the outcome fortunate. Two determined lovers gain the women they pursued, and the law that briefly imprisoned one of them yields to wealth and negotiation. Yet the narrative repeatedly leaves us with the same absent question: what do Iphigenia and Cassandra want? Iphigenia's fear in the grove is directly narrated. The violence of her abduction is directly narrated. Neither becomes an obstacle to the plot's assurance that marriage will solve the problem.

Panfilo's story blends several familiar forms: an education in courtly virtue, a sea adventure, the competition of suitors, captivity, rescue, and final reconciliation. Its pace carries the listener from an apparently miraculous awakening of intelligence to a succession of raids. The transformation is real within the fiction, but its meaning is divided. Cimon learns eloquence and command. He does not thereby learn to respect a refusal.

Reading with that distinction makes the tale stronger rather than weaker. Boccaccio allows us to admire a man learning skills and acting decisively, while showing that accomplishment can be put to destructive ends. The romance's final weddings describe the winners' fortune. They do not settle every question raised by the means of winning it.
