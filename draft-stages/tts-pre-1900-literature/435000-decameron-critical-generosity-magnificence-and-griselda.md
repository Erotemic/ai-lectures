# What Does It Cost to Be Generous?

The final day of the *Decameron* is devoted to great-hearted conduct. The subject sounds like a moral resolution after so many deceptions and quarrels: perhaps the book will end by showing people choosing the good without calculation. But Day Ten is not that simple. Its rulers and nobles can give away enormous things because they already possess power. A generous gesture may be beautiful, or it may be the public performance of someone who can impose the costs on others.

Nathan's meeting with Mitridanes in Day Ten, Tale Three comes close to an impossible ideal. Mitridanes envies Nathan's reputation for generosity and resolves to kill him. Nathan, unrecognized, gives guidance that would enable the murder and then reveals himself. The extreme absence of self-protection turns rivalry into shame and admiration. This is generosity as risk borne by the giver. The great gift is not a palace or a horse but the willingness to put one's own life in another person's hands.

Gentile's story in Tale Four invites a more difficult response. A woman thought dead is rescued while pregnant; Gentile cares for her and eventually presents her living to her husband. The tale frames his restraint and restoration as admirable. Yet the banquet argument uses a comparison to possession, asking who has the better claim to something apparently abandoned. When a woman's body is brought into the logic of objects and owners, we must distinguish Gentile's chosen restraint from the idea that either man owns her. The generosity praised inside the tale is entangled with a premise a careful reader need not accept.

Dianora's winter garden in Tale Five offers a chain of releases. She sets a seemingly impossible condition to avoid an unwanted suitor. The condition is fulfilled through a magician's art. Her husband, when told, advises her to honor her promise; the suitor, struck by the husband's magnanimity, releases her; the magician then refuses payment. The narrative is organized so that each man surpasses the previous one in relinquishing a claim. But the central anxiety belongs to Dianora, whose words created a dangerous obligation and whose ability to refuse depends upon other people's decisions. A contest of generosity can still place its greatest burden on someone who cannot simply leave it.

Torello and Saladin in Tale Nine bring generosity into the realm of hospitality across cultural and political difference. Saladin is welcomed while disguised, before Torello knows his guest's stature. Later recognition permits a reciprocal exchange of honor. The magic of Torello's return at night is a literary device, not a historical report of the Crusades. The appeal of the tale comes partly from an ethical test: is the welcome genuine when the guest's identity and power are unknown? Hospitality, unlike public display, can precede reputation.

Then Dioneo tells Griselda. Gualtieri marries her, tests her, removes her children while making her believe they have been killed, sends her away, and later forces her to prepare for a supposed new bride. All of this is arranged to examine her obedience. Griselda's patience is tremendous, but a person who already controls the terms of the test is not demonstrating magnanimity by inventing ordeals for someone else. Dioneo himself condemns the marquis's folly. The book ends with an exemplary woman and a profoundly defective husband, leaving the daily theme unsettled.

Readers across centuries have taken different lessons. Petrarch refashioned the Griselda story in Latin, giving it a more explicit exemplary direction; Chaucer later used Petrarch's version in the *Clerk's Tale*. Those transformations show the original's capacity to be made into quite different arguments. Griselda may represent constancy, but constancy under unjust command is not identical to the mutual generosity of a good marriage. Whether a story celebrates the patient's virtue or excuses the tyrant's conduct depends on what the adaptation emphasizes.

The final day can be read as a laboratory for distinguishing generosity from control. Ask who possesses the resource, who pays, who can withdraw the promise, and who receives recognition. Nathan risks himself. Saladin responds to care given before prestige is known. Dianora depends upon a release she cannot compel. Griselda survives a test that should not have been required. The contrast is not an embarrassing exception to a simple closing lesson. It is part of the lesson's complexity.

The book's final act of magnificence may, in the end, be the reader's labor of judgment. A tale can show a magnificent gift and ask whether it is really given; a tale can show obedience and ask whom obedience serves. Dioneo's criticism of Gualtieri makes it impossible to hear the conclusion as nothing but praise. We leave the tenth day with examples of generosity, but also with a sharper capacity to ask when power calls itself generous because it has allowed another person to keep what should never have been taken.
