# 009000 — Reading Drawings, Specifications, and Submittals

**Track:** Contracts, Estimates, and Contractor Literacy. **Background:** scope, bids, change orders, inspections, and acceptance criteria. **Purpose:** understand how construction documents communicate intent, and how a consumer can distinguish a priced promise from a product illustration.

# A drawing is not the whole contract

A homeowner receives two bids to replace an exterior staircase. Both contractors point to the same architectural drawing. One bid is substantially lower, but neither explains which lumber, connector system, railing details, or finish is included. If the drawing only shows the stair's location and overall dimensions, those important decisions may be governed elsewhere—or not yet specified at all.

Construction documentation is a collection of complementary descriptions. Drawings communicate geometry, relationships, and details. Specifications describe materials, performance, workmanship, testing, and sometimes required submittals. A contract identifies which documents are part of the agreement, what work is included, and how changes are authorized. A persuasive rendering does not necessarily contain enough information to construct or price the work accurately.

## Read the title block first

Before measuring from a drawing, identify its title, sheet number, date, revision history, scale, and purpose. Is it a schematic concept, a permit document, a construction issue, or an as-built record? Different drawing stages carry different levels of detail. A note that says 'not for construction' is materially different from a coordinated, approved construction set.

Then look at the drawing type. A plan is a view from above, showing arrangement and often horizontal dimensions. An elevation shows a vertical face. A section cuts through the construction to show layers and concealed relationships. A detail enlarges a particular assembly. Schedules list doors, windows, fixtures, finishes, or equipment with identifiers that connect back to plans and specifications. An entire stair cannot be understood from its plan alone if the critical guard connection appears in a separate section.

Dimensions written on the drawing generally carry more authority than dimensions someone estimates with a ruler on a printed sheet. Printing, scaling, and electronic display can change the apparent size. A dimension that does not close geometrically is a question to resolve, not an invitation to silently adjust the design in the field.

## Specifications often contain the decisive requirements

A plan may call for exterior decking, while the specification defines its material grade, treatment, fastening, finish, or performance criteria. Specifications are often organized using the Construction Specifications Institute's MasterFormat system, which is maintained in coordination with Construction Specifications Canada. The system provides a common organization; it does not itself guarantee that a project has complete, coordinated requirements.

A useful reading process follows one physical component through the documents. Find it on the plan, follow any detail references, locate its schedule entry, and identify the specification section. If these descriptions disagree, record the conflict and seek written clarification through the project's agreed process. Do not assume that the most visually prominent document automatically overrides the others. Contract-specific order-of-precedence clauses and applicable law govern such conflicts.

## Submittals show a proposed implementation

A contractor may submit product data, samples, shop drawings, or other materials for review. These are generally intended to demonstrate how the contractor proposes to meet the design and contract requirements. A shop drawing might show the actual fabrication geometry of a railing or the detailed equipment arrangement for a mechanical installation. It is not, by default, a replacement for the contract documents or an authorization to change scope.

The American Institute of Architects' A201 general conditions describe a distinction between contract documents and submittals and assign responsibilities to the contractor for coordination and verification of particular details. But not every homeowner contract incorporates A201. The operative document is the actual signed agreement, and the lecture's principles should not be mistaken for legal advice about a specific job.

An approved submittal has a purpose and a review scope. Its approval does not necessarily waive an unreported deviation or transfer all responsibility for field measurements to the reviewer. Likewise, a glossy manufacturer's brochure does not show that a proposed product is permitted in the specific application. Identify the model, performance data, installation requirements, substitutions, and compatibility with the design.

## Requests for information prevent invisible decisions

Suppose a stair detail calls for a certain handrail height while a written note gives a conflicting dimension. A contractor might call the designer, receive an informal verbal answer, and proceed. Later there is a dispute about whether that conversation changed the required work. A written request for information, or RFI, documents the question, the references, and the response. It creates a traceable record rather than relying on incompatible recollections.

An RFI is not necessarily a change order. Clarifying an ambiguity and authorizing a change in price or time are distinct functions. If the response changes materials, geometry, or effort, the parties may need a formally documented change under their contract. Keeping these steps separate protects both the person paying for the work and the contractor performing it.

## A worked review of a stair package

Imagine the drawings show a five-step exterior stair with a landing. The contract calls for a finished, inspected installation, but the bid lacks a material schedule. Before signing, ask what governs the tread and riser details, how the landing is supported, which guard and handrail details apply, what foundation work is included, who obtains inspections, and which product substitutions require consent. These are not demands for excessive paperwork. They are attempts to translate the visible drawing into a shared, buildable scope.

As work proceeds, compare proposed products and field conditions against that scope. If buried conditions require a different foundation detail, get the consequences documented before accepting the change. At closeout, keep final revisions, approved product information, warranty instructions, test results where applicable, and inspection documentation. Future maintenance becomes easier when the construction record reflects what was installed rather than merely what was planned.

Good construction literacy is not about learning every drafting symbol. It is about treating drawings, specifications, contracts, and submittals as related but distinct sources of authority. When they are coordinated, they make expectations testable. When they disagree, the right response is to make the question visible before someone builds an irreversible answer into the structure.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Cross-reference worksheet

| Item | Where to look | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | Plan, elevation, section, detail | Which dimensions control? |
| Materials and workmanship | Specifications, schedules, detail notes | What exact product and performance are required? |
| Scope/price | Signed agreement and incorporated exhibits | Who provides what, and what is excluded? |
| Product substitution | Submittal and approval procedure | Has the deviation been explicitly accepted? |
| Conflict or hidden condition | RFI, change-order procedure | What written decision resolves it? |
| Closeout | Inspection approvals, as-builts, manuals | What was actually installed and verified? |

### Source scope and attribution

MasterFormat is maintained by Construction Specifications Institute (CSI) and Construction Specifications Canada (CSC). The American Institute of Architects' A201 is an example of formal document allocation and submittal rules. It applies only when incorporated into the project contract or otherwise relevant. Local permit law, professional-design requirements, and the actual written contract are independent considerations.

### Exercise

Take a fictional deck drawing with missing ledger, guard, and flashing details. Draft three precise RFIs, identify which missing items prevent meaningful pricing, and distinguish clarifications from potential changes to scope or price.
