# Two Bids That Are Not the Same Price

A homeowner requests bids to renovate a small bathroom. Contractor A offers a fixed price of twelve thousand dollars. Contractor B offers ten thousand five hundred dollars, with an allowance for tile and a note that hidden water damage will be billed separately. Is B cheaper? There is no defensible answer from those totals alone. The two prices may describe different work and distribute uncertainty differently.

The first lesson in reading estimates is to treat the price as a statement conditioned on scope, quantities, assumptions, exclusions, and a method for handling changes. Without those terms, a low number may be merely a partial description of the job.

## Estimate versus commitment

An estimate is a prediction of likely cost based on current information. A quote or fixed-price contract may commit a contractor to deliver a specified scope for an agreed amount, subject to its actual terms and change provisions. People use these words loosely in conversation, so the operative written language matters more than the label at the top of the page.

A fixed price transfers some ordinary cost risk to the contractor, but it does not necessarily include every possible condition hidden behind a wall. The contract may exclude concealed rot, asbestos, unexpected framing repairs, or other conditions. The scope and exclusions define what the fixed price actually fixes. It is a mistake to think a fixed number makes an incomplete description complete.

Time-and-materials work allocates risk differently. A customer may pay for actual labor time plus supplies and agreed overhead or markup. That can be appropriate for uncertain troubleshooting, but the final cost may be less predictable. Useful controls include documenting labor rates, material markup, reporting intervals, authorization thresholds, and what records support invoiced charges. A time-and-materials arrangement is not automatically dishonest or economical; its merits depend on how uncertainty is handled and documented.

## An allowance is a placeholder, not a selection

An allowance reserves a budget amount for an item that has not yet been chosen precisely. Tile, fixtures, hardware, or lighting are common examples. Suppose the bid includes a one-thousand-dollar tile allowance. If the owner selects tile costing two thousand dollars, the price may rise by the difference, perhaps with additional labor or markup depending on the agreement. If the selected tile costs less, the contract should explain how the credit works.

This makes allowances important when comparing bids. Contractor A may include two thousand dollars for tile while contractor B includes five hundred. Their final totals are not directly comparable until the allowances and associated installation assumptions are normalized. A luxurious-looking low price can be obtained by using very small placeholders.

A good allowance description states whether it covers only the product or also taxes, delivery, waste, installation accessories, labor effects, and contractor markup. If a selection changes the installation method, a material-only comparison is insufficient.

## Unit prices price a quantity, not the whole project

Some work can be priced per unit: per square foot of floor repair, per linear foot of drain line, or per cubic yard of excavation. Unit pricing is useful when the quantity is uncertain but the type of work can be described. Its total cost is unit rate times measured quantity, plus any other agreed charges.

Suppose excavation is priced at two hundred dollars per cubic yard and the eventual quantity is between eight and twelve cubic yards. The variable portion would range from sixteen hundred to twenty-four hundred dollars. A quote listing 'two hundred dollars' without the unit, measured quantity rule, or disposal terms tells you almost nothing about the likely final price.

Unit prices can coexist with fixed-price components. A renovation may have a fixed amount for the planned work and a unit rate for replacing hidden damaged subfloor discovered after demolition. The important question is who determines the quantity, how it is documented, what triggers the unit-priced work, and whether the customer approves it before it occurs.

## A change order protects the original agreement

Construction rarely proceeds with no surprises. A change order records the modification: what work is added or removed, why, the change in cost, the effect on schedule, and who authorizes it. Without a documented procedure, a disagreement over a verbal request can become a dispute about the entire job.

Consider a bathroom where water damage is found behind a wall. The contractor may need to expose the condition and propose additional repairs. A sound process separates the original work from the newly observed condition and states what evidence, pricing method, and approval apply. The owner should be able to understand whether the cost increase arose from a genuine change in scope, a previously stated allowance, or an item that was always included but left ambiguous.

## Compare the work, not only the totals

Create a comparison sheet with identical rows: demolition and disposal, protection of adjacent rooms, substrate preparation, waterproofing details, specified products, installation labor, permits and inspections where required, cleanup, schedule, warranty, and excluded work. Then place each bid's actual promise into those rows. A blank is not automatically a 'no'; it is a question to resolve before signing.

Two contractors may use different legitimate methods. The goal is not to force identical products in every case. It is to understand whether the methods achieve comparable performance and who bears the risk of failures or hidden conditions. References, licensing, insurance, and permits require verification under the laws and practices governing the work location. A persuasive salesperson is not proof of a compliant contract.

## A small arithmetic test

Return to the two bathroom bids. Suppose B's tile allowance is one thousand five hundred dollars below A's, and B excludes a waterproofing step that A explicitly includes. The visible price difference is now less meaningful; adding the tile adjustment alone can erase much of it. You still need to determine whether the waterproofing is necessary and what remedy is actually specified. The arithmetic does not tell you which contractor is better. It tells you which questions must be answered before price becomes comparable.

An effective contract is a shared map of expectations. It says what success looks like, what evidence will demonstrate it, what is included, who decides when conditions change, and how money follows those decisions. Reading it carefully is not adversarial. It is a way to make a practical project governable by both parties.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Comparable bid exercise

For two bids with base totals `A=$12,000`, `B=$10,500`, if B's tile allowance is `$1,500` lower than A's, normalizing only that allowance yields `B_adj=$12,000`. The bids are **still not comparable** if waterproofing details, demolition disposal, labor consequences, permit fees, or change terms differ. This illustration is invented for pedagogy, not a market quote.

Unit-rate example: `$200 / cubic yard × [8,12] cubic yards = [$1,600,$2,400]` before other agreed fees.

### Contract questions

1. What precisely is the deliverable and acceptance condition?
2. What product selections and allowances are included?
3. What quantities are assumed or measured?
4. What conditions are excluded and how will changes be authorized?
5. Which fees, taxes, permits, inspections, disposal, and warranties are included?

FTC home-improvement guidance emphasizes written estimates, scope, and comparing bids. The American Bar Association offers general contract-content guidance; the People's Law Library of Maryland explicitly discusses allowances and fixed versus time-and-materials arrangements. None supersedes the jurisdiction-specific contract law that actually applies.
