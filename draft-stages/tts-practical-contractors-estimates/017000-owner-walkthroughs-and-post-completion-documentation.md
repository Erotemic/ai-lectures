# 017000 — Owner Walkthroughs and Post-Completion Documentation

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A contractor says the renovation is finished and asks for final payment. The new bathroom looks attractive, but the exhaust fan is noisy, one outlet is not labeled, and the owner has no instructions for the waterproofing or the new valves. Is the project complete? That question has at least three parts: whether the contracted work was delivered, whether it performs as required, and whether the information needed to operate and maintain it has been handed over.

## A walkthrough begins with the agreement

Before visiting the finished rooms, bring the signed scope, drawings, approved selections, change orders, and relevant specifications. A walkthrough is not an opportunity to invent entirely new expectations after the work is done. It compares the actual installation against documented obligations and applicable requirements. A cosmetic preference may be negotiable; an unapproved material substitution or untested safety function is a different issue.

Organize observations by location and system. 'Kitchen, north wall, cabinet door rubs at top edge when fully opened' is specific. 'Cabinets bad' is not. Photographs should show a general view locating the issue and a close-up with sufficient context. Record date and accessible conditions. Avoid altering the work before the contractor has a chance to inspect it, unless immediate safety or damage prevention requires action.

A punch list records items that need correction, but not every observation is automatically a defect. Some are unfinished scope, some are cosmetic damage, some are incomplete testing, and others are questions about what was specified. Distinguish them. Each entry should have a measurable or observable acceptance criterion, a responsible party, and a way to verify completion. 'Confirm bath fan exhausts outdoors through approved duct and terminal' gives a better test than 'fan seems wrong.'

## Operational acceptance is different from appearance

A new shower may look complete yet leak behind the wall. A heat pump may blow warm air but have an incorrect control mode. A door may close beautifully but fail to latch when the building moves with seasonal humidity. Acceptance should include functional checks that match the work. Some tests belong to qualified trades or inspectors rather than owners, but owners can ensure the records exist.

Test results can include documented pressure or leak tests, commissioning sheets, permitted inspection approvals, electrical labeling, airflow measurements, equipment startup reports, or manufacturer warranty registrations. The correct set depends on the project and contract. A verbal 'we tested it' is weaker than an actual signed record identifying the procedure, conditions, and result. Conversely, the absence of a particular test form is not proof of noncompliance if it was never required; verify the actual obligations.

A practical walkthrough also examines access. Can shutoff valves be reached and identified? Can filters be replaced without dismantling trim? Do service panels open? Are cleanouts and drain valves accessible? Has an access hatch been covered by new cabinetry? Functional serviceability is often cheapest to correct before everyone leaves the job.

## Document what the next owner or repairer will need

Post-completion information should explain both what is visible and what is now hidden. Photograph walls before they were closed if records exist, identifying plumbing and wiring routes without making unsupported claims about exact depth. Preserve equipment model numbers, serial numbers where appropriate, finish products, paint colors, tile and grout specifications, spare materials, and the final approved drawings or as-built deviations.

A useful handover folder contains operating instructions, maintenance intervals, warranty conditions, manufacturer service information, subcontractor contacts, permit closeout records, approved changes, and outstanding items. Keep a digital copy in a durable location and an accessible local copy of information needed during an outage, such as where the main water shutoff is. Privacy and security still matter: publicly shared handover records should not include alarm codes, access credentials, or unnecessarily detailed security layouts.

If a basement is waterproofed, document which surfaces and drainage components were actually changed. 'Basement sealed' is too vague to guide future work. If a heat pump is installed, retain the settings and commissioning values that make later diagnosis possible. The archive is not paperwork for its own sake; it shortens the next repair and reduces the risk of undoing an important hidden detail.

## Separate completion, payment, and continuing obligations

Payment milestones, retainage, lien rights, warranties, and final acceptance have legal consequences that differ by contract and jurisdiction. Do not assume that signing a punch list automatically creates or waives a particular right. Use the contract terms and appropriate legal advice for consequential disputes. The practical principle is to document remaining issues and agreed remedies before the parties lose a shared view of the work.

Imagine a contractor has completed most of the kitchen but a replacement cabinet door will arrive in three weeks. The parties may agree on a written closeout arrangement: exact outstanding item, required finish, delivery and correction schedule, access arrangements, and the payment consequence provided by the contract. That is more robust than either refusing to discuss payment indefinitely or paying everything based on an undocumented promise.

Check completed corrections one by one. When a defect is repaired, preserve evidence of the result and note whether it affects another system. Replacing a door threshold might disturb floor waterproofing or weather sealing. A correction is complete when the functional and contractual acceptance condition is met, not merely when a worker reports that the item has been handled.

A good owner walkthrough is cooperative but precise. It converts a vague sense of completion into shared evidence about scope, performance, access, and future maintenance. The result is not simply a finished room; it is a finished system whose essential history can be recovered when someone needs to understand it years later.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Punch-list record:** item ID; precise location; photo; applicable contract/drawing reference; observed condition; required acceptance criterion; trade/responsibility; target date; recheck result and date.

**Handover inventory:** permits/inspection closeout, approved change orders, commissioning/test records, equipment model and serial, shutoffs and access, consumables/filters, manufacturers' manuals, finish schedules and spares, concealed-work photographs, warranties and service contacts.

**Functional acceptance examples:** water fixture leakage under intended flow; HVAC delivered performance/control states; exhaust termination; cabinet/door fit; electrical panel/controls labeling. Use qualified inspectors and prescribed methods when required; owners should not conduct hazardous energized tests.

**Legal boundary:** Final payment, lien waivers, retainage, warranties and disputes depend on contract and jurisdiction. The lecture offers recordkeeping principles, not a legal interpretation of specific terms.
