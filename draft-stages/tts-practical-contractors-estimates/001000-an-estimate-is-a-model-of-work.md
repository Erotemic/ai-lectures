# An Estimate Is a Model of Work

You invite three contractors to quote a roof repair. One offers two thousand dollars, another four thousand, and the third refuses to give a price until an additional inspection. Which is the best offer? We cannot know from those numbers. One contractor may propose sealing a visible joint; another may remove roofing to replace damaged flashing; the third may be concerned that the source of the leak is not yet established. An estimate is not merely a prediction of money. It is a compressed description of what someone believes the problem is and what work they intend to do.

Learning to read that description is a practical skill. It helps you avoid paying for work that does not solve the problem, and it helps good contractors communicate why a durable repair costs more than the cheapest visible patch.

## Define the problem before defining the solution

A homeowner often describes a symptom: "water enters above the window." A proposal may quietly translate that into a diagnosis: "recaulk window." But the water could arrive from flashing above, from cladding joints, from condensation, or from another exterior connection. When the diagnosis is uncertain, a sound scope may include investigation, documentation, temporary protection, and then a separately defined repair decision.

A scope of work should state what will be done, where it will be done, what materials or performance requirements apply, what is excluded, and how completion will be judged. If the proposal only says "fix leak," there is room for disagreement about what was promised. If it says "remove trim, inspect flashing and sheathing at identified window, report hidden damage, replace specified damaged components subject to an approved change order, and test or inspect water management," we have a much more useful agreement. The words need not be elaborate, but they should describe the actual work.

## Why bids can differ legitimately

Materials differ in quality, availability, warranty, specification and compatibility. Labor estimates differ with site access, hidden conditions, equipment, skill, scheduling and safety measures. A bid may include disposal, permits, protection of landscaping, cleanup, inspection fees and restoration of finishes, or omit some of them. A lower total can reflect efficiency, narrower scope, excluded risk, or a mistaken estimate. The total alone does not distinguish these cases.

Consider two bids for exterior painting. One includes cleaning, removal of loose paint, surface repair, primer on bare areas, two specified finish coats, protection of adjacent surfaces and cleanup. The other says "paint exterior" with no preparation or coating specification. Comparing their totals is not a price comparison for the same job. The missing details are part of the price difference and part of the durability question.

It is useful to request comparable written descriptions while allowing contractors to propose different methods. If a professional suggests another method, ask what failure mechanism it addresses, how success will be verified, and what assumptions differ from the other proposals. A thoughtful disagreement can be informative. It may reveal an uncertainty the cheapest quote ignored.

## Estimate, quote, allowance, and change order

An estimate often expresses an expected cost under assumptions. A fixed-price quote, time-and-materials arrangement, cost-plus agreement and unit-price contract allocate cost risk differently. The precise legal effect depends on contract language and governing law. An allowance is a budgeted amount for an element not yet selected or fully defined, such as fixtures or finish materials. If that item costs more, the final total may change according to the contract.

A change order documents an alteration to the agreed scope, schedule or price. Hidden conditions can make changes legitimate. But a procedure should establish who can authorize them and how the cost and time impact will be described before work proceeds, except for whatever immediate protective action is actually necessary. Without that discipline, the contract gradually stops describing the project.

Different payment schedules also distribute risk. An upfront deposit may be reasonable in some circumstances, but paying everything before work begins reduces leverage and creates exposure. On the other side, withholding payment for unrelated reasons can be unfair to a contractor who fulfilled the agreement. A clear milestone structure connects payment to verifiable progress or materials under terms permitted by local law. The Federal Trade Commission advises comparing written estimates, reading agreements carefully, checking credentials and avoiding pressure tactics. Specific deposit limits and cancellation rights depend on state law and the transaction.

## Licenses, insurance, and permits are different evidence

A contractor's license may establish that certain legal requirements have been met in a jurisdiction. It does not automatically prove expertise in every specialty or guarantee workmanship. Insurance is a separate matter; coverage types, limits, exclusions and current status should be checked against project needs. Permits and inspections are processes created by the relevant authority having jurisdiction and depend on the work. A manufacturer's certification or training credential addresses still other questions.

The right question is not just "Are you licensed?" It is "What license is required for this exact scope here, who holds it, and how can its status be verified?" Similarly, ask whether the company intends to use subcontractors, who supervises them, who is responsible for permits, and what inspections are included. Different states and municipalities divide those responsibilities differently.

A warranty also deserves specificity. A product manufacturer's warranty may cover a defective product but not consequential water damage or installation labor. A contractor's workmanship warranty is a different promise and depends on its terms and the business's ability to honor it. Ask what is covered, for how long, how claims are made, and what is excluded.

## Design acceptance before the last invoice

Suppose a contractor installs a sump pump. Completion should not be defined only as "pump is installed." Useful acceptance questions include whether the specified pump and controls were installed, how discharge is routed, whether the intended alarms or backup features function as agreed, and whether the operator receives documentation. But any performance testing must respect the actual system design and safe operating procedures. An acceptance check for a roof might rely on inspections and documentation rather than a homeowner improvising destructive water tests.

It helps to distinguish workmanship evidence from promises of perpetual performance. A roof can be installed according to its specified system and still be damaged later by a severe storm. No one can guarantee every future event. The agreement should identify what is being delivered and how it will be evaluated, rather than substitute an impossible guarantee for clear scope.

## How to handle a bid that seems too simple

Ask the contractor to identify the assumed cause, the materials and method, what hidden conditions could change the work, the expected duration, and what evidence will establish completion. Record the answers in writing. If the explanation is unclear, it may be because the contractor has not inspected enough, because the homeowner's request is ambiguous, or because the proposed scope is inadequate. Clarification protects both parties.

The same habits help when hiring a specialist for a project you cannot personally inspect. You can still require a coherent diagnosis, documented materials, traceable scope, required permits and acceptance evidence. Technical literacy does not mean pretending to be the trade expert. It means asking questions that expose the actual engineering and contractual choices.

A good estimate is an honest model of work under uncertainty. Learn to compare its assumptions, scope, risk allocation and verification, and the price becomes meaningful. Without those elements, the cheapest number may be the most expensive misunderstanding.

## Technical companion

**Bid comparison worksheet:** Problem statement; inspection performed; diagnosis and uncertainty; exact scope; exclusions; specified products/materials; required permits/inspections; protection and cleanup; hidden-condition/change-order procedure; schedule; payment terms; warranty; documented completion criteria. Compare like-for-like scopes before comparing totals.

**Source scope:** FTC consumer guidance recommends comparing written estimates, checking licensing/insurance, and scrutinizing contract terms and pressure tactics. Legal effects of estimates, cancellation rights, deposits, lien protections, permit requirements and credential verification are state- and transaction-specific; obtain the controlling local sources before relying on a rule.
