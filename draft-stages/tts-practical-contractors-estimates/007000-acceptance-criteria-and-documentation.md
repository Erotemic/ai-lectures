# 007000 — Acceptance Criteria and Documentation

**Track:** Contracts and Contractor Literacy. **Background:** Scopes, estimates, permits, change orders, payments, and warranties. **Purpose:** turn a promised repair into a verifiable result with a practical documentation trail.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

# What does 'done' mean?

A contractor says the work is finished. The new window looks straight, the trim is painted, and the invoice is ready. But nobody photographed the flashing before it was covered, and the interior wall develops a stain after the first heavy rain. The parties may now disagree over what was promised, what was inspected, and which conditions are excluded. A useful contract prevents some of this ambiguity by defining success before the wall is closed.

A scope of work describes what will be performed. Acceptance criteria describe the conditions under which the result can be judged complete. A documentation plan preserves the evidence. All three are necessary for work that cannot be understood by looking at the finished surface alone. They do not need to be elaborate, but they should correspond to the real failure modes of the job.

## Make the outcome observable

Take an exterior drainage repair. 'Fix the gutters' could mean cleaning debris, replacing hangers, changing downspout routes, installing new sections, or repairing fascia. A stronger scope identifies the locations, materials, and intended discharge path. Acceptance criteria might require secure attachments, continuous flow toward suitable outlets, and no observed discharge against the foundation under an agreed test condition. The criteria need to account for what can realistically be tested; a brief hose test is not proof of performance under every future storm.

For a window replacement, criteria may include operation, attachment, weather-control details, air sealing, and finish quality. Some of these are visible only before siding and trim are installed. A staged inspection or photograph record matters more here than a photograph of a clean final room. Inspections should be performed by people with appropriate qualifications where structural or regulated work is involved.

For a heating-system repair, 'runs' is not a sufficient performance description. The parties might specify that documented fault indications are resolved, operating checks follow manufacturer service procedures, air or water flow is verified where relevant, and controls respond as intended. A technician should record objective measurements with conditions rather than asserting that the unit 'seemed fine.'

## Write down exclusions and assumptions

A good acceptance standard also states what is outside scope. An exterior door project may not include repairing concealed subfloor damage until it is exposed. A contract can specify the discovery procedure and how a change order is approved. Without such a mechanism, a contractor may feel pressured to improvise and the customer may feel surprised by unavoidable extra work.

Assumptions matter when comparing bids. One quote may include disposal, replacement of damaged substrate, inspections, and restoration of surfaces. Another may include only the visible replacement item. The prices are not interchangeable. A bid comparison should reconcile these differences before deciding which offer is actually lower for the same outcome.

Pay attention to wording. 'Waterproof basement' is a broad promise unless the expected water exposure, system boundaries, and verification method are defined. 'Replace damaged board in the marked area and restore specified flashing continuity' is narrower and more testable. The right level of detail depends on the job; it need not turn every small task into an engineering specification.

## Evidence while the work is accessible

Construction destroys evidence as it progresses. A wall conceals piping and wiring. Siding hides flashings. Concrete covers reinforcement. Build a record at hold points: before work, after opening the assembly, after the hidden control layers are installed, and after final finishes. Photographs should include context, scale when useful, and an identifiable location. Close-ups without orientation are often hard to interpret later.

Collect documentation relevant to the job: material and equipment model identifiers, approved substitutions, inspection records, warranties, service settings, test results, and maintenance instructions. Record what was changed from the proposal and why. The goal is to support future maintenance and objective dispute resolution, not to create paperwork for its own sake.

An inspection report should say what was checked and under which conditions. If a water test was done for ten minutes at one location, record that. It does not establish full-year water resistance. If a compressor pressure test was performed at specified conditions, record those conditions and applicable manufacturer criteria. The most trustworthy record includes limitations as well as successes.

## Punch lists and final payment

A punch list is a record of incomplete or defective items identified near project completion. It should describe the location and required correction rather than simply say 'bad finish.' A photograph or agreed measurement makes it easier to resolve. Items can be categorized as safety-critical, functional, or cosmetic, with different urgency. Never accept concealed or safety-related work merely because the trim looks finished.

Payment timing, retainage, lien waivers, contractor licensing, and required inspections vary by contract and jurisdiction. The general consumer principle is not to make the final payment before required work is completed and reasonably reviewed. The Federal Trade Commission advises confirming satisfaction and retaining receipts and records. Local rules and a signed contract govern specific rights and duties; an acceptance checklist does not override them.

A warranty is also a promise with boundaries. It may apply to workmanship, an individual product, or both, and may contain exclusions and notification requirements. Record the responsible party, start date, coverage, and how to make a claim. A manufacturer's product warranty does not necessarily cover installation defects.

## A concrete scenario

Suppose a contractor is replacing a leaking shower. Before work, define what is being replaced, how the waterproofing layer will be installed and checked according to the chosen system, what inspection or flood testing is specified, and when photographs will be taken. Establish how concealed damage is handled. After completion, confirm the drain and fixtures operate, access to required records is preserved, and the finish meets the agreed scope. The final appearance is the last check, not the only one.

The underlying lesson is that quality can be specified and observed. A clear acceptance criterion does not express distrust. It tells everyone what the work is for and what evidence will demonstrate that it has been carried out.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Acceptance matrix template

| Item | Agreed requirement | When observable | Verification evidence | Responsible reviewer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Substrate | Condition assessed and discovered damage documented | After opening | Dated photographs, written finding | Contractor / qualified reviewer |
| Hidden layer | Manufacturer-specified assembly and continuity | Before concealment | Inspection, photographs | Qualified reviewer if required |
| Function | Defined test with operating conditions | Before completion | Test record and measured values | Appropriate party |
| Finish | Defined dimensions and appearance | At completion | Punch-list walk-through | Owner and contractor |

### Documentation exercise

Rewrite 'repair the leaky exterior door' into a scope, exclusions, one hidden-stage inspection, an operating check, and a water-control acceptance check. State what remains unknown until the opening is exposed.

**Review boundary:** Contracts and payment rules vary by jurisdiction. This is consumer-literacy education, not a legal opinion or substitute for licensed inspection and applicable building requirements.
