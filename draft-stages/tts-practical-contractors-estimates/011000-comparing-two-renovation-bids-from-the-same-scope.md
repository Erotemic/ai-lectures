# 011000 — Comparing Two Renovation Bids from the Same Scope

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two contractors visit a house to estimate a bathroom renovation. The first offers a price of sixteen thousand dollars. The second offers twenty-one thousand. Is the first five thousand dollars cheaper? Not necessarily. A quote is a price attached to promises, exclusions, assumptions, and a process for handling change. Until those are understood, the two numbers may not describe the same project.

Suppose both contractors received the same initial sketch. Contractor A's proposal includes demolition, new tile, a standard shower valve, and painting. Contractor B includes demolition, explicit waterproofing details, electrical work, permits, disposal, testing, and a named fixture schedule. A may be a good contractor with a concise document; B may have overpriced some items. But the apparent difference cannot be evaluated until both are mapped onto a common scope.

## Define a comparison unit

The first useful document is a scope matrix. Rows identify work that must happen: demolition, protection of adjacent rooms, waste disposal, framing repairs, substrate preparation, plumbing rough-in, waterproofing, tile, grout, ventilation, electrical, fixtures, final cleaning, permits, inspections, and closeout. Columns identify whether each contractor includes, excludes, assumes, or leaves each item unspecified. "Not mentioned" is not the same as "included." A missing item is a question, not an automatic accusation.

Specifications matter within the rows. "Waterproof shower" is a desired performance outcome. A stated system, substrate compatibility, transition details, drain connection, and documented verification method make the obligation more testable. "Install tile" does not identify the substrate, tile size, layout, waterproofing, transition, or lippage expectations. You need enough detail to compare work and later decide whether it was delivered.

## Separate fixed costs from variable allowances

An allowance is a placeholder for a selection not yet made, such as a vanity or tile. Suppose one estimate includes a five-hundred-dollar tile allowance and the other includes two thousand dollars. The totals are not comparable until the purchaser's realistic tile choice and any markup or labor consequences are accounted for. Ask whether the allowance covers material only, delivered cost, tax, waste factor, or installation. A unit-price provision can be useful when the quantity is uncertain, such as repair of a concealed damaged subfloor.

Contingency is different. It is a reserve for uncertainty rather than a purchase selection. A homeowner can have a personal contingency budget while the contractor separately identifies what conditions trigger a written change order. Treating every surprise as automatically included in a vaguely worded "contingency" invites disagreement.

## A lower price can have higher expected cost

Consider a hypothetical normalized comparison. Bid A is sixteen thousand but excludes permit fees estimated at eight hundred, electrical work at twelve hundred, and disposal at five hundred. It also carries a tile allowance one thousand below the intended selection. Under those assumptions, its comparable total is nineteen thousand five hundred before any change orders. Bid B at twenty-one thousand includes those elements. A remains fifteen hundred cheaper on this simplified basis—not five thousand—but the scope difference is now visible.

This is not a prediction of which contractor will perform better. Quality, warranty terms, schedule, exclusions, supervision, subcontractors, and contractor capacity still matter. And the estimates used for omitted work must be grounded in actual proposals, not made-up numbers presented as market prices. The exercise shows how to compare obligations, not how to declare a winner.

## Timing changes cost and risk

One proposal promises four weeks; another proposes seven. Ask how the sequence works. Waterproofing has preparation and curing requirements, inspections occur at particular stages, and hidden damage can stop downstream work. A schedule with every step stacked without time for inspection or material delivery may be less credible than a longer schedule with clear dependencies. Conversely, a contractor's proven crew organization may support a shorter plan. The calendar needs a causal explanation.

The payment schedule should correspond to identifiable progress and legally appropriate contract terms. A deposit or milestone payment is not by itself evidence of misconduct, but the amount, timing, and relation to delivered work deserve scrutiny. Rules concerning deposits, licenses, permits, cancellation, liens, and required written terms vary by jurisdiction. Verify them from the relevant state or local authority rather than treating a neighboring state's consumer guide as binding law.

## References are evidence, not proof

A license or registration number may be required in some jurisdictions and not others. Confirm it with the responsible authority if applicable. Verify insurance coverage appropriate to the work and understand who employs subcontractors. Ask about recent work similar in technical complexity. A reference from a satisfied customer can tell you about communication and workmanship, but cannot substitute for inspecting contract scope or confirming required insurance and permits.

If one proposal changes after clarifying questions, preserve the revised version and date. Do not compare an old price with a newly expanded scope. A bid comparison should be auditable: each adjustment should identify the underlying question, contractor response, unit, assumption, and effect on price or schedule. An unresolved line belongs in a column marked unresolved, not silently in an invented estimate.

## Choose the commitment, not the cheapest headline

A credible proposal identifies the result, compatible materials, work sequence, responsibility for hidden conditions, verification and acceptance, payment schedule, and procedure for change. Its value is partly that it reduces the number of disputes that otherwise occur when two reasonable people imagined different work.

The lesson is to make price comparisons fair before making them decisive. Normalize scope, expose assumptions, quantify allowance differences when possible, and retain uncertainty where the evidence is weak. Only then compare cost, quality, risk, schedule, and trustworthiness. The best procurement decision is not the lowest number written on page one; it is the clearest acceptable commitment for the work that actually needs to happen.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Scope comparison specimen

| Work element | Bid A | Bid B | Clarification needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disposal | Excluded | Included | Quantity, hauling, fees |
| Shower waterproofing | "Standard" | Named system | Substrate, transitions, test/acceptance |
| Tile allowance | $500 | $2,000 | Material-only? tax? labor? |
| Electrical | Excluded | Included | Circuit, permits, inspection |
| Hidden water damage | Not stated | Unit price | Approval method and measurement |

### Worked normalization

Illustrative, non-market figures: $16,000 + $800 permit + $1,200 electrical + $500 disposal + $1,000 tile-allowance shortfall = $19,500. Bid B $21,000 inclusive gives $1,500 nominal difference *under these assumptions*. Compare tax, markup, quantities, warranties and risk before drawing conclusions.

### Evidence and regulatory scope

Federal Trade Commission home-improvement guidance; New York Attorney General consumer information as an example of jurisdiction-specific rules; CSI/AIA documentation concepts for scope clarity. Requirements for deposit ceilings, cancellation and licensing are local. Verify with governing authorities before treating any proposal as legally sufficient.
