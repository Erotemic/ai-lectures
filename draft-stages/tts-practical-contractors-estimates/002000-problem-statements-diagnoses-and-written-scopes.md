# 002000 — Problem Statements, Diagnoses, and Written Scopes

**Track:** Contracts, Estimates, and Contractor Literacy. **Background:** Estimating, risk and contractor judgment. **Purpose:** explain how to specify work so competing bids describe comparable outcomes and uncertainty remains visible.

# What Exactly Are You Buying?

A homeowner notices water staining on a ceiling. One contractor proposes painting it. Another proposes replacing shingles above it. A third wants to inspect the flashing and attic before quoting a repair. The prices may be radically different, yet the bids do not necessarily disagree about the cost of the same job. They may be describing entirely different jobs.

A good scope of work begins by separating the observed problem from the suspected cause and the chosen intervention. “There is a brown stain near the upstairs window after wind-driven rain” is an observation. “The window flashing leaks” is a hypothesis. “Remove trim, repair the flashing and repaint” is a possible scope. Those statements belong in different parts of a decision record because each has a different level of certainty.

## State the problem so it can be tested

A problem statement should identify location, symptom, timing, severity, known context and what success would mean. It should avoid prescribing a method before the cause is understood. For example: “Water appears on the lower inside corner of the west-facing window during prolonged rain with strong wind; it does not appear during dry cold spells. Determine the entry path and propose a durable correction.”

That statement gives a contractor room to investigate. By contrast, “Caulk the window” presupposes that an exposed joint is the problem. It may produce a low estimate for an irrelevant repair. A narrower prescribed task can be legitimate when the diagnosis is already established, but the buyer should be explicit about that certainty.

## Diagnosis is work

Inspection, testing, selective opening and engineering analysis consume time and may deserve their own scope and price. If a wall is closed and the failure mechanism cannot be seen, nobody should pretend to know every hidden condition. A diagnostic phase can include agreed access, photographs, moisture measurements, a written finding, alternative explanations and a recommendation for the next decision.

The result may be that several repair options remain plausible. A diagnostic report should distinguish confirmed observations, reasoned inferences and unresolved questions. It is acceptable to say, “The evidence suggests this route but the concealed condition is not yet visible.” That statement is often more useful than unwarranted certainty.

## The scope turns a decision into obligations

Once a repair method is chosen, a written scope should specify location, limits of demolition, materials or performance requirements, preparation, sequence where it matters, protection of adjacent work, waste removal, restoration, testing, cleanup and documentation. It should state what is included and what is excluded. Who supplies access? Who removes a cabinet? Who protects a floor? Who repairs opened drywall? Are finishes returned to match existing surfaces, or merely left paint-ready?

These details are not decorative. They are where costs hide. Two estimates may differ because one includes waste disposal, permits, finish restoration and testing while the other prices only the visible installation. Comparing their totals without normalizing scope is not a meaningful comparison.

## Quantities and unknown conditions

Some tasks can be quoted by fixed scope: replace a specified fixture, provided supply and connections are sound. Other tasks contain unknown quantities, such as concealed rot behind siding. A useful contract can define a base scope plus unit prices or conditional allowances for work that cannot reasonably be determined until opening. But an allowance is not a magic fixed price; it is a placeholder with defined assumptions and a process for adjustment.

Change orders should identify the discovered condition, proposed change, price or pricing method, schedule effect and required authorization. The American Bar Association's consumer-oriented contract guidance specifically discusses making change orders written and attaching them to the original agreement. The practice creates a record of what changed and why.

## Define acceptance before work starts

A repair proposal becomes stronger when it specifies how completion will be judged. For a door replacement, acceptance might include smooth operation, correct clearances and weather sealing. For a plumbing branch, it might include an appropriate pressure/leak test and restoration of finishes. For network cabling, a specified certification test might matter more than simply observing a blinking light. The test must suit the system and the standard, not merely produce an impressive-looking certificate.

No one can reasonably guarantee that every future storm will behave like the one that revealed a leak. But the scope can require specified flashing details, inspection photographs and a defined water test where appropriate. Good acceptance criteria are observable, relevant and within the contractor's responsibility.

## Comparing proposals fairly

Suppose three bids arrive. Create a table with the same rows: diagnosis assumptions, work limits, materials, disposal, restoration, permits, inspection, schedule, access requirements, exclusions, warranty and test evidence. Fill the table with what each proposal actually says. Blank cells are questions; they are not permission to assume inclusion. The lowest price may be the best value when the scopes are comparable, but it has no special authority when one proposal omits significant work.

The Federal Trade Commission recommends written estimates and careful contract review, and advises against choosing solely by the lowest bid. It also emphasizes checking credentials and contract requirements, which vary by jurisdiction. Do not mistake a professional-sounding estimate for evidence that a license, insurance policy or permit is valid. Verify these details with the appropriate sources.

## Negotiation is about uncertainty, too

A useful conversation with a contractor may be less about shaving five percent from a price and more about assigning responsibility for unknown conditions. If the old pipe breaks when disconnected, what happens? If the wall contains damage outside the agreed removal limit, who documents it and who authorizes the added work? If an inspection discovers noncompliant existing work, is correction included? These questions can prevent an apparent bargain from turning into an open-ended obligation.

There are limits to written certainty. Renovation is performed on a real, imperfect building. The goal is not to ban every change; it is to make changes explicit and traceable.

## A specification is a hypothesis about success

Return to the stained ceiling. The buyer wants a durable end to water entry, not merely a paint application. A strong process identifies symptoms, tests plausible causes, chooses a remedy, specifies the work, and records evidence that the chosen remedy was carried out. If the cause remains uncertain, the scope says so.

Once you can distinguish problem, diagnosis, intervention and acceptance, an estimate becomes more than a price. It is a model of what two parties believe will be done, what might change, and how they will know the work has succeeded. That is the basis for a fair comparison and a much better chance of getting the outcome you intended to buy.

## Technical companion: scope template and evidence

**Four-part statement:** `Observed condition` (what, where, when, evidence) -> `Hypotheses and diagnostic work` (what is known/unknown) -> `Specified intervention` (materials, limits, preparation, responsibility, exclusions) -> `Acceptance` (tests, restoration, documents, completion record). Keep observations distinct from inferred causes.

**Bid comparison columns:** Base scope; hidden-conditions assumptions; unit prices/allowances; permits; materials and substitutions; demolition; restoration; testing; schedule; warranty; payment milestones; exclusions; change-order process. Review local law and written agreement requirements with appropriate expertise.

**References:** FTC *How to Avoid a Home Improvement Scam* recommends multiple written estimates and careful contract review. ABA *The Law & Your Home: What Should a Contract Include?* describes scope, materials, permit responsibility and change orders. These are public consumer/legal-education references, not a substitute for jurisdiction-specific legal advice.
