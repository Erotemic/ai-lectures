# Four Questions Hidden Inside 'Are You Licensed?'

A homeowner asks a contractor, 'Are you licensed and insured?' The contractor says yes, and both move on. The exchange sounds responsible, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. Licensed for which trade, in which place, and under which business name? What kind of insurance, with which limits and exclusions? Does this particular job require a permit? Who will request the inspections and correct any problems they reveal?

The words license, registration, insurance, permit, and inspection describe different systems. They are not interchangeable badges of general trustworthiness. Each answers a narrower question, and the answer can vary with state, county, city, trade, building type, and scope of work.

## A license concerns authorization, not a promise of quality

A trade license typically concerns legal authority to perform a defined kind of work under a jurisdiction's rules. Requirements may involve training, examinations, supervision, bonding, or experience; they can differ markedly among places. A business registration may establish that an entity is recorded with the government without proving trade-specific skill. A contractor can be legally registered and still lack a particular occupational authorization where one is required.

Verification is more useful than merely asking for a number. Which licensing body maintains the record? Is the license active? Does its scope cover the work? Does the name match the entity on the contract? Does the work require a designated supervisor or a licensed subcontractor? These checks should be made with the issuing authority or its current official lookup, not solely from a photograph of a credential.

It is equally important not to assert that every home-improvement contractor must hold one statewide general license. For example, New York has local jurisdictions with particular home-improvement licensing requirements, alongside trade-specific rules. A rule applying in one county may not apply in the next. The generalizable lesson is to locate the actual authority before concluding that a particular credential is mandatory or optional.

## Insurance addresses selected financial risks

General liability insurance may respond to certain covered claims for property damage or bodily injury. Workers' compensation concerns injury to workers under applicable law. Commercial auto and professional liability cover different exposures. Policy limits, exclusions, endorsements, effective dates, and the identity of the insured matter. The word 'insured' by itself does not tell you whether a loss related to your project would be covered.

A certificate of insurance can provide information about coverage reported at the time of issue. It is not the insurance policy, and does not automatically give the homeowner the rights of an insured party. For larger projects, the owner may ask about additional-insured status, contractual indemnity, and the appropriate verification method. Those are legal and insurance questions, not just administrative checkboxes. A contractor's assurance is useful, but important details are best confirmed with the relevant insurer or licensed adviser when stakes warrant.

## The permit attaches to the project

A building permit typically authorizes work within the governing authority's process. It may require plans, calculations, inspections, or revisions. The permit concerns a *particular scope* at a *particular address*; possessing a contractor license does not mean every job can begin without one. Some minor work may be exempt. Structural changes, certain electrical and plumbing work, and other alterations may trigger requirements depending on adopted local rules.

Who pulls the permit matters because that determines who is listed as responsible in parts of the process and who receives notices. A contractor who asks the homeowner to pull a permit to avoid the contractor's obligations deserves careful scrutiny of the local rules and proposed contract. An exemption should be verified, not guessed from the small visible size of the project.

## Inspections are milestones, not magic guarantees

An inspection checks defined elements at a certain stage against applicable requirements. Rough framing or rough electrical work may need to be visible before walls are closed. A final inspection may assess other conditions after completion. Passing one inspection does not prove every concealed detail was examined or that all workmanship will remain durable. But skipping required inspections can remove a critical independent checkpoint and create legal or resale complications.

It is useful to build the inspection sequence into the written work plan. Identify work that must remain accessible, who schedules each inspection, who addresses corrections, and which signoffs are required before final payment or occupancy. If the contractor says 'the inspector will catch everything,' ask which inspections are actually included and what they cover. Inspectors examine against an official scope, not every personal preference the owner may have about finish quality.

## Connect the paperwork to the real work

Imagine hiring a crew to open a load-bearing wall. The written scope should identify the structural design responsibility, the actual framing and connection details, permit obligations, inspections, protection of adjacent finishes, and what happens if hidden conditions differ from assumptions. The contractor's trade credentials and insurance are relevant, but they do not replace a sound structural plan. Likewise, a permitted installation with poor water management can still fail in service if its details are deficient.

Good contractor literacy means separating independent claims. Is this firm authorized? Is an identified risk insured? Is this work approved for a defined scope? Was the necessary stage inspected? Has the owner received the required documents? These questions can be checked one by one. The result is a much stronger decision than accepting a single reassuring phrase.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Verification matrix

| Term | Question it answers | Who to verify with |
|---|---|---|
| Trade license | May this person/business perform this regulated trade here? | Issuing licensing authority |
| Business registration | Is this entity properly registered as claimed? | State/local business registry |
| General liability | What covered claims and limits may apply? | Insurance agent/carrier, policy documents |
| Workers' compensation | How are worker injuries covered where required? | Applicable insurance/workers' compensation authority |
| Permit | Is this scope authorized under the local process? | Local building/code department |
| Inspection | What was checked, when, and what remains open? | Permit record, inspection reports |

### Location-sensitive scope

New York Attorney General consumer guidance illustrates why city/county licensing rules must be checked locally. It should **not** be generalized to every U.S. location. Verify current licensing, bonding, insurance, permit, and inspection rules with the actual jurisdiction; legal review may be appropriate for major contracts. A certificate of insurance does not by itself alter policy coverage or certify contractor competence.

### Contract exercise

Write a one-paragraph scope for a window replacement identifying required water-management details, who identifies permit requirements, who obtains approvals, who schedules inspections, and what documentation counts as completion. Keep price allowances and hidden-condition change orders separate from credential verification.
