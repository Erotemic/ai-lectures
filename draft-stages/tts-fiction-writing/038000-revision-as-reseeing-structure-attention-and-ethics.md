# Revision as Re-Seeing: Structure, Attention, and Ethics

At six in the morning the heating system fails in a neighborhood greenhouse. The seedlings will freeze if nobody opens the equipment room and starts the backup heater. Leila, the volunteer on duty, sees the equipment room padlocked. She concludes that the coordinator, Tomas, has locked everyone out because he is angry about last week's argument over budgets. She breaks the lock and starts the heater. Later Tomas arrives, furious, and accuses her of putting the building at risk. By evening, the plants are safe, and neither will speak to the other.

This is a draft. It has an initiating problem, a choice, a consequence, and some room for a deeper relationship. It also has a dangerous shortcut. The story tells us what Leila concludes about Tomas, but gives us little reason to believe her or to understand his objection. In its present form, he may be no more than an antagonist placed at the door so that Leila can look decisive. We could polish the dialogue for an hour and never fix that problem.

Revision begins when we stop assuming that our first arrangement of the material is the story itself. We ask what the draft has made possible, what it prevents, and what more truthful or interesting arrangement might emerge. Some changes are local: substituting a verb, deleting an explanation, correcting an awkward rhythm. Others are structural: changing whose account begins the work, what knowledge arrives when, whether the apparently victorious decision actually resolves the conflict, or whether the story has mistaken its subject.

There is no universally mandated sequence of drafts. Writers' methods differ; different pieces require different kinds of labor. But it helps to distinguish kinds of diagnosis. Otherwise we can spend days fixing sentences that belong to a scene the story no longer needs, or dismantle a working structure because a reader was confused by one pronoun.

Three approaches provide useful pressure: close attention to the reader's changing experience; structural work on the relation between parts; and an ethical examination of the people and assumptions the draft has made too convenient. These are not competing doctrines that must be ranked from best to worst. They operate at different scales, and their findings can conflict.

## First ask what the reader thinks is happening

George Saunders's *A Swim in a Pond in the Rain*, published in 2021, reflects on the craft of short fiction through close readings of Russian stories. Its teaching stance repeatedly attends to what a reader knows, expects, and revises during the act of reading. We should not transform that into a magical rule that every sentence must deliver a surprise. The useful principle is to test the actual progression of a reader's understanding rather than merely checking whether the author has included all the planned events.

Let's follow the greenhouse draft sentence by sentence. We see the failed heater. We learn about the seedlings. We encounter a padlock. Then the narrator says Leila concludes that Tomas locked the room because he is angry. At that point, a reader may take her inference as reliable if the narrator has given no reason to distinguish interpretation from fact. The draft then presents Tomas's fury as confirmation. The author's intended ambiguity may never reach the reader, because the text has trained us to agree with Leila too early.

A local revision could change the sentence to: 'The padlock was new. Tomas had bought a box of them after last week's argument, and Leila remembered the way he had set the box down between them.' Now her suspicion has an observable basis. But it remains a suspicion. We could also include a detail that complicates it: the padlock is attached to a tag from the city's electrical inspector. This may be enough to shift the reader's stance from judgment to investigation.

Notice what this local change does and does not do. It creates a meaningful uncertainty. It does not by itself determine the best structure for the story, or settle whether the two volunteers deserve equal sympathy. If the whole narrative remains an exercise in making Tomas look guilty until the last page, the new detail may simply become a more effective trick.

A useful reader-attention test is to stop at the end of every scene and write down three things: what a reader can reasonably know, what a reader may currently believe, and what question encourages them to continue. These categories must be kept separate. Authors often mistake the question they intend to pose for the one the prose has actually posed.

You can perform the test yourself after a break from the manuscript, but a fresh reader can be especially revealing. Ask them to mark the moment they first suspected Tomas and the moment they became willing to reconsider. Do not ask whether the story is 'clear' in the abstract. Ask what they thought occurred and why. Their interpretation is evidence about the text's effects, not a command to obey every preference.

## Then find the structural problem

John McPhee is primarily a writer of nonfiction, not a theorist of fiction. His essays 'Structure' and 'Draft No. 4,' published in *The New Yorker* in 2013, offer a precise outside comparison because they distinguish discovering an arrangement for material from revising words within that arrangement. His own account concerns researching and composing nonfiction, so we should borrow the craft distinction without treating his workflow as an empirical claim about how every novelist must work.

Suppose we had planned three scenes. First Leila finds the lock. Second Tomas confronts her. Third they sit in the greenhouse at sunset, staring at seedlings that survived. The sequence works if the story is about one morning's crisis. But what if the actual subject is the way an institution distributes authority among volunteers who bear different risks? Then the opening may need to change. Tomas might have been instructed to lock the equipment room because the backup heater had been tagged unsafe. Leila might know that the seedlings were purchased with money the volunteers cannot replace. A city electrician might be expected at noon, too late to save the trays. Each person confronts a real cost.

We could start on the previous afternoon, when Tomas receives the inspection notice. Or start after Leila breaks the lock, then revisit the earlier warning. Or keep the opening but insert a scene in which Leila checks the notice and must decide whether to disobey it. Changing structure changes what counts as the choice. One version is about impulsive courage; another about deciding what a repair community owes to its own safety; another about the miscommunication of responsibility.

A structural revision should be described in terms of a change in the story's actual meaning, not simply the rearrangement of its sections. Moving the final confrontation to the beginning creates an effect only if it changes the reader's questions. Adding three explanatory flashbacks may make the situation more complete but reduce the force of the cold morning.

One diagnostic tool is a reverse outline. After drafting, write one sentence for each scene describing what changes for the characters or reader. Do not use the original outline; use the manuscript as it exists. If two consecutive scenes produce exactly the same change, decide whether their repetition deepens a pattern or merely duplicates information. If a scene contains the only evidence for Tomas's view but occurs after the story has already settled him as a villain, examine whether it arrived too late to do its job.

A reverse outline can reveal that a subplot is essential even though it looks detachable. The custodian who refuses to lend Leila a tool may initially seem peripheral. But perhaps his refusal is the story's first demonstration that everyone has been asked to carry liability for decisions they cannot control. Remove him and the ethical problem becomes merely a quarrel between two personalities. Keep him, and the work acquires a third perspective not absorbed by either side.

## Ethics enters through craft choices

Matthew Salesses's *Craft in the Real World*, published in 2021, challenges the idea that familiar writing-workshop judgments are culturally neutral. His analysis asks whose expectations about character, plot, conflict, and believability become the defaults by which other narratives are measured. That does not mean every criticism is merely a matter of taste. It means we should ask who is being imagined as the reader and which ways of telling have been silently treated as normal.

Our greenhouse draft may contain a subtler distortion. Leila is a volunteer who can leave the greenhouse when the morning shift ends. Tomas is a paid coordinator who is legally responsible for the equipment. Perhaps the electricity inspector is a contract worker whose signature can end their employment if the machinery is used while tagged unsafe. We can write a story in which Leila's decision is admirable. But if we treat Tomas's fears as mere cowardice without showing the conditions of his job, we have not established that verdict; we have assumed it.

Ethical revision is not the instruction to make all characters equally right or equally likable. It is a discipline of distinguishing the point of view through which we tell a scene from the full possibilities of the world it depicts. Leila may reasonably believe Tomas cares more about rules than plants. We may choose to stay inside her belief. But then the narrator should not accidentally present her interpretation as an established universal truth unless the story intends that effect.

Try an exercise in which you tell Tomas's morning without using the words 'bureaucracy,' 'cowardice,' or 'responsibility.' Show him receiving a call from the inspector, calculating the cost of a failed heater, remembering a previous electrical fire, and looking for a way to reach Leila before her shift. You may still conclude he behaved badly. His conduct might prove evasive or self-protective. But the conclusion will be earned from the actions rather than inherited from an epithet.

There is a trap here: writing a sympathetic history for every antagonist can become its own formula. Some characters choose cruelty. Some institutions systematically obstruct others. A story need not contrive an excuse for every harm. The question is whether the writer knows what the fictional people can see, what they cannot, and what has made their choices intelligible within the world.

## When the diagnoses disagree

Imagine that a test reader says the story is most exciting when Tomas remains suspicious until the confrontation. The reverse outline says we need his inspection notice earlier to make the decision coherent. An ethical reading says the current draft stacks evidence so strongly against the coordinator that his later explanation becomes implausible. What should we do?

Not every demand can be satisfied at once. We need to choose the story's governing experience. If our aim is suspense about the cause of the lock, we can withhold the notice while giving enough observable evidence that the reader later recognizes the explanation as plausible. If our aim is moral pressure at the moment of decision, we may show the notice immediately and move suspense from 'what happened?' to 'what will Leila choose, knowing the risks?' The ethical concern does not necessarily require earlier information; it requires that the fictional world not be simplified merely to protect our chosen effect.

This is why revision cannot be reduced to a checklist. 'Increase tension' and 'make characters complex' are not executable instructions without a theory of what the story is trying to accomplish. A scene can become more tense by becoming shorter, or by letting a difficult silence endure. A character can become complex through additional history, or through one choice that contradicts the narrator's confident description. We must diagnose the mechanism before selecting the intervention.

## An actual multiscale revision

Let's rewrite the decisive moment. In the first draft, Leila breaks the lock because she believes Tomas has acted out of spite. She starts the heater and saves the plants. That is a clear trajectory, but the story may not be much more than a moral cartoon.

In a second draft, she discovers the inspection notice after she has cut the lock. Now she must decide whether to stop. This produces a retrospective shock: an act that looked heroic is suddenly dangerous. But this version still leaves the decisive choice in the past. Once she realizes what she has done, the only remaining question may be whether Tomas will forgive her.

In a third draft, she sees the inspection notice first. She phones the emergency number, waits while the recorded system repeats that requests are handled in order, and watches frost appear on the soil. She could move the trays, but the only available van belongs to a volunteer who is caring for a sick child and cannot come. She could use a portable heater, but there is one outlet and she does not know whether the wiring can support it. She cuts the lock only after testing the visible alternatives. Tomas arrives as the heater begins to run. Instead of shouting, he asks whether she has disconnected the ventilation fan, because it was the fan motor that failed inspection, not the heater.

This version is not automatically superior. It is more complex, and that complexity might crowd the story. But it puts causes, constraints, and decisions into a shared world. The confrontation now changes the reader's understanding of the technical problem. Leila and Tomas might have been able to cooperate, yet their communication failed. The seedlings are still alive, but the damaged lock has become evidence of a fracture in their ability to work together.

A fourth draft could be quieter. Keep the technical matter clear. Let Tomas help Leila carry trays into the warm corridor. Let them finish before speaking about the lock. The emotional climax becomes the fact that they can still cooperate physically while finding it difficult to trust each other's judgment. Whether this scene belongs in the story depends on whether the central concern is the morning's risk or the lasting shape of their partnership.

## Testing the result without polishing away the discovery

After a substantial change, return to the beginning. Does the opening still prepare the reader for the story that now exists? A manuscript can retain an arresting first paragraph that promises a mystery even though the later work has become a study of mutual dependence. That mismatch is often more damaging than a weak sentence. Likewise, the final paragraph may need to stop explaining a lesson the new structure allows readers to infer.

Read aloud for local rhythm only after you understand the large change. Listen for repetitions of information, sentences that announce a motive already visible in action, and places where pronouns or time shifts require unwanted effort. A smooth sentence is not always a good sentence; sometimes an abrasive one exposes the friction a character is experiencing. But if readers struggle simply to identify who has entered the room, the prose may be using their attention for the wrong purpose.

Make an experimental copy and alter one major variable at a time. Change the starting scene, or the point of view, or the reason the lock was installed. Record what that change makes possible and what it destroys. You do not have to keep the alternative, but its existence allows you to stop defending the first draft as if it were inevitable.

For feedback, ask three kinds of questions. A factual one: what did you believe caused the lock, and at what point? An experiential one: where did your curiosity sharpen or fade? An interpretive one: whose motives seemed legible, and whose remained a convenient mystery? Different readers may disagree. Their disagreements help reveal where the manuscript is ambiguous in a productive way and where it merely omits a crucial cause.

Finally, ask what the revision has cost you. Perhaps the simple heroism of the first draft was appealing. The new story may make the protagonist harder to endorse, and the coordinator harder to dismiss. You may miss the cleaner moral curve. That loss is not automatically a problem. It may be the price of discovering the story you were capable of writing after you understood its people more fully.

In the final scene, Leila finds a small green shoot surviving in a tray they nearly abandoned. She reaches for her phone to send Tomas a picture, then pauses. The photograph would show one successful plant and hide the trays they lost. She takes a second photograph, wider, and sends both. Nothing is reconciled by this act. But the revision has found an image that makes the story's central difficulty tangible: the difference between a pleasing account of what happened and an account another person might be able to trust.

**Attribution boundary:** Saunders's 2021 book is a craft reference for reader attention. McPhee's 2013 essays are accounts of nonfiction composition; their distinction between structure and line-editing is borrowed here as a comparison, not as a direct theory of fiction. **Practice:** Reverse-outline a draft and annotate what a reader knows after each scene. Rewrite one decisive choice to show two real alternatives. Then test whether the revision complicates or unfairly pre-decides the reader's moral judgment.
