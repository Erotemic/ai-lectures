# Saunders and the Beat Sheet: Two Scales of Revision

You have finished a draft. It is a story about Mara, who wants to prevent her town from closing a public bathhouse. Her campaign has a beginning, middle, and end. She discovers a suspicious repair estimate, loses allies, confronts the council, and faces the consequences. Yet when you read it back, something is wrong. The first chapter feels slow; the middle feels busy but inert; the public hearing, which should be decisive, is dull. What should you do?

One school of craft would begin with structure. Map the story's major turns. Identify what Mara wants, when she commits, when the central situation changes, and where the consequences become unavoidable. A popular version of this approach is Blake Snyder's Save the Cat! beat sheet, devised for screenplays and later adapted for novels by Jessica Brody. Another writer might use John Truby's character-and-moral-design approach, or a scene-change diagnosis influenced by Story Grid. None of these systems is identical to the others, but each provides an explicit vocabulary for discussing the functions of story parts.

Another approach begins much smaller. Read the draft as though you have not written it. Notice the moment your attention rises or falls. Ask what the reader believes after the first sentence, what the second changes, and why the next paragraph belongs exactly there. George Saunders describes a highly iterative revision process informed by this kind of real-time response. His A Swim in a Pond in the Rain, published in 2021, studies the Russian short story partly through the reader's changing expectations. Saunders also cautions against treating his personal process as a universal method. The useful principle is not a fixed revision rule but an effort to respond attentively to what is actually on the page.

A disagreement seems to appear. The structural writer says, first repair the architecture. The Saunders-like reviser says, first notice what the prose does to a mind as it is read. But these two methods measure different things. One asks whether the whole story moves. The other asks whether that movement is perceptible and interesting one step at a time. Let us compare them without caricaturing either.

## The structure can be right while the reading is wrong

Our plot outline is perfectly serviceable. In chapter one, Mara learns the bathhouse will close. In chapter two, she organizes a petition. At the end of the first section, the council refuses to reconsider. Mara obtains a copy of the repair estimate and begins an investigation. In the middle, her allies divide over whether preservation is affordable. Late in the story, her daughter discovers that one of the estimates includes a genuine safety problem. At the hearing, Mara must decide whether to expose the inflated costs while admitting the real danger. In the ending, the building closes, but a temporary public service is negotiated.

We can label the major moments and make a handsome diagram. That diagram helps reveal whether choices cause consequences and whether the early questions are eventually addressed. It cannot tell us whether the second page is excruciatingly repetitive. The opening might spend six paragraphs stating that Mara is angry about the closure without allowing her to do anything with that anger. A scene may occupy the correct structural position while withholding the information that would make its action matter.

Read this invented opening:

Mara was upset about the closure of the bathhouse. Many people in town were upset too. The bathhouse was very old and had been important for years. She had many memories of going there with her mother. The closure would have serious consequences for the neighborhood. Mara decided that she should do something. She picked up her phone and called the council office.

The sequence is clear, and every fact is potentially relevant. But the sentences largely repeat an abstract account of the same situation. After the first sentence, the reader knows Mara is upset. The second provides a broad generalization but no new pressure. The third announces historical importance without making it palpable. The fourth offers the beginning of a relationship but no distinct memory. The fifth repeats the assertion that the situation matters. The sixth and seventh finally move to action, yet the action feels generic because we do not know what has tipped Mara into making the call.

A structural diagnosis might say the inciting incident is weak. That is fair, but too broad to guide a sentence-level repair. The problem is not only that something dramatic needs to happen. It is that the prose does not help the reader construct a changing picture.

Try another opening:

The notice was taped to the bathhouse door, over the height chart Mara's mother had painted for children who wanted to know when they were tall enough for the deep pool. By noon, someone had drawn a black line through the word temporary. Mara peeled the notice away. On its back, in pencil, was a list of names she recognized from the early-morning shift. Her mother's name was the last one. The woman at the desk said, almost apologetically, that the list had been there for years.

This version has not yet introduced a major reversal or a clear plan. But it creates a succession of adjustments. The notice makes closure concrete. The height chart ties the public space to a private memory. The altered word suggests the town is already arguing about what the closure means. The names introduce a social history and a question. The clerk's remark reframes our assumption that the list was recently made. By the time Mara acts, there are things to investigate and feelings that have not been named for us.

The second opening also has weaknesses. If the list of names never matters again, it is a manufactured hook. If the clerk cannot plausibly know its history, her line is only a device to distribute mystery. A close reader's interest is not a reliable certificate of long-term value. It is one measurement in a larger design process.

## The reading can be lively while the structure is wrong

Now imagine that every scene is written like the second opening. Each has a fresh object, a small surprise, and a vivid exchange. Nevertheless, Mara visits the same council office four times. Each visit produces a new document, and each document leads to another visit. The reader is continually stimulated but does not sense a developing problem. A clever sentence can keep us moving through a chapter whose underlying causal movement is circular.

Here the structural diagnosis becomes indispensable. Draw a line from each scene to the change it produces. After the first office visit, Mara learns that the grant was never requested. After the second, she learns that requesting it would require a matching local contribution. After the third, the council agrees to accept evidence of alternative funding. If the fourth merely confirms a fact established in the second, the scene may be redundant. If each visit yields a more interesting clue but none changes Mara's decisions, the novel could become a corridor of discoveries without commitments.

A framework can make the defect obvious. The plot's middle has no meaningful shift from gathering information to bearing responsibility for a choice. The fix might not be to add a fight or a betrayal. It might be to give Mara evidence that the building is unsafe and force her to decide whether to disclose it publicly. From that moment, collecting facts is no longer cost-free. Her campaign's argument could harm the people she intended to help. This creates a new set of future possibilities for both plot and prose.

The distinction matters. A story is encountered linearly by a reader, but it is also an organized whole. Readers may notice a wonderful sentence and then lose patience with the chapter's repetition. They may forgive a dull line when they are deeply invested in the surrounding situation. The relative importance of local and global problems changes from draft to draft.

## An actual two-scale revision pass

Let us build a repeatable experiment, without treating it as a law. First, read the complete draft without changing it. Make a simple map of major decisions and changes in the central question. For each scene, note what is different afterward. Also write down the promise made to the reader: is this mainly a political investigation, a family story, or a communal portrait? If your opening poses a concrete question, find where the book pays attention to it again.

Second, choose one scene that occupies an important place in the map. Read it one sentence at a time. After each sentence, stop and state what the reader can reasonably infer. This is not a demand for twists in every line. You may be clarifying where someone stands, allowing a feeling to register, or preparing an object to gain meaning later. The question is whether the sentence's placement helps the reader experience the scene.

Suppose the council chair says: The public cannot enter the old boiler room because it contains dangerous equipment. Mara asks for the inspection report. The chair refuses. This is the scene's entire dramatic mechanism. A fine structural purpose exists: it blocks Mara and makes her suspicious. Yet it may not be the best scene.

Try giving the chair a practical reason for the refusal. The report contains the names of employees who complained about safety hazards and fear retaliation from a private contractor. Mara has to decide whether to insist on access anyway. Suddenly we have more than a barrier. The council chair may be protecting someone; Mara's demand for transparency has a cost; the reader must revise an easy alignment of hero against institution. The local improvement creates an alteration in the larger plot, because a later exposure of the report now threatens vulnerable workers.

This is how the two scales can work together. Line-level curiosity reveals a moral complication. Structural analysis shows where that complication must echo. The chapter's improved exchange becomes a later consequence. A sentence that seemed minor changes the novel's architecture.

The converse can also happen. An outline reveals that Mara must confront a real maintenance danger before the vote. The writer returns to an earlier scene and plants a quiet observation: a maintenance worker listening to a rattling pump while the bathhouse children laugh nearby. That detail is not a clue inserted simply to satisfy a checklist. It can carry ordinary human attention in the moment and later acquire a second significance. A global decision guides local prose without dictating its wording.

## Templates are hypotheses, not evidence

Some craft discussions overstate the case for structural templates. A story may have a recognizable midpoint or a final confrontation. That does not demonstrate that every effective novel is governed by precisely the same events at fixed proportions. The language of beats is useful partly because it organizes common problems of commitment, escalation, reversal, and consequence. It becomes less useful when a writer treats the number of beats as an empirical law that should overrule the actual material.

It is equally easy to romanticize intuition. One writer may possess strong editorial instincts developed through years of reading, drafting, and revision. Another may experience a lively first draft that conceals circular structure. Saunders's own statements about his method are full of qualifications. He presents it as a changing personal practice, not as a cure for the limits of planning. His emphasis on a reader's moment-by-moment response should encourage experiment rather than deference to his authority.

Both types of diagnosis can be wrong. A structural critic may propose a turning point that makes the story more conventional and less true to its subject. A line editor may remove a deliberate pause that the reader needs before a difficult scene. The point of trying a method is to generate a more precise question: What experience is the story trying to make possible, and is this arrangement of scenes and sentences delivering it?

Sometimes the right answer is to preserve a slow passage. A quiet scene in which Sefa inventories unused towels might seem to interrupt Mara's campaign. If the novel is also about forms of labor that public decisions fail to count, that scene may be central to its art. Its value could be missed by an outline that sees only Mara's goal. Equally, it could be missed by a first-page attention test if the passage's meaning depends on a pattern established across the whole book. A writer has to decide which kinds of attention the work is asking for.

## A practical editorial protocol

Here is a way to combine these approaches without creating a bureaucratic revision ritual. On a first pass, identify the story's main changes and promises. On a second pass, read a single representative scene as a first-time reader, marking the exact points where you become uncertain, bored, surprised, or moved. On a third pass, revise the scene, then ask whether the change makes later scenes stronger or obsolete. Do not repeat the protocol if it is not helping. Use it as a diagnostic instrument, not an obligation.

We can test our bathhouse story at both levels. The chapter map tells us that Mara must move from seeking evidence to deciding what she owes others. The line reading reveals that the clerk's hesitation feels more interesting than a surprise legal document. We follow that hesitation and discover that the clerk has quietly helped families retrieve their belongings. The new fact changes Mara's understanding of the institution and gives the clerk a life beyond obstruction. It also complicates the ending: an exposed injustice does not restore the everyday acts that held the place together.

The revision has become more than tightening prose or obeying beats. It has changed what the story knows. A successful structural map can help a writer discover where a decision belongs. A sensitive reading practice can reveal what the decision actually means. The two methods are not rival machines for manufacturing fiction. They are instruments with different resolutions.

And this is the final question to ask of any craft theory: Does it make you more able to notice the particular thing the manuscript is trying to become? If the answer is yes, borrow the instrument. If it begins to erase the work's discoveries, put it down. The reader does not encounter a template. The reader encounters one sentence, then another, until the whole arrangement of them has changed what can be imagined.

## Companion (display only)

**Interpretive boundary:** The comparison juxtaposes methods from different authors rather than claiming Saunders publicly debated every named plot system. Beat sheets and reader-response revision are treated as tools with different scales, not universal rules. All scenes are original.

**Practice:** Map the central decisions and value changes in a complete draft. Then perform a sentence-by-sentence first-time-reader pass on one structurally pivotal scene. Revise, and check for consequences elsewhere. Retain changes only when they serve the work.
