# Psychological Realism and the Opacity of Other Minds

A daughter returns to the house where she grew up. Her mother has died. The house has been sold, and today she must hand over the keys. She pauses outside the kitchen because the light is still on. She knows that nobody is inside. She also knows the switches in this house have always needed an extra push.

What is happening in her mind?

An impatient novelist might answer: she is grieving. A more confident novelist might add that she has never accepted her mother's death. But these sentences explain the daughter from a position of authority. They may be correct as diagnoses of the author's plan and still fail to give a reader the experience of meeting this particular person. Perhaps she is not thinking about death at all. Perhaps she is thinking about the electricity bill. Perhaps, seeing the light, she is angry at the real-estate agent. Perhaps she wants the ordinary problem of a badly pressed switch to protect her from a thought she cannot yet bear.

The challenge of psychological realism is not simply to supply more interior detail. It is to make a character's mental life persuasive while remembering that human motives are not transparent, either to observers or to the people who have them. A novel can enter a mind in a way ordinary life cannot, yet a novelist must still choose what to reveal, what to interpret, and what to leave in productive uncertainty.

This problem connects late nineteenth-century psychological fiction, modernist experiments with consciousness, and contemporary arguments about the future of realism. It also offers a practical way to revise a scene without resorting to a list of feelings.

## Three ways to show a thought

Dorrit Cohn's 1978 study *Transparent Minds* provides a useful vocabulary for the presentation of fictional consciousness. Cohn distinguishes, among other techniques, a narrator's account of a character's mental life, directly rendered thought, and thought filtered through the narrator's grammatical form. Her names are psycho-narration, quoted monologue, and narrated monologue. These are analytic categories, not a menu of mandatory writing rules. They help us see what changes when a sentence is presented from a different distance.

Take the daughter in the kitchen. In psycho-narration, a narrator can say: “For years she had resented the way her mother left the lights burning, although she had never admitted that resentment while her mother was alive.” We hear an interpretation of a continuing mental condition, one the daughter may be unable to formulate herself. The narrator knows enough to connect years of habit to the present moment.

In quoted monologue, we might write: “She thought, I told her a thousand times to push the switch twice.” Here the words are presented as a thought the character could plausibly articulate. The sentence has the immediacy and limits of her own phrasing. It may conceal as much as it reveals.

In narrated monologue, often associated with free indirect discourse, we can write: “Of course the light was on. One push had never been enough in this house.” The past tense and third-person narrative frame remain, but the judgment, rhythm, and irritation appear to belong to her. The voice of the narrator and the consciousness of the character briefly overlap. Depending on the surrounding context, a reader may not be able to separate them entirely.

These variations do not differ only in grammar. They make different promises about knowledge. The first says the narrator can locate a buried history. The second hands us the character's apparent words. The third allows her attitude to leak into the prose without announcing a transition. A skilled novel will move among distances when the material requires it. A close narrator need not remain pinned to one grammatical setting.

## How can a character be known too well?

Psychological fiction can go wrong through over-explanation. Consider this invented paragraph:

“She was terrified of being alone because her mother had always been emotionally distant. The light reminded her of an unhappy childhood. She felt guilt, anger, and longing simultaneously. That was why she stood motionless in the kitchen doorway.”

The paragraph may contain an accurate private outline. But the reader has received a causal report without discovering anything. It gives no opportunity to misread, reconsider, or connect a small observation to a larger life. In ordinary encounters, we infer other minds from incomplete signals. Fiction may offer access beyond ordinary inference, but if every action arrives with an authorized explanation, a reader is denied the pleasure and discomfort of interpretation.

Now revise the scene:

“She found the switch behind the crockery shelf, exactly where it had always been. One push. The bulb dimmed and returned to full brightness. She could hear a truck shifting gears outside. She pressed again, harder. Darkness. There it was. She had been right, all these years, about the switch. She stood in the dark long enough for the truck to leave.”

The moment contains no direct declaration of grief. Yet the intensity of “all these years” is disproportionate to the switch. A reader can wonder whether she is briefly vindicated by a complaint she made to her mother, whether she is ashamed of that vindication, or whether she simply needs one problem in the house to have a solution. The final silence is available to more than one interpretation.

That does not mean ambiguity is automatically depth. A scene that supplies no intelligible pressure can become a puzzle with no reason to solve it. The daughter must have recognizable stakes: the sale, the handover, perhaps a disagreement with her brother over which objects to keep. Her actions should be causally situated even if we cannot reduce her inner life to one cause.

## Realism as an agreement, not a window

Realism often presents itself as a way of seeing life without distortion. But what counts as convincing human behavior is shaped by conventions. A character in a nineteenth-century novel may be explained by a confident narrator. A modernist novel may break time into associations. A contemporary minimalist may show a person arranging the table and refuse access to motives. None is a literal transcript of experience. Each is a constructed agreement between writer and reader about how mental life can be represented.

James Wood's *How Fiction Works* gives sustained attention to free indirect style and to the interplay of authorial language and a character's perceptions. Another useful intervention came from novelist and critic Zadie Smith. In her 2008 essay “Two Paths for the Novel,” Smith contrasted Joseph O'Neill's *Netherland* with Tom McCarthy's *Remainder*. Her argument was not that every realist novel should be abandoned. She challenged the dominance of a particular lyrical realism and its assumptions about the coherent, inexhaustibly interesting self, while examining an alternative fascinated by reenactment and the artificiality of gesture.

Let us borrow this tension without pretending that those two novels are representative specimens of every possible style. In our kitchen scene, a lyrical realist might give the daughter a dense inward history, unfolding memory through the grain of the countertop and the smell of dust. An anti-psychological version might list the motions needed to make the light go off: fingers, switch, flicker, second push. It could be exacting about physical procedure while refusing to confirm what any movement means. A third version might move between them, allowing an ordinary bodily action to create an opening for a memory that resists its own interpretation.

The question is not, which is more faithful to life? It is, what does each construction allow the reader to experience, and what illusion does it require? One offers deep access to an inner world. Another makes our hunger for that access visible. Both can produce serious fiction; both can become mannerisms.

## The character's account can be mistaken

Suppose the daughter tells her brother, “I went back because I needed to check the wiring.” This may be a deliberate lie, a sincere account that misses its own motives, or the literal truth. A writer should not rush to settle the matter. The brother knows she never cared about the wiring. But he may also have a reason to insist that her visit was sentimental: he wants to believe she misses their mother as he does. Their interpretations are themselves actions in the story.

We are now dealing with what philosophers sometimes call the problem of other minds, but a fiction writer need not solve that problem abstractly. We can stage it. The brother asks why she was in the house after handing back her keys. She says she forgot a charger. He says the electricity was switched off last week. She remembers seeing the light. He answers that the estate agent turned the power back on for an inspection. Nothing supernatural is required. The ordinary facts unsettle both of their accounts.

A narrative can make that uncertainty fruitful by distinguishing three levels. There is what happened in the material world: a switch was pressed, a light went out, a truck drove away. There is what the daughter experienced or thinks she experienced. There is the account she gives afterward, under the pressure of her brother's question. When these levels diverge, the story can ask how identity is assembled through memory, self-description, and the explanations others demand.

A novelist is permitted to know what the character does not know. But the novelist is also permitted not to fix every motive. Authorial uncertainty need not mean lack of craft. It can be a carefully designed limit on what the form claims to know.

## An exercise in distance

Write a short scene in which a character must perform an ordinary task while avoiding an emotionally difficult decision. Do not start by naming the emotion. Give the task an observable sequence: labeling boxes, washing a cup, untying a knot, finding a lost receipt.

First, write the scene with a narrator who explains the character's motives from outside. Let that narrator connect present action to something the character has forgotten, denied, or never understood. Second, rewrite it as direct thought, using the character's own words. Notice how the thoughts may become self-protective, repetitive, or inconsistent. Third, write it in free indirect style, so the character's judgments color sentences that retain the narrator's grammatical frame. Finally, write an external version that supplies only gestures, speech, objects, and timing.

Read all four as a first-time reader. Which version makes the character feel nearest? Which makes you work hardest? Which preserves the mystery that the story needs, and which only obscures necessary facts? There is no requirement that the most interior version win. The useful result may be a combination: a single sentence of inward judgment after a page of surfaces, or an apparently confident narrator contradicted by a small action.

Do not mistake a technique for a claim about what real minds are like. Cohn's categories describe how novels present consciousness. They do not prove that thoughts arrive as verbal monologues, that readers have identical mental experiences, or that there is a single psychological model for fiction. Narrative psychology, literary criticism, and cognitive science ask overlapping but different questions. A writer can learn from their distinctions without treating any of them as a machine for constructing a person.

The daughter eventually hands over the keys. Perhaps she tells her brother the switch was faulty. Perhaps she says nothing. Perhaps the new owners repair it with an inexpensive part and never know the argument that took place around it for thirty years. The scene matters because one detail can hold several accounts of a life without being reduced to a single emblem.

Psychological realism is most interesting when it offers genuine intimacy while preserving the resistance of another mind. A narrator can lead us inside a character's consciousness; a story can also remind us that the person, even from the inside, is not exhaustively explained. That tension is not a defect to be eliminated. It is one of fiction's central resources.

## Companion (display only)

**Method distinction:** Dorrit Cohn's terms are narratological descriptions of techniques, not personality types or proven psychological mechanisms. Zadie Smith's 2008 intervention critiques a dominant style; it is not a universal rejection of realism. The daughter, switch, brother, and example passages are original illustrations, not quotations or summaries of named novels.

**Practice:** Rewrite a single action using psycho-narration, direct thought, free indirect style, and exterior narration. For each, record what the narrator claims to know, what the character claims, and what the reader can infer.
