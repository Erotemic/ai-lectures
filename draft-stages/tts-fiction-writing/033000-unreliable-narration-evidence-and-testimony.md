# Unreliable Narration: Evidence, Error, and Testimony

The archivist tells us she never opened the red envelope. She says it plainly on the first page: 'There was no reason to open it. I was only responsible for locking the room.' Three pages later she remembers that the envelope was already split along one seam. Near the end of the chapter we learn she knew what color the paper inside was.

Has she lied? Perhaps. She might have opened the envelope and concealed the fact. She might have looked through a torn edge without thinking of that act as opening it. Someone else might have described its contents to her. The reader can begin to doubt her account without yet knowing what happened.

That is where unreliable narration becomes interesting. It is often taught as a twist: the narrator tells us something false, and near the end the author pulls away the curtain. But it can be a far more patient form of inquiry. It asks what a human voice can accurately report, what that voice thinks the reported facts mean, and what values it applies when deciding which facts matter.

The tradition has an identifiable theoretical history. Wayne C. Booth gave influential form to the concept of the unreliable narrator in *The Rhetoric of Fiction*, published in 1961. In his framework, the reliability of a narrator is evaluated against the norms the work establishes, a distinction associated with his concept of the implied author. Subsequent narratologists have debated whether that implied author is the best standard and how readers infer those norms. An unreliable narrator is not simply any character who happens to be wrong.

James Phelan later clarified three activities of narration: reporting, interpreting, and evaluating. Dan Shen's overview in *The Living Handbook of Narratology* gives a useful account of the distinctions, including versions in which a narrator does not so much get something wrong as fail to tell, understand, or judge enough. That is a stronger tool for writers than the question 'Is my narrator trustworthy?'

## First separate a narrator from a perceiver

Suppose a novel is told in close third person. The archivist, Iona, sees a shape under the table and assumes it is the red envelope. The external narrator reports Iona's thought without confirming her assumption. Iona is a fallible perceiver, but she is not necessarily the teller of the novel. Calling her an unreliable narrator would confuse focalization, the restriction of what is perceived or known, with narratorial unreliability, the quality of the account actually being given.

Now imagine the book is written as Iona's first-person statement: 'I saw the red envelope beneath the table.' Here Iona is reporting what she believed she saw. If later evidence suggests it was a folded coat, we can speak about a possibly unreliable report. If she admits that it looked like an envelope but was too dark to tell, she may be unusually careful rather than unreliable.

In neither case is uncertainty a defect to be removed. A skilled narrator may know the limits of her own knowledge; an apparently confident narrator may be unable to recognize them. Reliability is not a synonym for omniscience. A witness can be honest and mistaken, competent in one domain and ignorant in another, or accurate about events while drawing disastrous conclusions from them.

That distinction prevents the technique from turning every gap in the reader's information into a gotcha. Suspense arises because there are reasons to inspect the account, not because the author hides the only facts needed to make sense of the story.

## Reporting: what actually happened?

Build the archive case with a few stable facts in the writer's notes. During a fire drill, Iona locked the reading room. The next morning the red envelope was missing from a cabinet. Iona had signed for its delivery. There is a key log, a cracked seal, and a letter from a visitor asking whether the envelope's contents were public. Those are possible sources of evidence. We need not decide at the outset whether Iona took the envelope.

Here is one narrated version: 'I locked the room at four. Nobody else entered. Therefore the envelope must have been stolen before I arrived.' The first statement might be supported by the key log. The second may overstate what Iona could know; she was not watching the room for the entire afternoon. The final claim moves from a report to an inference. If we revise the passage carelessly, the reader might be unsure whether the author understands the distinction. If we design it deliberately, the unjustified 'therefore' reveals something about Iona's need for a tidy chronology.

We can also create underreporting. 'A visitor asked about the envelope,' she writes. She leaves out that the visitor was her sister, or that the request arrived after Iona had secretly copied the catalog entry. The missing detail may become legible through another source, a sudden change of topic, or a later admission. A narrative can underreport without inventing a false event.

A fair design puts at least some evidence in reach of the attentive reader. The clue need not be a glaring contradiction. It can be a shift in precision: a narrator who remembers every signature but becomes vague about an hour spent alone. Or it can be an apparently ordinary detail that becomes significant when the narrative returns to it.

## Interpreting: why did it happen?

Suppose Iona accurately tells us that her colleague Sam visited the archive twice in one afternoon. She interprets the second visit as proof that Sam intended to steal the envelope. Yet Sam may have returned for a forgotten notebook or to check a leaking window. Iona has reported the visits correctly but attached an unwarranted explanation.

This is not a cheap way to make the narrator foolish. The most compelling interpretations emerge from character. Perhaps Iona has spent years watching Sam receive credit for work she did. She is intensely sensitive to signs of his entitlement. Her suspicion has a history, and that history should be allowed its own truth even if this particular inference is wrong.

One version of the scene reads: 'Sam returned at three forty because he knew the cabinet would be unattended.' Another reads: 'Sam returned at three forty. I had never trusted a man who took two trips to do one job.' The second account reveals a judgment alongside the event. The reader may recognize both the suspicion and its inadequacy.

The resulting tension is richer than deciding which speaker is the villain. The narrator's habits of interpretation can organize an entire novel. She may connect unrelated events into a pattern, continually assign motives, or fail to recognize that others act from obligations she does not share. She need not invent any facts for her account to be deeply misleading.

## Evaluating: what should we think of it?

Imagine Iona admits that she read a confidential letter but says, 'It was only fair. The committee would have hidden it from people who deserved to know.' She may report the event accurately. She may even understand why she chose to do it. The reader can still question her evaluation of the act.

This is the axis of values. A narrator may describe cruelty as efficiency, coercion as kindness, or private curiosity as public service. The work can establish a distance between the narrator's vocabulary and the consequences visible to readers. But a writer should be careful not to confuse a reader's personal disagreement with evidence that the narrator is deliberately designed as unreliable. Fiction can host incompatible moral positions without declaring one speaker defective.

Suppose the document contains evidence that a town official ignored a safety warning. Iona's decision to disclose it might have defensible public value even if her route to disclosure harms an innocent person. The novel gains depth when the conflicting obligations are real. A narrator who calls every action justified because of a grand cause may be morally blinkered, but the story should not settle the problem merely by attaching the label unreliable to her voice.

This distinction also matters when fiction represents testimony by someone with little institutional power. A witness's fractured recollection is not a writer's permission to dismiss the witness. Trauma, social pressure, unequal access to records, and language differences can affect how a story is told. None licenses the simple conclusion that an uncomfortable account is false. A careful novel may question the authority of an official archive just as it questions a private memoir. Whose records survive is itself a story question.

## Designing the reader's inference

Let us return to the red envelope and place three passages beside each other.

First, the blunt twist: 'I never opened it. I had opened it that morning. I lied.' This can work when confession is the subject, but as a mystery mechanism it settles the matter at once.

Second, a contradiction with no narrative preparation: 'I never opened it.' Much later, a newly introduced security camera shows her opening it. The revelation may surprise, yet the reader had no tools to participate in the inquiry. The camera is not inherently a bad device. It becomes unsatisfying if it exists only to cancel everything the reader was encouraged to think.

Third, a version in which the language itself creates pressure: 'I did not break the seal. The adhesive was already loose, and the letter slid toward me as I moved the file. I know exactly how the pale blue paper looked against the table because the window was open.' Here Iona is painstakingly defending a distinction between breaking and seeing, while supplying sensory evidence she cannot stop recalling. We are drawn into her attempt to define the act rather than merely waiting for a secret to be announced.

Notice that we do not need to decide whether she is a thief. The scene can expose a struggle over the meaning of opening, responsibility, permission, and curiosity. A good unreliable account has shape: it protects something, desires something, and reveals something despite itself.

Another design question is when the reader learns to distrust a claim. If suspicion comes early, the pleasure may lie in reading two stories at once: the one Iona believes she tells and the one her details permit us to infer. If suspicion comes late, the story must account for why the reader's earlier trust was reasonable. If every sentence is suspect from the beginning, uncertainty may become monotonous; a reader needs some foothold in the world, even if that foothold is later revised.

The author should keep a private ledger of facts, reports, interpretations, and evaluations. That ledger is not a final answer the novel must disclose. It is a way to avoid confusing intentional ambiguity with an accidental continuity mistake.

## A practical revision test

Write two hundred words from the point of view of someone explaining a consequential action. In your notes, separate the facts from the narrator's account. Then make three versions. In the first, the narrator misreports one verifiable event. In the second, every reported event is correct, but the narrator misreads another person's motive. In the third, the facts and motives are substantially understood, but the narrator evaluates the harm in a way the surrounding story complicates.

Give each version two clues that a reader could notice without an explanatory appendix. Ask a second reader not whether they guessed the twist, but what they believe happened, what they believe the narrator wants, and what evidence shaped those beliefs. If their interpretation differs from yours, inspect the text before blaming the reader. The difference may reveal productive ambiguity or a missing causal step.

Finally, write an honest but limited narrator who says 'I do not know.' Compare that voice with the confidently unreliable one. Which can the reader trust, and about what? The answer may change from scene to scene.

The envelope may eventually be found. Perhaps Iona had opened it, perhaps she had only seen its contents, and perhaps the person who removed it had a legitimate claim. The point of the form is not necessarily to convict Iona. It is to make the act of telling itself part of the drama. When a narrator reports, interprets, and judges, the reader is invited to inspect not only the events but the human effort to turn events into an account worth believing.

**Source boundary:** Booth's *The Rhetoric of Fiction* was published in 1961. Phelan's reporting, interpreting, and evaluating distinctions are discussed in Dan Shen's scholarly overview of unreliability. The archive, red envelope, and all characters are invented demonstrations. The discussion of testimony is a craft and ethical distinction, not a method for assessing actual legal witnesses.

**Practice:** Draft one consequential first-person account. Make separate variants that misreport, misinterpret, and misevaluate without treating them as interchangeable. Give each variant textual evidence a reader can analyze, and write a fourth narrator who recognizes genuine limits to knowledge.
