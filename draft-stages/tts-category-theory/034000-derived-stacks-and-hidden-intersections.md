# Derived and Higher Stacks: What an Intersection Can Remember

Draw the horizontal axis on a sheet of paper, then draw the same line again directly over it. What is their intersection? As a set of points, the answer is the whole line. But suppose we are studying how geometric constraints meet. If two constraints coincide rather than meeting independently, that dependence should matter. Ordinary set intersection cannot tell us how the same equation has been imposed twice.

Now make the example even smaller. Take the number line described algebraically by a variable x. Inside it, the origin is the point where x equals zero. Intersect that point with itself inside the line. The usual geometric answer is a point. Yet a derived intersection remembers that the two copies of the same constraint have collided without being independent. Alongside the ordinary point, it carries an additional piece of algebraic information.

That extra information is one of the motivations for derived algebraic geometry. The word derived is connected to resolutions and homological algebra: instead of applying ordinary tensor products or intersections directly, we compute a homotopy-invariant version that retains higher information about dependencies. When we combine these constructions with descent, local geometric objects, and symmetries, we arrive at derived stacks.

This subject brings together several threads of category theory. Pullbacks explain intersections. Homotopy pullbacks explain why witnesses of compatibility matter. Sheaves and stacks explain how local data are assembled globally. Derived geometry adds the higher algebra needed when equations, deformations, and intersections are not transverse.

We will work through a small algebraic example carefully. It is elementary enough to calculate, but it exposes the central distinction between a classical intersection and its derived refinement.

## Equations define more than pictures

Let k be a field, such as the real or complex numbers. The polynomial ring k of x is the algebra of polynomials in one variable. Geometrically we regard it as the ring of functions on the affine line. The origin is defined by setting x equal to zero. Algebraically, its coordinate ring is k of x divided by the ideal generated by x, which is isomorphic to k.

Now consider the origin as a geometric object mapping into the affine line. We have two copies of this map, and we ask for their fiber product over the line. In elementary geometry, a fiber product over the line is an intersection with maps that remember the ambient space.

The algebraic counterpart of this ordinary fiber product reverses arrows and uses tensor products. The coordinate ring of the intersection is k tensor over k of x with k. Both appearances of k are modules over the polynomial ring by evaluating x at zero.

What does this tensor product produce? It gives k. The ordinary intersection has the coordinate ring of a single point. So far nothing surprising has happened. Imposing x equals zero twice seems to produce the same answer as imposing it once.

But there is something suspicious about that cancellation. If two independent equations cut down a space, we expect each equation to remove a degree of freedom. Here the second equation repeats the first. The ordinary tensor product has forgotten that the constraints were dependent. Derived tensor products are designed to detect this lost relation.

## Why tensoring can destroy information

Tensor products behave well with certain exact sequences, but they are not exact in general. A map that was injective before tensoring may cease to be injective. This failure of exactness can be measured with derived functors called Tor groups.

Let's build a simple resolution. Multiplication by x gives a map from k of x to itself. Its image is precisely the polynomials divisible by x. Quotienting by those polynomials leaves only the constant term, which is k. Thus the sequence starting with k of x, followed by multiplication by x into another copy of k of x, then evaluation at zero onto k, is exact.

Why is that useful? The two copies of the polynomial ring in this sequence are free modules over the polynomial ring, so we can use them to calculate derived tensor products. Instead of tensoring the module k directly, we replace it with this two-term free resolution and tensor each term with the other copy of k.

After tensoring, the multiplication-by-x map becomes multiplication by zero, because x acts as zero on k. We therefore obtain a two-term complex consisting of k in degree one and k in degree zero, with the zero differential between them.

Its degree-zero homology is k, matching the ordinary tensor product. Its degree-one homology is also k. All higher homology groups vanish. That additional k in degree one is the first Tor group of k with itself over k of x.

We have now computed the simplest derived self-intersection of a point in a line. It has the same underlying classical point, but its derived structure includes a nontrivial degree-one homological witness of the redundant constraint.

## Check the resolution carefully

This calculation is worth doing without skipping the crucial cancellation. Start with the exact sequence: polynomial ring, multiply by x, polynomial ring, then evaluate x at zero to obtain the field. Multiplying a nonzero polynomial by x cannot produce zero because k of x has no zero divisors. Thus the first map is injective.

The kernel of evaluation at zero consists of polynomials whose constant term vanishes. Those are precisely the multiples of x, so the image of the multiplication map equals the kernel. The sequence is exact.

Now apply tensoring over the polynomial ring with k. A free rank-one module tensored with k becomes k. The map induced by multiplication by x sends a class c to x times c, but x acts as zero in the quotient module. So the induced map is zero.

A two-term complex with zero differential has homology equal to each term. The degree-one homology is k because every degree-one element is a cycle and there are no boundaries entering that degree. The degree-zero homology is k because the image of the differential is zero.

The result does not come from inventing a new geometric point or doubling the number of ordinary points. It comes from remembering a failure of independence in the equations defining the intersection. The underlying classical space remains a point. The derived enhancement adds algebraic directions that are visible in homological degree one.

## The derived tensor product

We write the refined intersection algebra as k derived-tensor over k of x with k. Its homology groups are Tor zero equal to k and Tor one equal to k, with all higher Tor groups zero.

An ordinary tensor product keeps only the degree-zero part of this information. The derived tensor product retains the whole complex, up to the appropriate notion of equivalence. This is analogous to taking a homotopy pullback rather than an ordinary pullback of spaces, though the algebraic construction uses derived tensor products because of the reversal of arrows under the spectrum construction.

There are two ways to describe a derived geometric object depending on conventions and characteristic. One approach uses simplicial commutative rings. In characteristic zero, a suitable model can use nonpositively cohomologically graded commutative differential graded algebras. The specific model is less important for the example than the invariant homology: one extra copy of k appears in degree one in homological grading.

We should be cautious about translating that degree into a physical spatial direction. Derived directions encode relations, infinitesimal deformation, and obstruction information. They are not additional visible coordinates in the original number line.

A useful distinction is between a singular object and a derived object. Singularities are familiar from classical algebraic geometry, where tangent spaces or local equations behave badly. Derived structure can record more about their intersections and deformation theory, but it is not simply a synonym for singularity.

## Intersections that meet properly

Compare this self-intersection with two distinct lines crossing transversely in the plane. Let the coordinate ring of the plane be k of x and y. The horizontal axis is defined by y equal zero. The vertical axis is defined by x equal zero. Their ordinary intersection is the origin.

In this case the two equations constrain independent directions. Under the appropriate Tor-independence condition, the derived tensor product has no higher Tor, so the derived intersection agrees with the classical intersection in the relevant homotopical sense.

We can test the elementary example directly. Resolve one axis by multiplication by y in the plane's polynomial ring. Then tensor with the coordinate ring of the other axis. In that ring, y remains a polynomial variable rather than being set to zero. Multiplication by y is injective, so the degree-one homology vanishes.

The degree-zero quotient sets y to zero as well, leaving k. We obtain the ordinary origin and no extra Tor one. Thus the derived construction distinguishes two axes meeting independently from a constraint intersected with itself, even though the classical result in both cases may be a single point.

This is a very small computation, but it demonstrates the power of the derived viewpoint. It records how an intersection occurred, rather than only which points survive after the equations are imposed.

## From derived affine objects to derived schemes

Ordinary affine algebraic geometry treats a commutative ring A as the coordinate algebra of an affine scheme Spec A. Derived algebraic geometry replaces ordinary commutative rings with homotopical or derived commutative rings, then builds geometric objects from these richer affines.

A derived affine scheme can be represented by the derived spectrum of such a ring. Its classical truncation recovers an ordinary affine scheme by taking the degree-zero homotopy or homology of the ring. The higher homotopy groups contain the derived information invisible to that truncation.

To build larger derived schemes we glue derived affines using a topology, commonly related to the étale topology. This is the familiar local-to-global process from scheme theory, but the local objects are now derived and the compatibility conditions are homotopical.

The same categorical principle governs ordinary and derived geometry: a global object must be determined by compatible local data. The difference is the category of objects being glued and the kind of equivalence used to compare them. Working only with sets of isomorphism classes is inadequate when local objects have nontrivial automorphisms and higher homotopies.

## Why stacks enter

Suppose we want to classify vector bundles. A vector bundle over a space can be trivial on each member of a cover while failing to be globally trivial. To reconstruct it, we need transition functions satisfying cocycle relations. We must also remember the automorphisms of the bundle. Merely listing isomorphism classes at each local patch can lose the gluing maps.

An ordinary stack retains objects and their isomorphisms, often organized in groupoids. A higher stack retains higher equivalences and their coherences. A derived stack adds sensitivity to derived test rings and related deformation data. It combines homotopical descent with richer infinitesimal geometry.

One common formal description of a derived stack starts with a functor from a category of derived commutative algebras to spaces. It sends equivalences of derived rings to equivalences of spaces and satisfies a suitable descent condition for a chosen topology. The direction of the functor follows the convention that an affine object Spec A is contravariant in A, while its functor of points is covariant in test algebras.

This is a general notion of higher sheaf on derived affines. Additional finiteness, smoothness, and representability conditions are needed to obtain geometric derived stacks in the more restricted sense used in derived algebraic geometry. We should not treat every space-valued functor satisfying descent as automatically geometric.

## A deformation seen through dual numbers

A related elementary idea comes from the ring of dual numbers, consisting of expressions a plus b times epsilon, with epsilon squared equal to zero. It is a commutative ring with a nilpotent element. A map from a polynomial ring k of x to the dual numbers is determined by the image of x. If we send x to an expression a plus b epsilon, we can interpret a as a base point and b as a first-order direction of motion.

Consider the equation x squared equals zero. Substituting x equals a plus b epsilon gives a squared plus two a b epsilon, because the epsilon-squared term vanishes. Over a field of characteristic different from two, the equation imposes constraints on a and b. At the point a equals zero, the coefficient of epsilon vanishes for every b, so there are infinitesimal directions even though the reduced classical variety has only one point.

This dual-number calculation detects first-order tangent information in a classical nonreduced scheme. It is related to, but not identical with, derived intersection theory. Nilpotent elements can exist in ordinary commutative rings, while derived structure can appear in higher homological degrees even when the classical coordinate ring is reduced. The example is useful because it separates two ways geometry can remember information invisible in a picture of its points.

A tangent complex extends this first-order perspective into homological degrees. Its positive or negative degrees, depending on conventions, describe automorphisms, deformations, and obstructions. That interpretation requires hypotheses about the moduli problem; the terms are not interchangeable for every complex. But the general principle is that a deformation problem asks not only which solutions exist, but which small changes are allowed and what prevents changes from extending.

## A second calculation: remove the redundancy

We can compare the derived self-intersection with an ordinary point cut out of a line by a single equation. If we simply study the inclusion of the origin, the coordinate ring is k, and there is no tensor product of two copies over the ambient polynomial ring to compute. The Tor-one group arose specifically when we formed the derived intersection of two copies of that inclusion.

This distinction prevents a misleading interpretation. The point itself does not automatically come equipped with an extra degree-one class merely because it is defined by x equals zero. The class appears in the derived fiber product of the point with itself over the line. The ambient map is part of the data, and forgetting that map changes the question.

Likewise, the same classical point can have different derived enhancements arising from different intersections or deformation problems. The classical truncation gives a convenient summary but need not characterize the original object. To tell those objects apart, we inspect their higher homotopy or homology data, sometimes through their cotangent complexes.

When two constraints meet transversely, the derived correction may vanish. When they meet with excess dependence, higher Tor can survive. This is why derived intersections give a systematic replacement for ad hoc multiplicity corrections: they encode dependencies in the algebra that is actually computed.

## Automorphisms, obstructions, and deformation problems

Why go to this trouble? Moduli problems often classify objects that have automorphisms and can be deformed. Consider families of algebraic structures, bundles, maps, or other geometric data. A moduli space of isomorphism classes may identify objects while hiding their symmetries. A stack retains the symmetries; a derived stack can additionally retain higher deformation and obstruction information.

The derived self-intersection is a small model of an obstruction phenomenon. The repeated equation x equals zero creates an excess relation detected by Tor one. In more complicated moduli problems, analogous homological groups measure infinitesimal directions, equations among equations, and obstructions to extending deformations.

The cotangent complex is one systematic tool for this analysis. It refines the usual module of differentials to a derived object, enabling tangent and obstruction calculations that remain meaningful when the space is singular or derived. The full construction requires homological algebra beyond the small example, but the motivating pattern is now visible.

The key is to distinguish the role of each layer. Higher categorical structure remembers maps and homotopies. Stack structure remembers descent and automorphisms. Derived algebra remembers higher relations among equations. A derived stack brings these layers into one framework for problems where all of them interact.

## A warning about truncation

Suppose we take a derived ring, discard all its higher homology, and keep only the ordinary ring in degree zero. Then we pass to an ordinary affine scheme. That truncation can be useful, but it loses the Tor information we calculated.

Similarly, suppose we take a stack of objects and replace each groupoid by its set of isomorphism classes. That loses automorphism groups. The two losses are distinct. One comes from truncating homological degrees; the other comes from truncating the homotopical structure of the space of objects and equivalences.

It is possible to have nontrivial automorphisms without nontrivial derived intersection data, and to have derived intersection data in an example with no interesting global group actions. We should not conflate derived geometry with the study of symmetries, even though derived stacks are built to handle both.

Keeping track of which information a truncation forgets is often the best diagnostic for deciding which categorical model a problem requires. If the desired invariant survives truncation, a simpler model may suffice. If it does not, moving to higher or derived objects is mathematically justified.

## Return to the point intersected with itself

We began with an ordinary point on a line, intersected with itself. At the level of classical sets, the answer remained a point. At the level of ordinary coordinate rings, the tensor product remained k. That answer was mathematically correct, but it omitted the redundancy of imposing the same constraint twice.

The free resolution exposed the missing information. Multiplication by x became zero after tensoring with the quotient where x is already zero. A copy of k survived in degree one. That is the simplest calculation showing how a derived intersection can remember an equation among equations.

When a moduli problem combines equations, deformations, automorphisms, and local gluing, the same demand for faithful information becomes more complicated. Derived stacks organize those demands without requiring every computation to be a new ad hoc construction.

The larger lesson is about what mathematical equality and compatibility can conceal. Two objects may meet at the same set of points while their intersection theories differ. Two local descriptions may agree up to isomorphism while the choice of isomorphism matters. Two algebraic presentations may have the same ordinary quotient while their derived relations differ.

Higher and derived category theory provide ways to preserve these distinctions. They do so by retaining the witnesses and relationships that ordinary answers may erase. The extra structure earns its place when it changes the mathematical question we can answer.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

Let \(R=k[x]\), \(k=R/(x)\). The classical intersection of the origin with itself in \(\mathbb A_k^1\) is \(\operatorname{Spec}(k\otimes_R k)=\operatorname{Spec}k\). Resolve \(k\) as an \(R\)-module by
\[
 0\longrightarrow R\xrightarrow{\cdot x}R\longrightarrow k\longrightarrow0.
\]
Tensoring with \(k\) gives \(k\xrightarrow{0}k\). Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{Tor}_i^{k[x]}(k,k)=\begin{cases}
 k,&i=0,1,\\0,&i>1.
 \end{cases}
\]
The derived self-intersection is modeled by \(\operatorname{Spec}(k\otimes_{k[x]}^{\mathbb L}k)\) with this homology; its classical truncation is still \(\operatorname{Spec}k\).

For transverse coordinate axes \(R=k[x,y]\), \(A=R/(y)\cong k[x]\), \(B=R/(x)\cong k[y]\), resolving \(A\) by multiplication by \(y\) and then tensoring with \(B\) gives \(B\xrightarrow{\cdot y}B\). This map is injective, so \(\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(A,B)=0\), and the degree-zero tensor product is \(k\).

A derived stack on a suitable site of derived affines can be viewed as a space-valued functor on derived commutative algebras satisfying equivalence-invariance and homotopical descent. Representability and geometricity are additional properties. The **classical truncation** of a derived affine \(\operatorname{Spec}A\) is \(\operatorname{Spec}\pi_0 A\) in connective homotopical grading.

### Exercises with answers

1. **Classical intersection.** Compute \(k\otimes_{k[x]}k\). **Answer:** \(k\).
2. **Derived intersection.** Why is \(\operatorname{Tor}_1^{k[x]}(k,k)\) nonzero? **Answer:** the resolution differential \(\cdot x\) becomes zero after tensoring with \(k\).
3. **Transversality.** What is \(\operatorname{Tor}_1^{k[x,y]}(k[x],k[y])\) with the coordinate-axis module structures? **Answer:** zero, since multiplication by \(y\) on \(k[y]\) is injective.
4. **Two truncations.** What distinct information is lost by taking \(\pi_0\) of a derived ring and by taking isomorphism classes of a groupoid? **Answer:** higher homological relations versus automorphism information.
5. **Geometricity.** Does every descent-satisfying functor on derived commutative rings define a representable geometric derived stack? **Answer:** no; additional geometricity and representability conditions are needed.
