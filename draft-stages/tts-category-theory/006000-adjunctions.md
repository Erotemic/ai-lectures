# Adjunctions: Why Free Constructions Fit Forgetful Ones

Suppose you want a group generated by two symbols, a and b, but you do not want to assume any relation between them except the rules that every group must satisfy. You want to multiply these symbols, take inverses, and form combinations such as a followed by b followed by the inverse of a. The result should be as unconstrained as a group can be while still containing the chosen generators.

One way to build it is with words in a, b, and their formal inverses. We simplify adjacent pairs consisting of a symbol and its inverse, and multiply words by concatenation followed by reduction. The empty word serves as the identity. This object is called the free group on the two-element set of generators.

The interesting fact is not merely that we can build a group of words. It is that every function assigning the two generators to elements of any group G determines exactly one group homomorphism from the free group to G. If we choose the image of a and the image of b, the value of the homomorphism on any reduced word is forced. For example, a b inverse-a must be sent to the chosen image of a times the chosen image of b times the inverse of the image of a.

Thus a problem about functions from a set to the underlying set of G is equivalent to a problem about homomorphisms from a free group to G. This is the prototype of an adjunction. Two different mathematical contexts describe the same choices, with a correspondence that behaves coherently under changes of the input set and the target group.

## Work out the free-group correspondence

Start with a set S, say the two-element set containing a and b. Let F of S denote the free group on S. Let U take a group G to its underlying set. We claim that a function from S into U of G corresponds to a group homomorphism from F of S into G.

From a function h on the generators, construct the homomorphism h-bar by substitution: replace each occurrence of a generator with its assigned group element, each inverse symbol with the inverse of its assigned element, and then multiply in G in the order prescribed by the word. The group axioms ensure this is compatible with cancellation and concatenation.

Conversely, given a homomorphism k from F of S to G, restrict k to the original generators. This produces an ordinary function from S into the underlying set of G. Extend and restrict are inverse procedures: extending a restriction returns k, because the images of generators determine a homomorphism out of the free group; restricting an extension returns h by construction.

The correspondence is a bijection between two sets of maps. On one side are the group homomorphisms from F of S to G. On the other side are the ordinary functions from S to U of G. The phrase 'free group on S' is best understood through this mapping property, because that property describes what the free group does, independently of the chosen word construction.

If S is empty, there is exactly one function from S to the underlying set of any G. Therefore the free group on the empty set must have exactly one homomorphism into every G. It is the trivial group, the initial object in the category of groups. The example is a useful consistency check on the universal property.

If S contains one generator, the free group is the integers under addition, with the generator corresponding to one. Choosing an element g of G determines a unique homomorphism from the integers into G, sending n to the n-th power of g, including inverse powers for negative n. The universal property makes an old algebraic fact part of a general pattern.

## What makes the correspondence natural?

An arbitrary bijection between two sets of functions is not enough. Suppose we change the generator set by a function from S-prime to S. This changes functions on the generators by precomposition, and it induces a homomorphism between the corresponding free groups. Similarly, if we change the target group G by a group homomorphism into H, both sides of the correspondence change by composition with that homomorphism.

A valid adjunction requires the bijection between map sets to respect both kinds of change. The algebraic procedures of extending and restricting agree with those compositions. This is naturality in two variables. It ensures that the correspondence belongs to the functors themselves rather than to a collection of arbitrary choices.

We write F left adjoint to U. The word left describes the position of F in the mapping correspondence: homomorphisms from F of S into G correspond to functions from S into U of G. Reading the equation the other way, U is right adjoint to F.

The order is significant. There is no general symmetry that makes U left adjoint to F as well. Many mathematical constructions come in left-right pairs, and their directional properties differ.

The free-monoid construction gives a simpler version. For a set S, let L of S be the collection of finite words over S with concatenation. This is a monoid. A function from S to the underlying set of any monoid M extends uniquely to a monoid homomorphism from L of S to M. The empty word goes to the identity, and a word becomes the product of its letter images.

This example needs no inverses, and it appears in computer science as the structure of finite strings or lists. The free-monoid functor is left adjoint to the forgetful functor from monoids to sets. The same mapping property explains why the empty word and concatenation are the required operations, not arbitrary conveniences.

## Another adjunction in elementary order theory

Adjunctions also appear where categories have at most one arrow between any two objects. Consider a monotone function L from a poset P to a poset Q and a monotone function R from Q to P. The adjunction condition becomes a pair of inequalities: L of p is below q exactly when p is below R of q.

A concrete example uses subsets of a set X. Let P be the poset of all subsets of X, ordered by inclusion. Let Q be the same poset. Fix a subset A of X. Define L of S to be the union of S with A. Define R of T to be T with A removed? This tempting guess fails: the equivalence would require S union A contained in T exactly when S contained in T minus A. In fact, if A is not already contained in T, the left side is false for every S while the right side may be true for the empty set. So this example does not define an adjunction on the full powerset.

A correct and instructive example begins with a function f from X to Y. Direct image sends subsets of X to subsets of Y. Inverse image sends subsets of Y back to subsets of X. We have the equivalence: the image under f of S is contained in T if and only if S is contained in the inverse image of T. This is exactly an order-theoretic adjunction.

Check it with a finite set. Suppose X contains one, two, three, and f assigns one and two to red, three to blue. Let S contain one and three, and let T contain red and blue. The image of S is red and blue, contained in T. The preimage of T is all of X, which contains S. If T contains only red, neither statement holds because three maps to blue. The two descriptions agree for every choice of S and T.

Thus direct image is left adjoint to inverse image between powerset posets. Notice how the universal-property idea has become an inequality rather than a bijection of large function sets. The core relationship is the same: one kind of map corresponds to another in a way determined by the direction of arrows.

## The unit: send a generator into its free construction

A hom-set bijection can be repackaged into two natural transformations. Start with the identity homomorphism on F of S. Under the adjunction, it corresponds to a function from S into the underlying set of F of S. This function sends each generator to the corresponding one-letter word in the free group. It is called the unit at S.

The unit gives the universal entry point for the original data. In the free-monoid example, it sends an element to the one-element list containing it. In the direct-image/inverse-image adjunction, the unit at a subset S expresses S contained in the inverse image of its direct image; every element of S is certainly mapped into the image of S.

The direction is consistent: for a left adjoint F and right adjoint U, the unit goes from the identity on the source category of F to U after F. It compares an object with what remains after freely adding structure and then forgetting that structure.

The unit is not generally an isomorphism. A set S and the underlying set of its free group can be vastly different. Even for one generator, the free group has infinitely many elements, while the original generator set has one. Adjunctions concern a mapping correspondence, not an assertion that free construction leaves objects unchanged.

## The counit: evaluate the free construction in a given object

Now begin with the identity function on U of G. Under the adjunction, it corresponds to a group homomorphism from the free group on the underlying set of G into G. This map evaluates a word of formal generators at the actual elements of G that those generators name.

For example, take a formal word consisting of the generator named g, followed by the generator named h, followed by the inverse of the generator named g. The counit sends it to the actual product g h g-inverse in G. It performs the relations of G, many of which were deliberately absent in the free construction.

The counit goes from F after U to the identity on the target category. A free object built on the underlying set of a genuine group maps back to that group by evaluation. This map is typically surjective, because every element of G is the image of its own one-letter generator. It is usually not injective, because the actual group may satisfy relations not present in the free group.

The unit and counit obey two triangle identities. These say that there are two ways of inserting the unit and then evaluating with the counit, one involving an object in the source and the other in the target, and each returns the corresponding identity. The names sound diagrammatic, but they express exactly the fact that extension followed by restriction, and restriction followed by extension, recover the original map.

We can check one triangle directly for free groups. Start with a word in F of S. Regard each letter as a one-letter word inside a free group on the underlying set of F of S. Then evaluate those one-letter words back in F of S. The result is the original word. The other triangle says that an element of the underlying set of G, inserted as a generator into the free group on that set and then evaluated in G, returns the element itself.

## The left-right preservation theorem

A major payoff of adjunctions is a preservation theorem. A right adjoint preserves limits that exist in its source category, and a left adjoint preserves colimits that exist in its source category. In particular, a right adjoint preserves terminal objects and products, and a left adjoint preserves initial objects and coproducts.

Why is this plausible? A limit is characterized by arrows into the diagram satisfying compatibility equations. An adjunction converts arrows into one category into arrows in another in a natural way. The unique-factorization condition for a limit survives the conversion when we apply the right adjoint. The formal proof uses the hom-set bijection and the universal property; the companion gives the pattern.

For our free-group example, F as a left adjoint preserves coproducts. The coproduct of two sets is their disjoint union. The free group on that disjoint union is isomorphic to the free product of the two free groups. Both constructions express the freedom to choose images of generators independently in an arbitrary target group.

The forgetful functor U, being a right adjoint, preserves products and other limits. The underlying set of a product of groups is a Cartesian product. Here a familiar calculation illustrates a general theorem.

Be careful about a converse. A functor preserving some products does not automatically have a left adjoint. Adjoint functor theorems provide conditions under which adjoints exist, but those conditions require more than one isolated preservation check.

## Two different explanations for the same correspondence

An adjunction can be described by the hom-set bijection or by the unit and counit with triangle identities. Why bother with two forms? They make different calculations easier.

The hom-set form is ideal when constructing an adjunction. For a free monoid, we naturally ask how to extend a function on generators into a homomorphism from lists. For direct image and inverse image, we naturally state a containment equivalence. The adjunction is visible as a correspondence between mapping problems.

The unit-counit form is useful after the adjunction is known. It lets us compose natural transformations and prove structural facts without repeatedly unpacking a two-variable bijection. We can write the monad associated with an adjunction, characterize the induced comparison maps, and verify the triangular equations by ordinary composition.

Let's derive the unit from the hom-set bijection a little more carefully. For each object X of C, take the identity on F of X. The adjunction sends that identity to an arrow from X to U F of X. That is eta at X. Why is eta natural? Given an arrow f from X to Y, the two ways of going from X to U F of Y correspond under the adjunction to the same arrow F of f from F of X to F of Y. The naturality follows from uniqueness of adjoint transposes.

Derive the counit similarly. For each Y of D, take the identity on U of Y. Under the inverse direction of the adjunction, it corresponds to an arrow from F U of Y to Y. That is epsilon at Y. Its naturality follows from the same compatibility of the transpose operation.

Now why do the triangle equations hold? One triangle says that if we insert the unit at X, then apply F and evaluate using the counit at F of X, the resulting map on F of X is the identity. Under the hom-set correspondence, both routes are the same identity map. The other triangle works in the reverse direction at an object Y of D. The proof is the algebraic expression of the fact that adjoint transpose in either direction is an inverse bijection.

This helps prevent a frequent misunderstanding. An adjunction does not generally provide inverses between F and U as functors. The unit and counit need not be isomorphisms. They provide the exact comparison maps required for the hom-set correspondence, and the triangles say those comparisons behave coherently.

## A geometric adjunction with no formal words

Consider a set X and the set of all its subsets, ordered by inclusion. Suppose we have a map f from X to Y. Direct image sends subsets forward. Preimage sends subsets backward. The relation f of S is contained in T if and only if S is contained in the preimage of T is an adjunction.

The unit inequality says S is contained in the preimage of the image of S. This may be strict. If f identifies different points, the preimage of the image of S can contain points outside S. For example, let X contain one and two, and send both to red. For S consisting of one, its image is the singleton red, whose preimage contains both one and two. The unit records that collapsing information may enlarge the recovered subset.

The counit inequality says the image of the preimage of T is contained in T. This may also be strict. If T contains a target value never attained by f, taking its preimage discards that value, and mapping forward cannot recover it. For example, if Y has red and blue and f always returns red, then the image of the preimage of the whole Y is only red, a proper subset.

These examples show how the unit and counit measure the asymmetry of moving information forward and backward. They are not claims that direct image and preimage exactly undo one another. They are canonical comparison inequalities produced by the adjunction.

Now choose a map f that is bijective. Direct image and preimage then become inverse order isomorphisms, and both unit and counit equalities hold. This is the special case where the adjunction is actually an equivalence of the relevant poset categories. The same formulas cover both lossy and lossless translations.

## Free constructions as solutions to equations

The free-group construction looks like a large set of formal words, but its universal property can help solve equation problems. Suppose we want a group homomorphism from the free group on a and b into a particular group G. We can choose any values g and h in G. No additional relations among a and b must be checked, because the free group imposes none beyond group axioms.

Now suppose the source were a quotient of the free group with a relation saying the generators commute. A homomorphism into G would exist for a chosen pair g,h only when those elements commute in G. The relation becomes a constraint on the possible generator images. A universal mapping property therefore describes both what is free and what relations restrict.

For example, if G is the group of invertible two-by-two real matrices, we can choose two noncommuting matrices as images of a and b and obtain a homomorphism from the free group. We cannot necessarily extend that assignment to a homomorphism from the free abelian group on a and b, because its generators are required to commute. The distinction is invisible if we look only at the cardinality of the generator set; it is visible in the allowed homomorphisms.

This example connects free constructions to presentations of algebraic objects by generators and relations. Left adjoints are often free or universal constructions, while right adjoints often forget structure or impose a form of restriction. The words often are deliberate: there are many adjunctions whose interpretations are less easily summarized by free and forgetful language.

The unifying test remains the same. Write the mapping correspondence, check that it is a bijection, and verify its naturality in both variables. Once that is done, the unit, counit, triangle identities, and preservation properties follow from the adjunction's structure.

## Why the correspondence does the work

An adjunction can look like a formidable new abstraction: two functors, a natural family of bijections, a unit, a counit, and triangle identities. But we arrived at it by making one ordinary demand. Assigning values to a set of free generators should be exactly the same choice as defining a homomorphism from the free structure they generate.

The mapping property determined the free construction. Naturality made the correspondence consistent under changes of generator sets and target objects. The unit inserted generators, and the counit evaluated formal expressions in actual structures. The triangle identities expressed the reversibility of the two ways of describing a mapping problem.

Return to the free group on a and b. A random group homomorphism from this free group to G cannot make an independent choice for every word. Its values on a and b decide every other value. That is why the large algebraic object is controlled by a small amount of data. The adjunction makes this observation exact and transferable to many other mathematical contexts.

Adjunctions occur in algebra, topology, logic, programming-language semantics, and order theory. The concrete constructions can be very different. Their common mathematics is a natural equivalence between two kinds of arrows. Once we understand that equivalence, we can recognize when a construction is free, when a forgetful operation has a universal partner, and why some transformations preserve limits or colimits.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

An adjunction \(F:\mathcal C\rightleftarrows\mathcal D:U\) with \(F\dashv U\) is a natural family of bijections \(\mathcal D(FX,Y)\cong\mathcal C(X,UY)\), natural in \(X\in\mathcal C\) and \(Y\in\mathcal D\).

**Unit and counit:** \(\eta:1_{\mathcal C}\Rightarrow UF\) and \(\varepsilon:FU\Rightarrow 1_{\mathcal D}\). Triangle identities: \(\varepsilon_{FX}\circ F(\eta_X)=1_{FX}\), and \(U(\varepsilon_Y)\circ\eta_{UY}=1_{UY}\).

**Free groups:** \(\operatorname{Hom}_{Grp}(F(S),G)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{Set}(S,U(G))\). The left-to-right map is restriction to generators; its inverse is word evaluation.

**Powersets:** For \(f:X\to Y\), \(f_!(S)\subseteq T\iff S\subseteq f^*(T)\) defines \(f_!\dashv f^*\) between inclusion posets.

**Preservation proof sketch:** For \(U\) right adjoint, the cone-bijection \(\mathcal C(X,U\lim D)\cong\mathcal D(FX,\lim D)\cong\lim\mathcal D(FX,D_j)\cong\lim\mathcal C(X,UD_j)\) shows \(U\lim D\) is a limit when the indicated limits exist.

### Exercises and answers

1. **Exercise:** What is the free monoid on the empty set? **Answer:** The one-element monoid containing only the empty word.
2. **Exercise:** Describe the counit for the free-monoid/forgetful adjunction. **Answer:** Evaluate a finite list of elements of a monoid by multiplying them, sending the empty list to its identity.
3. **Exercise:** Does \(F\dashv U\) imply the unit is invertible? **Answer:** No; embedding generators into a free group is typically not surjective.
