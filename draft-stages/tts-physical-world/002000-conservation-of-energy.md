# Where Does the Energy Go? Conservation, Work, and Heat

Imagine dropping a metal weight into a bucket of sand. Before the drop, the weight is motionless in your hand. As it falls, it speeds up. When it strikes the sand, it stops. Where did its ability to cause motion go? A child might say the motion was used up. A physicist needs a more exact answer, because the sand has been disturbed, the weight and sand may have warmed, and sound has passed through the air.

This is our question: what must we count so that nothing goes missing? The answer will lead us from simple calculations about moving bodies into one of the broadest principles of physics. Energy conservation is more powerful than saying that moving things have some quantity called energy. We need to define the system, identify what crosses its boundary, and account for transformations that we cannot see merely by following the object with our eyes.

## First count the motion

Consider a cart rolling on a level track. If its mass is one kilogram and its speed is two meters per second, its kinetic energy is two joules. If the same cart moves at four meters per second, its kinetic energy is eight joules. Double the speed and kinetic energy becomes four times larger.

The formula is one half the mass multiplied by the square of the speed. For a cart of mass m and speed v, that is one half times m times v squared. It is worth asking where the square comes from. Suppose a constant force accelerates a cart through a distance. Newton's second law relates force to mass times acceleration; the relation between speed and distance says the increase in speed squared is twice acceleration times distance. Combining them makes the work, force times distance, equal to the change in one half mass times speed squared.

You do not need to memorize the algebra to hear the lesson. A force acting over a distance changes a body's kinetic energy. That change depends on the distance through which the force acts, not merely the duration of its action. The product of force and displacement in its direction is called work. If the force points opposite the motion, its work is negative, and the body's kinetic energy can decrease.

There is already a small warning. A moving cart can go around a bend while keeping the same speed. Its direction changes, so its velocity changes, but its kinetic energy does not. A force perpendicular to the motion can redirect the cart without doing work on it. Work is a relationship between force and displacement, not merely another name for having a force present.

## Gravity and a stored capacity for motion

Lift that one-kilogram cart, or a one-kilogram weight, two meters above a chosen reference height. Near Earth's surface, gravity exerts roughly nine point eight newtons of force on it. Raising it by two meters against that force requires about nineteen point six joules of work, if we lift slowly and ignore other losses.

We describe this by saying the raised weight has nineteen point six joules more gravitational potential energy than it had at the reference height. When we release it and it falls without air resistance, gravitational potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases. Just before reaching the reference height, its kinetic energy has increased by the same nineteen point six joules.

This is a striking economy of thought. We could calculate the fall by following acceleration at every moment. Or we could compare the starting and ending energies. Conservation of mechanical energy supplies the answer about speed without reconstructing every instant of motion.

But the result has conditions. It assumes gravity behaves as the conservative force described by the potential, and that we have not lost mechanical energy to drag or friction. It says that kinetic plus potential energy remains constant for an isolated mechanical system with only conservative internal forces. It is not the broad claim that every form of energy stays mechanical forever.

A pendulum is a good demonstration. At its highest point, its speed is momentarily zero, and the energy we track is largely gravitational potential. At the bottom, speed is greatest and potential is smaller. A real pendulum eventually slows and stops. To treat that as a refutation of energy conservation would be to count only the bob's visible mechanical energy and ignore the surroundings.

## The difference between a useful model and a complete accounting

Return to the falling weight in the sand. The final kinetic energy of the weight is zero. If we count only the weight, we have a missing quantity. But the weight was never isolated. It pushed grains aside, compressed them, warmed the materials, made vibrations, and transferred energy into air and supporting structures.

The physical claim is that energy is accounted for when the chosen system and the transfers across its boundary are properly included. The detailed accounting may be hard to measure. One cannot infer the exact distribution among heat, sound, deformation, and remaining microscopic motion from the height of the fall alone. Conservation constrains the sum, while additional models and measurements determine the pieces.

This is an important pattern in science. A conservation law can provide a check on the correctness of a proposed explanation, but it is rarely a complete explanation by itself. It tells us what cannot happen without an unaccounted source or transfer. It does not tell us which changes happen, how quickly they happen, or whether a process can be reversed.

## A brewer's son and the measurement of heat

In the nineteenth century, these distinctions were being hammered out in workshops and laboratories. James Prescott Joule grew up in a family connected to brewing in the Manchester region. His education was largely private, including instruction from John Dalton. He experimented with electrical and mechanical devices while involved in the family business. Pumps, motors, heat, and efficiency were practical concerns, not merely blackboard abstractions.

Joule wanted to know whether electrical, mechanical, and thermal processes had a common quantitative measure. His apparatus became famous: falling weights turned paddles immersed in water. Friction from stirring produced a small increase in water temperature. The height and mass of the weights let him estimate how much mechanical work entered the system; the measured temperature increase let him estimate the resulting thermal change.

The challenge was sensitivity. Ordinary warmth from the room, changes in the apparatus, and errors in thermometers could compete with the small signal he wanted to detect. Joule's experimental work involved careful instruments, repeated measurements, and attention to heat lost to the surroundings. This was a human problem of credibility as much as a problem of algebra. Other investigators needed reasons to believe that the measured difference was real.

A surviving instrument in the Science Museum collection provides a material reminder of what this research required. The experiment was not a philosopher declaring that all changes are one thing. It was a person trying to measure a small temperature rise created by a known mechanical action, over and over, with apparatus whose imperfections had to be understood.

Joule's results were part of a wider, contested development. Julius Robert Mayer published an account of energy conservation in 1842. Hermann von Helmholtz presented a general argument in 1847. William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, helped shape the developing physics of heat and work. Attributing the entire first law to one person's flash of insight would hide both the multiple investigations and the disputes that made the principle persuasive.

## A calculation you can audit

Let's imagine an ideal version of Joule's question. A one-kilogram weight falls two meters, giving up about nineteen point six joules of gravitational energy. Suppose every joule goes into warming one tenth of a kilogram of water. Water needs about four thousand one hundred eighty-four joules to raise one kilogram by one degree Celsius, over an ordinary temperature range.

Multiply the water's mass by its specific heat capacity. The result is four hundred eighteen point four joules per degree. Divide nineteen point six by that number. The temperature rise is about zero point zero four seven degrees Celsius. It is a very small change. If we used a larger amount of water, the temperature rise would be smaller still.

The thought experiment is deliberately idealized. A real falling-weight apparatus loses energy into moving parts and the room, and the effective heat capacity includes more than the water alone. Nevertheless, the arithmetic illustrates both the conversion and why precise temperature measurement was demanding.

The equality behind the calculation is simply that the mechanical energy lost equals the thermal energy gained, after all relevant transfers are included. In the companion we will record the formula with units, but the crucial habit is to ask whether the modeled system really captures every path for energy to enter or leave.

## Heat and temperature are not synonyms

People often use the words heat, warmth, and temperature interchangeably. In thermodynamics they describe different things. Temperature is a property of the state of a material. Heat is energy transferred across a system boundary because of a temperature difference. Internal energy is energy associated with microscopic and other internal degrees of freedom in the system.

A metal bar can gain internal energy when you rub it, even when the immediate cause is mechanical work rather than heat transfer from a warmer object. A gas can warm because it is compressed. The language matters because the same change in internal energy can arise from different routes.

In the usual sign convention of thermodynamics, the first law for a closed system says the change in internal energy equals the heat added to the system minus the work done *by* the system. If you compress a gas, work is done on it, so work done by the gas is negative and its internal energy can increase even when no heat enters. Different books sometimes choose the opposite sign convention for work; the physics is the same once the convention is stated consistently.

A closed system can exchange energy but not matter with its surroundings. An isolated system exchanges neither matter nor energy. If we study a running engine by drawing a boundary around the cylinder alone, fuel and exhaust may cross that boundary, and an open-system energy balance needs additional terms. Conservation is not a magic word that eliminates the need to choose a boundary thoughtfully.

## Why engines forced a deeper understanding

Imagine a steam engine. Fuel produces thermal changes, water becomes steam, expanding fluid does work on a piston, and a moving shaft drives machinery. An engineer wants to know how much useful work can be obtained from a given amount of fuel. The first law says energy must be balanced. It does not say every unit of heat can become useful work.

That limitation belongs to the second law of thermodynamics, developed through work by Carnot, Clausius, Kelvin, and others. We will study it separately. For now, keep the distinction: the first law constrains energy bookkeeping; the second law constrains the direction and efficiency of processes. A perfectly balanced energy equation can describe a physically impossible engine if the proposed process violates the second law.

This is why the phrase 'energy cannot be created or destroyed' is insufficient as a complete course in thermodynamics. It is a fundamental accounting rule, but its practical use depends on the system, the forms of energy involved, and the additional physical restrictions governing possible transfers.

## When potential energy is defined, and when it is not

The gravitational example involved a special kind of force. If we carry a weight upward by two meters and then lower it back by the same route, the net work done by gravity over the round trip is zero. The change in potential energy depends on the endpoints, not on the route taken.

A force with this property is called conservative. For such a force we can define a potential-energy function so that work done by the force equals the decrease in potential energy. Springs in the ideal elastic range provide another example: the spring's stored energy depends on the square of its extension. We may transfer energy between a mass's motion and the spring's deformation while preserving their sum.

Sliding friction acts differently. Rubbing a block along a longer path generally transfers more mechanical energy into internal energy. We cannot assign a single position-only potential energy to ordinary dry friction and then recover its work from the endpoints. If a textbook treats friction as a negative change of potential energy, it risks hiding the physical mechanism it should explain.

The correct response is to widen the accounting. Kinetic plus potential energy can decrease while total energy including internal and environmental changes is conserved. The broader law makes sense of the narrower mechanical rule and specifies where that rule stops applying.

## Chemical energy and food make the boundary question personal

A person riding a bicycle up a hill increases gravitational potential energy. The energy does not appear from nowhere. Chemical processes in muscles are coupled to mechanical work, and the body also releases energy as heat. Food has supplied chemical energy through a biological system that is much more complicated than a weight and pulley.

Let's give the hill a number. Suppose a rider and bicycle together have a mass of eighty kilograms and climb a vertical height of one hundred meters. The gain in gravitational potential energy is about seventy-eight thousand four hundred joules. This is the minimum mechanical energy change associated with the increase in height, not the total chemical energy the body must expend. Rolling resistance, air drag, and physiological inefficiencies make the required energy input larger.

If someone measured only the heat leaving the cyclist's body, they would also miss energy used to lift the combined mass. If they measured only the increase in height, they would miss substantial internal and environmental transfers. The law is the same in each description, but the boundary and the counted forms of energy determine what the equation can tell us.

This example makes conservation tangible outside laboratories. Eating, climbing, braking, and warming are not unrelated events. They are connected through transfers that can be calculated approximately and, with better measurements, more precisely. Yet a balanced energy equation by itself does not tell us how metabolism works or what a person will experience. It constrains the account without replacing the underlying biology.

## Conservation as a diagnostic tool

Suppose a numerical model of a frictionless pendulum reports that the bob's kinetic plus gravitational potential energy doubles after every oscillation. Perhaps someone forgot a coefficient. Perhaps the integrator is unstable. Perhaps the chosen time steps are too large. In a model with no external work, sustained energy growth signals a failure of the calculation or the modeling assumptions.

Yet the opposite conclusion is dangerous. If a simulation preserves an energy-like quantity exactly, it may still produce the wrong trajectory. Several incorrect mathematical systems can have conserved quantities. Conservation is a necessary condition in certain models, not always a sufficient test of correctness.

Likewise, suppose an engine's energy balance closes on paper. The model could still ignore dangerous temperatures, pressure limits, frictional damage, or the second law. An engineering certificate needs the right invariants together with tests of the model's assumptions and intended operating conditions.

Conservation is useful because it survives changes in description. A weight falling, a motor turning, a battery discharging, or a metal rod warming can be analyzed with the same unit, the joule. That common measure lets physicists and engineers compare processes which look nothing alike.

## Why measuring energy is harder than naming it

Energy is measured in joules, but different experiments infer joules from different observables. A cart on a track supplies mass and speed. A raised weight supplies mass, gravitational acceleration, and height. A warming sample supplies mass, specific heat capacity, and temperature change. A battery and an electric circuit require electrical measurements and an account of time. The formulas are not interchangeable recipes; they are different physical routes to estimating a common conserved quantity.

That diversity is the reason Joule's work mattered historically. If the mechanical and thermal calculations had yielded incompatible conversion factors even after accounting for losses, the hoped-for unification would have faced serious trouble. Experimental agreement made the claim credible. The principle gained authority because people could test quantitative predictions across phenomena that had once appeared separate.

In modern models, conservation can also be used to diagnose missing variables. If a numerical simulation transfers mechanical energy into heat but no variable stores the thermal change, the model has omitted a state component. If an experimental energy balance shows a gap larger than the stated measurement uncertainty, we should look for overlooked transfers or inaccurate calibration before announcing a violation of a fundamental law.

## The historical surprise

It can be difficult now to appreciate that heat, electricity, chemistry, and mechanical motion once belonged to partly separate explanatory traditions. People could measure a hot object or a moving wheel without knowing that the relevant changes were commensurable. The nineteenth-century energy principle brought them into one quantitative framework.

The people who established that framework had distinct backgrounds and methods. Mayer reasoned from physiological and physical observations. Joule performed painstaking experiments influenced by industrial problems. Helmholtz sought a general framework for natural phenomena. Their conclusions were discussed, criticized, and refined through a scientific community rather than emerging fully formed from a single instrument.

Return to the weight falling into sand. We no longer have to say that its motion simply vanished. Its initial energy has entered a network of transformations. We may have to work to measure each part, and we may have to admit incomplete knowledge of the microscopic details. But the conservation principle tells us what a full accounting must respect.

The next time a system appears to lose energy, ask three questions. What exactly is inside the boundary? What transfers cross that boundary? Which forms of energy have we included in the accounting? Those questions turn a slogan into a tool for physical reasoning.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

**Work–energy theorem.** For a constant mass \(m\), \(\Delta K=\int \mathbf F_{\rm net}\cdot d\mathbf r\), with \(K=\frac12 m\|\mathbf v\|^2\). For a one-dimensional constant acceleration, \(v_f^2-v_i^2=2a\Delta x\).

**Potential.** If \(\mathbf F=-\nabla U\), then \(W_{\mathbf F}=-\Delta U\). In a purely conservative system \(\Delta(K+U)=0\). Near Earth's surface, \(U=mgh\), relative to a chosen reference height.

**Thermal calculation.** \(mgh=(1\,\mathrm{kg})(9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})(2\,\mathrm m)=19.6\,\mathrm J\). With \(m_w=0.1\,\mathrm{kg}\), \(c_w=4184\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}\), ideal \(\Delta T=19.6/(0.1\cdot4184)\approx0.0468\,\mathrm K\).

**Thermodynamic first law.** For a closed system, using \(Q>0\) for heat into it and \(W>0\) for work by it, \(\Delta U=Q-W\). This internal-energy balance omits macroscopic kinetic and potential energy terms; a general total-energy balance includes them as needed.

**Dissipative example.** If \(E_{\rm mech,i}-E_{\rm mech,f}=E_{\rm transferred}\), the transferred energy may be distributed among internal energy and surroundings; it is not represented by a path-independent potential for dry friction.

### Exercises with answers

1. **Exercise:** Find the kinetic energy of a two-kilogram cart moving at three meters per second. **Answer:** \(\frac12(2)(3^2)=9\,\mathrm J\).
2. **Exercise:** A pendulum slows in still air. Has energy conservation failed? **Answer:** No. Mechanical energy has been transferred to air, suspension, and internal degrees of freedom.
3. **Exercise:** In an adiabatic closed system, external work of 50 joules is done *on* a gas. What is \(\Delta U\)? **Answer:** \(Q=0\), \(W=-50\,\mathrm J\), so \(\Delta U=+50\,\mathrm J\).
4. **Exercise:** What does energy balance fail to establish about a heat engine? **Answer:** Whether its efficiency or direction of operation is compatible with the second law.
