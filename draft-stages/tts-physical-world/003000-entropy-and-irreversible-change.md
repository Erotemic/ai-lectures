# Why Does Heat Flow One Way? Entropy and Irreversible Change

Place a warm metal spoon in a cool glass of water. After a while, the spoon and water approach a common temperature. They have exchanged energy. Now imagine the reverse: the water and spoon begin at that common temperature, and with no intervention the spoon becomes hot while the water becomes cooler by just enough to preserve total energy. Nothing in energy conservation, taken by itself, forbids that imagined exchange. And yet we do not see it happen as an ordinary spontaneous process.

This is the problem that leads to the second law of thermodynamics. The first law is an accounting rule: energy entering, leaving, or being stored must balance. The second law adds a restriction on the direction and feasibility of processes. It tells us something that a balanced energy equation cannot tell us. In this lecture we will be careful not to confuse those two jobs.

Begin with a thought experiment. Put two solid blocks in an insulating box. They have equal heat capacities, and one starts at four hundred kelvin while the other begins at three hundred kelvin. We use kelvin rather than Celsius because absolute temperature belongs in thermodynamic ratios. Imagine an idealized case in which the blocks exchange no energy with the outside and their heat capacities remain constant across this temperature interval. Conservation of energy predicts a final common temperature of three hundred fifty kelvin. The final temperature is the average because the blocks have equal heat capacities; unequal heat capacities would produce a weighted average.

Now reverse the starting condition. Put both blocks at three hundred fifty kelvin. The total internal energy in our simplified model would permit them to reach three hundred and four hundred kelvin, one cooling as much as the other warms. Yet without supplying work or creating some compensating change elsewhere, such a spontaneous separation of temperatures violates the second law in the macroscopic conditions we are considering. Conservation permits a great many imagined rearrangements. The second law explains why some directions dominate physical experience.

The word entropy is often introduced with the slogan “disorder.” That can help for one example and confuse almost everything else. Entropy is a thermodynamic state quantity with units of joules per kelvin. For a system passing between equilibrium states, we can calculate its entropy change by imagining a reversible path between the same endpoints and adding, at each step, the reversible heat supplied divided by the absolute temperature. This is a prescription for a state change. It is not permission to divide the heat transferred during an arbitrary irreversible event by one convenient temperature.

Return to the two blocks. When a small amount of energy leaves the warmer block, it loses entropy in proportion to the heat divided by its temperature. The cooler block gains entropy from that energy, and because its temperature is lower, the gain is larger in magnitude than the warm block's loss. Summed over the complete exchange, the total entropy of the isolated pair increases. Neither energy nor entropy vanished; energy was redistributed while a second quantity changed in a direction constrained by the second law.

An exact result is available under our ideal assumptions. If each block has heat capacity one hundred joules per kelvin, each changes from its starting temperature to three hundred fifty. The change in total entropy is one hundred times the natural logarithm of three hundred fifty over four hundred, plus one hundred times the natural logarithm of three hundred fifty over three hundred. That works out to roughly two point zero six joules per kelvin, a positive amount. The final energy balances exactly. The total entropy increases. Both conditions matter.

Why do we call the reverse impossible, when microscopic molecules continually fluctuate? Because there is a distinction between the statistical mechanics of a finite system and an operational law about macroscopic systems. The second law is overwhelmingly reliable for the thermodynamic behavior of very many constituents. In sufficiently small systems and over short times, fluctuations that reduce a suitably defined entropy-related quantity can occur; their probability and the measurement protocol matter. “Entropy never decreases anywhere, ever” is not a careful version of the law. The useful macroscopic statement is about the entropy production of an isolated whole or of a system together with its environment, with the relevant idealizations stated.

A striking thought experiment makes the statistical aspect visible. Imagine an insulated rigid container divided into two chambers, with gas confined initially to one side and a vacuum on the other. Remove the internal partition. The gas spreads into both chambers. For a dilute ideal gas undergoing free expansion into vacuum, there is no work against an external opposing pressure and no heat enters through the insulated walls. Its internal energy remains constant, and its temperature remains constant in the ideal-gas model. Yet the expansion is irreversible. The gas now occupies a larger volume, and its entropy has risen.

For a fixed number of ideal-gas particles, doubling the available volume at the same temperature increases entropy by the gas constant times the number of moles times the natural logarithm of two. This calculation comes from a hypothetical reversible route connecting the same equilibrium states, not from treating the actual violent mixing as reversible. For one mole the increase is about five point seven six joules per kelvin. The ideal-gas law and the entropy calculation use assumptions: the gas is dilute enough to approximate as ideal, the initial and final states are equilibrium states, and we neglect interactions with the container beyond the model.

The microscopic explanation is compelling. There are vastly more particle configurations compatible with gas throughout the whole volume than with every particle confined to one half. Random molecular motion carries the system into the overwhelmingly larger collection of configurations. We need not pretend that individual molecules have a wish to spread out. Nor should we equate entropy with whether a picture looks messy. A sorted pile of stones might be visually orderly and have high thermodynamic entropy in an unrelated sense. The physical counting concerns accessible microscopic states under specified macroscopic constraints.

It is worth noticing what the statistical explanation does not say. It does not say the microscopic equations of motion contain a built-in sign pointing toward the future in the same simple manner as friction in a coarse-grained model. At the level of idealized time-reversal-symmetric dynamics, the puzzle is how macroscopic irreversibility emerges from typical initial conditions, enormous numbers of microscopic degrees of freedom, and the loss of accessible details in our descriptions. The low-entropy character of many relevant initial conditions matters. Entropy is not just a fancy word for clocks ticking.

Now introduce a device: a heat engine. A heat engine receives energy as heat from a hot reservoir, delivers useful mechanical work, and rejects some heat to a colder reservoir. Its operation is cyclical, meaning the engine itself returns to its starting state after each cycle. Over a full cycle its internal energy does not accumulate indefinitely, so the work output equals the heat absorbed minus the heat rejected. That equation comes from the first law. Nothing in it alone prevents the engine from converting every joule received from one reservoir into work.

The second law supplies the missing limitation. A cyclic engine operating between two reservoirs cannot outperform the ideal reversible efficiency of one minus the cold reservoir's absolute temperature divided by the hot reservoir's temperature. If the hot side is at six hundred kelvin and the cold side at three hundred, the maximum ideal thermal efficiency is fifty percent. Real engines do worse because of irreversibility: finite temperature differences, friction, fluid resistance, chemical and material limits, and other practical effects. The fifty percent figure is not a claim that half of all energy everywhere must be wasted. It belongs to that specified two-temperature heat-engine arrangement.

Someone may object: a refrigerator pumps heat from a cold interior into a warmer room every day. Does that refute the second law? Not at all. The compressor consumes electrical work, and the whole refrigerator also rejects the work input as heat. It is possible to drive heat from cold to hot if you account for a compensating input and for what happens to the surroundings. The word spontaneously in “heat does not flow spontaneously from cold to hot” carries this distinction.

History helps show why this was hard. Sadi Carnot analyzed heat engines in 1824, before the modern energy principle had been fully consolidated. His reasoning was embedded in contemporary conceptions of heat, some of which were later replaced. Clausius and Kelvin developed different formulations of the second law during the nineteenth century, after evidence had made the equivalence of heat and work central. Boltzmann helped link entropy to the statistics of microscopic states. These contributions were not merely successive names for one obvious law. The meaning of heat, reversibility, and the possibility of a microscopic explanation were changing together.

Think again of the spoon in water. We can describe the direction in which heat flows, account for the energy transferred, and measure the entropy produced. But the same ideas apply far beyond a spoon: to cooling servers, to engines, to weather, to stars radiating into space, and to biological systems. A living organism can maintain highly organized internal structure while taking in usable energy and exporting heat and other products. A local decrease of entropy is compatible with a larger positive entropy production in the surroundings. Never decide the second law from a photograph of the subsystem alone.

If you encounter a proposed machine that runs forever using a single uniform-temperature heat reservoir and returns to its initial state while delivering net work, ask for both balances. Does the energy accounting close? Does the entropy balance close? An answer to only the first question is incomplete. Physical laws are not isolated slogans; they place intersecting constraints on what our models and machines can do.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

**Two-block thought experiment.** For two isolated bodies with constant, equal heat capacity \(C\), initial temperatures \(T_1,T_2\), and final equilibrium temperature \(T_f=(T_1+T_2)/2\):
\[
\Delta S=C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_1}+C\ln\frac{T_f}{T_2}.
\]
For \(T_1=400\,\mathrm K\), \(T_2=300\,\mathrm K\), \(C=100\,\mathrm{J/K}\), \(T_f=350\,\mathrm K\): \(\Delta S\approx2.06\,\mathrm{J/K}>0\). This is a **hypothetical calculation**, not a measured experiment.

**Ideal gas free expansion.** \(\Delta U=0\) and, for an ideal gas, \(\Delta T=0\). The entropy change for \(n\) moles from \(V_i\) to \(V_f\) is \(\Delta S=nR\ln(V_f/V_i)\); for one mole doubling volume, \(\Delta S\approx5.763\,\mathrm{J/K}\). This entropy integral is evaluated using an imagined **reversible comparison path**, not the actual free expansion.

**Reversible reference path.** \(\Delta S=\int_A^B \delta Q_{\rm rev}/T\), where \(T\) is in kelvin. Do **not** substitute actual \(Q\) from an irreversible path into \(Q/T\) without the requisite assumptions. For the ideal two-reservoir Carnot limit, \(\eta\le1-T_c/T_h\) for positive absolute temperatures with \(T_h>T_c\).

### Exercises with answers

1. An insulated ideal gas doubles its volume in free expansion into vacuum. Has its total energy changed? **No**, under the ideal assumptions; its entropy has increased.
2. A two-reservoir engine has \(T_h=500\,\mathrm K\) and \(T_c=300\,\mathrm K\). What is the maximum ideal efficiency? **40 percent.**
3. A refrigerator removes 100 J from the cold side and consumes 40 J of work. How much heat is delivered to the warmer room in a cycle? **140 J**, neglecting other transfers.
4. Why is “entropy means visual messiness” not a reliable definition? **Because** thermodynamic entropy depends on a physical state and microscopic possibilities, not the appearance of an image.
