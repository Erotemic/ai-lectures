# Light from Mirrors to Stars: How Optics Makes Images and Evidence

An apple looks red in daylight. If you carried that same apple into a room lit only with a narrow band of green light, its appearance would change. Nothing about the apple's basic identity would have changed. Its surface would be illuminated by a different set of wavelengths, and the light reaching your eyes would be different. This is a useful beginning for optics, because it shows how easily we mistake an interaction for a permanent property of an object. Color is the result of illumination, material response and perception, not a coat of red light living permanently inside the apple.

Consider what the eye actually receives. Light from a lamp or the Sun travels to an object. Some wavelengths are absorbed, some scattered, and some reflected toward us. The visual system uses signals from several kinds of photoreceptors, including cone cells involved in color discrimination, to construct an appearance. Two very different mixtures of wavelengths can even look like the same color to an observer because they stimulate the receptors similarly. This is one reason the image displayed on a screen can look like a real scene while containing vastly less spectral information than the scene itself.

For a first model, forget color and treat light as traveling along straight rays. A ray is not a tiny physical arrow or an individual photon; it is a line that indicates the direction in which energy propagates in the geometric-optics approximation. When light's wavelength is much smaller than the objects and openings involved, ray diagrams often predict imaging well. A mirror sends a ray away with its outgoing angle equal to the incoming angle, both measured from the surface normal. This simple law explains why a flat mirror appears to put an object behind its surface at a corresponding distance, even though no light must actually originate there.

Now look through the surface of a swimming pool. Objects under water can appear displaced from where they are physically located. The reason is refraction. Light propagates differently through different transparent materials, changing its direction at a boundary except in special geometries. For simple isotropic materials, Snell's law relates the sines of the angles to the refractive indices. The frequency of a monochromatic wave stays the same across a stationary interface, while its wavelength and phase velocity in the material change. Saying that glass 'slows light' is a useful shorthand for an effective propagation speed, but the microscopic response of charges in matter is richer than photons behaving like cars waiting at a speed-limit sign.

Imagine a light ray passing from air into ordinary water at an angle of thirty degrees from the normal. Using approximate indices of one for air and one point three three for water, Snell's law predicts a refracted angle of about twenty-two degrees. The ray bends toward the normal because the water index is larger. Now imagine reversing its path. Light retraces the same geometric route through those passive media under the appropriate reciprocal conditions. A line that seems broken through a glass of water is a clue about the wave's change of direction at the boundaries, not a physical kink in the object itself.

There is a special case when light travels from a higher-index medium toward a lower-index one. Beyond a critical incidence angle, the ray model predicts no propagating transmitted ray and we get total internal reflection for an ideal smooth interface. For water to air, the critical angle is roughly forty-nine degrees measured inside the water from the normal. The exact value depends on wavelength and material. This is one reason light can be guided through optical fibers: the geometry and refractive-index design keep suitable guided modes confined. Real fibers still have absorption, scattering and bending losses, and modern fiber optics ultimately requires a wave treatment rather than a collection of perfectly bouncing billiard balls.

Why do lenses bring some light rays together and send others apart? A lens has curved surfaces, so the local surface normal changes from point to point. Refraction therefore bends incoming rays differently across the lens. A convex lens can form a real image on a screen when the object is at an appropriate distance; a camera uses a lens or lens group to form an image on a sensor. Magnification does not create information from nothing. A lens trades field of view, distance, numerical aperture and resolution under practical constraints. Focusing sharply at one distance does not guarantee that objects at all distances will be equally sharp.

The ray picture explains many effects, but it reaches a limit. Shine light through a very narrow slit and examine the pattern on a distant screen. Instead of a perfectly sharp stripe defined by straight rays, the illumination spreads and shows structured bright and dark regions. Narrow the opening, and the diffraction becomes more noticeable. The wave description predicts this because contributions emerging from different parts of the opening can reinforce or cancel. The interaction is not the light rebelling against straight-line motion. A straight ray is simply no longer a complete description for that scale.

If two coherent paths bring light to a point with a phase difference corresponding to an integer number of wavelengths, they can reinforce. If they arrive approximately half a wavelength out of step, their field amplitudes can cancel in an idealized experiment. Intensity is related to the square of the summed field amplitude. You cannot generally add intensities first and expect to recover an interference pattern. This is why coherent light sources and careful control of phase make optical interference experiments possible.

Thomas Young became an important figure in this history. In 1801 he presented an argument for the wave theory of light to the Royal Society, and later experiments made interference patterns central to the case. The popular image of Young immediately performing today's neat laser-and-two-identical-slits demonstration is historically compressed. His early arrangements involved narrow beams and an obstacle such as a card, with later descriptions employing slits. The essential observation was that light from two paths could produce alternating enhancement and cancellation, a pattern hard to reconcile with a naive stream of independent mechanical pellets. His argument did not settle the future of physics in one afternoon, but it made a powerful prediction testable.

A particularly beautiful interference experiment requires no laboratory. Look at a soap bubble. Its film has two reflecting surfaces, separated by a thickness that changes over the bubble. Light reflected from the front and from the back surface can interfere, and the condition depends on wavelength, optical path and phase shifts at reflection. The result is swirling bands of color that change as the film drains and thins. The bubble does not need colored pigment to display those colors. The wavelengths that are reinforced for one film thickness differ from those reinforced for another. If the film becomes sufficiently thin, it can appear dark in reflected white light even while some light passes through.

The same distinction between geometry and wave behavior explains rainbows. Sunlight enters raindrops, refracts, reflects internally and refracts again as it leaves. Because different wavelengths refract by different amounts, their outgoing directions differ. For a primary rainbow, many rays emerge concentrated around angles of roughly forty to forty-two degrees from the direction opposite the Sun, with the exact angle differing among colors. To see a rainbow, the Sun is generally behind you and raindrops are ahead. The rainbow does not hang at one fixed physical address to which everybody can walk. Each observer receives rays from a different selection of drops satisfying the right angles. A secondary bow involves an additional internal reflection and has the color order reversed.

Rainbows are also a good reminder not to overgeneralize an optical description. The ray account explains the broad geometry and the strong primary bow. Fine supernumerary fringes near some bows require interference between rays following different paths. Polarization also appears in rainbow light. One observation can support several layered models, each resolving a different part of the phenomenon.

What is polarization? An electromagnetic wave has electric and magnetic fields that oscillate transversely to its propagation direction in a simple plane-wave description. Polarization tracks the orientation and statistical behavior of the oscillating electric field. Ordinary sunlight is not generally one single fixed linear polarization. A polarizing filter selects certain orientations of field oscillation and attenuates others. If linearly polarized light passes through an ideal analyzer turned by an angle from the polarization direction, the transmitted intensity follows the cosine squared of that angle. Two crossed ideal polarizers can strongly block light. Adding a third one between them at an intermediate angle can actually permit some light through, provided you track how each filter changes the state. It is a striking demonstration that optical components act on the state of light, not just on its brightness.

Polarization makes reflected glare manageable, can reveal stress patterns in transparent materials, and plays a role in liquid-crystal displays and remote sensing. None of these applications requires the fiction that light vibrates sideways like a rope in ordinary three-dimensional space. The electric field is the thing represented by the oscillating vector. A wave diagram is a model of field quantities, not a photograph of the interior of a photon.

In the nineteenth century, the description of light became deeper. Electricity and magnetism had already been studied as separate phenomena; James Clerk Maxwell's equations joined them into a field theory in which time-changing electric and magnetic fields support propagating waves. The predicted wave speed in vacuum agreed with the measured speed of light. This made visible light one part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays differ primarily in frequency and wavelength, not in being unrelated kinds of magical radiation. But their interactions with matter can be extremely different because the photon energy and material response change with frequency.

How can a wave be made of an electromagnetic field and yet light arrive at a detector in individual events? Quantum physics changes our answer without discarding optical interference. The electromagnetic field is quantized, and the energy associated with an individual photon is proportional to frequency. A highly attenuated experiment can register individual detection events, while the distribution of many events shows an interference pattern when the relevant coherence is preserved. This is not well explained by picturing a tiny classical ball that somehow splits into two independent half-balls. Quantum probabilities and amplitudes must replace the naive mechanical image. The earlier lecture on atoms and radiation introduced the photoelectric threshold; here the point is that wave optics and quantized detection are parts of one experimentally constrained picture.

A camera lens, microscope or telescope has another limitation even if its glass is flawless: diffraction. A circular aperture makes a point source into a spread-out pattern, with a central bright spot surrounded by rings under ideal conditions. Two neighboring points can have overlapping patterns so severe that they cannot be distinguished reliably. A standard estimate for the angular scale of this diffraction limit is about one point two two times the wavelength divided by the aperture diameter, in radians. If the aperture is one millimeter wide and the light wavelength is five hundred nanometers, the estimate is roughly six tenths of a milliradian, around three hundredths of a degree. This is only a diffraction estimate; actual image quality is also affected by aberrations, sensor sampling, motion, focus, atmosphere and signal noise.

Increasing aperture improves diffraction-limited angular resolution, but there are practical tradeoffs. Big telescope mirrors need precise shaping and stable support. Microscopes depend on numerical aperture and the optical medium surrounding the specimen. A higher pixel count on a sensor cannot restore distinctions that never reached it, although computational methods can improve estimates when supported by additional measurements or prior information. 'Resolution' is not a single number detached from what you are measuring and how much noise is present.

Optics also provides a way to identify things too distant to sample directly. If we spread the light from a star into a spectrum, narrow bright or dark features can reveal which atomic and molecular transitions influence that radiation. The pattern can depend on temperature, pressure, motion, magnetic field and composition, so interpretation requires a model. Hydrogen's spectral features helped reveal the quantum structure of atoms; a single black line does not automatically identify one unique element in a mixture. Calibration against known laboratory spectra and careful modeling make astronomical spectroscopy possible.

The position of spectral features can change if the source or observer moves, through the Doppler effect and other relativistic effects where appropriate. The width and profile of a line can provide evidence about motion and conditions in the emitting region. Light is therefore not merely the means of taking a pretty photograph of a galaxy. It is a messenger that carries measurable information about energy levels, velocities and fields at places humans cannot visit.

And it is a delayed messenger. Sunlight takes approximately eight minutes to reach Earth. Looking at the Sun through appropriate scientific instruments is observing it as it was minutes earlier. Looking at a distant galaxy is observing light emitted much longer ago, with cosmological expansion and observational geometry complicating the simple phrase 'how far back in time'. Astronomers are constrained by the travel time of signals just as laboratory physicists are constrained by the speed of propagation along an optical fiber.

Return to the apple. The light reflected from its skin can be described by rays when we ask where an image forms, by waves when we ask why a film displays colors, by fields when we ask how radiation propagates, and by quantum theory when we ask how photons are absorbed. Those descriptions do not compete for the title of the only respectable explanation. They answer nested questions at different scales. The most powerful outcome of optics is not a list of effects called reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference. It is that a measurable disturbance traveling through space can become an image, an energy transfer, a chemical fingerprint or a test of our account of the world.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

- **Reflection:** \(\theta_i=\theta_r\) measured from the normal.
- **Refraction:** \(n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2\); for air at \(n\simeq1\) to water at \(n\simeq1.33\), \(\theta_1=30^\circ\) gives \(\theta_2\simeq22.1^\circ\).
- **Total internal reflection:** For \(n_1>n_2\), \(\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1\). Water to air yields \(\theta_c\simeq48.8^\circ\) with representative indices.
- **Vacuum propagation:** \(c=\lambda f\); in a transparent nonmagnetic medium, \(n\) is the ratio of vacuum speed to phase velocity at a given frequency. Frequency is continuous across a static boundary.
- **Interference:** For two coherent scalar amplitudes \(E_1,E_2\), intensity is proportional to \(|E_1+E_2|^2\), not simply \(|E_1|^2+|E_2|^2\); vector polarization and coherence must be included when relevant.
- **Ideal linear polarizer:** \(I=I_0\cos^2\theta\) for an incident linearly polarized beam and an ideal analyzer.
- **Rayleigh criterion:** \(\theta\simeq1.22\lambda/D\) for an ideal circular aperture; \(\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(D=1\,\mathrm{mm}\) gives \(6.1\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}\approx0.035^\circ\).
- **Photon energy:** \(E=hf\). This supports quantized detection and does not by itself make classical ray optics invalid in its domain.

### Questions to test the model

1. Why does a submerged object appear displaced? **Refraction changes the direction of light at the water-air interface.**
2. Does a polarizer simply remove a fixed fraction of light independent of its state? **No.** Transmission depends on the incident polarization and analyzer orientation.
3. Can a larger number of camera pixels defeat diffraction by itself? **No.** Detector sampling and optical resolution are distinct limits.
4. Why does a soap bubble show color when its liquid is nearly clear? **Thin-film interference selectively enhances and suppresses reflected wavelengths.**
5. Does a star's absorption spectrum amount to a direct photograph of its atoms? **No.** It is evidence about atomic transitions interpreted through radiative-transfer and atmospheric models.
