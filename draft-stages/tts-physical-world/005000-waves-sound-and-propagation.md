# How Does a Disturbance Travel? Waves, Sound, and Resonance

Imagine a long rope stretched between two people. One person moves an end upward and back down. A bump travels along the rope. The bump may reach the other person, but a marked point halfway along does not travel all the way from one end to the other. It rises, falls, and stays near its original position. Something has moved through the rope without the rope itself being carried across the room.

This distinction is the beginning of wave physics. A wave is a disturbance that propagates through a system. In a mechanical wave, matter participates locally in the motion, and the disturbance can carry energy between locations. The things being transported and the things oscillating are not necessarily the same. The familiar phrase “a wave moves” can hide this question unless we specify what moves.

The rope example is a transverse wave: the marked point moves up and down while the bump travels horizontally. For a sound wave in ordinary air, the microscopic air motion is primarily back and forth along the direction in which the sound travels. Regions of compression and rarefaction move outward. Such a mechanical wave is longitudinal. A picture of air molecules marching from a speaker all the way to a listener would be misleading. The molecules jiggle around local positions while the acoustic disturbance progresses.

Let's give the movement three useful measurements. Frequency is the number of cycles per second. A frequency of one hundred seventy hertz means one hundred seventy cycles per second. Wavelength is the distance between successive equivalent points of a periodic wave, such as two neighboring compressions in air. Speed tells us how far the disturbance pattern advances per unit time. For a periodic traveling wave these are related: wave speed equals frequency times wavelength.

In air near room temperature, a typical sound speed is about three hundred forty meters per second. If the sound frequency is one hundred seventy cycles per second, its wavelength is about two meters. Notice that cycles cancel when we multiply cycles per second by meters per cycle. The result is meters per second. The numerical answer is not a universal wavelength for a note labeled one hundred seventy hertz. The wavelength changes if the speed of sound changes with the medium or temperature, even when the source frequency remains the same.

Here is another useful observation. During a thunderstorm, the flash is usually seen before the sound arrives. Light propagates vastly faster through air than sound. For a rough estimate, if three seconds separate the visible flash and the thunder, the source may be about one kilometer away: three seconds times roughly three hundred forty meters per second. This approximation assumes the light delay is negligible, the sound follows a reasonably direct path, and the flash and clap come from the same event. Wind, echoes, atmospheric temperature, and the geometry of the lightning path limit the precision. The calculation is not a distance-measuring instrument with guaranteed one-meter accuracy.

What sets the speed of a wave? It is tempting to say that harder shaking always makes a wave travel faster. That is not generally true. On an ideal stretched string, the speed is determined by the tension and mass per unit length. Raise the tension while keeping the string otherwise unchanged and the wave usually travels faster. Raise the mass per unit length while holding tension fixed and it travels more slowly. For a simple ideal string, speed is the square root of tension divided by linear mass density. The square root matters: multiplying the tension by four doubles speed, rather than multiplying it by four.

That equation passes a dimensional check. Tension is force, with units of kilograms times meters per second squared. Mass per unit length is kilograms per meter. Divide the former by the latter and kilograms cancel, leaving meters squared per second squared. Take the square root and we obtain meters per second, the correct unit for speed. A physical formula should survive more than a mnemonic; it must make sense in its own dimensions.

A real string complicates the model. Stiffness, damping, large-amplitude motion, changing tension, and the way it couples to an instrument can all matter. A guitar string is a useful example because we can adjust its tension and effective vibrating length, but the reasoning is not guitar-specific. Strings on other instruments, laboratory wires, and resonant structures obey related constraints. Instrument names are examples of implementations, not laws of nature.

What happens when the traveling wave meets a boundary? Some disturbance may reflect, transmit into another material, or be absorbed. On a stretched string fixed at both ends, reflections can combine into standing-wave patterns. Instead of a bump continuing outward, we see positions that remain nearly motionless, called nodes, and positions that oscillate with larger amplitude, called antinodes. Under an ideal fixed-end model, the lowest-frequency standing-wave mode fits half a wavelength into the string's length. Higher modes fit two, three, or more half-wavelengths. Their frequencies are approximately integer multiples of the fundamental.

That is one reason strings can produce musical tones with harmonic overtones. But not every resonator has the same mode series. A tube open at both ends, a tube closed at one end, a drum membrane, a metal bar, and a complex air cavity have different boundary conditions and different resonant frequencies. It would be an error to apply the ideal string formula to all of them merely because they can make sound.

Resonance deserves a more careful description than “things vibrate at their favorite frequency.” In a linear driven system, a driving force can transfer energy efficiently near certain characteristic frequencies. The response depends on damping, coupling, driving frequency, and geometry. A strongly damped system may have a broad, modest response rather than a dramatic peak. A resonator with several modes has several possible responses. Music and engineering both exploit these facts, but resonance is also central to unwanted vibration and structural fatigue.

Now imagine two tuning forks with nearby frequencies, perhaps four hundred forty and four hundred forty-two hertz. When both sounds reach the ear together at similar amplitudes, their superposed pressure variations reinforce and cancel periodically. We hear a changing loudness called beats, with a rate of about two cycles per second. The sound waves have not begun taking turns existing. Their instantaneous pressures are adding, and their relative phase slowly changes. This simple demonstration illustrates superposition: within the linear regime, disturbances add to form a resultant disturbance.

Superposition can produce quieter regions as well as louder ones. Two equal sound waves that arrive exactly out of phase can cancel at a point in an idealized arrangement. But cancellation at one point is not proof that all acoustic energy has been destroyed. The energy may be redistributed spatially, reflected, absorbed, or exchanged with sources and boundaries. If two sound fields arrive with changing phase across a room, different locations can experience different combinations. This helps explain why moving a microphone a short distance may change a measured bass response dramatically.

Frequency, amplitude, and wave speed answer different questions. Higher frequency often corresponds to higher perceived pitch in simple periodic tones, but pitch perception is more complex than a frequency meter. Larger acoustic pressure amplitude commonly corresponds to greater sound intensity, yet perceived loudness depends on frequency, duration, context, and the human auditory system. A wave's frequency does not automatically determine its speed in a given nondispersive medium. In some media, different frequencies propagate at different speeds; this is called dispersion.

Consider a hypothetical experiment with a loudspeaker and two microphones placed at known distances along the same line in a quiet space. Record a brief sound pulse. If the first microphone detects the pulse and the second detects it one hundredth of a second later, and they are three point four meters apart, the inferred propagation speed is about three hundred forty meters per second. That calculation assumes the pulse timing is correctly identified and the relevant path is direct. Echoes and electronic delays can produce misleading arrival times. Repeating the measurement at different separations helps distinguish a real propagation time from a fixed instrument delay.

The mathematical picture becomes even more useful when we describe what travels in a wave. In sound, pressure and particle motion fluctuate. On a string, transverse displacement varies. In an electromagnetic wave, oscillating electric and magnetic fields propagate without needing air, water, or a rope. Light from the Sun reaches us through nearly empty space. That is not just sound moving through an invisible kind of air. The physical quantities and the equations differ, even though certain wave patterns and concepts appear in both descriptions.

The broad wave concept also has limits. Quantum theory uses wave-like probability amplitudes, but those are not simply little classical ripples in a material substance. Ocean waves can involve dispersive surface motions, fluid flow, and breaking; treating them as small-amplitude sinusoidal waves only works in selected circumstances. The value of the wave model is not that everything is secretly the same wave. It is that a family of mathematical relations repeatedly helps us understand propagation, interference, and response.

Return to the rope. The person at one end sends a pulse. A marked point moves locally. Another person receives the disturbance later, after it has traveled along a physical system. With measurements of distance, time, frequency, and boundary conditions, we can calculate the speed and behavior of the propagation. We have moved from asking how a body accelerates under forces to asking how the effect of a disturbance moves through many coupled parts of a system. That change of viewpoint is the bridge from mechanics to acoustics, optics, and modern field theory.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

**Wave variables.** \(f=1/T\), \(v=f\lambda\). If \(v=340\,\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(f=170\,\mathrm{Hz}\), then \(\lambda=2\,\mathrm m\).

**Ideal string.** Wave speed is \(v=\sqrt{F_T/\mu}\) with string tension \(F_T\) and linear mass density \(\mu\). For length \(L\) with **both ends fixed**, allowed ideal mode frequencies are \(f_n=nv/(2L)\), \(n=1,2,3,\ldots\). These are model-specific boundary conditions.

**Interference and beats.** With two equal-amplitude sinusoidal tones at nearby frequencies \(f_1,f_2\), the envelope oscillates at beat frequency \(|f_1-f_2|\); with 440 and 442 Hz the perceived beating rate is about 2 Hz when the tones are sufficiently audible and comparable.

**Mic timing.** For two microphones separated along a direct wave path by \(\Delta x\), \(v\approx\Delta x/\Delta t\). With \(\Delta x=3.4\,\mathrm m\), \(\Delta t=0.01\,\mathrm s\): \(v=340\,\mathrm{m/s}\).

### Exercises with answers

1. The speed of a water wave is 2 m/s and its frequency is 0.5 Hz. Wavelength? **4 m.**
2. On an ideal fixed-end string, if tension quadruples with mass density and length constant, the fundamental frequency changes by what factor? **Two.**
3. A 500 Hz tone and a 503 Hz tone of comparable amplitude play together. Beat rate? **About 3 Hz.**
4. Does a sound pulse propagate through vacuum? **No:** ordinary sound requires a mechanical medium; electromagnetic radiation does not.
