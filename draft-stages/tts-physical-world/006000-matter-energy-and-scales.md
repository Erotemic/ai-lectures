# What Changes Across Scales? Atoms, Radiation, and Mass–Energy

A glass of water on a table appears continuous. We can measure its temperature, volume, and mass without tracking a single molecule. A thermometer can report that the water warms by one degree. A pressure sensor can register a change in the surrounding air. These measurements are real and the thermodynamic equations can be remarkably predictive. But the water is not actually one continuous, featureless substance. It consists of interacting molecules, and those molecules consist of atoms whose behavior is not accurately described as miniature planets and springs.

How can the same physical world support both descriptions? The answer begins with scale. A model that makes reliable predictions about a liter of water may ignore every molecule. A model of chemical bonding must describe electrons and nuclei. A model of nuclear reactions must treat changes inside nuclei. The smaller model does not automatically replace the larger one: calculating the precise quantum state of all the particles in a swimming pool is neither necessary nor practical to predict how much it warms when we supply a known amount of heat.

The ability to simplify across scales is a major achievement of physics. It is also a source of traps. If we forget which details have been averaged out, we may mistake a convenient macroscopic law for a microscopic statement. Temperature is a good example. A single molecule does not have a temperature in the same thermodynamic sense as a gas at equilibrium. The gas has temperature because we can describe the statistical behavior of many constituents under specified conditions. In an ideal monatomic gas, the average translational kinetic energy per particle is proportional to absolute temperature. That relation is useful, but it is not a universal rule for every molecule's instantaneous kinetic energy.

Let us compare sizes. A typical atom is on the order of one ten-billionth of a meter across. Atomic nuclei are roughly one hundred-thousandth of that scale, though exact sizes vary with the element and with how we define a boundary. If we expanded an atom to the width of a sports stadium, its nucleus would occupy only a comparatively tiny central region. That analogy does not mean an atom is empty in the everyday sense, with hard classical particles circling through a vacuum. Electrons are described by quantum states, and the distribution of electric charge and probabilities matters throughout the atom.

A familiar historical clue came from light. When gas atoms are excited and then emit light, their spectra contain characteristic frequencies rather than every conceivable frequency in one smooth sequence. Those discrete spectral lines are a challenge for a naive model of orbiting classical charges. Modern quantum mechanics explains atomic structure through allowed energy states and transitions among them. The difference between two atomic energy levels determines the energy of a photon that can be emitted or absorbed in an appropriate transition. This account has selection rules, line broadening, and interactions with the environment; it is not a claim that each atom emits only one perfectly sharp tone under all circumstances.

What is a photon? In quantum theory, electromagnetic radiation exchanges energy in quantized amounts. For a photon of frequency f, the energy is Planck's constant times f. Higher frequency means greater energy per photon. Wavelength and frequency in vacuum are related by the speed of light, so shorter wavelengths correspond to higher photon energies. Do not confuse this photon energy with how bright a beam looks. Intensity also depends on how many photons arrive and how their energy is distributed over space and time.

The photoelectric effect gives a particularly clear experiment. Shine light on a suitable metal surface in a controlled apparatus and measure the electrons that escape. For ordinary single-photon photoemission, there is a threshold frequency below which no photoelectrons are emitted, regardless of increasing the light's intensity under those conditions. Above the threshold, increasing intensity can increase the number of electrons emitted, while changing frequency changes the maximum kinetic energy of an emitted electron. The maximum energy is the photon energy minus the work function, the energy needed to liberate an electron from the material under the model.

Imagine a simplified metal surface with a work function of four electronvolts. An electronvolt is a unit of energy: the energy associated with moving one elementary charge through a potential difference of one volt. In the approximate relation between photon energy and wavelength, we can express energy in electronvolts as twelve hundred forty divided by wavelength in nanometers. A photon of wavelength five hundred nanometers carries about two point four eight electronvolts, too little for our four-electronvolt threshold in the single-photon model. A two-hundred-fifty-nanometer photon carries about four point nine six electronvolts, leaving roughly zero point nine six electronvolts of possible maximum electron kinetic energy. The example is hypothetical; the threshold is a chosen model value, not a claim about an unnamed real metal.

This is not just an equation with a new constant. It represents a change in what the experiment tells us matter and light can do. Classical wave equations are extremely successful for many optical effects such as interference and diffraction. Quantum theory is needed to explain the quantized energy exchange in this experiment. We do not throw away waves and keep particles instead, as if one were the true picture and the other an embarrassment. We use a quantum theory that reproduces different observed phenomena under their appropriate conditions.

Another scale transition occurs between chemistry and nuclear physics. Many chemical changes involve electron arrangements and energies conveniently measured in electronvolts per particle. Nuclear binding and reactions typically involve much larger energies per participating nucleon, commonly expressed in millions of electronvolts. This contrast helps explain why a chemical battery and a nuclear reactor are different technologies, but it does not mean every nuclear reaction releases energy. Whether a particular reaction can release energy depends on its reactants, products, conservation laws, and changes in binding.

The relation between mass and energy is famously associated with Einstein. The energy of a system at rest includes an amount proportional to its mass, with the factor given by the speed of light squared. A difference in a system's rest mass corresponds to a difference in rest energy. In nuclear processes, the masses of the initial and final systems can differ, with energy carried away by particles and radiation or other forms of motion. It is common to say mass is “converted into energy,” but that phrase can obscure the accounting: energy is always conserved, and the rest mass of a chosen collection of objects need not be conserved separately.

The numbers are startling because the speed of light is so large. A rest-mass difference of one nanogram corresponds to roughly ninety thousand joules. A nanogram is one trillionth of a kilogram. We are not claiming that a nanogram of ordinary matter spontaneously releases that energy. The calculation states what energy difference corresponds to that rest-mass difference if a physical process permits it. Available reactions, reaction products, constraints, and engineering feasibility decide what actually occurs.

There is a more modest way to see why the relation matters. A warm sealed container has, in principle, slightly more total rest energy and therefore slightly more invariant mass than an otherwise identical cooler container, provided we compare their complete systems in their rest frames. The mass difference associated with a familiar laboratory heat transfer is far too small to weigh with ordinary scales. Mass-energy equivalence does not mean we could run a household appliance on the ordinary mass decrease of a spoon without an actual mechanism for delivering energy.

Scale enters again when we think about forces. An electron in an atom responds to electromagnetic interactions, but its stable states are not simply trajectories under the classical force law. Nuclear structure involves strong interactions not captured by Coulomb attraction between charges. Gravity governs the large-scale orbital motion of planets in most familiar examples, but at ordinary atomic scales it is extraordinarily weak compared with electromagnetic effects. The same word “force” can be useful across these contexts, while the mathematical representation of an interaction can change substantially.

Return to water. A glass can be well described by mass density, fluid dynamics, and thermodynamics. If we ask why water has its characteristic molecular structure, we need quantum chemistry. If we ask why hydrogen and oxygen nuclei exist, we move into nuclear physics and the history of cosmic nucleosynthesis. If we ask how a signal travels from the Sun to warm the water, we need radiation and fields. The questions are connected, but the useful state variables and approximations are different.

This layered picture also provides a discipline for testing explanations. When someone claims that a microscopic quantum effect explains a large-scale behavior, ask for the bridge: what quantities are measured at each scale, how are they linked, and what competing macroscopic explanations have been ruled out? When someone claims thermodynamics is false because a single molecule behaved unpredictably, ask whether the thermodynamic description even applies to that object. Good physics earns its authority by specifying what a model explains and what it does not.

We began with a cart, learned to measure motion and forces, followed energy into heat and entropy, described influences with fields, and watched waves carry disturbances. At the scale of atoms and nuclei, those same themes reappear with new mathematical descriptions. Quantities are still measured; conservation laws still constrain outcomes; boundaries and approximations still matter. The physical world is not one formula copied across all scales. It is a network of theories whose connections have been tested, and whose limits tell us where to look next.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

**Photon energy.** \(E=hf=hc/\lambda\) in vacuum. Numerically \(hc\approx1240\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}\). For \(\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(E\approx2.48\,\mathrm{eV}\); for \(250\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(E\approx4.96\,\mathrm{eV}\). In a simplified photoelectric model, \(K_{\max}=hf-\phi\) when \(hf\ge\phi\), where \(\phi\) is the work function.

**Mass–energy.** \(E_0=mc^2\). With \(m=10^{-12}\,\mathrm{kg}\) (one nanogram), \(mc^2\approx8.99\times10^4\,\mathrm J\). This is **an equivalence calculation**, not a description of an available source of power.

**Microscopic energy vs temperature.** In an equilibrium classical monatomic ideal gas, the average translational kinetic energy per molecule is \(\langle K_{\rm trans}\rangle=\tfrac32 k_B T\). This is an ensemble average under specified idealizations, not an identity for every particle.

**Scale guide.** Typical atomic extent \(\sim10^{-10}\,\mathrm m\); many nuclear radii \(\sim10^{-15}\) to \(10^{-14}\,\mathrm m\). These are rough orders of magnitude, not hard-edged universal boundaries.

### Exercises with answers

1. Under the simplified photoelectric model with \(\phi=4\,\mathrm{eV}\), will \(500\,\mathrm{nm}\) photons eject electrons via the ordinary one-photon process? **No:** each carries about 2.48 eV.
2. What is the approximate maximum photoelectron kinetic energy for \(250\,\mathrm{nm}\) photons and the same work function? **About 0.96 eV.**
3. Does doubling a light beam's photon count automatically double the energy of each photon? **No:** energy per photon depends on frequency; count affects total beam energy.
4. Why is temperature an incomplete description of a single isolated molecule? **Because** standard thermodynamic temperature describes a statistical equilibrium state, not an individual instantaneous kinetic energy.
