# Product Measures, Fubini, and Conditional Distributions

Suppose you test two sensors. One records temperature and the other records pressure. You want the expected cost of running a system that depends on both readings. A natural first instinct is to compute the expected cost by averaging over pressure for each temperature, and then averaging those results over temperature. Or perhaps to integrate in the opposite order. Why should those two procedures agree? What does it mean for the two readings to be independent, and what changes when they are not?

Those questions lead to product measures and the theorems of Tonelli and Fubini. They are easy to take for granted because ordinary finite sums can be rearranged without trouble. But once there are infinitely many possible outcomes, a double expectation is not automatically interchangeable. We need a construction that says what a joint experiment is, and a theorem that says when successive integration faithfully represents it.

Start with two finite experiments. The first sensor reports cold or warm; the second reports low or high pressure. If the temperature and pressure readings are independent, the chance of cold and high pressure together is the chance of cold multiplied by the chance of high pressure. That multiplication rule determines the probabilities of the four possible pairs. We may then calculate the expected system cost either by averaging over the four pairs at once, or by averaging over one reading and then the other.

It is tempting to conclude that products and iterated averages are merely notation for ordinary arithmetic. That is true in a finite table. The subtlety arises when we pass from tables to measurable spaces, and when we try to combine conditional distributions that do not arise from independence.

## The joint space is not merely a list of pairs

An experiment has a space of outcomes and a collection of events on which probabilities are defined. If one outcome belongs to a space A and another to a space B, the combined outcome is a pair. The combined set of outcomes is the Cartesian product of A and B. But simply drawing the Cartesian product does not yet tell us which subsets are measurable, or what probabilities they receive.

Begin with measurable rectangles: events of the form “the first outcome lies in this measurable subset of A, and the second lies in that measurable subset of B.” These are the directly interpretable conjunctions of events from the two experiments. The product sigma algebra is generated by all such rectangles. It contains events formed from them by countable unions, complements, and related operations.

For independent experiments, the desired probability of a rectangle is the product of the two marginal probabilities. Product measure is the mathematical construction extending that rule from rectangles to all events in the product sigma algebra. In probability spaces such a product measure exists and is unique. More general measure spaces require standard qualifications, such as sigma finiteness, for the familiar uniqueness statements.

Notice exactly what has been built. We have not proved that arbitrary measurements on the same physical system are independent. We have defined one particular joint law from two chosen marginal laws and an independence assumption. A correlated pair with the same marginals requires a different joint probability measure on the same product space.

For an extreme counterexample, take two fair binary sensors. They each read zero or one with equal probability. In one model, they are independent, so four pairs each have probability one quarter. In another model, the second always copies the first. The pairs zero-zero and one-one each have probability one half, and the other pairs never occur. Both models have exactly the same marginal distributions. Only the joint law distinguishes them.

This matters whenever a model computes reliability or risk. A single failure probability for each component tells us little about the probability of simultaneous failure until we say how failures depend on each other. Multiplying marginal probabilities without a dependence assumption is an additional model choice, not a fact supplied by the marginals.

## Integration in two stages

Take a system cost that depends on both readings. In a finite independent model, the expected cost is a weighted sum over pairs. For each fixed temperature we can first take the conditional average over pressure, and then average the result over temperature. Because all sums are finite, we can reverse their order. This is an everyday use of the distributive law.

For continuous outcomes we write integrals instead of sums. A product measure gives us a joint integral, and we can ask whether the joint integral equals an iterated integral. One theorem answers for nonnegative functions; another answers for signed functions whose absolute value has a finite integral. Their distinction is not a technical ornament. It protects the meaning of rearrangement.

Tonelli's theorem concerns a measurable function that is never negative. On a product of sigma-finite measure spaces, the integral over both variables equals the integral formed by integrating one variable and then the other, regardless of which order is chosen. The common value may be infinity. That possibility does not invalidate Tonelli: the theorem is about equality of extended nonnegative integrals, not about guaranteeing finite answers.

Why is nonnegativity so helpful? We can approximate a nonnegative measurable function from below by a growing sequence of simple functions. A simple function is a finite weighted collection of indicator functions, so its integral is built from measures of sets. First establish the integration rule for rectangles and indicator functions, then for simple functions, and finally take increasing limits. The monotone convergence theorem lets the limit pass through each integral, because nothing can cancel along the way.

This proof strategy is worth remembering. We do not rearrange an infinite expression because it looks like a finite sum. We first verify the rule on objects where it is evident, and then extend it using a convergence theorem whose hypotheses are satisfied. Tonelli is an elegant example of constructing a powerful general result from indicators, linearity, and monotone convergence.

## The role of absolute integrability

Now consider a function that can be positive or negative. An integral over some region may have large positive and negative contributions that cancel. If each contribution is individually infinite, their formal difference is not a legitimate real number. We cannot simply treat infinity minus infinity as an ordinary cancellation.

Fubini's theorem says that if the integral of the absolute value is finite, then both iterated integrals exist in the appropriate almost-everywhere sense, and their values equal the joint integral. Absolute integrability gives a finite budget for positive and negative contributions together. That is what prevents a change of order from changing the answer.

A common mistake is to check only that one iterated integral happens to be finite. That alone does not imply absolute integrability, and it does not establish that the reverse order gives the same result. Conditional convergence is exactly where rearrangement becomes dangerous.

To see the danger without elaborate calculus, consider an infinite table indexed by positive integers. In row one, place a positive one in column one and a negative one in column two. In every later row, place a positive one on the diagonal and a negative one in the next column. All other entries are zero. Every row sums to zero. So if we first sum across rows and then add those row sums, we get zero.

Now sum by columns instead. The first column contains a single positive one. Every later column contains a negative one from the preceding row and a positive one from the matching row, which cancel. Summing the columns therefore gives one. Both iterated sums are perfectly defined and finite, but they disagree. The total sum of absolute values is infinite. Fubini does not apply, and the contradiction disappears.

That table is not a loophole in arithmetic. It is a warning that an infinite double sum is not fully specified by the words “sum everything.” The method and the convergence conditions matter. The same issue can arise in improper integrals, Fourier series, and calculations with signed measures.

## Independence as a property of an integral

Product measures make independence feel structural rather than accidental. If two random variables are independent, their joint distribution is the product of their marginal distributions. As a result, the expectation of a product of integrable functions of the separate variables factors into a product of expectations, provided the relevant integrability conditions hold.

In the finite sensor model, this says that the mean of a temperature-derived reading multiplied by a pressure-derived reading is the product of the two separate means, assuming independence. It is not generally true for two correlated measurements. In the copying-sensors model, the product of the two readings equals the reading itself, so its expected value is one half. The separate means are each one half, whose product is one quarter. The factorization fails exactly as it should.

There are two directions to appreciate. Starting from independence, we derive factorization of certain expectations. Conversely, if sufficiently many bounded measurable test functions factor in this way, then the joint law must be a product law and the variables are independent. Testing only a single product, such as the product of the raw sensor readings, is not enough. Uncorrelated variables need not be independent.

That distinction keeps a useful numerical diagnostic from being promoted into a stronger probabilistic claim. Zero covariance tests one particular relationship between variables. Independence describes the entire joint distribution. In complicated models, the gap between those two ideas can be very large.

## What conditional distributions add

Independence is not always realistic. Temperature and pressure may be linked by weather. If we know the temperature, the distribution of pressure may change. The correct joint averaging procedure then uses a conditional distribution of pressure given temperature, rather than the unconditional pressure marginal.

In a finite example, the formula is familiar. To find the expectation of the cost, take the expected cost under the pressure distribution appropriate for each temperature and then average over temperatures. This is the law of total expectation, often called the tower property. It works with dependence because the inner distribution carries the information about the first variable.

For variables with a joint density, a typical conditional density is the joint density divided by the marginal density of the conditioning variable, wherever that marginal density is positive. We must not apply that quotient at points where the denominator vanishes. Such points cannot be rescued by pretending an undefined ratio has a unique numerical value.

This is where regular conditional distributions enter. Rather than giving one conditional number for a single event, a regular conditional distribution assigns to the observed value a whole probability law for the other variable. Under standard conditions, including random variables taking values in standard Borel spaces, such versions exist. They are generally determined only up to sets that the conditioning variable visits with probability zero.

The qualification matters. On completely arbitrary measurable spaces, a regular conditional distribution need not exist. The abstract conditional expectation of each bounded measurable function is more general than a single nice family of conditional probability laws for all events. When writing a generative or Bayesian model, we often assume exactly the measurable structure that makes these conditional kernels available.

## The generative interpretation

Imagine simulating a day in the sensor system. First draw a temperature from its marginal distribution. Then draw a pressure from a conditional distribution tailored to that temperature. The resulting pair has the intended joint distribution, assuming the conditional kernel is genuinely the one associated with that joint law. Averaging a cost over simulated pairs should agree with integrating its conditional expectation over temperatures.

This is not the product construction unless the conditional pressure distribution is the same for almost every temperature. If it is the same, the two variables are independent. If it varies with temperature, the generative model represents dependence. The order of simulation reflects a probabilistic factorization, not necessarily a causal story about temperature physically producing pressure.

A joint law can admit more than one conditional factorization. We might instead sample pressure first and then temperature conditioned on pressure. Both may describe exactly the same joint distribution. A causal interpretation requires additional assumptions about interventions and mechanisms; probability factorization by itself cannot tell which variable causes the other.

There is a practical dividend to keeping the distinctions clear. Many algorithms use nested expectations: estimate a parameter, average over latent variables, compute a predictive risk, then average over future data. Every exchange of order either uses a legitimate theorem or makes a modeling assumption. Product measures, kernels, and Fubini provide a vocabulary for stating which is happening.

## What can go wrong in computation

Even when Fubini applies, evaluating the iterated integrals numerically can still be challenging. A rare region might contribute most of the expected cost. Sampling independent pairs can underrepresent that region, while naive numerical quadrature may miss a narrow peak. A mathematically valid change of integration order does not guarantee that each numerical method has comparable error or stability.

Conversely, Monte Carlo estimates sometimes appear to agree when an interchange is mathematically unjustified. Finite truncations can conceal an infinite cancellation problem. Agreement at several truncation levels is evidence about those computations, not a proof that a limit exists independently of summation order.

And there is a modeling question before any integral is evaluated. If two components fail together because of a shared power supply, the correct joint law includes that common source. Using independent marginal failure models may deliver a beautifully converged numerical answer to the wrong probability problem.

## The conclusion the theorems actually support

Three ideas fit together. Product measure constructs a joint law for independent experiments by specifying what happens on measurable rectangles and extending that specification. Tonelli allows an order of integration to be exchanged for nonnegative measurable functions, even if the value is infinite. Fubini allows the corresponding exchange for absolutely integrable signed functions, guaranteeing a finite common value.

With dependence, an appropriate conditional distribution replaces the second independent marginal, and the tower property recovers the total expectation. This is a statement about the joint law. It is not automatically a causal claim, and it is not a promise that a numerical algorithm can evaluate the quantities efficiently.

The governing question is simple enough to ask every time we see two integrals: what joint law are we integrating against, and what theorem justifies the order we chose? Getting those two answers right turns a familiar-looking calculation into a reliable piece of probability theory.


## A calculation where dependence changes the answer

Return to the two binary sensors, but now interpret zero as no alarm and one as an alarm. Each sensor alarms on half the observations. Suppose an expensive shutdown occurs only when both alarm. If the two sensors are independent, the chance of a shutdown is one quarter. If they copy the same hidden signal, the chance is one half. If exactly one of them alarms each time, the chance is zero. All three systems have exactly the same marginal alarm rates.

A planner who knows only the two marginal probabilities can calculate each individual sensor's expected alarms, but not the shutdown probability. To compute that risk, the planner needs a joint law, or at least enough dependence information to bound the desired joint event. This is not a corner case. In reliability analysis, shared power supplies and environmental shocks create dependent failures. In financial portfolios, common shocks can produce simultaneous losses. In machine learning, samples may share a source or a preprocessing artifact. The cost function is being integrated against a joint distribution, not against a list of marginal means.

For a second calculation, assign shutdown costs of one hundred units whenever both alarm, and zero otherwise. Under independence, expected cost is twenty-five. Under complete copying, expected cost is fifty. Under perfect opposition, expected cost is zero. A numerical integration package could compute any of those results exactly, but it cannot decide which joint law is appropriate. That is a scientific modeling question, not an integration problem.

## Infinite expectation is not an integration-order error

Now suppose a random variable N counts the number of retries until a procedure finishes. Let the probability of N equaling k decrease roughly as one divided by k squared, with a normalizing constant chosen so the probabilities sum to one. This is a valid probability distribution: the sum of one over k squared is finite. But the expected number of retries involves the sum of k times that probability, which behaves like one over k, and that sum diverges.

If we represent the expected retry count as the sum over all possible retries of the probability that the procedure survives to that retry, we have changed the order of a nonnegative double sum. Tonelli justifies the interchange even though the answer is infinity. Its conclusion is not that the expected runtime is finite; it is that two sound calculations give the same infinite result. This is useful when studying randomized algorithms with heavy tails or rare nontermination-like delays.

An infinite answer is still information. It says that a finite typical runtime and an infinite mean can coexist. In fact, there may be a high probability of quick completion while rare extreme runs dominate the expectation. The temptation to treat an observed sample average as a reliable estimate of a finite mean would be especially dangerous in this situation.

## Repeated conditioning and simulation

Many realistic systems have three or more stages. A weather state is drawn, demand is drawn conditional on weather, and equipment failure is drawn conditional on both. A joint probability distribution can be represented by those successive conditional kernels when the relevant measurable structure supports them. The expected loss can be evaluated by integrating backward through the stages, one conditional expectation at a time.

This is the mathematical pattern behind dynamic programming, hidden-state models, and probabilistic programs. The order of stages explains one useful factorization of the joint law; the tower property then explains why averaging in stages recovers the full expectation. A different valid factorization might compute the same joint quantity more efficiently. But the order in which an analyst factors a distribution is not by itself a causal chronology, and the existence of a factorization does not guarantee it is numerically easy to sample or integrate.

A final subtlety is that independence should be declared at the appropriate level. Independent weather realizations across days do not imply that failures within one storm are independent. Independent random seeds do not imply independent outputs when an algorithm shares trained parameters. Product structure belongs to specific variables or sigma algebras, not to an entire scientific narrative by default.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

For sigma-finite measure spaces \((S,\mathcal S,\mu)\) and \((T,\mathcal T,\nu)\), the product \(\sigma\)-algebra is \(\mathcal S\otimes\mathcal T=\sigma\{A\times B\}\), and the product measure satisfies
\[
(\mu\otimes\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B).
\]
For a nonnegative measurable \(f\), Tonelli asserts
\[
\int f\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)=\int_S\left(\int_T f(s,t)\,d\nu(t)\right)d\mu(s)
=\int_T\left(\int_S f(s,t)\,d\mu(s)\right)d\nu(t)\in[0,\infty].
\]
For a signed measurable \(f\) with \(\int |f|\,d(\mu\otimes\nu)<\infty\), Fubini gives equality of finite integrals, and the sections are integrable for almost all outer-variable values.

**Proof sketch of Tonelli.** Establish equality for indicators of measurable rectangles, extend to indicators of product-measurable sets by a monotone class argument, extend to nonnegative simple functions by linearity, then to arbitrary nonnegative measurable functions by increasing approximation and the monotone convergence theorem.

**Nonabsolute counterexample (counting measure).** For \(i,j\ge1\), let \(a_{ij}=\mathbf1_{j=i}-\mathbf1_{j=i+1}\). Then \(\sum_i\sum_j a_{ij}=0\) (all row sums vanish), but \(\sum_j\sum_i a_{ij}=1\) (the first column totals one; others zero). Moreover \(\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|=\infty\).

**Joint laws and kernels.** On standard Borel spaces, a joint law \(P_{XY}\) can be disintegrated as \(P_X(dx)K(x,dy)\). For integrable \(g\),
\[
E[g(X,Y)]=\int\!\int g(x,y)K(x,dy)P_X(dx).
\]
Independence corresponds to \(K(x,\cdot)=P_Y\) for \(P_X\)-almost all \(x\). Where densities exist, \(p_{Y|X}(y|x)=p_{XY}(x,y)/p_X(x)\) for \(p_X(x)>0\).

### Exercises with answers

1. **Dependent Bernoulli pair.** Two fair bits are equal almost surely. Find \(E[XY]\) and \(EX\,EY\). **Answer:** \(1/2\) and \(1/4\).
2. **Tonelli.** Can a nonnegative integral be changed in order if its value is infinite? **Answer:** yes, equality holds in \([0,\infty]\) for measurable \(f\) under the theorem's measure hypotheses.
3. **Fubini.** Is one finite iterated integral enough to justify reversing the order for a signed \(f\)? **Answer:** no; absolute integrability suffices, but finiteness in one order does not.
4. **Conditional factorization.** Which marginal is used if \(Y\) depends on \(X\)? **Answer:** an appropriate conditional kernel \(P_{Y|X=x}\), integrated against \(P_X\), rather than the unconditional \(P_Y\).
5. **Causal inference.** Does \(P_{XY}(dx,dy)=P_X(dx)K(x,dy)\) establish that X causes Y? **Answer:** no; joint laws factor in both directions when appropriate conditionals exist.
