# Sigma Algebras and Measure: How Probability Handles Infinite Events

Choose a number uniformly at random from the interval between zero and one. What is the probability that the number is exactly one half? It is zero. What is the probability that the number is rational? That is also zero, even though the rational numbers are dense: every open interval, however small, contains infinitely many of them. What is the probability that the selected number belongs to a particular interval? Its length. All three answers can coexist, but ordinary finite counting offers no explanation.

The sample space contains infinitely many possible outcomes. In fact, it contains uncountably many real numbers. If we try to assign the same positive probability to every individual number, the total becomes impossible. Instead, we give probabilities to carefully chosen sets of outcomes, called events. We require those events to be stable under the operations that naturally appear in questions about limits, unions, and complements. The resulting structure is a sigma algebra, and the rule assigning sizes to its events is a measure.

This framework is the mathematical foundation of continuous probability. It also lets us distinguish three claims that sound similar in everyday language: a statement is true at every outcome, true with probability one, or true with probability tending to one along a sequence. These claims have different logical strengths. We will develop them through the uniform interval model, prove a continuity property of probability from its axioms, and construct a sequence of random variables whose pointwise limit does not preserve expectation.

## The sample space and the events we may ask about

Take the sample space to be the closed interval from zero to one. Each outcome is a real number. A sample space alone, however, does not tell us which collections of outcomes can be assigned probabilities coherently. In a finite model it is often convenient to allow every subset as an event. With an uncountable continuous model, that convenience cannot be retained alongside all the ordinary properties we expect of length.

A sigma algebra is a collection of subsets of the sample space. It contains the whole space. Whenever it contains an event, it contains the complementary event. And whenever it contains countably many events, it contains their union. From those rules we can derive that it contains the empty set, finite unions, and countable intersections. A countable intersection is the complement of the union of the complements.

Why insist on countable rather than merely finite operations? Because a limiting event often has the form 'the outcome belongs to at least one of these infinitely many sets' or 'the outcome belongs to every set in this sequence.' Probability theory needs to speak about events built by infinitely many successive approximations. The sigma-algebra conditions ensure that those resulting sets are still legitimate events.

Countable does not mean arbitrary. The requirement covers finite or countably infinite sequences of events, not the union of a completely arbitrary uncountable family. That difference is significant. Every singleton in our interval model is measurable. But taking an arbitrary union of singletons could produce an arbitrary subset of the interval, and not every such subset is Lebesgue measurable.

There are at least two natural sigma algebras on the real line worth distinguishing. The Borel sigma algebra is generated by open intervals: it contains the smallest collection of sets closed under the sigma-algebra operations and containing every open interval. The Lebesgue sigma algebra extends the Borel one by including all subsets of Lebesgue-null sets. The distinction is invisible in many elementary calculations, but it matters when we discuss completeness and the precise class of allowed events.

## Measuring events, not counting outcomes

A probability measure assigns a number between zero and one to each measurable event. The whole sample space has probability one. For any countable collection of pairwise disjoint measurable events, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities. This last condition is countable additivity.

Countable additivity includes ordinary finite addition as a special case. Two disjoint events can be added, as can three, or any finite number. Its strength emerges when an event is assembled from infinitely many disjoint pieces. Those pieces may become smaller and smaller, yet their total measure remains the sum of the entire sequence.

For the uniform model on our interval, the probability of a subinterval is its length. The probability of the interval from one quarter to three quarters is one half. Whether we include or exclude either endpoint makes no difference, because each individual endpoint has probability zero. This fact will soon follow from our broader treatment of singleton events.

We cannot obtain this continuous model by dividing one by the number of possible outcomes. There is no finite number to divide by, and the number of real points in an interval is not a useful normalization constant for this purpose. The measure is defined on events using length, and extended consistently to a rich family of measurable sets.

The measure axioms also explain why probabilities are monotone. If event A is contained in event B, then B can be decomposed into the disjoint union of A and the portion of B outside A. Both portions have nonnegative measure. Therefore B has probability at least as large as A. Subtraction gives the probability of the difference as the difference of probabilities for nested events.

These facts may seem modest, but they let us derive limit laws without guessing how probability should behave when events accumulate.

## A theorem about increasing events

Suppose we have a growing sequence of events. Every event is contained in the following one. For example, the first event might say a number lies below one half, the second below two thirds, the third below three quarters, and so on. Their union contains every outcome that belongs to at least one stage. We want to know the probability of that union.

The theorem says its probability is the limit of the probabilities of the individual events. We can prove this directly from countable additivity. The proof is a useful exercise in converting overlapping events into disjoint pieces.

Take the first event as the first piece. Form a second piece consisting of what the second event adds beyond the first. Form a third piece consisting of what the third event adds beyond the second, and continue. These new pieces are pairwise disjoint. Up to any finite stage, their union is exactly the original event at that stage. Across all stages, their union is exactly the union of the original growing events.

By finite additivity, the probability at stage n is the sum of the first n piece probabilities. By countable additivity, the probability of the full union is the sum of all the piece probabilities. But an infinite sum of nonnegative terms is defined as the limit of its partial sums. Therefore the probability of the union is the limit of the increasing sequence of probabilities. The theorem follows.

There is a matching statement for decreasing events in a probability space. If events keep shrinking, the probability of their intersection is the limit of their probabilities. To derive it, take complements, which form an increasing sequence, apply the first theorem, and subtract from one. For general measures the decreasing statement needs the first event to have finite measure; probability measures satisfy that requirement automatically.

This proof shows where the hypotheses enter. Countable additivity does the substantive work. Monotonicity ensures the sequence of probabilities has a limit. The nested structure lets us create disjoint increments. An arbitrary sequence of events with no nesting does not obey the same limit formula.

## Why rationals have probability zero

A single point has length zero, so in our uniform model the probability of choosing exactly one particular real number is zero. The rational numbers in the interval can be listed in a sequence, even though there are infinitely many of them and they occur in every open interval. Each listed rational point has probability zero.

Now use countable additivity, or simply countable subadditivity, on the union of those singleton events. Its probability is at most the sum of countably many zeros, which is zero. Therefore the probability of selecting a rational number is zero. Selecting an irrational number has probability one, because irrational outcomes are the complement of the rational outcomes.

This is a vivid example of the difference between geometry and probability. Rationals are dense: no matter how finely we zoom in, there are rational points nearby. Density tells us about where points occur. Measure tells us how much of the interval a set occupies in the relevant quantitative sense. One can be dense while having zero measure.

We must also avoid a familiar mistake. Probability zero does not mean impossible. Every individual outcome in our continuous model has probability zero, yet some outcome necessarily occurs when we sample. Probability one does not mean that the complementary event contains no elements. It means that the exceptional outcomes form an event of measure zero.

For example, define a function that equals zero at rational points and one at irrational points. This function is one at almost every outcome under our uniform measure. Nevertheless, it is zero at a dense subset of the interval. It is discontinuous at every point because both rational and irrational numbers occur arbitrarily nearby. A statement about almost every value is therefore distinct from a statement about continuity or pointwise behavior.

## Random variables require measurability

A random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers, but a probability space supplies a further requirement. For every suitable measurable set of output values, the outcomes that map into that set must form a measurable event. That is the meaning of measurability for a real-valued random variable.

Why do we need it? Suppose we ask for the probability that our function is greater than a threshold. The question describes the preimage of an interval in the output line. If that preimage were not measurable in the original sample space, our probability measure would have no assigned value for it. Measurability ensures that such ordinary probability questions are meaningful.

Many familiar functions are measurable automatically. Continuous real functions are Borel measurable. Indicators of measurable events are measurable. Limits of measurable real-valued functions are measurable wherever the relevant pointwise limit is defined, with the usual extended-real conventions when necessary. These closure properties make it possible to build sophisticated random variables from simple ones.

The rational-versus-irrational indicator is measurable, because the rational set is countable and hence Borel measurable. Its graph may be visually chaotic, but no difficulty arises in assigning a distribution. In the uniform model, the variable equals one with probability one and zero with probability zero.

Measurability is thus a statement about which sets the function pulls back, not a promise that the function is smooth, easily computed, or continuous. It is the bridge that lets a function carry the original measure forward to its distribution.

## From finite weighted sums to an integral

For a discrete random variable, expectation is a weighted sum of values. Each possible value contributes itself multiplied by its probability. For a variable taking infinitely many values in a continuum, a sum over individual points will not work. Most individual point probabilities are zero, even when the overall expectation is not.

We begin with a simpler class of functions. An indicator function takes value one on an event and zero outside it. Its integral is the event's measure. More generally, a nonnegative simple function takes only finitely many values. We can divide its domain into measurable pieces on which it is constant, multiply each value by the measure of its piece, and add the results. This is the finite weighted-average idea in a form that survives the move to a continuum.

For a nonnegative measurable function, we approximate it from below by nonnegative simple functions and take the supremum of their integrals. That defines the Lebesgue integral. The approximations can be arranged to increase toward the target function. The integral may be infinite; nonnegativity makes the approximation process well behaved even before we know integrability in the finite sense.

For a signed measurable function, we separate its positive and negative parts. An ordinary finite expectation exists when the integral of the absolute value is finite. Otherwise, a formal subtraction of two infinite quantities may be undefined. Keeping that requirement visible prevents formulas for expectation from being applied where the terms are not meaningful.

Let us integrate the identity random variable on our uniform interval. Its value at an outcome x is just x. Divide the interval into equal small subintervals and approximate the function by the height of the left endpoint of each subinterval. The resulting staircase is a nonnegative simple function below the identity. Its integral is the average of those left endpoint heights, multiplied by the subinterval widths.

As the partition becomes finer, the staircase approaches the identity at every point. The staircase integrals approach one half. This is the expected value of a uniform random number between zero and one. We can calculate that value by elementary geometry as the area of a triangle, but the simple-function construction explains why such geometric calculations fit within a general theory of probability.

## Monotone convergence and why it is useful

The monotone convergence theorem states that when nonnegative measurable functions increase pointwise to a measurable function, their integrals increase to the integral of that limit, allowing the value infinity. It permits an exchange of limit and integral under a clear hypothesis: monotonicity from below together with nonnegativity.

Our staircase approximations illustrate the theorem. Each finer partition produces a function at least as high as the earlier one, using nested dyadic subdivisions, and none exceeds the identity. Pointwise they converge to the identity. The theorem identifies the limiting integral with the integral of the limit, giving the expected value one half.

This is more than a clever way of computing area. It allows us to establish expectation formulas for complicated nonnegative variables by proving them first for simple approximations, where every calculation is a finite sum. Many apparently infinite arguments in probability are developed by this strategy: prove a finite statement, choose an increasing approximation that preserves the needed structure, and pass to a limit under a theorem whose hypotheses have been checked.

The word increasing is doing mathematical work. If the approximating functions oscillate, or if large values concentrate into smaller sets, the integral of the limit need not match the limit of the integrals. We need an explicit convergence theorem suited to the sequence rather than a general intuition that close functions have close averages.

## A sequence that converges but keeps the same expectation

Here is a concrete warning. On the uniform interval, define a sequence of random variables. The nth variable equals n when the sampled number lies strictly between zero and one divided by n, and equals zero everywhere else. Its graph is a narrow rectangle near zero, with width one divided by n and height n. Its area, and therefore its expectation, is exactly one for every n.

What happens pointwise? Fix any sample outcome x greater than zero. Eventually one divided by n becomes smaller than x. From that stage onward the random variable at x is zero. At x equal to zero it is defined to be zero at every stage. So the sequence converges pointwise to zero everywhere on our sample space.

The expectation of the pointwise limit is zero, yet the sequence of expectations remains one. We have pointwise convergence, indeed almost-sure convergence, and still the expectations do not converge to the expectation of the limit. The mass of the functions is concentrated in taller and narrower spikes. The pointwise limit loses that concentration because each fixed point eventually lies outside the spike.

This same sequence converges in probability to zero. For any fixed positive threshold, once n exceeds it, the probability of seeing a value above the threshold is one divided by n, which tends to zero. But it does not converge in mean absolute error: its expected absolute distance from zero remains one.

We have encountered three notions of convergence. Pointwise almost-sure convergence says that outside a null set, the numerical sequence converges at each fixed outcome. Convergence in probability says that for every positive error threshold, the probability of exceeding that error tends to zero. Convergence in mean, for the first power, says the expected absolute error tends to zero. These are related but different requirements, and the spike example separates the third from the first two.

Dominated convergence is one important theorem that repairs this kind of problem. It allows passing limits through integrals when the functions converge almost everywhere and are bounded in absolute value by one integrable function. Our spikes have heights increasing without bound and do not admit such a common integrable dominating function. The failure of expectation convergence is therefore consistent with the theorem.

## Interpreting almost sure statements correctly

Suppose a theorem says an estimator converges to the true parameter almost surely. It identifies a set of sample outcomes with probability one on which the estimator sequence converges. There may be exceptional sample outcomes where it does not converge. The theorem does not promise a fixed finite sample size at which every outcome is close to the parameter.

A theorem about convergence in probability instead controls the probability of a large error for each sample size. For sequences on a fixed probability space, almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability. The reverse generally fails. Both are different from convergence of expected absolute error, which can fail even when pointwise convergence is everywhere, as our spikes showed.

These distinctions become crucial in statistics, random algorithms, and stochastic processes. A model that assigns probability zero to a pathological input may still encounter it as a concrete possibility. A high-probability guarantee at one time does not by itself give a simultaneous guarantee over infinitely many times. And a probability of large error that tends to zero does not automatically control the expected size of the error if the remaining rare errors are very large.

The sigma-algebra framework keeps each of these statements precise. It makes the relevant events measurable, gives countable unions and intersections a probability, and gives us integrals that reflect both the size of an error and its likelihood.

## Returning to the randomly chosen number

The probability that the selected number equals one half is zero. The probability that it is rational is also zero, because a countable union of measurable singletons of measure zero still has measure zero. The complement, the irrational outcomes, has probability one. Every interval still has probability equal to its length, and the identity random variable has expectation one half.

Each statement follows from a coherent arrangement of events and measures, rather than from counting points. Sigma algebras supply the allowed events. Countable additivity controls unions and limits of nested events. Measurable functions produce distributions, and the Lebesgue integral extends finite weighted averages to continuous and general random variables.

The spike example locates the limit of intuition based only on pointwise behavior. A sequence may converge to zero at every outcome while its expectation remains one. Exchanging limits and expectations requires a theorem with suitable hypotheses, such as monotone convergence for increasing nonnegative functions or dominated convergence under an integrable bound. These are the precise tools that let probability reason about infinitely many possibilities without confusing 'almost everywhere' with 'everywhere' or 'convergent' with 'convergent in mean.'

## Mathematical companion (display only)

A probability space is \((\Omega,\mathcal F,P)\), where \(\mathcal F\) is a sigma algebra and \(P:\mathcal F\to[0,1]\) is countably additive with \(P(\Omega)=1\):
\[
 A_n\in\mathcal F,\quad A_n\cap A_m=\varnothing\ (n\ne m)
 \Longrightarrow P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty P(A_n).
\]

**Continuity from below.** If \(A_n\uparrow A=\bigcup_n A_n\), define \(D_1=A_1\), \(D_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}\) for \(n\ge2\). These sets are disjoint, so
\[
 P(A)=\sum_{n\ge1}P(D_n)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N P(D_n)=\lim_NP(A_N).
\]
Continuity from above follows by complements for a finite measure such as probability.

For Lebesgue probability on \([0,1]\), \(P(\{x\})=0\) and the rationals are countable. Therefore
\[
 P([0,1]\cap\mathbb Q)\le\sum_{q\in[0,1]\cap\mathbb Q}P(\{q\})=0.
\]
The Borel sigma algebra is generated by open sets. Its Lebesgue completion includes every subset of every null set.

**Integral construction.** For a nonnegative simple function \(s=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\mathbf1_{A_j}\) with disjoint measurable pieces,
\[
 \int s\,dP=\sum_j a_jP(A_j),\qquad
 \int f\,dP=\sup\{\int s\,dP:0\le s\le f,\ s\text{ simple}\}.
\]
Dyadic staircases \(s_n(x)=2^{-n}\lfloor2^n x\rfloor\) on \([0,1)\), with \(s_n(1)=1\), satisfy \(s_n\uparrow x\), and
\[
 \int_0^1s_n(x)\,dx=\frac{2^n-1}{2^{n+1}}\longrightarrow\frac12.
\]

**Convergence counterexample.** Define \(X_n(x)=n\mathbf1_{(0,1/n)}(x)\) on \([0,1]\). Then \(X_n(x)\to0\) for every \(x\), and \(P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)=1/n\) for \(n>\varepsilon\), but \(E|X_n|=1\) for every \(n\).

### Exercises with answers

1. **Closure.** Why is a countable intersection of events measurable? **Answer:** it is the complement of the countable union of their complements.
2. **Null sets.** Why does a countable set have uniform probability zero? **Answer:** each singleton has measure zero, and countable subadditivity bounds their union by zero.
3. **Limit events.** When \(A_n\downarrow A\) in a probability space, what happens to \(P(A_n)\)? **Answer:** it decreases to \(P(A)\), by complements and continuity from below.
4. **Spike integral.** What is \(E[X_n]\) for \(X_n=n\mathbf1_{(0,1/n)}\)? **Answer:** \(n\cdot(1/n)=1\).
5. **Convergence.** Does almost-sure convergence alone imply convergence in expected absolute error? **Answer:** no; the spikes converge everywhere to zero but retain unit expected absolute error.
