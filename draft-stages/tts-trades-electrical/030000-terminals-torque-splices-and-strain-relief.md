# Connections Under Pressure: Terminals, Torque, Splices, and Strain Relief

A copper conductor can carry current for many meters with very little trouble, yet the last few millimeters, where it enters a terminal, can become the hottest part of the circuit. There may be more metal in that terminal than in the wire. Why would the larger piece be the problem?

Because a connection does not behave like a continuous piece of metal. Two nominally smooth metal surfaces touch only at microscopic high points. Current must squeeze through those points, cross an interface, and spread out again. The electrical resistance of this interface depends on its material, cleanliness, geometry, and especially the mechanical condition that keeps the surfaces together. A reliable electrical joint is simultaneously an electrical component and a mechanical assembly.

The useful way to understand a termination is to ask three questions. What establishes the electrical contact? What maintains that contact through temperature changes and movement? And where do forces on the cable go, other than through the conducting joint?

## Contact area is not the area you can see

Imagine pressing two flat metal plates together. Through a magnifying glass, their surfaces look polished. At a much smaller scale they are hills and valleys. Most of the apparent area is separated by tiny gaps, films, or oxides. Electrical current concentrates at the spots where conducting material actually meets. Engineers describe the resulting restriction as constriction resistance, often considered together with film and interface resistance as contact resistance.

Clamping force can enlarge the true contact regions and help break or control some surface films. But force is not the only design variable. The conductor may be round or stranded; a terminal may be a screw clamp, pressure plate, spring cage, crimp barrel, compression lug, or other engineered geometry. Its surface finishes and permissible conductor constructions matter. Two connections with the same conductor and nominal force can have different electrical and mechanical behavior.

Contact resistance also creates a feedback loop. A simple circuit has the familiar heating term: current squared times resistance. If a connection's effective resistance increases, more energy is dissipated at the joint. Heating changes dimensions and material properties. Repeated expansion and contraction can alter contact pressure. Under some conditions, damage and resistance can reinforce one another.

A numerical example makes the location of the problem clear. At twenty amperes, an idealized joint with two milliohms of resistance dissipates zero point eight watts. If degradation raises that resistance to twenty milliohms, the same current produces eight watts. Those figures are deliberately hypothetical, not a rating or an acceptable resistance specification. They show why a small resistance in a confined connection can matter thermally.

The ordinary branch-circuit breaker observes total circuit current. A resistive joint in series with a load can run hot while drawing less than the breaker's overcurrent threshold. That is one reason a healthy protective-device rating does not prove the quality of every splice.

## Why specify torque?

A screw-type terminal uses a rotating fastener to create pressure on a conductor or pressure plate. Torque is the moment applied to that fastener. The temptation is to think that a particular torque uniquely produces a particular contact force. It does not. Part of the applied moment overcomes thread friction and friction under the fastener head or pressure-bearing surface. Geometry, lubrication, finish, thread condition, and tool behavior affect the result.

Torque nevertheless gives manufacturers a practical, repeatable installation variable for a joint that has been designed and evaluated with particular conductors. The point is not that more torque is always better. Too little tightening may leave inadequate pressure or a joint susceptible to movement. Excessive tightening may damage strands, deform a pressure plate, strip threads, or exceed the mechanical limits of the connector. Both directions can harm the connection.

A published torque value therefore belongs to a specified terminal and its instructions. It is not a property of copper wire in the abstract. The manufacturer's marking may also prescribe conductor material, size range, number of conductors, solid or stranded construction, preparation, and particular fitting types. A terminal approved for one copper conductor is not automatically suitable for two, or for aluminum, or for very fine flexible strands.

The American workplace rule in OSHA's electrical standard is useful here because it states the general engineering obligations distinctly. Electrical connections must suit the conductor material; a terminal for multiple conductors must be identified for that use; and listed or labeled equipment is to be installed according to instructions included in its listing or labeling. These are requirements in the covered workplace setting, not a claim that OSHA itself governs every private domestic installation. Product evaluation standards such as UL 486A-486B concern connector designs and tests; their existence is not a substitute for the particular connector's instructions.

It is tempting to recheck an old screw by turning it and reading a torque wrench. That reading is not a trustworthy reconstruction of the torque originally applied. Static friction, settling, oxidation, and movement change the relationship between a present wrench reading and the connection's history. Maintenance decisions need an equipment-specific method rather than the assumption that a passing retorque reading proves the contact was sound.

## The connector is a system

Consider several connection families. In a screw or pressure-plate terminal, tightening creates the contact force. In a spring-clamp connector, a purpose-built spring maintains force over an allowed range of conductors and temperatures. In a crimp or compression lug, a specified connector, tool, die, and deformation create the joint. Some connectors join multiple wires; some accept exactly one conductor. Each family has a different installation and inspection logic.

The visible appearance of two connectors is not a reliable way to infer interchangeability. A generic-looking lug may have a specific conductor-class limitation. An unapproved ferrule may alter the geometry that a terminal was tested with. A compression die chosen by eye may produce a joint that looks crimped but fails to develop the intended mechanical and electrical contact. Likewise, the presence of a spring does not make a connection universally maintenance-free; its suitability is established for a stated product range and use.

Dissimilar metals introduce another layer. Copper and aluminum have different mechanical, thermal, and surface-chemistry behavior. Connecting them reliably requires an interface identified for the materials and conditions. An antioxidant compound is not a magic substitute for a suitable connector, and adding a compound contrary to product instructions can alter the tested joint. This is a product-compatibility problem, not merely a choice of which metal has lower resistivity.

The historical attention to aluminum branch-circuit connections in parts of North American housing is an example of this system perspective. Problems in particular conductor-and-device combinations prompted investigations and repair guidance. The accurate lesson is not that every aluminum conductor is defective. It is that material, connector construction, thermal cycling, and approval conditions cannot be separated when evaluating a joint.

## Where does the cable's weight go?

A termination can be electrically correct and still fail because it is carrying a mechanical load it was never designed to support. Think of a suspended cable entering an enclosure. Its weight may pull continuously on the terminal. A vibrating machine adds cyclical movement. A sharp cable entry can concentrate stress in the insulation. Pulling a cord can transmit force directly to its conductors if there is no appropriate strain relief.

A strain-relief fitting, cable clamp, or approved entry device is meant to transfer the relevant forces to the cable's sheath and the enclosure or structure, not to the electrical contact itself. The design must be compatible with the cable diameter, construction, environmental rating, and expected movement. A gland may also help preserve an enclosure's environmental classification; a random hole with a tight-looking cable does not necessarily do that.

Mechanical support is not the same as squeezing a cable as hard as possible. Excessive compression can damage insulation or change the geometry of conductors within the sheath. Supports must resist movement while respecting the cable construction, bend radius, and manufacturer's installation limits.

The result is a sequence of distinct functions. The electrical terminal conducts current. The strain-relief fitting manages pulling and bending forces. The enclosure contains and protects the joint. The surrounding wiring method supports the route. Good design avoids forcing one component to perform the job of another.

## Reading a termination specification like an engineer

Suppose a drawing shows a feeder entering a distribution enclosure. The feeder has a conductor material and size, insulation rating, strand class, and expected operating temperature. The enclosure has terminals with markings and a manufacturer document. An engineer must reconcile the two sets of requirements, not merely verify that the wire physically fits under the clamp.

The review questions are concrete. Is the conductor material accepted? Is its size within the terminal's identified range? Is the number of conductors per opening permitted? Are the strand class and connector type compatible? Are termination temperature limits consistent with the cable and current-rating calculation? Is an appropriate mechanical entry provided? Are the tools and tightening or compression method specified? What evidence of the completed work is meaningful for this equipment?

Notice that neither a torque value nor a continuity beep answers all these questions. A low-current continuity test can confirm a path, yet say almost nothing about how a joint behaves under load and thermal cycling. A thermal image can locate a temperature difference under particular loading and environmental conditions, but it cannot alone identify the cause or prove that a cooler connection is correctly constructed. Better observations are useful; they are not substitutes for the mechanical and product requirements.

The final lesson of a termination is that the smallest part of an electrical system can have the largest local consequence. Conductivity supplies the path, contact engineering preserves it, torque or another specified joining method establishes the initial condition, and strain relief protects that condition. Treating those as separate but coordinated functions explains both what makes a reliable joint and why superficially similar joints can age so differently.

## Non-spoken technical companion

### Contact-resistance thought experiment

At a fixed current, a connector's local resistance `R_c` dissipates `P_c = I²R_c`. For `I = 20 A`:

| Hypothetical contact resistance | Heat at contact |
|---|---:|
| `2 mΩ = 0.002 Ω` | `0.8 W` |
| `20 mΩ = 0.020 Ω` | `8 W` |
| `100 mΩ = 0.100 Ω` | `40 W` |

These are **constructed arithmetic cases**, not acceptable field readings or real connector performance data. Current, resistance and temperature may change together; actual temperature depends on thermal pathways and duty cycle. A series high-resistance fault need not draw enough total current to trip an ordinary overcurrent device.

### Why torque is an imperfect proxy for clamp force

A generic fastener approximation is `T ≈ K F d`, where `T` is applied torque, `F` an idealized bolt preload, `d` a characteristic fastener diameter, and `K` bundles friction and geometry. This relation is **not** an installation formula for electrical terminals. Actual terminal designs can use pressure plates, multiple contact points and non-bolt geometries. The manufacturer's product-specific method governs.

### Compare connector categories on paper

| Connection type | Contact-force mechanism | Specification to retrieve |
|---|---|---|
| Screw/clamp terminal | Fastener, clamp, pressure plate | Conductor range and construction, terminal torque, conductor count |
| Spring-clamp connector | Elastic spring geometry | Allowed conductor type/range and insertion or release method |
| Crimp/compression lug | Defined plastic deformation | Connector, conductor, tool, die, crimp pattern; listing compatibility |
| Multi-conductor splice | Connector-specific mechanism | Number and combination of conductors; material and size |

A product-class comparison does not make the products mutually interchangeable.

### Exercise: identify missing specifications (desk)

A drawing specifies a hypothetical `20 A` feeder using aluminum conductors into a control enclosure. The terminal product page gives a copper-only conductor range; its face has two screws, but no statement about multiple conductors per opening. Write a materials-query memo rather than choosing a torque. The key missing pieces are an identified aluminum-compatible connection, acceptable strand class and count, suitable mechanical cable entry, insulation temperature and sizing compatibility, and instructions for the actual listed parts.

### Limits and counterexamples

- High conductor conductivity alone does not establish a low-contact-resistance joint.
- A correctly recorded torque is evidence of using the specified method; it is not a direct measurement of contact pressure or absence of future degradation.
- More force is not always better; excessive force can damage the assembly.
- Thermal scanning and continuity measurements require interpretation and may miss failure modes.
- An installation code's language, a connector test standard, and a particular manufacturer's instructions answer different questions; do not merge their scopes.
