# 042000 — Rules That Help and Rules That Trap: Constraints, Chance, and Musical Judgment

**Track:** Music Theory and Composition. **Audience:** listeners, composers, arrangers, and builders of music-generating systems. **Prerequisites:** none beyond the idea of a beat and a short melody. **Core question:** how do we use rules to make an original piece possible without confusing obedience to a rule with musical success? **Musical material:** eight complete original sixteen-bar versions arranged in four pairs, rendered from plain-text source events.

# Rules That Help and Rules That Trap

Imagine sitting in front of an empty score. Every pitch is available. Every rhythm is available. You could write one voice or twenty, begin with a melody or a percussion pattern, modulate, repeat, interrupt, or never repeat at all. This sounds like freedom. But the immediate practical problem is that almost nothing tells you what to do next.

A rule can change that. Write using only five pitch classes. Keep a pattern on the beat for eight measures. Make one voice repeat another exactly one measure later. Build each new measure by choosing from a small bank of gestures. These are not the same kind of restriction, and they do not all produce the same kind of music. But each one narrows the decisions we face.

Now imagine that the piece has reached a place where the rule works against what you want to hear. A melodic line needs a note outside the pitch set. A regular rhythm wants to lean between beats. An imitating voice needs a moment of independence. A mechanically chosen ending does not suit the destination you have in mind. Do you obey the rule? Revise it? Keep it until the end because its stubbornness is the point?

**A rule can be a source of musical identity without becoming a universal command.** That is the subject of this lecture. We will construct four paired experiments, all using original notes. Each pair changes one defined aspect of a score. By the end, we will be able to distinguish a rule that generates a piece, a rule that listeners can recognize, a rule used for technical validation, and a judgment made by an actual listener. Those four things are related. They are not interchangeable.

## What kind of constraint is it?

There is a useful distinction between *what you have to work with* and *what you are allowed to do with it*. A palette of five notes restricts the material. A requirement to return to the opening section after a contrasting passage restricts the form. A convention associated with a style or genre creates a different sort of expectation. A procedure that moves every note upward or delays one voice by a measure restricts the process of composition. These are four different dimensions of musical constraint.

The music theorist Robert Hasegawa makes a related four-part distinction between material constraints, formal constraints, style or genre constraints, and process constraints in his chapter *Creating with Constraints*. His examples span composition, improvisation, performance, and listening. I want to credit that classification rather than present it as a new taxonomy invented for this course. His larger argument is also useful: constraints can organize creative work, but what they do depends on which decisions they restrict and which decisions remain available.

Consider the contrast between two instructions. The first says: write only with C, D, E, G, and A. The second says: the final phrase must recall the beginning. One limits the available pitches; the other creates a relationship between two times. A piece can satisfy both, one, or neither. Neither rule alone says how long the notes should be, how many voices should sound, or whether the result will hold a listener's attention.

There is also a distinction between a hard constraint and a preference. A hard constraint has a checkable condition: no melodic F or B appears, or every answer begins exactly four beats after its model. A preference is a goal whose fulfillment needs interpretation: the ending should feel prepared, the center should build tension, or the accompaniment should not conceal the melody. A computer can check the first kind from symbolic data. It cannot convert the second kind into a settled musical fact by asserting that a score passed a test.

We should add a third category: a diagnostic. Suppose a system tells you that the same phrase occurs twelve times, or that the melody has not left one register, or that the percussion never rests. Those are observations. The observations become musical criticism only after we ask what the piece is trying to do. Twelve identical returns might be a compositional failure in one work and the exact point of another.

## The common musical ground

Our four comparisons use eight versions of a sixteen-bar study. Every version is in four-quarter meter, at one hundred quarter-note beats per minute. Every version begins with the same four quarter notes, E, G, A, G. There is a fixed bass part and a quiet inner voice, and those accompaniment note events are identical across all eight versions. The same simple oscillators, source gains, and stereo settings are assigned to corresponding voices.

Here is the accompaniment pattern. In the first bar, the bass plays C followed by G. In the second, A followed by E. In the third, F followed by C. In the fourth, G followed by D. That four-bar pattern is repeated four times. One held inner note per bar supplies an additional point of reference. This is a deliberately compact harmonic environment, not an imitation of an entire musical culture or a prescription for what good bass writing must be.

I chose such a small environment so that a listener can compare changed and unchanged details. But this is not a controlled psychological experiment. A change in notes may also change intervals, voice separation, beating, or perceived loudness. The difference between two audio files might be audible to one listener and imperceptible to another. We know which events changed because the scores specify them. We do not know how all listeners will experience those changes unless we ask and measure.

We will also avoid calling an exercise successful simply because it is technically valid. A rule checker can tell us that a melody uses five pitch classes. It cannot tell us whether the five-note restriction was an interesting choice. That distinction will accompany every example.

## First pair: a pitch palette and the decision to open it

In the first version, the lead is restricted to the five pitch classes C, D, E, G, and A. Octaves are allowed. Notice that F and B are missing. The melody still moves upward, falls, pauses at points of arrival, and repeats small gestures. There is enough material to create direction, but some familiar stepwise approaches are unavailable. From E, for example, moving upward to F is forbidden; from A, approaching C through B is unavailable. The restriction has not removed music. It has changed the local routes that a composer can choose.

The second version begins with exactly the same restricted material. For the first eight bars, nothing changes. Later, in bars ten, eleven, fourteen, and fifteen, the lead gains one previously excluded pitch at each location. There are exactly four changed note events in the whole sixteen-bar melody: a B replaces a nearby C at bars ten and fourteen, and an F replaces an E at bars eleven and fifteen. Each substitution is one semitone. Their onset times and durations remain fixed. The bass and inner voice do not change. This means the comparison focuses on a small number of melodic substitutions rather than a wholesale rewriting of the composition.

We can call this a selective release of the palette. Listen to the last eight bars of both versions, and try to hear the moment where one of the allowed pathways opens into F or B. The extra notes do not necessarily sound startling. Our ears encounter a large amount of pitch information from the accompaniment as well. What matters is the relationship between the self-imposed melodic restriction and the line that emerges when it is relaxed.

Here are two competing intentions a composer might have. If the aim is to maintain the quality of a narrow melodic vocabulary, the released pitches might weaken the identity the rule was creating. If the aim is to let the second half depart from the first, those four substitutions might make the expansion meaningful. Both interpretations are possible. The score alone does not determine which intention is right, and no general principle says that more available notes must be more expressive.

The same reasoning applies when a composer chooses a restricted instrument, a particular percussion set, or a narrow register. The limit makes some gestures hard or impossible and emphasizes other relationships. It becomes useful when that asymmetry serves the piece. **Do not confuse having fewer choices with having less musical potential.** Also do not confuse a small palette with a guarantee of coherence. Coherence still depends on what happens over time.

## Second pair: the beat as a rule, and syncopation as a choice

The third version keeps every lead attack on a numbered beat. In each bar, the four melody notes begin on beats one, two, three, and four. They each last one beat. The bass continues its two-note pattern below. This is a clear and stable rhythmic arrangement: melody and meter are easy to align because each melodic attack coincides with a beat.

The fourth version preserves the same pitches in the same order. The first eight bars are identical to the regular version. After that midpoint, two attacks in each bar move away from their original beat positions. The second note begins on the offbeat after beat two, and the fourth begins on the offbeat after beat four. Those two notes become shorter, and the pauses preceding them become part of the phrase. The bass and inner voice remain exactly as they were.

The score checker can verify all of that. It can compare note names and confirm that the ordered melody is unchanged. It can verify that the altered onsets occur halfway between beats in bars nine through sixteen. It can confirm that the earlier eight bars remain untouched. What it cannot verify is whether the revised half will make a listener want to move, or whether the syncopation disrupts a long phrase too abruptly.

Listen to bars nine through twelve. Ask whether the bass feels more exposed when the melody no longer attacks on every beat. Ask whether the shifted notes create an anticipation of the next bar or an interruption of the current one. Then listen back to the opening eight bars: they may now seem more regular in retrospect, although their events have not changed. The second version has altered the larger experience of the first by providing a contrast.

A rhythmic rule can therefore be the musical subject rather than a background bookkeeping requirement. A piece built around regular pulses may derive its character from that regularity. A piece that begins regularly and changes at a recognizable moment may use the breach itself to create a form. Neither approach needs to imitate a call-and-response exchange. The organizing relationship is between a learned timing pattern and a changed one.

## Third pair: an exact echo and a voice that takes responsibility

The fifth version adds a follower to the original melody. Its rule is exact and testable: every note in the lead is repeated one full measure later, an octave lower. The follower begins at bar two, and the final bar has no newly generated continuation because our sixteen-bar study ends there. This is a mechanical delayed imitation, related to a canon in construction. It is not a certification that every simultaneous interval satisfies an eighteenth-century counterpoint exercise.

At the moment an echo enters, the texture changes. A note that sounded by itself may now sound over another note from the earlier melody. Some overlaps are consonant, others create friction, and still others may lose their identity in the synthetic playback. The rule supplies consistency; it does not guarantee attractive vertical combinations. It also constrains what the later voice can do. As long as the imitation is strict, the follower cannot adjust a local phrase ending independently of the leader.

The sixth version keeps the same lead and accompaniment. Most of the follower remains an exact delayed octave copy. But in bars eleven and twelve, where eight separate quarter-note entries would otherwise occur, the follower uses four two-beat notes instead. After those bars, it returns to the prior imitation rule. The deviation is local, not a new rule silently applied to the whole work.

This is a useful moment to ask what the constraint was for. If the piece was intended as an exercise in exact canonic imitation, then the sixth version fails that exercise. Calling it better would ignore the assignment. If the goal was to make two voices sound independently directed within a piece that began with imitation, the deviation may be worth exploring. It changes how long the follower holds notes and whether its movement coincides with every movement of the leader.

A question emerges: does the listener hear the follower as a separate musical thought, or merely notice that the echo has become less dense? That question cannot be answered by counting attacks. A composer can use listening, register changes, articulation, and reharmonization to investigate it. Our plain oscillator study isolates the notes and their durations but leaves those expressive resources mostly untouched.

The wider lesson is that a rule may be an audible contract. Breaking an established contract can create an event. But an exception that arrives without preparation may sound arbitrary, and an exception used after every eight bars may become a new formula. A rule and its exceptions have to be heard in the time of the piece, not just described in a design document.

## Fourth pair: a seeded draw and the editor who comes afterward

The final pair explores a different kind of rule: a procedure that chooses among prewritten musical fragments. We begin with a bank of six four-note melodic gestures. One is the familiar E, G, A, G opening. Others rise, descend, or close in different ways. The bass and inner voice remain the same as before. In bar one, we deliberately keep the shared motif. For each of the remaining fifteen bars, a pseudorandom generator chooses one of the six gestures.

We record its seed, forty-two thousand. This makes the result reproducible: running the specified algorithm with the same source and seed produces the same fifteen choices. A pseudorandom draw is not an unrepeatable physical event. It is a deterministic computation whose outputs are difficult to predict without following the algorithm. Its contribution here is to replace certain per-bar compositional decisions with an explicit selection procedure.

The seventh version is the raw draw, exactly as selected. It does not listen to the previous bar and decide whether a leap was graceful. It does not know that the last bar is an ending. It knows only that its choices belong to the permitted bank and that the procedure has selected them. The event file stores the chosen indices as well as the notes, so that we can audit the relationship between rule and result.

In the eighth version, the composer intervenes in exactly three measures: eight, twelve, and sixteen. At bar eight, a different bank gesture replaces the drawn one. At bar twelve, the opening figure returns in place of the selected gesture. In the last measure, the revised line uses D, E, G, C instead of the original draw's D, F, E, D. The result moves to C at the close. Every other lead measure remains as selected; all bass and inner notes remain unchanged.

Why choose those particular edits? The bar-eight substitution changes the end of the first half. Restoring the opening motif at bar twelve gives a distant reference before the end. Altering bar sixteen changes where the final melodic line comes to rest. Those are *declared compositional intentions*, not empirical findings that the edits improve the piece. A listener may prefer the raw draw, dislike the new ending, or hear both as essentially the same. The purpose is to make the difference between selection and later evaluation concrete.

There is an important historical distinction here. John Cage used chance operations in a way that could deliberately displace the composer's taste from decisions. That is not what our eighth version does. Our exercise deliberately invites editorial judgment after a pseudorandom draw, and it restricts the draw to an author-selected bank of tonal gestures. It should not be described as a realization of Cage's philosophy, even though the exercise provides a way to understand one difference between methods.

We must also distinguish a chance procedure used to *compose a fixed score* from indeterminacy left for a *performance*. In our seventh version, the notes are fixed after the seeded selection. Replaying its WAV produces the same score. In other works, performers might be authorized to choose pitches, order, durations, or entrances at performance time. Those are different allocations of decision-making. Carmen Pardo's account of Cage at IRCAM discusses the interplay of chance and indeterminacy in Cage's development. It does not suggest they are interchangeable words for every automated generator.

## How rules relate to creativity

Why do composers place themselves under constraints at all? Sometimes a restriction is part of the inherited musical practice. Sometimes it is a composer's private challenge. Sometimes the material itself suggests what might be repeated or transformed. Sometimes a constraint lets a group of collaborators agree on what must stay the same while their individual decisions differ. And sometimes a procedure produces a result the composer would not have selected by habit.

Jorge Variego's *Composing with Constraints* is expressly organized around practical exercises in composition. Its publisher's foreword contrasts a simple rule with a larger system of interacting conditions in which varied musical structures can emerge. This is a helpful reminder that constraints do not necessarily specify every note. A composition can be strongly constrained in one respect and open in many others.

For music-generating systems, the distinction becomes particularly important. Margaret Boden's work on computational creativity distinguishes combining existing ideas, exploring a structured conceptual space, and changing the space itself. She also points to a difficulty that remains pertinent: generating candidates is not the same as evaluating them. We can use her framework to ask whether an agent is merely selecting from a fixed menu, exploring variations of a set of rules, or proposing changes to the rules. But invoking her categories does not prove that any particular agent is creative or that its output is artistically valuable.

Consider an agent instructed to write every piece in four eight-bar sections, with a question in the first two bars and an answer in the next two. It can produce material that meets this plan again and again. That may be useful for learning phrase relationships, but as a universal default the plan can narrow the output more than the task requires. The system's success at fulfilling the plan can mask failure to ask whether the plan belongs in this piece at all.

A more discriminating workflow begins with a musical goal. Does the piece need a recurring reference for the listener? A growing sense of density? An austere pitch identity? An exact process that becomes audible as it unfolds? Or a line whose independent voices pull against one another? Then select constraints that make the desired relationships possible. The rule should be a means of solving the compositional problem, not a trophy to collect.

## A process that can be heard, and a process that only the composer knows

Steve Reich makes a distinction in his 1968 essay *Music as a Gradual Process*. He is not simply describing composers who use procedures while drafting. He is interested in musical processes the listener can perceive as they happen. That is a much narrower claim. An invisible algorithm choosing one of six melodic cells might be a process of composition, but unless its operation becomes perceptible, it does not meet the same aesthetic description.

This difference is useful in our own examples. The ground-bass lecture already showed a regular pattern that may become recognizable over repeated cycles. Our exact echo may also become audible as an imitative relationship. By contrast, a listener hearing only the seeded-draw result cannot infer the exact pseudorandom seed or the six-candidate bank from sound alone. We can explain the procedure and ask whether knowing it changes the listener's interpretation. We should not pretend that the procedure's existence guarantees an audible experience of process.

Reich later cautioned against treating his 1968 essay as a manifesto for all of his later work, let alone for everybody else's. This matters pedagogically. Even a compelling artistic method is not a law of composition. We can learn from an artist's chosen limits without converting those limits into a test every other piece must pass.

The distinction between acoustic, notated, and *perceived* repetition is also important. Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis has analyzed these as distinct aspects of musical repetition, along with the way recurring sounds can redirect attention. Our validator detects exactly repeated score events. It does not detect what a listener treats as a return. A transposed motif might be recognized as the same thought, while an exact sequence played under a radically altered texture might go unnoticed. That is precisely why we keep listening in the loop.

## An honest evaluation protocol

Let us describe a way to evaluate the four pairs without declaring winners in advance. First, listen to the two files in a pair without consulting the names. Use the same bar range and the same monitoring chain. If one file is louder, check whether that difference is a consequence of different note density or a playback gain mismatch. Do not independently normalize away a compositional difference and then claim you made a neutral comparison; record what was changed.

Second, answer a question tied to the compositional intent. For the palette pair: does the second half sound more open, or does it lose a useful identity? For the rhythmic pair: does the new offbeat placement create a purposeful contrast with the first half? For the imitation pair: can you still follow the follower as a line after the deviation? For the seeded pair: does the edited return at bar twelve and revised ending change the sense of arrival? Then listen a second time and ask what you missed.

Third, report the observations separately from preferences. It is a score fact that the pitch-palette pair changes four notes. It is a listener report to say those four notes make the melody more expressive. It is a methodological observation that we have not matched perceived loudness, tested a sample of listeners, or evaluated multiple instrument timbres. All three kinds of statement may be useful, but each answers a different question.

If several people listen, let them write judgments before discussing the examples. Do not ask only which version is better. Ask which rule they heard, what changed, which version better fulfilled a stated goal, and whether they want to revise either. Agreement and disagreement are data about this listening exercise, not universal musical laws. We can use Ardour to align the same passages for comparison, and we can render the note events with alternative patches in DawDreamer to ask whether the contrasts survive a change of timbre. New renderings are new conditions; their settings belong in the experiment record.

## A practical method for composing with agents

Here is a compact way to turn all of this into a useful instruction for a music-generating agent. Begin by naming one musical purpose. Perhaps the piece is to evolve from contained to expansive. Perhaps the accompaniment should stay stable while the melody becomes less predictable. Perhaps a strict process is itself the subject. Do not begin by requiring call-and-response or a fixed sectional plan unless that structure serves the purpose.

Next, choose one or two hard constraints that support it. Specify what they restrict: pitch material, time placement, interaction between voices, instrumentation, or an actual formal relationship. Make those rules mechanically checkable where possible. Do not bury a subjective preference inside a fake mathematical score. A test for a motif returning at bar twelve is meaningful. A test that pretends the return must therefore be emotionally satisfying is not.

Then generate several genuinely different candidates. In one candidate, retain the pitch restriction and change the rhythm. In another, keep the rhythm and open the pitch set. In another, build the piece around imitation. Preserve the score data and the decisions that produced each candidate. If a random seed or algorithm played a role, record it. If a composer makes a discretionary edit, record that too. You want to be able to trace why two pieces differ without suggesting that a source code log can substitute for hearing them.

Now listen, preferably without the labels, and make an editorial decision. The agent can propose what might be improved, but it should not force a rule breach because an instruction says every composition needs an exception. Sometimes strictness is the art. Sometimes an almost imperceptible departure is the point. Sometimes the most important revision is removing a layer rather than changing a note. The decision belongs to the piece, not to a universal checklist.

Finally, distinguish a process log from an artistic explanation. The log says: the melody stayed within five pitch classes; the rhythm changed after bar eight; the follower was rewritten in bars eleven and twelve; three seeded choices were replaced. The explanation asks: why did these decisions matter to a listener? That second question requires more than proof that the first statements are true.

## The rule is not the music

Return to our empty score. The full set of possible choices was never the real problem. The problem was making some of those choices form a musical relationship worth following. Constraints can do that by creating continuity, by encouraging variation, by reserving resources, by making a process understandable, or by forcing the composer to discover an approach that habit would have overlooked.

But no single rule deserves permanent priority. Not call-and-response. Not exact canonic imitation. Not uninterrupted development. Not an eight-bar return. Not a limited pitch palette. Every one of those can be the organizing idea of a good piece, and every one can become mechanical when applied without regard to the musical circumstances.

The most interesting question is therefore not whether a composition obeys its rules. It is whether the rules are the right ones for the work, and whether listening gives us reason to preserve, refine, or abandon them. A composition can have a rule; it can also have a reason to keep that rule, or a reason to let it go.

### Exercises and open questions

- Before revealing the pair labels, listen to bars 9–16. Which difference can you identify without the score? Record uncertainty rather than forcing a confident classification.
- Ask whether preserving an exact octave echo would be the right choice in a *canon study*, and whether a small departure might instead serve a piece about growing independence. The assignment changes the evaluation.
- Make a ninth version: keep the pitch palette closed for all sixteen bars but alter the last four bars' rhythm. It should be possible to explain the change without adding notes.
- Produce a tenth version from the raw seeded draw, but replace no notes. Instead, change only register or articulation; log changes and compare in Ardour.
- For an AI composition agent, write two specifications with identical hard checks but different artistic briefs. The challenge is to see whether the outputs meaningfully differ instead of converging on a generic question-and-answer template.
