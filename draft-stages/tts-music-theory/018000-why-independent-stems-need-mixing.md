# Four Separate Tracks, One Musical Space: Why Mixing Matters

Suppose a machine generates four musically correct tracks. The melody is in tune. The guitar plays the intended harmony. The bass follows the chord progression. The drums mark the pulse. You align all four files at the beginning of the song and press play. Nothing is technically missing. Yet the melody seems to disappear whenever the guitar strums, the low notes feel muddy, and the chorus is somehow smaller than the verse. Have the agents failed to compose? Maybe not. You may have reached the point at which the separate performances must become one recording.

Mixing does not mean that four good parts automatically add up to one good sound. It means deciding how the parts relate to each other through time. At every moment, the listener receives a single changing pressure wave at each ear, whether it is built from one person singing or a hundred independent channels. The listener does not receive a label saying, “Ignore the guitar's energy and attend to the vocal.” The mix establishes, or fails to establish, the relationships needed to make the intended parts intelligible.

First, though, a vocabulary correction. In everyday conversations, people call every independently generated audio file a stem. Production engineers often use “stem” more specifically for a premixed subgroup, such as all the drums rendered together, or all backing vocals rendered together. The underlying separate microphone recordings or instrument outputs are often called multitracks or individual tracks. A stem can already contain levels, processing, panning and effects that cannot later be adjusted independently. If someone hands you a stereo drum stem, you might not be able to lower only the hi-hat. When an AI service promises separate stems, ask exactly what its exports contain: isolated audio parts, grouped submixes, or estimated separations from an existing recording. Those are different kinds of control.

Consider a four-part arrangement of our own making. The lead instrument is a single human voice, or a synthetic melodic lead standing in for one. The second part is a softly picked guitar with notes that ring into one another. A bass part traces the roots. A drum part contains a kick and a higher snare-like sound. Assume that all parts are original, aligned to the same timeline, and available as separate uncompressed recordings. We have no audio example to play, so we will test the design by describing what would happen and by making explicit predictions we could later check by listening.

At the first pass, we turn every part down and bring them up one at a time. There is no universal starting fader value. What matters is leaving room for the combined signal and finding a musical hierarchy. Bring the voice or lead melody up until it is easy to follow. Add the guitar quietly enough that its harmony supports the phrase without covering the ends of words or the expressive details of the lead. Bring in the bass so that the chord motion is evident. Add the drums until the pulse is convincing. This rough balance often answers more questions than a dozen sophisticated processors.

Now the chorus arrives, and the guitar changes from picked notes to continuous strumming. The guitar occupies more moments in time and probably carries more energy in the frequency region where the lead is understandable. Even if its peak level does not increase, the denser texture can make the lead feel less present. This is a useful reminder: peak amplitude is not the same as subjective prominence. A part may conceal another by overlapping it in pitch region, by starting at the same moments, by sustaining through moments that needed space, or by attracting attention through its rhythm. Frequency masking is one part of that story, not the entire story.

The first possible repair might be compositional, not technological. Ask the guitar to play fewer notes while the vocal is busy. Move part of an accompaniment to another register. Let a sustained chord replace a repeated strum. Delay an entrance by one beat. An arrangement adjustment can solve a clash more clearly than cutting a large band of frequencies from both instruments. When the original performances are already fixed, a mix engineer can still adjust volume and tonal balance to make that same intention audible. But the most effective change may be to regenerate one track or edit its part.

A bass note and a kick drum pose a different problem. Both often contribute energy to low frequencies. If each dominates at the same instant, the combined low-frequency signal may become very large even though the two tracks sound controlled alone. There is also a possible conflict of function. Does the kick establish short transients and the bass sustain harmonic pitch? Or is the bass itself percussive, leaving room for a rounder kick? That decision shapes the mixing response. It would be careless to prescribe a fixed bass frequency to remove from every project. Some bass sounds are designed around upper harmonics that make their pitch perceptible on small speakers; some kicks have tonal tails; different genres balance these roles differently.

Level measurements help us understand one kind of risk. Imagine two sample values both equal to positive zero point seven at the same instant. If we add them at full gain, the result is one point four. That exceeds the numerical full-scale sample range of a conventional fixed-point audio export. If we simply clip the output to one point zero, we change its waveform. Repeating such clipping can produce audible distortion. A sensible first mix uses enough headroom that these combined peaks can be handled without accidental limiting. Modern floating-point DAWs can temporarily represent values above this range internally, but the export or converter still needs an appropriate final level.

There is an opposite case, just as instructive. Suppose two nearly identical tracks have been recorded with one microphone's polarity reversed. At matching instants, positive pressure in one becomes negative pressure in the other. The two can cancel. A wave that begins a little later may create frequency-dependent reinforcement and cancellation rather than silence. Engineers call the resulting pattern comb filtering. This matters when the same sound reaches two microphones by different paths, when a stereo effect duplicates and delays a signal, or when an automatic stem generator produces overlapping content in multiple tracks. It does not mean unrelated musical parts always need meticulous phase alignment. The question is whether the signals are sufficiently related for their phase relationship to cause unintended changes.

Panning offers a different form of separation. Put a guitar slightly left and another supporting sound slightly right, and the two may be easier to distinguish than if both occupy the same apparent location. But stereo positions do not eliminate masking, and there is no guarantee that a mix remains clear on every speaker system. Some listeners use a single Bluetooth speaker; some devices combine left and right into mono; some people listen with one earbud. If your arrangement relies on a trick that disappears in mono, a mono check can reveal it. A good mix does not have to be identical in stereo and mono, but it should behave deliberately in both.

Another complication is space. If the vocal was generated with a long, reverberant tail and the guitar is almost perfectly dry, their apparent acoustic environments may disagree. That might be an artistic choice, or it might be an accident. A shared reverb can give multiple sources a related sense of distance, while different amounts of reverb preserve their roles. But placing every signal through a large reverb can make transients and consonants harder to identify. Reverb is not a mandatory polish layer. Silence and dryness can be important production decisions.

Now return to our chorus, because a static mix may not be enough. During the verse, the guitar is quiet. When the chorus begins, its strumming intensifies and the vocal melody moves higher. You could apply the same fixed gain and equalization to both sections. Alternatively, you could automate a modest reduction in guitar level during dense vocal phrases, bring it back between phrases, and gradually increase a backing sound toward the final cadence. Automation is a time-dependent change to a parameter. It is not cheating; it is one of the ways a mix follows the musical form. A compressor may respond automatically to amplitude, but it does not know which word the composition considers important unless someone supplies that information, perhaps through a sidechain or hand-authored automation.

There is a subtlety when comparing mixes. Human listeners often prefer a louder presentation of the same recording, at least within limits. If one version is one or two decibels louder, you may mistake that difference for better tonal balance. A fair comparison tries to match overall loudness between candidates while preserving the dynamics being judged. It also uses a stable listening level and, when possible, more than one listening system. This does not turn aesthetic judgments into objective measurements. It makes the comparison less misleading.

Working with agents introduces its own provenance problem. An agent may deliver a WAV file named “guitar,” but that name does not tell us whether the export includes amplifier simulation, chorus, reverb, compression, or an internal normalization step. A second agent may normalize its own file independently. A third may begin at the first audible note and discard any count-in. A fourth may produce an MP3 containing encoding delay and a lossy approximation of the original signal. If we assume that the four results are clean, synchronized dry tracks, some mixing problems will be caused by invisible metadata disagreements rather than musical incompatibility.

A robust exchange therefore includes a few plain facts: the sample rate and channel layout, the absolute start time of the first sample, whether the file is dry or processed, the tempo map or a shared time reference, and whether any time-stretching or loudness normalization occurred. The name of the instrument is useful, but not sufficient. If the drums and bass have a common timing contract, the mix engineer should not have to guess which sixteenth note they intended to share. If their rhythms deliberately disagree, the contract should preserve that too.

We can make this concrete without playing a sound. Write down three versions of the same chorus. In the first, the guitar strums on every beat while the lead sings long notes. In the second, the guitar plays only on beats two and four. In the third, the guitar sustains the first beat of the bar and rests while the lead moves. All three could use the same chords and identical source timbres. Predict where the melody would be most visible. Then ask which version best fits a chorus that should feel energetic. The answer is not purely a matter of separation: a dense texture might be the desired artistic effect. Mixing chooses among trade-offs rather than maximizing clarity at every instant.

This process maps particularly well to two complementary tools. DawDreamer can print controlled variants of the same synthetic or plugin-generated material, with the render graph and gains documented in Python. Ardour can align the printed files on a common timeline, let you compare an arrangement edit with an equalizer edit, and export the result either as one mix or as separately processed stems. When using Ardour's stem export, choose deliberately whether the per-track processing is included. Otherwise another editor cannot know whether a file is supposed to be the raw instrument or the already-mixed instrument.

So when someone says the tracks are independent but cannot simply be combined, ask what they mean by independent. They may mean separately editable, which is valuable. They may mean mathematically uncorrelated, which may be false. They may mean time-aligned to a shared clock, which must be checked. And they may mean musically compatible, which is not guaranteed by file format. A functioning mixer can add numbers. A compelling mix makes a designed relationship among sounds apparent. The difference is not an unnecessary layer of mysticism. It is the distance between possessing parts and constructing a whole.

## Display-only companion: a hypothetical four-track session

All settings below are **illustrative starting hypotheses**, not recommended defaults. No actual waveform has been measured, so no numerical gain is claimed to be optimal.

| Part | Export expectation | Musical role | First diagnostic | Possible first intervention |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lead voice/synth lead | Mono, preferably dry; full-length render | Carry melody or words | Is it masked during dense accompaniment? | Adjust arrangement or automate support levels |
| Guitar | One stereo/mono file; declare whether effects are baked in | Harmony and pulse | Does strumming occupy every vocal entrance? | Rest, rhythmic simplification, context-sensitive gain/EQ |
| Bass | Mono or stereo; full-length with leading silence retained | Harmonic root and low motion | Does it overlap strongly with kick at attacks? | Shape note lengths, level and dynamic relationship |

### Two sample-alignment exercises

1. Two coherent copies of a mono sinusoid produce `0.7 + 0.7 = 1.4` at a shared peak. If the output must remain within ±1.0 with a *single* common gain, a factor of at most `1/1.4 ≈ 0.7143` prevents clipping at **that** instant. It is **not** a general peak guarantee for an unknown session.
2. Two identical copies with opposite polarity produce `0.7 + (-0.7) = 0` at that instant. If one copy is delayed, cancellation depends on frequency and delay. This exercise concerns correlated signals, not independent melodies.

### Stems and multitracks

- **Multitrack**: a component intended for independent manipulation, such as one recorded guitar microphone or one software-instrument output.
- **Stem**: in many professional workflows, a printed subgroup such as “all drums,” “all music,” or “all vocals,” possibly processed and stereo.
- **Source separation**: estimation of parts from an already mixed waveform; artifacts and residual leakage can remain even if the files have individual names.
- **MIDI/events**: time-stamped instructions, not an audio waveform; rendering requires instruments and controller interpretation.
