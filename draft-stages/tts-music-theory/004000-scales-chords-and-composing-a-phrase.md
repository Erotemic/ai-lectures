# Scales, Chords, and Harmonic Direction: Compose a Four-Bar Phrase

Imagine a short melody that ends on the note C. It has climbed a little, turned downward, and arrived there. If you hear it alone, that ending may feel settled, or it may not. Now imagine the same final C sounding over a low C played by a second instrument. The two parts may reinforce the same point of arrival. Change only the low note to G and you can hear a different relationship, even though the melody did not move. Harmony begins with that kind of interaction: one note acquiring a new role because of what sounds around it and what the music has led us to expect.

For this composition exercise, we will use a familiar tonal vocabulary built around the C major scale. That makes the arithmetic easy to check on a piano: the scale uses the white keys from C to the next C. The system is important in a great deal of Western classical and popular music, but it is not a grammar for every musical tradition. We will learn what its choices accomplish in a small invented piece, rather than treating its categories as laws of sound.

## A scale is a collection; a key is a relationship

Start with the notes C, D, E, F, G, A, and B, then return to C above the starting point. The major scale follows the interval pattern whole step, whole step, half step, whole step, whole step, whole step, half step. In twelve-tone equal temperament, a whole step is two semitones and a half step is one. You can test the pattern by moving from one piano key to the next required pitch.

These seven notes are a palette. The term 'key of C major' means more than using that palette. It also means that C functions as a tonal center, a pitch or chord around which other events are understood. A passage can use the C major collection while emphasizing A as a center, or avoid a clear center entirely. Conversely, a passage in C major can borrow notes outside the collection and still maintain C as its center. Context and behavior, not membership in a list alone, establish tonality.

If we number the scale degrees from C as one, then D is two, E is three, F is four, G is five, A is six, and B is seven. We can refer to those numbers without naming an absolute key. If we transpose the whole piece to G major, its first degree becomes G. That allows a composer to discuss a role such as 'fifth scale degree' rather than memorizing each note name separately in every key.

## A chord is a set of sounding relationships

A chord is commonly understood as a group of pitches heard as a harmonic unit, whether sounded at once or spread through time. One important family in tonal music is the triad: three pitch classes arranged as a root, a third above that root, and a fifth. From C, take C, E, and G. That is a C major triad. It contains a major third from C to E, four semitones, and a perfect fifth from C to G, seven semitones.

Now take A, C, and E. That is an A minor triad. From A to C is a minor third, three semitones, while A to E is a perfect fifth. The difference between these two kinds of triad is specific: the third above the root is lowered by a semitone in a minor triad relative to a major one with the same root. That is a structural statement; it is not proof that one chord is intrinsically cheerful or sorrowful. A minor triad can appear in a lively dance, and a major triad can sound distant or threatening when placed in another context.

In the C major scale, we can build a triad on each scale degree by selecting every other note of the scale. On C: C, E, G. On D: D, F, A. On E: E, G, B. On F: F, A, C. On G: G, B, D. On A: A, C, E. On B: B, D, F. The first, fourth, and fifth are major triads. The second, third, and sixth are minor. The seventh is diminished: B to D is a minor third, and B to F is only six semitones rather than a perfect fifth of seven.

You do not need all seven chords for your first phrase. It is more useful to hear the relationship between a few of them than to memorize a table. We will choose C major, F major, G major, and C major, one chord per measure. The four roots will be C, F, G, C. This is a short harmonic journey away from and back toward our starting point.

## Why the fifth chord often points home

In common-practice tonal harmony, the chord built on scale degree five is called the dominant. In C major, that is G major, containing G, B, and D. The chord built on the first degree is the tonic, C major. A dominant-to-tonic move is frequently heard as a strong arrival in that style. One reason involves the note B, which sits only a semitone below C. When B rises to C over a G-to-C harmonic movement, it can sound like a directed step into the tonal center.

That note B is called the leading tone in C major. It does not mechanically force every listener to hear a particular emotion, and it does not have to rise in every melody. But a composer working in this harmonic language can make the movement salient. Sing B, then C; hear the small rising distance. Now put a low G under B and change the low G to C as the melody arrives on C. You have built a simple cadence-like gesture from the meeting of a bass change and a melodic arrival.

We should distinguish a harmonic progression from a complete cadence. A cadence describes a musical ending, often involving harmony, rhythm, melodic motion, and position within a phrase. Merely writing the chord symbols G and C does not ensure a convincing ending. If both chords pass by quickly in the middle of an active melody, a listener may hear continuation. If the last C is long, accented, and placed at the end of a phrase, the same harmonic movement may sound conclusive. Form and rhythm help determine the interpretation.

What does F major contribute between C and G? In this style, F major often acts as a predominant harmony, preparing a move toward the dominant. Our progression C, F, G, C therefore gives us a familiar pattern of tonic, predominant, dominant, tonic. The terms name functions in a context, not permanent properties of isolated chords. F can take other roles in other keys and styles. Functional harmony is a useful way to describe one musical grammar, and a source of options for a composer, not a command that all pieces must move this way.

## Chord names do not say which note must be lowest

Imagine playing C, E, and G together. Put C below the other notes, and you have the chord in root position. Move C above E and G so that E becomes the bass; the chord still contains C, E, and G. This is an inversion of the same triad, called first inversion. Move G to the bottom instead, and it is second inversion. The root names the chord's identity; the bass names its lowest sounding pitch. They are not always the same.

This matters when writing a bass line. A repeated sequence of chord names can sound different depending on which note the bass plays. A bass that leaps from C to F to G to C emphasizes the roots. A bass that moves through nearer pitches can make the progression smoother and leave the sense of harmonic change to the upper voices. Neither choice is universally better. The root movement is clear and direct; the smoother bass might offer continuity or a more subdued effect.

Voice leading describes how individual parts move from one harmony to the next. Rather than placing every chord in root position and accepting whatever jumps occur, identify notes that can stay the same and notes that can move by a step. The C major and F major triads share C. G major and C major share G. A composer can keep a common pitch sounding while surrounding notes shift. That continuity makes a change of harmony easier for a listener to follow.

For example, take the notes G, B, D in a higher group of voices. Move the B up by a semitone to C, move D up by a whole step to E, and keep G unchanged. The result is G, C, E: still the three pitch classes of a C major chord, but arranged with G in the lowest position of this small group. The G-to-C change in harmonic function can therefore occur with surprisingly little motion above the bass. Add a low C below everything at the destination, and you have a clear tonic bass supporting closely connected upper voices.

## Build the four measures

Let's write the composition as a sequence we can count aloud. Choose four beats per measure. Keep a steady, moderate tempo. Give the bass one sustained chord root in each measure: C for the first four beats, F for the next four, G for the next four, and C for the final four. If you have a keyboard, play those as low notes. If you have only a voice, sing them one at a time before adding the melody. They are reference points, not a requirement that a chord be physically played on every beat.

For the melody, use quarter notes on each numbered beat in the first measure: C, D, E, G. This line climbs by two scale steps and then leaps upward from E to G. The starting C, E, and G are members of the underlying C major triad; D is a passing scale tone between C and E. That is a useful first compositional idea: a chord can establish a framework while a melody moves between its members.

Over F major in measure two, place A on beat one, G on beat two, and hold F for beats three and four. A is part of the F major chord. G is not one of its three basic chord tones, but it steps between A and F. The long F becomes the destination of that local descent. You have also changed the rhythm: two short notes followed by a longer one, which contrasts with the four equal notes in the opening measure.

Over G major in measure three, use G, B, A, G, one note on each beat. The B is the third of the G major triad and the leading tone of C major. It can create a sense of direction toward C, but the melody does not resolve it immediately. Instead, it returns through A to G. We are holding the final strong resolution for the next measure. That delay keeps the progression from becoming a simple exercise in always following the nearest rule.

On the final C major chord, place E on beat one, D on beat two, and C on beats three and four. The melody descends into the tonal center and then gives it time. The last movement D to C is a whole step, not the leading-tone movement B to C; the larger harmonic context supplies the arrival. This detail is important. A cadence does not require every voice to demonstrate the same stock gesture. It can emerge from several interacting strands: bass roots, chord succession, melodic contour, position, and duration.

Read the full melody in groups, with a short breath between measures. First: C, D, E, G. Second: A, G, F held. Third: G, B, A, G. Fourth: E, D, C held. You can sing it in any comfortable register. If this is too many note names to hold at once, practice the final measure first: E, D, C. Then add the previous measure and hear the move from G harmony into C harmony. Finally add the opening two measures so the ending has something to answer.

The piece contains sixteen quarter-note beat positions, even though the melody attacks fewer than sixteen notes because some notes are held across two positions. We have also given each chord four beats of duration, so the harmonic rhythm is one chord change per measure. Increasing the number of chord changes would alter harmonic rhythm without changing the overall tempo. Increasing the melody's number of attacks would change surface rhythm without requiring the harmony to move faster. These are independent controls.

## Test a different ending

Now change just the final chord. Keep the melody E, D, C, but place an A minor triad beneath it instead of C major. The bass plays A and the harmony contains A, C, and E. The final C is still a chord tone, but it is now the third of A minor rather than the root of C major. The bass no longer arrives at C. The phrase may feel less like a return to the original tonic and more like a turn toward a neighboring destination. In C major tonal analysis, a dominant moving to the chord on scale degree six is often described as a deceptive move, especially when the expected tonic has been prepared.

Be careful with the labels. If the listener has not established C major as home, the word 'deceptive' may overstate the experience. A, C, and E might instead be heard as the center of an A minor phrase. The same pitches become different harmonic information when their larger context changes. The useful compositional question is what expectation your passage has established, and whether a replacement chord redirects it.

You can also change only the melody. Keep the four chords C, F, G, C, but end on G instead of C. G is a member of the final C triad, so it need not sound like an error. Yet it does not place the melodic ending on the tonic. You have separated a chord's identity from the top line's final pitch. Try holding E at the end instead. Both alternatives can be made into convincing endings with suitable phrasing. This is why harmony should be listened to through real choices, not reduced to a worksheet in which only one note is allowed.

## A minor key changes the center, not merely the mood

It is helpful to recognize the related scale A natural minor, which uses the same collection of pitches as C major: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A. The notes have not changed, but A now acts as the center. The A minor tonic triad is A, C, E. In much tonal music in minor keys, the seventh scale degree is raised when a strong dominant is needed: in A minor that gives G sharp, which lies a semitone below A. The resulting E major chord, E, G sharp, B, can point toward A minor. This is one use of the harmonic minor collection, but composers choose among forms of minor and chromatic alterations according to the passage.

That distinction prevents a common confusion: it is not enough to look at a list of white keys and declare the music to be in C major. To hear a key, locate where phrases settle, which harmonies are emphasized, how the bass behaves, and what the melody treats as a goal. The ear learns a system of relationships through time. The scale supplies possible pitches; the music gives them roles.

## Composing is also choosing what to leave out

Our four-bar phrase uses one harmonic pattern, a mostly stepwise melody, and plain four-beat measures. It may sound deliberately simple when performed. That is not a reason to crowd it with seven more chords and six decorative runs. First ask what you can hear as its identity. Perhaps the opening climb C, D, E, G is memorable. Perhaps the held F in the second measure gives the phrase needed breathing room. Perhaps the last C should be lower than the first, making the ending physically descend into a different register.

Choose one of those relationships and develop it. Repeat the first two measures, but replace C, D, E, G with C, D, F, G, widening the climb. Or keep the melody unchanged but let the bass sustain C through both first and second measures, delaying the arrival of F. Or shorten the third measure's final G so that a small silence precedes the last chord. Each revision has a limited scope, which makes it possible to compare the result to the original.

A successful first harmonized phrase is not one that checks every rule in a textbook. It is one in which the composer knows the intended listening path and can make a deliberate change without losing that path. We now have named pitches, measured durations, a tonic center, three chord functions, a bass line, and a melody that arrives. The score, when you eventually write one, will record those decisions. The composing happened when we chose relationships that made four measures feel like a coherent statement.

### Exercises

1. Play or sing the four-bar melody alone, then add the bass roots. Which parts of the ending change in your perception?
2. Compose a new third measure over G major that contains B and ends on D, then return to C major in bar four.
3. Write a variant with two chords in one bar. State whether you changed the tempo, harmonic rhythm, or surface rhythm.
4. Replace the held F in measure two with two quarter notes, F and E. Does the phrase become more continuous? Remember that E is not a basic chord tone of F major.
5. Transpose the miniature to another major key. Preserve interval roles and check the key signature rather than replacing letters mechanically.
