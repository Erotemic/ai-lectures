# How Hearing Makes Music: Pitch, Loudness, Timbre, and Time

Imagine a composer printing a chart of a recording. The first page shows a waveform. The second shows a spectrum, with a peak near 220 hertz and smaller peaks above it. The third shows an amplitude envelope. The composer has three accurate descriptions of the recording, but none of them says whether the sound is a cello, a voice, a reed, or a synthesizer; whether it seems near or far; whether it feels delicate or abrasive; or whether two notes will be heard as one instrument changing pitch. Those questions involve a listener. A score is not a waveform, and a waveform is not an experience.

This distinction is essential when using a DAW. We can measure and modify stored samples exactly. We cannot assume that the perceptual result follows a simple one-to-one formula from those measurements. The auditory system selects, transforms, integrates, and groups evidence. Our central problem is to understand enough of those transformations to make better musical decisions, without claiming to compute somebody's experience from a file.

Start with the path from air pressure to neural activity. Sound reaches the outer ear, vibrates the eardrum, travels through the middle ear's ossicles, and moves fluid in the cochlea. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders describes this sequence in its guide, How Do We Hear. The cochlea contains a basilar membrane whose mechanical responses vary by position. In broad terms, higher-frequency sound excites more basal regions and lower-frequency sound more apical regions. Hair-cell transduction contributes to the neural signals carried by the auditory nerve. This is not a little piano inside the ear, with exactly one cell assigned to each musical note; responses overlap, depend on level, and belong to a living nonlinear system.

Why does this matter for composition? Because the spectrum of a sound and the perceptual identity of that sound are not the same. A bowed note can have many harmonics, and a plucked note on the same pitch can have a different envelope and distribution of energy. At a sufficiently broad descriptive level, both may share a fundamental periodicity but differ in timbre. The perceptual review by Josh McDermott and Andrew Oxenham, Music Perception, Pitch, and the Auditory System, carefully distinguishes pitch from the entire physical spectrum. Its discussion of periodicity and the missing fundamental helps us confront an initially surprising fact: listeners may hear a pitch at a frequency for which the recording contains no strong component, and sometimes no component at all.

Build a toy example. Take three sine waves at 400, 600, and 800 hertz, and add them together at equal or moderately different levels. Each component is present. Two hundred hertz is not. Nevertheless, these are the second, third, and fourth harmonics of a 200-hertz fundamental. Under appropriate listening conditions, many listeners perceive a pitch related to that 200-hertz periodicity. The combination repeats in one two-hundredth of a second. We should not imply that every listener always hears precisely 200 hertz; pitch strength and interpretation can depend on spectral content, level, and context. But this example demonstrates why the highest spectral peak does not automatically define perceived pitch. Removing a low harmonic is not the same operation as moving the whole note upward.

There are two complementary ways to speak about hearing frequency. One describes which cochlear regions respond most strongly. Another describes temporal patterns in neural responses and their relation to periodicity. The contribution of each type of information depends on frequency range and the sound itself. A serious theory of pitch cannot be reduced to choosing between a purely spatial spectrum meter and a purely time-domain pulse counter. McDermott and Oxenham survey the experimental constraints rather than offering a single universal shortcut.

Now consider two notes separated by an octave. In common Western notation, we describe their fundamentals as having a two-to-one frequency ratio. If one note is 220 hertz, the note an octave above it is 440 hertz. That is an exact statement about nominal fundamental frequencies in the example. Yet the subjective resemblance of the notes does not establish that every musical culture uses Western octave-based tuning in the same way, or even that listeners everywhere classify all pitch intervals identically. The auditory system offers constraints and possibilities; experience and musical organization influence how we interpret them. This is one reason the comparative music lecture distinguished the grammar of a tradition from a fixed list of frequencies.

Loudness introduces a different problem. A sample value of point five is not a quantity of sound pressure in the room without a calibrated playback chain. The electrical signal, amplifier, transducer, listening distance, room, and listener all intervene. Even if we calibrate the physical sound pressure, equal physical pressure at different frequencies need not produce equal perceived loudness. Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson published measurements and models of auditory loudness in 1933, helping establish the tradition behind equal-loudness contours. A modern source is ISO 226:2023, Normal equal-loudness-level contours. Importantly, the scope of that standard is limited: it concerns pure continuous tones in specified free-field frontal, binaural listening conditions and a specified normal-hearing young-adult population. It is not a formula that converts any pop song, heard through any headphones, into a universally agreed loudness.

Imagine setting a 100-hertz sine wave and a 1,000-hertz sine wave to the same digital sample peak. They need not seem equally loud. At relatively quiet playback levels, bass can require a higher physical sound-pressure level than mid-frequency tones to be judged equally loud under the standardized conditions. But do not turn this observation into a recipe to boost every bass track. The result changes with listening level, playback response, and other sounds. Hearing a sine wave is not the same experiment as hearing an ensemble. A frequency-dependent perceptual effect does not imply a single corrective equalizer setting.

An important distinction is the difference between loudness and annoyance, or between loudness and musical prominence. A very brief snare transient may have a high instantaneous peak but contribute relatively little energy to a long passage. A sustained synth pad may have modest peaks yet dominate the sense of fullness. A soft lead melody may be musically salient because of its contour, onset pattern, or position in an arrangement. Peak level, time-averaged signal energy, standardized programme-loudness estimates, and listener attention answer different questions. Confusing them encourages an agent to adjust the wrong control.

Timbre is more difficult still. It is sometimes introduced as what distinguishes instruments playing the same pitch at the same loudness, but that definition is a beginning, not a complete physical model. Attack, decay, harmonic balance, inharmonic partials, noise, microvariation, and dynamic changes can all contribute. Imagine removing the opening transient of a plucked string while retaining its later sustain. Its identity might change substantially, because the ear has lost evidence about how the sound began. Now imagine preserving the attack but filtering out upper partials. That edit changes other evidence. A spectrogram can display some of it, but it does not assign a trustworthy psychological label like warm, thin, or intimate by itself.

Time changes what we perceive as a unit. If two sounds begin together, have similar harmonic movement, and stop together, listeners may group them more readily as one perceptual object. If their attacks alternate, their pitches separate, and their rhythm develops independently, the scene may split into distinct streams. Albert Bregman's book Auditory Scene Analysis organizes decades of research on this problem: the physical mixture at the ears must be parsed into plausible sources and events. The brain does not receive isolated instrument stems. The stems are an engineer's privilege, not a listener's starting point.

Consider a composer programming three identical notes on a software piano. One occurs in the left hand, one in the right hand, and one is doubled by a second instrument. In the score, these are separate events. In the audio, they may reinforce, interfere, or blend. In perception, they may seem like one stronger note. If the duplicated performances have slightly different attack times or timbres, they may be distinguished or heard as a wider composite. None of these outcomes is decided solely by the count of tracks in a DAW.

There are practical consequences. When trying to make a lead audible, raising its level is only one choice. The composer can alter the entry time of a competing line, choose a contrasting attack envelope, lower the density of simultaneous notes, change register, or let a phrase rest. Those are edits to the source scene before it reaches the mix bus. If everything else stays constant, even a simple mute comparison can help decide whether a part supports or obscures the intended musical event. But the mute comparison is a local test of two versions, not proof that the muted instrument was inherently bad.

Hearing also has limited temporal discrimination and integration. We may notice a transient distinctly while still combining information over a short interval to judge its loudness or pitch. Those are not contradictory claims. Different auditory tasks use different evidence and time scales. A producer's choice of a five-millisecond crossfade, a fifty-millisecond compressor attack, or a two-second reverberant tail may matter in quite different ways, because each manipulates temporal structure at a different scale. A fixed signal-processing time constant cannot be equated with one universal perceptual integration time.

An instructive experiment requires no elaborate studio. Create two copies of a short original melody. In the first, keep the beginnings of the notes crisp. In the second, apply a slow envelope that softens each attack, while adjusting playback so the versions have comparable overall level. Ask: are the pitches easier to identify, the rhythm clearer, or the instrument's character different? Do not tell the listener which is processed until after the comparison. The planned differences are mathematical; the answers are observations about a listener. They can be recorded, tested, and compared without masquerading as universal results.

Try another original experiment with the harmonic complex. Render 400, 600, and 800 hertz together, then compare them with a single 200-hertz sine wave at a controlled playback level. If both suggest approximately the same pitch, does one sound richer, brighter, or more buzzy? That distinction separates pitch from timbre. Next change the relative amplitudes of the three components without changing their frequencies. This alters timbre and possibly pitch strength, while leaving the nominal common fundamental unchanged. The mathematics is an original construction, not an experiment claimed by the McDermott–Oxenham review. Their work explains why the construction is interesting.

A third experiment reveals the limits of visual diagnosis. Open two short clips in Ardour, one a soft sustained tone and another a brief percussive attack. Normalize both to the same sample peak, then listen at a sensible, fixed monitor setting. Do not assume that peak normalization makes their apparent loudness comparable. Check their measured integrated or short-term levels where appropriate, but remember that programme meters depend on the specific algorithm and observation interval. If you perform the rendering in DawDreamer, store each processing graph, parameter set, and source file so the comparison is repeatable. A listening exercise gains credibility from reversible inputs, not from attaching a decorative scientific number to it.

The broader lesson is about epistemology. A waveform tells us how a sampled signal varies. A frequency analysis tells us about its component structure under stated assumptions and windows. A hearing study tells us how tested listeners responded to controlled stimuli and tasks. A composer or mix engineer decides how those responses matter for a particular work. These levels inform one another, but they are not interchangeable. ISO's contours are not a mix preset, a cochlear map is not a musical key signature, and a spectrum analyzer is not a substitute for an ear.

When an agent says that a note is masked, bright, loud, or emotionally prominent, ask what kind of claim it is making. Is it reporting sample values, analyzing a model, citing a listening experiment, describing a listener's response, or proposing an artistic interpretation? Answering that question is already progress toward a more defensible musical workflow. We do not need to deny perceptual complexity to work with it. We need to name what was measured, what was heard, and what remains a decision.

## Non-spoken companion: exact mathematical constructions

- **Harmonic complex:** \(x(t)=a_2\sin(2\pi 400t)+a_3\sin(2\pi 600t)+a_4\sin(2\pi 800t)\), where amplitudes are chosen to prevent clipping. The greatest common divisor of component frequencies is 200 Hz, so a shared waveform period is 5 ms. The 200-Hz sinusoid is **absent**; a perceived 200-Hz missing-fundamental pitch is possible, not guaranteed for every listener.
- **Octave relation:** \(f_2=2f_1\) in an idealized nominal-frequency example (220 and 440 Hz). This is not a claim about universal tuning practice.
- **Peak conversion:** a digital amplitude of 0.5 relative to full scale has a level of \(20\log_{10}(0.5)\approx-6.0206\) dBFS for that sample. This does not specify acoustic dB SPL or perceived loudness.
- **Experiment controls:** note event timestamps, render settings, equal or documented output gains, reference playback setup, and listener reports. Record whether a listener knew which processing was active.
