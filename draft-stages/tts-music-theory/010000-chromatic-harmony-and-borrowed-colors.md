# 010000 — Chromatic Harmony: Borrowed Colors and Temporary Destinations

# Chromatic Harmony: Borrowed Colors and Temporary Destinations

Imagine that you have written a short piece using the notes C, D, E, F, G, A, and B. It returns to a C chord at the end. Nothing is wrong with the passage, but you want one moment to point especially strongly toward G. Later you want a different moment to sound related to C minor, even though the piece keeps C major as its home. Does that require changing the key of the entire piece? No. A single altered pitch, carefully placed in its context, can redirect the music for a few beats.

This is the basic idea behind several forms of chromatic harmony. In the twelve-semitone system we are using for our examples, chromatic notes lie outside the currently assumed seven-note scale or involve pitch alterations that call attention to a different harmonic relationship. Chromatic does not mean complicated, dark, or dissonant. A raised note might make a resolution feel especially pointed. A lowered note might move a line smoothly into a note already present in the next chord. There are many uses, and a written score can show us the precise mechanism even before we hear how a performance realizes it.

To see one mechanism, begin with an ordinary C-major setting. A C major triad contains C, E, and G. A G major triad contains G, B, and D. In traditional tonal language, C is the tonic, the central or home harmony, and G is the dominant, the chord built on the fifth scale degree. A change from G to C often supports a sense of arrival in that style. But a G chord can itself be approached as if it were temporarily important. We can make another harmony point toward it.

The chord we will use is D major, spelled D, F sharp, A. A D major triad is a fifth above G in root motion, and its F sharp is one semitone below G. That makes F sharp a leading tone to G: a note close enough to the destination to offer a particularly direct motion into it in a tonal context. D major is not made entirely of the seven notes of C major because the C-major scale has F natural. We have changed one pitch in the chord and created a new local direction. If we add C natural to the D major triad, we obtain D dominant seventh, with D, F sharp, A, C. Its seventh, C, can move downward to B when the chord resolves to G major, while F sharp moves upward to G. Those are **two different simultaneous lines**: F sharp to G can be carried by the upper part while C to B moves in another part. A single monophonic melody cannot play both paths at once. The chord symbols indicate the available notes, not that a complete voicing has already been supplied.

This D seventh chord is called the dominant of the dominant in C major, often abbreviated five of five. The first five means a dominant-type chord; the second identifies its target, the G major chord, which is itself the dominant of C. A listener need not memorize the Roman numerals to understand the action. We briefly treat G like a destination, use D seventh to prepare it, arrive on G, and then potentially continue to C. The temporary emphasis on G is called tonicization. It does not necessarily mean we have modulated, or moved the whole piece into G major. A true change of key involves wider contextual evidence: continued pitch organization, cadences, phrasing, and the degree to which the new center becomes established.

Consider four chords of one bar each: C major, D seventh, G major, and C major. Start with C as the established center. D seventh enters in the second bar and brings in F sharp, a pitch the opening harmony did not require. In the third bar, F sharp can rise to G, and the C from D seventh can fall to B, both of which belong to G major. In the final bar, the G harmony gives way to C. That is a complete little harmonic excursion. The altered note does not merely add color to D; it helps specify a direction out of D.

Now suppose you want a different kind of chromatic change. Keep C as the home note, but near the end place an F minor chord between a C major harmony and the next C major arrival. F minor contains F, A flat, and C. F major, the familiar fourth-degree triad of C major, contains F, A, and C. The only difference is A becoming A flat. That A flat belongs to the parallel C minor collection, so in a major-key context we can describe F minor as a borrowed minor-four chord. This is one instance of mode mixture: bringing a chord associated with a parallel mode into an otherwise major-mode setting.

Notice how its voice leading differs from the D seventh example. An F minor chord can move directly to C major while keeping C as a common note. F might move down to E. A flat might move down to G. Each of these movements is a semitone or whole tone. The bass may move from F to C, but the inner pitches can shift by tiny distances. The effect is not that F minor is a new home. Its borrowed A flat introduces a chromatic inflection and can make the return to C feel altered by the passing harmony. The emotional meaning is a matter of actual musical context, not a universal label attached to the chord.

One explanation for this contrast is useful to remember. A secondary dominant points toward a temporary destination, such as D seventh pointing to G. A borrowed chord brings material from a related mode into a still-recognizable tonal environment, such as F minor inside C major. Both are chromatic in the same simple sense that they use a note outside the unaltered C-major collection. But their jobs and resolutions need not be the same. The pitch F sharp in D seventh encourages upward motion toward G; the A flat in F minor can encourage downward motion toward G or another nearby note. The mechanism is audible through lines, not merely visible in a chord label.

A composer can build these chords from a question rather than from an arbitrary desire to use advanced harmony. First ask which destination would benefit from additional preparation. If the destination is G major, a D dominant seventh is a plausible candidate because the D-to-G root motion and internal semitone resolutions are clear. Next ask whether a passage should depart from the established mode without abandoning its center. An F minor chord in a C-major environment offers that possibility. Write the altered pitch deliberately, then follow its path. If a voice simply leaps away instead of using the expected resolution, that is a permissible musical choice; it just produces a different argument from the standard resolution we have been discussing.

There is another way to use an altered note that does not require a new chord. Suppose the melody over a C chord goes E, F, F sharp, G, with all notes moving upward by a semitone or whole tone. The F sharp is a chromatic passing tone between F and G. We do not have to relabel the underlying C harmony as D major just because F sharp has appeared in the melody. The note's duration, placement, accompaniment, and destination matter. If F sharp is brief and the C chord remains stable, we might hear it as a passing melodic inflection. If the bass changes to D and other parts supply A and C, the entire harmony can change. A pitch is not a chord, and a chord is not a key.

Try making a controlled revision to a progression you already understand. Take four bars: C, A minor, G, C. Each chord lasts four beats. In the first version, keep those chords and write one note per beat in the upper melody using C-major notes. In the second, replace A minor with D seventh and keep the G and C bars. You have altered both root motion and one important scale pitch, F sharp. Decide whether the melody should expose F sharp clearly before resolving it to G. In the third version, return to the original harmonic path but insert an F minor chord before the final C, adding a bar if necessary. The two revisions change different questions. One says, where are we going just now? The other says, which color from a neighboring mode can we bring into this place?

You can also combine both mechanisms in a longer phrase, but not every passage needs them. Imagine an eight-bar sequence: C, A minor, D seventh, G, C, F minor, G seventh, C. The D seventh in bar three directs attention to G. Later, F minor briefly introduces A flat. G seventh, spelled G, B, D, F, prepares the final C in a more conventional dominant-to-tonic move. Each chord has its own reason for appearing. The phrase is richer because there is a sequence of comprehensible changes in direction, not because we have maximized the number of pitch classes.

We should be precise about what the theory does not guarantee. A D seventh can fail to resolve to G, or resolve irregularly, by design. An F minor chord might be heard as part of a repeating groove instead of a short dramatic departure. A passage might use the same notes in jazz, film music, blues, rock, folk music, or classical writing with different expectations about resolution. Nor is every sharp or flat an intrusion from a distant key. Some styles treat pitches that Western common-practice theory calls chromatic as stable members of their regular vocabulary. The labels are tools for explaining relationships under a stated interpretation.

The practical skill to retain is very specific. When you add an altered note, identify what changed. Ask whether it creates a local leading tone, modifies a chord quality, belongs to a borrowed pitch collection, serves a passing melodic motion, or does something else entirely. Track where the altered note goes next. And distinguish temporary emphasis on a chord from a genuine change of tonic. With those checks, chromatic harmony stops being a catalogue of complicated symbols. It becomes the art of creating a small directional change while keeping the overall musical map in view.

`q` is one beat, `h` is two beats. Four quarter-note beats per bar, harmony begins on beat one and lasts the whole bar. The melody is deliberately simple enough for verbal reading; chord labels name pitch classes, not required voicings.

**Voice-leading caveat:** The melody shows an F#4 → G4 resolution at the bar 3–4 boundary and Ab4 → G4 at the bar 6–7 boundary. The other parts that complete each chord are **not** individually notated in this grid; they must be assigned if a performer wants full four-part counterpoint. The illustrative D7 contains C, which *may* move to B in another voice, but the single upper melody does not specify that motion.

### An explicit two-voice resolution for bar 3 into bar 4

The eight-bar melody above presents only one upper line. Its final F♯4 in bar 3 resolves to G4 at the start of bar 4, but the other tendency C→B is **not** sustained as a second part in that melody. Here is a separate, optional two-part realization of the same D7→G harmony:

| Part | D7 (end of bar 3) | G (start of bar 4) | Intervallic motion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | F♯4 | G4 | upward semitone into G |
| Inner | C4 | B3 | downward semitone into B |
| Bass | D3 | G2 | down perfect fifth (or up fourth with octave adjustment) |

The D7 voicing omits A; the arrival voicing omits D. The table illustrates an available resolution, not another note secretly present in the original single-line score. An arrangement could sustain the inner and bass parts while keeping the score's existing upper melody.

### Three versions to write

1. Replace D7 with D minor (D F A), leaving the G bar intact. What leading-tone movement disappears?
2. Replace Fm in bar 6 with F major (F A C). Which single pitch changes? Write a one-voice path from that pitch into the G7 chord.
3. Retain all eight chord names, but make bars 3–4 a two-bar melodic duet. Assign one voice F# → G and a second C → B on the boundary. Specify each note's register and whether it sustains until the next chord.
4. On guitar, distinguish a chord **shape** from harmonic function. A D7 grip played in a C-centered song may tonicize G; the same sonority in another context can play a different role. On an instrument without chords, have one voice play the chord root and another outline the crucial chromatic line.
