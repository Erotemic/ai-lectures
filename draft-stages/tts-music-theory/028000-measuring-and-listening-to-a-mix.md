# Measuring and Listening to a Mix: Loudness, True Peaks, Blind Comparisons, and Provenance

Two agents independently mix the same composition. One returns a dense, bright master. The other returns a quieter, more open arrangement. When the files are played back at their default levels, the brighter master seems more exciting. The first agent declares victory. But suppose we reduce its playback level until the two versions are roughly matched in loudness. Perhaps the second mix now makes the melody easier to follow. Perhaps the first remains preferable. Until we control the comparison, we do not know whether the preference concerned its arrangement, its spectral balance, or simply its higher level.

The subject of this lecture is not how to make every song equally loud. It is how to tell what our observations establish. A good audio production pipeline needs measurements, listening trials, and a careful record of what happened. Those are different kinds of evidence. We will build an evaluation workflow around Ardour, which can analyze exports, and DawDreamer, which can render controlled variants from a processing graph. The objective is to make comparisons possible without pretending that a meter has opinions about music.

Start with a sample peak. A digital waveform is represented by values at discrete sample times. If the largest absolute value in a floating-point render is point eight, its peak relative to a full-scale amplitude of one is about minus 1.94 decibels relative to full scale. This tells us about the maximum stored sample magnitude. It does not tell us how loud the entire piece sounds, how much low-frequency energy it contains, or whether a listener finds its rhythm compelling. An isolated click can have a high peak while contributing little energy over a long passage.

True peak is a related but distinct measurement. Converting digital samples to a continuous output waveform involves reconstruction and filtering. Between the sample locations, the reconstructed waveform may reach a greater magnitude than any stored sample. A true-peak estimator uses oversampling or other suitable reconstruction to estimate those inter-sample extrema. Recommendation ITU-R BS.1770-5 provides standardized algorithms for audio-program loudness and true-peak level. The recommendation's annex describes a practical four-times oversampling estimator at a forty-eight-kilohertz rate. It is important to say estimator: a digital meter cannot literally observe every analogue circuit and playback chain.

Now consider the loudness of an entire programme. A one-second impact followed by thirty seconds of quiet ambience will have a different integrated loudness from thirty seconds of dense music, even if their sample peaks are equal. A straightforward RMS measurement averages squared sample magnitudes and takes a square root. It is a physical-style signal measure, not a full model of perceived loudness. Standardized loudness measurement introduces frequency weighting, channel weighting, temporal integration and gating. It is designed to describe programme level more usefully than an unweighted peak. The resulting integrated loudness is commonly expressed in LUFS. LUFS does not claim that every listener, room or piece of music will feel equally loud at the same reading.

The European Broadcasting Union's R 128 recommendation is a real publication and policy framework, not a vague rule of thumb. Its November 2023 version recommends an average programme loudness of minus twenty-three LUFS for the broadcasting applications it addresses, together with descriptors including loudness range and maximum true peak. That value is not an eternal optimum for all streaming platforms, games, soundtracks, podcasts, or experimental music. A delivery target is a constraint of a specified context. It cannot be derived from the statement that minus twenty-three is good for the EBU's use case.

A subtle practical point follows: gain normalization is not compression. If a file is turned down by four decibels using a constant multiplier, its dynamics relative to itself remain the same. Its peak and average move together. A compressor instead applies time-varying gain related to a detector and can change the relation between transients and sustained passages. If a mastering limiter prevents new peaks from crossing a ceiling, it may change the waveform whenever the peaks would exceed that ceiling. A loudness target combined with a true-peak ceiling can therefore require more than simply raising the entire file. It can also be impossible to reach without additional processing or accepting a lower loudness result.

Ardour exposes these ideas in concrete tools. Its export report gives file format, sample rate, peak, true peak and integrated loudness. Its Loudness Analyzer and Normalizer allows measurements before committing an export. The manual also warns against accidentally applying one normalization in the loudness assistant and another during export. This is the kind of double-processing mistake that can survive an otherwise elegant workflow. A human reviewer should be able to tell which stage was allowed to change gain, and whether the accepted master is the same file that was actually measured.

Imagine two candidate mixes of a thirty-second original piece. Mix A measures minus twelve LUFS integrated and minus one dB true peak. Mix B measures minus sixteen LUFS integrated and minus three dB true peak. These are hypothetical measured values, not output from Ardour in this lecture. We could reduce A by roughly four decibels to approach B's programme level and compare at that gain, remembering that integrated loudness estimators can depend on gating and that a fixed offset need not make every passage equally loud. The peak of A would then also move downward by four decibels in a simple gain-only operation. We should not move B up without checking its true-peak ceiling and whether further processing would become necessary.

Which mix is better after that adjustment? We need to listen. But listening has its own experimental pitfalls. The order of two versions can influence judgment. The label “professional” or “AI generated” can alter expectation. A visually impressive spectrogram can bias the observer before a note has sounded. Even a tiny level difference may change a person's choice. An informative comparison therefore hides the identities of the candidates when feasible, randomizes their order, controls playback level, uses representative excerpts, and allows the reviewer to revisit difficult passages. This is a method of reducing confounds, not proof that every musical preference can be made objective.

There are established standards for particular listening questions. ITU-R BS.1116-3 concerns the assessment of small impairments in audio systems. Its formal method uses a known reference and concealed candidates, one of which is a hidden reference. ITU-R BS.1534-3 describes a method for intermediate-quality impairments and is widely associated with the MUSHRA family of listening tests. Neither standard is a generic recipe for choosing the best chord progression. They were designed for controlled audio-system assessments, with defined stimuli and judgments. Still, their discipline can inspire better informal mix reviews: include references where meaningful, conceal identity, state the question clearly, and do not change several variables unknowingly between conditions.

It is especially important to distinguish discrimination from preference. Suppose a reviewer can reliably tell two mixes apart. That does not imply either one is preferred. Suppose a reviewer prefers one version today. That does not establish that the difference would be preferred by every listener or on every playback system. Suppose a group rates one version higher in a controlled study. That is stronger evidence about a specified population under specified conditions, not a universal aesthetic theorem. The wording of the result must match the experiment.

For agent-produced stems, we can design an inexpensive initial comparison before any formal listening study. Preserve one fixed set of dry source files. Construct two candidate signal graphs in DawDreamer that differ in one documented parameter group: perhaps the guitar EQ, perhaps the amount sent to the reverb bus, perhaps the attack setting of a compressor. Render both to the same sample rate and duration. Measure peaks and loudness on their actual output. Then import both into Ardour on separate tracks or playlists, arrange for matched playback loudness, and audition blind if the routing permits. Keep the graphs, preset states and audio hashes. The comparison can now be repeated, and any remaining uncertainty is visible rather than buried inside a label such as better mix.

When we say “change one parameter group,” we should be precise. Changing the reverb-send amount changes the energy in the entire wet path, which may affect stereo image, peaks, perceived distance and the level at the master. That is still a controlled experiment if we write down the change and allow it to produce its real consequences. It is not a controlled experiment if the same agent quietly revises melody, gain, compression and the mastering limiter while claiming that a new reverb setting caused the improvement.

There is a second way to be fooled: comparing files that are not actually aligned. One export may have a leading silence, a plug-in delay, or a longer effect tail. A listening switch in the middle of the phrase then changes musical timing as well as mix. Compare the same passage at the same musical position, confirm channel layouts, and account for silence and tails when calculating integrated loudness. Equal file lengths do not themselves prove event alignment. If the source parts were independently rendered by different agents, this verification should happen before the first evaluation.

Measurements also depend on the monitor chain. Headphones, loudspeakers, room modes, background noise, and playback level can reveal or obscure different problems. If two mixes sound indistinguishable on one set of speakers but divergent on headphones, the disagreement may contain useful information. For example, a stereo widening method that uses phase differences can sound spacious on headphones yet lose important material when downmixed to mono. Check mono compatibility, but do not assume that a mono render represents every modern listening experience. A technically valid mix can still require artistic choices about width and intimacy.

We should be careful about CPU performance claims too. A render finishing twice as fast is evidence about that engine, graph, hardware, and workload, not about sonic superiority. A processor that is cheap offline may miss a real-time deadline if placed behind a long serial dependency. DawDreamer's documented threading guide describes private engines per worker and the release of Python's GIL during rendering; it also notes that some plugin-loading and compilation operations are serialized internally. Those facts make a parallel batch design plausible, but no manual promises that every plug-in is thread-safe or that doubling workers doubles throughput. Benchmarks need recorded engine and plug-in versions, sample rates, graph structures and hardware.

Imagine a production review that reports only “Mix B won, because the score was higher.” We cannot tell whether the score measured peaks, loudness, an audio similarity metric, or a human preference. A more defensible review might say: The two masters came from the same source stems; only the guitar EQ and reverb send differed; both were exported as 48-kilohertz stereo float audio; the playback gain was adjusted to approximate matched programme loudness; the test was blind but involved one listener; on the first verse the melody was easier to distinguish in B; the listener preferred B, though the instrumental break sounded richer in A. That is a much narrower claim, but it contains actual evidence and a clear opening for revision.

For a future automated mix-ranking system, there is a similar hierarchy. Mechanical tests can reject invalid files, incorrect channels, clipping beyond an agreed ceiling, unwanted silence, nonfinite samples, wrong timing and lost stems. Standardized meters can characterize programme loudness and estimated true peaks. Signal features may identify possible masking or spectral imbalance. None of these automatically establishes the intended emotional effect or musical hierarchy. An algorithm that maximizes a convenient number may damage the music unless the number corresponds closely to a genuine production goal.

The lesson ends with a practical definition of a finished master. It is an artifact chosen through some combination of technical checks and listening judgment, with a clear delivery specification and enough provenance to explain how it was made. Finishing is not identical to pushing the loudness as high as possible. The composer supplies an intended experience; the production system helps realize and communicate that experience. Good measurements protect the experience from preventable technical mistakes. Good listening keeps the measurements from becoming the purpose of the music.

## Display-only companion: a non-normative review protocol

**This is an original practical workflow inspired by, but not identical to, formal ITU listening-test procedures.**

1. Freeze symbolic parts or audio stems and record their hashes, channel layout, sample rate and common time origin.
2. Choose one explanatory variable (for example, one EQ band or one send level). Identify all downstream changes it can legitimately cause.
3. Render two candidates with DawDreamer using recorded processor states; document version, host environment and deterministic or stochastic settings.
4. In Ardour, inspect both files and measure sample peak, true peak, integrated loudness, and export duration. Do not infer preferences from these figures alone.
5. Compare at approximate loudness-matched playback levels; record how matching was performed and whether an additional limiter or gain change modified the candidates.
6. For an informal blind check, conceal version labels and randomize order. Ask two separate questions: “Can I tell them apart?” and “Which serves this passage better?”
7. Capture specific passage/timestamp observations; save the selected master and the rejected candidate with the same audit metadata.

## Display-only companion: two levels and two measurements

- Hypothetical Mix A: `-12 LUFS` integrated, `-1 dBTP` estimated true peak.
- Hypothetical Mix B: `-16 LUFS` integrated, `-3 dBTP` estimated true peak.
- A static `-4 dB` gain applied to A will nominally reduce its true peak from `-1 dBTP` to `-5 dBTP` and may make its programme loudness closer to B's; **an exact post-gating integrated measurement should be run again**, and the -4 dB change is not a compressor.
- These are invented example values, not a measurement of any included audio file or a universal delivery target.

## Claim-to-source map

- **ITU loudness and true-peak algorithms:** [ITU-R BS.1770-5 (November 2023), “Algorithms to measure audio programme loudness and true-peak audio level”](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I/en). Provides the normative measurement algorithms; consult the actual recommendation before implementing conformance. This lecture makes no claim of a certified meter.
- **The -23 LUFS recommendation in a specific broadcasting context:** [EBU R 128, version 5.0 (November 2023), “Loudness normalisation and permitted maximum level of audio signals”](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/r128). It is **not** asserted as a universal music-streaming target.
- **Ardour's analysis and normalization controls:** [Ardour Manual, *Loudness Analyzer and Normalizer*](https://manual.ardour.org/mixing/basic-mixing/loudness-analyzer-and-normalizer/); [*Export Dialog*](https://manual.ardour.org/exporting/export-dialog/). Read the normalization warning to avoid duplicate gain processing.
- **Small impairment test design:** [ITU-R BS.1116-3 (2015), “Methods for the subjective assessment of small impairments in audio systems”](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1116-3-201502-I/en). The formal method is more demanding than the informal comparison proposed here.
- **Intermediate impairment tests:** [ITU-R BS.1534-3 (2015), “Method for the subjective assessment of intermediate quality level of audio systems”](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1534/en). Cited for the scope and existence of MUSHRA-style methodology, not as a proof of taste.
- **Threading and CPU constraints:** [DawDreamer, *Multithreading and Multiprocessing*](https://github.com/DBraun/DawDreamer/blob/main/docs/user_guide/threading.rst), including GIL-release and serialized plugin-loading statements.
- **Production comparison/protocol:** The two-mix scenario and seven-step checklist are the **lecture author's constructed examples**. No ITU or EBU document is represented as endorsing that particular production workflow.
