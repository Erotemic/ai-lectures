All necessary terms are restated in context. **Purpose:** follow the creation and revision of a single complete original study, distinguishing a compositional edit from a textural rearrangement, a mix decision, and an unsupported claim of musical improvement.

# From Motif to Finished Piece

A four-note melody is not yet a composition. It could become a song, an instrumental miniature, an ostinato, an argument among several independent voices, or nothing worth preserving. The notes do not contain a hidden instruction telling us which outcome is correct. To make a piece, we must decide not only what comes next, but what deserves to return; which relationships listeners may remember; where the music should change direction; which sounds belong in the foreground; and, finally, whether our decisions survive actual listening.

Imagine being handed five versions of a forty-bar piece. The first offers only a melodic line. The second places a bass and continuous chords beneath that line. The third removes some of those chords and introduces quieter secondary figures. The fourth changes exactly two pitches, leaving the rest of the third version intact. The fifth changes only the levels and positions of the sound sources, without changing a single musical note. Five versions could be generated by one computational workflow, but they answer different questions. Which one is better organized? Which one is more transparent? Which one feels finished? Are these even the same question?

Our case study will give us the evidence to answer carefully. Its title is *The Returning Line*. It is a new, forty-bar classroom composition in four-four meter, at a reference tempo of one hundred and four quarter-note beats per minute. Every pitch and event in the accompanying score belongs to this original study. We are not copying a score by Bach, Beethoven, or a contemporary songwriter. A text-based event file can render the melody, the draft, the revision, and an alternate mix into ordinary WAV files. Listening is optional for following the reasoning, but ultimately necessary for deciding how persuasive the result is.

The piece is modest by design. It uses tonal chords, a handful of related pitch areas, a simple melody, bass, and synthesized inner voices. Those restrictions give us control. They do not establish a universal theory of composition, and a synthetically rendered demonstration cannot stand in for the behavior of actual acoustic instruments or human performers. Think of this as a complete, inspectable argument about a short work rather than a claim that every composer should follow the same template.

## The tiny contract inside the first bar

Here is the entire seed. Hear four pitches: E, G, A, G. The first lasts a full beat. G and A occupy half a beat each. The returning G lasts two beats. The pitch contour rises a minor third, then a whole step, then falls a whole step. But the rhythm is equally important: one, half, half, two. There is a small burst of activity in the middle and a long breath at the end.

The first harmony is C major. E and G belong to that triad; A is not a chord tone of the simple C-major triad. We can hear A as a melodic neighboring excursion between G and its return, but the classification depends on the full gesture and context rather than a rule that every non-chord pitch has one fixed label. What matters for this exercise is that the melody contains an identifiable contour and a distinctive rhythm. You can change the instrument and keep those relationships; you can change the harmony and still recognize them; you can change the rhythm and potentially weaken recognition even if every pitch remains.

Arnold Schoenberg's *Fundamentals of Musical Composition* treats the motive and the ways it can be connected, varied, and developed as central problems of composition. His book is a particular pedagogical account of tonal composition, not a law governing every musical tradition. We borrow the practical question: what minimal feature can a listener recognize after the context changes? In this example, the answer is not simply the sequence of pitch names. It is the joint pattern of intervals, durations, and returns.

A first experiment is almost embarrassingly small. Render the four-note idea alone. Then play the first two bars, where a second bar moves through A, C, B, A over an A-minor harmony. The second bar is not a literal answer to the first. It keeps melodic motion alive and changes the harmonic floor. If we force the second bar to be a symmetrical call-and-response merely because that device is familiar, we may lose the more interesting question: what kind of continuity has the first bar already created?

## A map of a whole work

We organize the piece into five sections of eight bars. Bars one through eight state the main idea in a C-major setting. Bars nine through sixteen keep the recognizable opening while shifting the harmonic setting toward A minor. Bars seventeen through twenty-four visit a G-oriented contrasting region and transpose the four-note head upward by seven semitones. Bars twenty-five through thirty-two bring the original head back over C major while changing some of the surrounding activity. Bars thirty-three through forty simplify the material and lead toward a final tonic-colored arrival.

This map is a description, not an argument for a mandatory five-part form. William Caplin, writing about eighteenth-century Classical instrumental music, distinguishes formal functions—such as initiating, continuing, and cadential activity—from mere labels for sections. His terminology helps ask whether a passage actually performs a beginning or continuation role in a particular style. It does not authorize us to call our homemade five-part study a canonical Classical form. Here we choose five sections because they make a controlled comparison possible.

The opening eight bars use this harmonic path: C major, A minor, F major, G dominant seventh, C major, F major, G dominant seventh, and C major. For a listener who knows tonal harmony, the route establishes a recognizable pull toward C and returns there. For someone who does not, the important thing is simpler: the bass keeps giving the melody a changing but related floor, and several arrivals sound more settled than the notes leading into them.

In bar seven, the melody moves G, F, D, B. In bar eight it proceeds C, E, G, C. At the bar boundary, the B can resolve to C and the bass moves into a C-root chord. We may describe that as a cadential gesture in this stylized exercise. We should not automatically certify every such boundary a perfect authentic cadence. That stronger historical-theoretical label depends on the actual voicing and phrase conditions, including what is in the bass at the moment of harmonic change. A model can inspect the pitches; it still needs the correct theory and conditions to make the claim.

The first eight bars are not compelling because they contain eight different chords. Most of those harmonies are common. Their purpose is to establish something that can be remembered and to place small changes around it. If a listener cannot remember any aspect of the opening, the later return will have little to reactivate. But recognition is not proof of beauty. Elizabeth Hellmuth Margulis's research-oriented book *On Repeat* makes a serious case for repetition's role in musical experience and attention. It does not say that an arbitrary repeated motif must be enjoyable. We use repetition to establish the possibility of recognition, not to guarantee an emotional result.

## The first return changes the meaning, not the notes

At bar nine, E, G, A, G returns with exactly the same rhythm and pitches as bar one. The harmony underneath is now A minor. That G can be heard as the minor seventh above A, which gives the motif a different harmonic relation from its earlier appearance over C major. We have changed the setting before changing the melodic identity. It is an economical compositional move, and it gives us a concrete test of how much harmonic context matters.

The middle of this section uses E minor and F major, then an E dominant seventh that includes G sharp. That G sharp is important. In the key area of A minor, a raised leading tone can support a stronger pull toward A; E major or E dominant seventh has a different relationship to A minor than E minor does. But we have not turned the piece into a strict functional-harmony examination. Some notes are passing or ornamental, and our oscillator rendering is not a historical performance of tonal style. We are selecting one harmonic expectation and using it as an audible contrast.

Bar sixteen ends on a melody C over A minor. C is the third of A minor, not the root. That ending is less final in the upper voice than an A would be. If you feel an open door rather than a locked conclusion, the actual score offers a reason worth testing. If you do not feel that effect, no amount of formal terminology should overrule your listening. The composer's claim is a hypothesis about the musical experience. It is not a measurement yet.

David Huron's *Sweet Anticipation* develops a psychological account of expectations in music. We need not adopt its entire theory to recognize a practical distinction: a change in harmony can change what a listener anticipates, even if the next few melody notes stay identical. We should also avoid making universal claims about expectation from this one classroom example. Familiarity, style, training, and context all matter.

## A contrast that keeps the family resemblance

At bar seventeen, the head returns as B, D, E, D. Relative to the original E, G, A, G, that is an exact transposition upward by seven semitones. The four durations are also unchanged. A simple validator can confirm this fact precisely. It does not need to make any judgment about expression. That distinction—between a property the score demonstrates and an experience a listener must assess—will matter repeatedly.

The G-oriented passage then moves through E minor, C, D dominant seventh, G, E minor, A minor, and D dominant seventh. It uses the notes of the G-major area frequently, including F sharp in the D-seventh harmony. But its final D-seventh chord does not resolve to G in the next bar. Instead, bar twenty-five brings back our opening C. You might hear that shift as a surprising redirection or as a smooth connection into the C-major home. Either response is possible; we should not describe this eight-bar visit as a confirmed textbook modulation ending in a root-position G cadence when the notated continuation does something else.

Bar twenty is a useful laboratory of choice rather than error correction. The harmony is D dominant seventh. In our deliberately less focused draft, the last melody note is F natural. In the revision, that note becomes F sharp. The F natural creates a chromatic color against a context in which the chord's third is F sharp. The revision aligns the melody more straightforwardly with the harmonic third. It is tempting to call the revised note correct and the draft note wrong. Resist that temptation. A blues-inflected or chromatically expressive passage might benefit from the F natural. We are choosing the F sharp because this particular arrangement aims to make the G-oriented passage more legible, not because tonal music bans competing thirds.

For an agent composing by rules, this is an important lesson. A pitch-class compatibility checker can tell you that F sharp belongs to the chosen D-seventh chord. It cannot tell you whether the original F natural was a purposeful friction, a mistake, or the seed of a different style. The next question should be about intention and context, not merely membership in a chord list.

## When the original comes back, has anything changed?

Bars twenty-five through thirty-two return to the opening harmonic path, and the four-note head in bar twenty-five exactly matches bar one. A naive system might report that the music has simply repeated. But when we hear something again, it follows an experience we could not have had the first time. We have heard the motif over A minor and in the shifted pitch area at bar seventeen. The return may therefore feel less like new information and more like a recognition. That is a perceptual interpretation informed by research on repetition, not an invariant of the score that a program can certify.

The return also differs in its continuation. Bar twenty-six uses C, B, A, G, while the early corresponding passage used A, C, B, A. Bar twenty-seven rises toward D before returning toward C. Later, bars twenty-nine through thirty-two move through figures that bring the ear back toward the opening pitch range. We did not copy eight bars and paste them unchanged. We chose a precise recurring head and allowed other details to adapt.

This is the opposite of treating call-and-response as an automatic universal solution. The relation here is memory and reinterpretation, not two characters taking turns talking. In other contexts, simultaneous counterpoint, an ostinato, an uninterrupted process, or a deliberately contrasting section could do the necessary work. Form is not a small menu of templates to cycle through. It is the way a piece makes its own events matter.

## Why the first full draft sounds crowded

We now have forty bars of melody, including a short coda from bar thirty-three through forty. We add a bass that places a chord root on the first two beats of each bar and its fifth on the next two beats. Two quiet synthetic inner voices hold related chord tones through the bar. There is no claim that such a bass pattern is the best possible bass line, nor that the inner voices are already a sensible chamber-orchestra score. This is a deliberately simple test bed.

In the first full draft, the inner voices are present in all forty bars. The result can sound continuous, but it has a structural cost: if everything is filled with similar sustained support throughout the work, the contrast between sections may depend too heavily on melody and harmony. This is not necessarily auditory masking in the strict experimental sense. We would need to examine levels, spectra, and listener reports to establish that. It is a compositional concern about attention and change in texture.

Stephen McAdams, Meghan Goodchild, and Kit Soden provide a useful research framework for orchestral grouping. Their account distinguishes concurrent blending, separated auditory streams, and segmental contrasts that help mark boundaries. Their 2022 *Music Theory Online* article is not an evaluation of our oscillator composition. It helps us formulate a hypothesis: changing the grouping and density of our supporting voices might help listeners distinguish sections without inventing a new melody every eight bars.

Notice that the first draft already has a complete beginning and ending. Completeness on a timeline is not the same as having made every important musical decision. An algorithm can correctly fill forty bars and still create an undifferentiated surface. Conversely, a piece built from a single nearly static sound may be artistically effective if that is its actual aim. We must judge our draft against its intended relationships, not against a compulsory texture-variation quota.

## Revision one: remove before adding

For the first revision, we do not change the melody at all. We change the supporting texture. In each eight-bar section, the sustained chord voices play only in its first four bars. They are absent during the next four. This means the original forty continuously padded bars become twenty padded bars. During selected later bars—six and seven, fourteen and fifteen, twenty-two and twenty-three, thirty and thirty-one—we introduce a quiet secondary figure made from chord-related pitches. Each figure has four short events, for thirty-two additional note events in the entire piece.

The numbers are not editorial targets. They are verifiable consequences of this particular score. More importantly, the revisions isolate a question. We can compare the dense draft with a texture-only revision in which the lead melody remains exactly the same. If the revised version now marks its section endings more clearly, we have reason to examine whether the orchestration is helping. If the listener hears a hole rather than relief, we may have thinned the texture at the wrong moment. The code can certify the changes; listening must assess them.

We have also resisted the habit of solving every issue by introducing a new answering phrase. The secondary figures occupy space left by the pads, but they do not function as a constant call-and-response partner for the melody. They are a local choice in a few particular bars. When the texture changes, the same melody may acquire a different role. That makes orchestration a compositional tool rather than merely the assignment of instruments after the score is finished.

## Revision two: change precisely two notes

Only after isolating the texture do we make two melodic edits. The first is at the final beat of bar twenty: F natural becomes F sharp over D dominant seventh. We discussed the reason. The second is at the final beat of bar forty, the last bar of the piece: G becomes C in the top voice. The bass and harmony in that last bar remain C based. The revision chooses a final melodic tonic rather than the fifth of the tonic chord.

Be careful about the word cadence. The arrival from the G-seventh bar thirty-nine into the C chord at bar forty offers a strong tonal boundary. In our score the bass starts bar forty on C, but the preceding bass has moved from G to D halfway through bar thirty-nine, and the final upper-voice C occurs later in bar forty. So we are not presenting the final event as a formal proof of a perfect authentic cadence by the most restrictive Classical definitions. We are testing a much narrower claim: when the rest of the notation is fixed, does the last sustained impression seem more finished if its final upper pitch is C rather than G?

A listener who prefers the fifth may have good reasons. G can produce a sense of openness, which another piece might need. The revised note is a design decision motivated by this study's intention to finish decisively; it is not a universal rule about the last pitch of music.

This distinction is useful for software evaluation. A model can inspect a diff between two event lists and discover that exactly two pitches changed. It can then compare the stated intention with the revised result. It cannot honestly report that a revision is emotionally more satisfying without listening evidence or an explicitly limited perceptual model. The best annotation for a compositional edit has three parts: the exact change, its intended function, and the observation that would count for or against that intention.

## Revision three: change the mix without changing the work

Our sixth rendering is not a sixth composition. It uses exactly the same note events as the revised score. Only fixed gains and panning positions differ. The lead has less gain; the sustained inner voices have more. The result may draw attention away from the melody or increase the sense of a blended chordal layer. But since gain affects perceived loudness as well as balance, it would be careless to declare one rendering superior after a quick louder-versus-quieter comparison.

The score checker proves that the musical events agree between these two renderings. It also proves that some gain and pan values differ. It does not prove that every audible difference is perceptually significant, or that the rendered mixes are matched in loudness. They are intentionally not auto-normalized in the example renderer. To evaluate them, first listen as produced. Then level-match them using the monitoring tools in a DAW and listen again. The difference between those comparisons is itself instructive.

The Ardour manual documents automation of track volume, trim, panning, and processor parameters. It also documents importing existing audio, routing through buses, and exporting individual stems. Those are engineering affordances. They allow you to turn a tentative judgment about the balance into an editable, documented session, rather than just writing down that a mix needs more clarity. They do not decide musical intent for you.

DawDreamer provides another route. Its documented rendering engine creates processing graphs, hosts instruments and effects, and captures rendered audio. For this example we deliberately use a smaller standard-library renderer, so the note-event results do not depend on a particular VST installation. An optional next step would replace the elementary oscillators with a fully specified DawDreamer instrument graph. To keep that comparison reproducible, record the synth identity, exact plugin version and state, sample rate, processing graph, gain settings, and output hashes. Where plugins are nondeterministic, record that limitation rather than pretending the hash will be identical on every machine.

## What a controlled comparison actually controls

Imagine evaluating four versions: dense draft, texture-only revision, revised score, and alternate mix. If you play them in that sequence, you know which one you are hearing and may bring your expectations to the task. If you compare them blind but let one play much louder, level becomes a confounding variable. If the sample instruments have different patches or reverberation between conditions, you have introduced another change. We should choose the comparison to match the question.

To study orchestration, compare the dense draft with the texture-only version. Their melody notes are identical, but their inner voices differ. To study the two pitch choices, compare the texture-only version with the revised score. Their timing and supporting parts are identical; two melody events differ. To study balance, compare the revised score with the alternate mix. Their note events are identical; the gain and panning assignments differ. The structure of the experiment now has an honest relationship to the conclusion we might draw.

It is legitimate to explore a more natural artistic comparison in which many decisions change at once. But such a comparison supports a judgment about the overall result, not a causal claim about one specific edit. If a new arrangement sounds better after you change the notes, the synthesis, the mix, and the tempo simultaneously, you have not learned which change mattered. The need to distinguish composition from mixing is therefore not an aesthetic pedantry. It is how we avoid pretending that our preference has a more precise explanation than the experiment provides.

This is also a helpful way to organize music-generation agents. One agent might propose formal and harmonic plans. Another might generate MIDI-like note events. Another might propose orchestration. Another could render sound in DawDreamer. A mixing agent could adjust a documented signal graph in Ardour. Having independent tracks does not guarantee the final musical result, but it gives us separate objects to inspect and revise. Every agent should be able to point to its own changes. No agent should take credit for an improvement that was produced by another stage without evidence.

## Read the whole piece, not just its parts

Let us return to *The Returning Line* from beginning to end. The first eight bars present a small ascending figure and a familiar harmonic floor. The second group retains the figure but changes that floor toward A minor. The third group transposes the head upward and visits a G-oriented region, without granting the exact cadence a rule-based agent might expect. The fourth group brings the original notes home and changes what happens around them. The final group strips back the texture, moves through a simple harmonic path, and closes with a C above a C-based harmony.

This is one complete-work analysis because we can account for the relationships across its entire timeline. Every section has a stated purpose, every major return corresponds to actual note events, and the examples can be rendered and compared. That is more substantial than producing a sequence of plausible eight-bar fragments and giving each one a descriptive name afterward.

But do not confuse accountability with inevitability. A different composer could replace the contrasting G section with an ostinato, eliminate the harmonic change, use two simultaneous lines rather than a solo melody, or cut the entire final coda. Those choices might yield a better piece. Our case study does not prove its form. It demonstrates how a form can be stated precisely, revised coherently, and investigated honestly.

A final exercise makes this lesson practical. Listen to the forty bars and decide which moment you would alter first. Name the bar, the voice, and the intended musical consequence. Then choose the least invasive test: change only that note, remove only that layer, move only that gain control, or change only the timing of one entrance. Render the two versions under stated conditions and listen again. If the effect is not audible or does not serve the intended purpose, restore the original and revise the hypothesis, not just the score. Composition becomes more disciplined when every revision is capable of teaching us something about the piece.

A finished musical work is not a motif plus a fixed number of repetitions, a completed bar counter, or a successful export from a DAW. It is a set of relationships that a listener can encounter in time. We may formalize some of those relationships, render many alternatives, measure signal differences, and maintain excellent provenance. The final task remains musical: hear what the whole thing does, decide what you intended it to do, and make the next change for a reason.
