# Music as Pattern in Time: A Listener's Way into Composition

Imagine that someone taps three times on a table, waits, and taps once more. Try to hold that small event in your memory. Three taps, a pause, and one tap. If the person immediately repeats it, you may recognize a pattern. If the last tap arrives earlier than expected, you may notice the difference, even without knowing a single musical term. The person has given you something to anticipate, and then either satisfied or altered that anticipation.

We can begin studying music there, without a keyboard or a staff. Music is not simply sound arranged pleasantly. Noise can be arranged, silence can be part of an arrangement, and a sound some listeners regard as harsh can be exactly what a composer intends. What matters for our first investigation is that sound events acquire relationships in time. One event can prepare another, answer it, interrupt it, or make it seem inevitable. A listener remembers part of what happened and develops expectations about what might happen next.

That description is broad, not a universal definition of music. Different traditions organize performance around different priorities: a dance pattern, a melodic mode, a repeating cycle, a relationship between soloist and group, or timbres that change more than pitches do. Some music avoids a regular pulse. Some foregrounds a stable drone; some makes changing texture the principal event. Rather than force all of it into one system, we will learn a set of questions we can actually use. What is happening? What stays recognizable? What changes? Where do we feel a beginning, continuation, interruption, and arrival? These questions will also help us compose.

## Four properties of an event

Return to a single tap. It has a time at which it begins. It lasts for some duration, even if that duration is short. It has a level of loudness, and it has a character of sound. A finger on glass, a pencil on wood, and a hand on a drum do not sound identical. Musicians call that distinguishing character timbre, pronounced tamber. Timbre depends on how a sound starts, which frequencies it contains, how they change, and how it decays. We often recognize a familiar voice from timbre even when the speaker uses the same pitch as somebody else.

A sound may also have a discernible pitch: a sense of highness or lowness that allows us to sing or imitate it. A sustained whistle usually offers a fairly clear pitch. A finger snap has a less definite pitch, though its sound still has frequencies. Pitch and timbre are different questions. Two people can sing the same note on the same vowel, but sound like two different people. A flute and a violin can play the same notated pitch while retaining distinct sound qualities.

Now try an experiment. Say the syllable 'da' four times at a comfortable rate. Keep approximately the same pitch and loudness. Next, say the third 'da' more loudly. You have changed the dynamics, or the shaping of loudness. Then return to equal loudness but lift the third 'da' to a higher pitch. You have changed the melodic contour. Finally, keep all four pitches the same but delay the third entrance. You have changed the rhythm. Each adjustment changes the identity of the pattern in a different way.

A composer can choose among these parameters. That is why a piece need not contain many notes to be interesting. One pitch with a changing rhythm can command attention. A repeated rhythm played by changing instruments can become a study of timbre. A slowly sustained note that grows louder may feel like an approach, even though the pitch has not moved. Calling one of these features 'the melody' and dismissing the rest would erase much of the composer's work.

## Memory creates musical relationships

Listen in imagination to our opening pattern again: tap, tap, tap, silence, tap. Suppose you hear it four times. After the second repetition, you may be able to predict where the final tap belongs. The pause is no longer empty. It has become a place in the pattern, a duration in which something is expected but withheld.

Now change the pattern. Instead of a final single tap, end with two quick taps. You may hear the change as an answer or a playful complication because a more familiar alternative was already in mind. If you had heard the changed version first, it might not seem like a change at all. This is a central part of musical form: the meaning of an event depends on what precedes it.

A short recognizable unit is often called a motive or motif. In one style it might be a sequence of pitches. In another it might be a drum pattern, a phrase of words, or a particular sound gesture. Not every memorable sound must be called a motif. The point is practical: when you create a small musical idea, you gain material that can recur and change. This is more manageable than deciding what an entire five-minute piece should sound like before you have written its first ten seconds.

Consider a spoken motif consisting of four syllables: 'da, da, dee, da.' Give the third syllable a higher pitch and the last syllable a longer duration. The phrase now has an outline: an opening, a lift, and a place to land. Say it again, but make the second syllable higher instead. It still resembles the first version because most of its features remain. You have made a variation, not an entirely separate idea. Then invert the outline so that the syllable that previously rose now falls. Some listeners may hear a more distant family resemblance; others may hear a new idea. The transformation is audible, and the judgment is worth exploring.

Repetition without any change can be powerful, especially when it invites movement or creates a stable environment for another layer. But it can also stop giving the listener new information. Continuous change can be exciting, but it can prevent recognition. The composer is therefore making decisions about balance, not following a law that says every third measure must be different. The useful question is whether repeated material still has a reason to be there.

## The listener does not hear separate columns

A score can separate pitch, duration, loudness, instrumentation, and harmony. Our ears receive an event in which all of those features happen together. Imagine that a singer repeats the same four notes, first quietly and then with a much stronger attack. Although the pitch sequence is identical, the second version may sound like a protest rather than a recollection. The notes are not the whole musical statement.

Tempo, the rate at which an underlying pulse moves, changes the result too. Repeat the words 'please come home' slowly, with a small pause before 'home.' Then speak the same words quickly with three equally spaced attacks. The language has not changed, but the time structure changes how the phrase is heard. A composer working with words must decide whether the musical accent reinforces a word's natural stress or deliberately rubs against it. A composer without words still shapes attention through accents and duration.

Texture introduces another choice: how many distinguishable musical parts are active, and how do they relate? A single voice on its own is one kind of texture. That voice over a continuous low tone is another. Two independent melodies moving at once create a different problem from a melody supported by a group of chords. Dense texture is not automatically more expressive. A single sound after a crowded passage can become more striking because of the contrast.

Imagine a room with three people. One holds a long 'ah' on a steady pitch. A second repeats our four-syllable motif. A third taps a regular pulse. No one needs advanced technique. Yet there are already three interacting layers: a sustained background, a foreground shape, and a time reference. Remove the tapping and the same motif may seem to float. Remove the drone and the singer may sound more solitary. Reverse which part is loudest and your attention shifts. Instrumentation and mixing make decisions about relationships, not just decoration.

## Form begins earlier than a verse or chorus

People sometimes encounter musical form as a list of labels: verse, chorus, binary, ternary, sonata. Those are useful descriptions in appropriate repertoires, but a form begins before we name a section. If a gesture occurs, is repeated, and then contrasts with a new gesture, we can already discuss form. We can call the first idea A, the second idea B, and describe an arrangement A, A, B, A. The letters say only which material returns; they do not specify the style or the emotional result.

Construct a very small A section by speaking 'da, da, dee, da' twice. Construct B by sustaining 'ah' and then stopping abruptly. Return to A. The whole piece might last ten seconds, but its shape is understandable: establish a pattern, depart from it, return. Try the same piece with B first. Now the listener receives the long sound before knowing what it contrasts with. The musical facts are nearly identical, but their order changes the experience.

Contrast need not mean complete opposition. You can keep the rhythm and change the pitch direction. You can preserve the pitch contour but alter the sound quality. You can shorten a four-event motif into two events, making it seem as if the speaker has become impatient. A composer who knows which parameter has changed can revise with purpose. 'Make this more interesting' is a vague request; 'keep the rhythm but gradually widen the melodic leaps' is a testable operation.

It is equally important not to assume that every piece tells a dramatic story of tension and final resolution. Some music is designed to circulate, sustain a ritual, support social dancing, or explore a slowly changing field of sound. Returning to the beginning may be the intended effect. Our vocabulary of expectation and contrast helps us listen, but does not entitle us to declare one tradition more complete because it ends with a strong final chord.

## The difference between writing and hearing

A plan on paper is not yet a musical experience. Suppose you write 'repeat four times, get louder, pause, and finish.' That instruction can describe many performances. To know what you have composed, you need to sing, tap, play, program, or otherwise hear an approximation. Even a clumsy demonstration reveals things the abstract plan concealed. You discover that the final silence is too short, that the louder repetition obscures the rhythm, or that a repeated syllable sounds comic when you wanted solemnity.

A useful working loop is small. Invent one gesture. Perform it. Describe what you actually heard without defending your original intention. Change one parameter. Perform it again. Keep the version that produces the relationship you want. This loop resembles editing prose, but musical time adds an extra constraint: the listener does not usually stop the performance to inspect a sentence. An idea must be available to be heard at speed, perhaps through repetition or a clear change of context.

That does not require you to make everything predictable. It does ask you to recognize the difference between productive surprise and a listener's inability to locate the pattern at all. A piece can deliberately refuse an expected ending after the expectation has been established. It is harder to meaningfully refuse something the listener never had a chance to expect.

## A complete first composition

We can make a miniature without an instrument. Choose a comfortable pulse, roughly the pace of a relaxed walk. Count four pulses out loud: one, two, three, four. On the first two pulses say 'da.' On the third say 'dee' at a slightly higher pitch. On the fourth, return to 'da.' That is A. Repeat A, keeping the pattern recognizable.

For B, stop speaking the four-syllable rhythm. Hold one 'ah' across the first two pulses, stay silent across the third, and make a single short 'ah' on the fourth. Give that last sound the higher pitch from A. Now return to A once. You have composed four groups of four pulses: A, A, B, A. Perform it without announcing the letters. If another person listens, ask them where they heard a repeated idea and where they heard a change. Their answer tells you more than asking whether they liked it.

Revise the miniature in three deliberate ways. First, leave the pitches alone and move the short sound in B from the fourth pulse to halfway between the third and fourth pulses. You will need to feel the pulse continuing beneath the sound. Second, restore the original rhythm but change the final A by making its last note longer, borrowing time from the silence after it. Third, try leaving that last note out altogether. Compare how the endings differ: arrival, extension, and withholding.

There is no single correct ending. But each choice now has a reason you can articulate. You are using duration, pitch, repetition, contrast, and silence to shape an experience through time. Notation will later help preserve and share such ideas; it is not what makes them musical. The essential starting skill is to make a choice, hear its consequence, and know what you would change next.

## Listening and composition companion (display only)

### The miniature, written as events rather than staff notation

| Four-pulse group | Pulse 1 | Pulse 2 | Pulse 3 | Pulse 4 | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | da, low | da, low | dee, higher | da, low | Establish a recognizable contour |
| A | da, low | da, low | dee, higher | da, low | Confirm the pattern |
| B | ah, sustained | sustain | silence | ah, higher, short | Create contrast |
| A | da, low | da, low | dee, higher | da, low | Return |

In B, the sustained `ah` occupies pulses 1 and 2; it is not restarted on pulse 2. Any pitch is acceptable as long as the higher event is clearly distinguishable.

### Checks for listening

1. Can another listener identify which group returned without being told the labels?
2. Can you change **only** rhythm, **only** pitch contour, and **only** timbre on three separate takes?
3. Does a pause feel like part of a pattern after repetition? Compare it with a pause presented before the pattern has been heard.
4. Design an A–B–A form for two contrasting everyday sounds; describe the listening effect before naming any emotion.
