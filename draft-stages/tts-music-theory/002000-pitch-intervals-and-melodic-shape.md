# Pitch, Intervals, and the Shape of a Melody

Imagine calling a friend across a quiet garden. You say their name in an ordinary speaking voice. No answer. You call again, now beginning a little higher and letting the last syllable rise. The words are the same, but the sound has changed its direction. We can hear a question, urgency, or a signal meant to carry farther. We have already made an expressive pitch choice without reading notes or naming a scale.

Melody is not simply a string of frequencies. A listener hears movement: up, down, staying level, arriving at a place that feels familiar, or leaving that place behind. The same four pitch names can produce different impressions depending on their rhythm, register, emphasis, and surroundings. Our practical task is to learn how to describe pitch relationships precisely enough to make them on purpose, while remembering that a name is not the experience itself.

## Pitch is a perceptual property

Consider a whistle with a steady, clear tone. If the vibration rate increases, its perceived pitch ordinarily rises. For a simple pure tone, doubling the frequency produces a pitch one octave above. A tone vibrating at two hundred cycles each second, followed by a tone at four hundred cycles each second, illustrates that physical ratio. The ear often hears the two tones as closely related even though the second is distinctly higher. This relationship is called octave equivalence in many musical settings.

Real instruments are more complicated than single-frequency whistles. Their sounds contain additional frequency components, and their pitch can be ambiguous or even change as a note unfolds. A bell may generate several prominent partials that are not simple integer multiples of a single fundamental. A drum strike can be very definite rhythmically without suggesting an equally definite pitch. A singer can bend a pitch rather than occupying one fixed frequency. These facts matter because musical pitch is something we hear, not simply a number read from a machine.

Register tells us roughly where in the high-to-low range a pitch sits. A high melody played by a piccolo may feel different from the same intervals sung in a low voice. Transposition moves a melody into another register or key while preserving its internal relationships. Imagine climbing three steps on a staircase, then stepping down one. You could do this near the ground floor or near the roof. The movement pattern remains recognizable even though the absolute height changes.

That distinction between absolute height and relative movement is central to composing. You do not need perfect pitch, the ability to name a note without an external reference, in order to create or understand a melody. Relative pitch is the skill of hearing relationships between successive or simultaneous notes. You can train it by singing an answer to a starting note, by checking it on an instrument, and by noticing the shape of familiar phrases without needing to know their absolute key.

## An interval is a relationship, not a piano key

The distance between two pitches is called an interval. If the second pitch is higher, we hear an ascending interval. If lower, a descending interval. If the two notes occur one after the other, the interval is melodic; if they sound together, it is harmonic. This distinction concerns timing, not the names of the notes. The same two pitches can form an upward melodic leap, a downward leap, or a simultaneous sonority.

One way to describe an interval is by the number of steps in a particular scale system. A second connects neighboring letter names, such as C and D. A third spans C through E when we count C as one, D as two, and E as three. A fifth spans C through G. These numbers are conventions of a diatonic naming system; they do not themselves tell us the exact number of equally spaced semitones. A major third and a minor third are both thirds in letter-name distance, yet they have different pitch sizes.

We will use the twelve-note equal-tempered system common in much contemporary Western music as a practical example. An octave is divided into twelve equal steps on a logarithmic frequency scale. Each step is called a semitone, or half step. On a piano, a semitone is usually the distance to the nearest key immediately to the left or right, including black keys. Two semitones make a whole step. This is a choice of tuning system, not a universal inventory of pitches. Other traditions use other pitch organizations, and even music written with Western note names may use unequal tuning or flexible intonation.

On the white keys of a piano, the repeating letter names are C, D, E, F, G, A, and B, then C again. Notice that E to F is a semitone, as is B to C. Most other neighboring white-key pairs are whole steps apart. A black key between C and D is commonly named C sharp when understood as a raised C, or D flat when understood as a lowered D. In ordinary twelve-tone equal temperament these can refer to the same key and sounding frequency. But the spellings can express different harmonic roles, and in other tuning systems the pitches need not coincide exactly.

Try building intervals from C. The step from C to D contains two semitones and is a major second. C to E contains four semitones and is a major third. C to E flat contains three and is a minor third. C to F contains five and is a perfect fourth. C to G contains seven and is a perfect fifth. C to the next C contains twelve and is an octave. You need not memorize the entire table immediately. Begin by checking the difference between a step, a small leap, a wider leap, and the octave that connects a pitch to its higher counterpart.

There is a subtle point here. If you count only piano keys, C to D sharp also contains three semitones, just like C to E flat in equal temperament. Yet the written interval C to D sharp is an augmented second, while C to E flat is a minor third. Their sounds coincide in that tuning, but their names capture different notation and voice-leading contexts. For a new composer, this means two useful descriptions coexist: how far apart the pitches sound, and how they are spelled within a musical grammar.

## Shape before note names

Now invent a melody of five events. The first stays low. The second goes one step higher. The third rises by another step. The fourth leaps higher. The fifth returns to the first pitch. Hear the outline: level only at the starting point, then a modest climb, a larger rise, and a return. Without specific note names, you could sing this in a comfortable range. The gesture already has a character because of its shape and the suddenness of the last return.

Let's give it concrete notes: C, D, E, G, C. To keep the register unambiguous, place the final C below the starting C, or choose the same C as the first note; these make quite different endings. For our first version, use the starting C at both ends. C up to D, up to E, up to G, then down to the original C. The last move is downward by a perfect fifth, since G down to that C spans seven semitones. Say the names slowly while imagining them played: C, D, E, G, C.

This melody contains two neighboring steps, a leap from E to G, and a larger downward leap. If you change only its final pitch from C to D, it stops one step above its starting note. It now leaves a different kind of question. If you change the G to F, the highest point is lower and the final drop is narrower. If you repeat the G instead of descending, you remove the return entirely. These are small local changes, but they alter the relationships that make the line recognizable.

A phrase also has a rhythm. If you sing those five notes as five equal short sounds, the melody may feel like a list. Suppose instead you give the starting C a longer duration, shorten D and E, let G be brief, and hold the final C. Now the outer notes frame a quicker journey. If you emphasize the G, the high point becomes a destination. If you make the G quiet and the final C loud, the return may become the focus. A pitch outline is raw material; performance and timing reveal its use.

## Scale, tonic, and the temptation of a universal rule

A scale is an ordered collection of pitches, often presented across an octave. One familiar example is the C major scale: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C. Its pattern of steps is whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. You can verify that pattern with a piano. It explains why the white keys from C to C form this scale in twelve-tone equal temperament.

But the same set of note names does not force a piece to be in C major. Which pitch feels like a center depends on musical context: repeated arrivals, emphasized notes, accompaniment, cadences, and style. The notes A, B, C, D, E, F, G can belong to A natural minor if A acts as their point of reference. A composer can also use the white keys without establishing a clear tonal center at all. Calling one scale 'happy' and another 'sad' will not teach you how they actually work. Associations of mood depend on tempo, register, culture, instrumentation, and what surrounds the notes.

A scale can offer a working palette, but it is not a fence. Chromatic notes can approach a goal from a semitone away. A melody can bend between nominal pitches. In styles organized around a mode, a characteristic interval or melodic turn may matter more than the major-minor label. In a raga-based practice, the permitted paths, ornaments, and emphasized tones may be part of the identity; a bare list of seven notes would not capture it. We should avoid translating every musical system into the same collection-of-white-keys exercise.

## Motive, sequence, and transposition

Return to the little three-note beginning C, D, E. Its successive movements are up a whole step and up another whole step. Call that the motive. Now begin at F and make the same pattern of whole-step movements: F, G, A. You have transposed the motive. The absolute pitch names changed, but its interval structure did not. It may still be recognizable, especially if the rhythm is the same.

Next try a diatonic sequence instead. Within C major, move the shape one scale step higher: D, E, F. That contains a whole step followed by a half step. It imitates the scale-step pattern but does not preserve the exact semitone distances. Both techniques are useful and both may be called sequences in different analytical contexts. Knowing which kind of resemblance you intend lets you control the result rather than hearing a discrepancy as a mistake.

You can also invert a shape, meaning that an upward movement becomes a corresponding downward movement around a chosen reference. From C, a simple exact inversion of C, D, E in semitone distance is C, B flat, A flat. The pitches descend by two semitones and then two more. That is not identical to merely walking downward through the C major scale, which would give C, B, A, with a one-semitone move followed by two. The first preserves chromatic distances; the second preserves the idea of successive scale steps. There is no need to choose one permanently. The distinction gives you two different compositional tools.

Another transformation is to keep the notes but reverse the order, a procedure called retrograde in some compositional traditions. E, D, C is a reversal of C, D, E. Retrograde of a longer melody may produce new leaps and phrasing. Many listeners will not recognize a complex retrograde immediately, so do not assume that a mathematically exact transformation guarantees an audible relationship. Composition is where formal operations meet perception.

## Voice leading: melodies can coexist

Even if you plan eventually to write chords, think of them as collections of moving voices rather than blocks stamped onto a timeline. A second melody beneath the first may move in the same direction, in the opposite direction, or remain still. When both lines climb at once, they can sound like a single rising gesture. When one line climbs and the other descends, the space between them changes. When one sustains a note while the other moves, the steady voice becomes a reference.

Try holding C in a lower voice while another voice sings C, D, E, G, C above it. The melody's notes take on changing relationships to the fixed C. C feels like a shared starting point; D rubs against it as a second; E forms a third; G forms a fifth. Whether these are heard as tension or rest depends on the musical situation, but now you can hear how a stable accompaniment changes the role of a moving line. You have taken a first step from melody into harmony without needing a catalog of chords.

## Build a melody you can revise

Make a phrase of four events. Start on any pitch you can sing. Move up one step, then leap up a third, then return downward to your first pitch. Hold the final event twice as long as the others. Repeat it. On the second repetition, keep the duration pattern but replace the leap with one smaller step. Ask whether the second phrase sounds like a quieter answer or just a different tune. It may be either; what matters is that you can identify what changed.

Now write the phrase as both a contour and a note list. For example, C, D, F, C has an upward step, an upward minor third from D to F, and a downward perfect fourth from F to C. Change F to E and the upper leap from D becomes a whole step; the total range narrows from a fourth to a third above C. If you can hear or sing the difference, the names have done their job. If you cannot yet hear it, use a keyboard or a tone generator and compare slowly. The goal is not a theory exam. The goal is to acquire choices you can recognize by ear.

Finish by transposing your phrase to begin on G. Preserve the semitone distances: G, A, C, G for the original C, D, F, C figure. Perform both in the same rhythm. The higher version may sit differently in your voice, but its structural identity survives. That is the composer's freedom: a melody can be moved, shortened, inverted, sequenced, answered, or placed against a held tone, while retaining enough of its shape to create continuity.

Names, semitones, and interval qualities are a map. Singing and listening tell you what the map is for. A melody is not compelling because it contains a perfect fifth or obeys a scale. It becomes purposeful when its movements establish a relationship that another movement can confirm, modify, or contradict. Start with a short line you can remember; then make the smallest change that lets it say something new.

### One motive, multiple operations

- **Motive:** `C4 D4 E4` (two whole steps rising).
- **Chromatic transposition up a perfect fourth:** `F4 G4 A4`.
- **Diatonic stepwise sequence from D in C major:** `D4 E4 F4` (whole step, then half step).
- **Chromatic inversion about C4:** `C4 B♭3 A♭3` (two whole steps falling).
- **Retrograde:** `E4 D4 C4`.

These are not claims of stylistic equivalence. They are compositional operations to compare by ear.

### Exercises

1. Sing or play `C4 D4 F4 C4`, then change just the third pitch to E4. Identify the changed intervals.
2. Hold C while singing a D, E, and G above it. Describe each sound without relying on 'good' or 'bad'.
3. Write a four-note phrase with a clear highest point. Move that high point to a different event; hold the rhythm fixed.
4. Try equal-tempered pitches and a freely bent note between two of them. Describe which aspects of the melody remain recognizable.
