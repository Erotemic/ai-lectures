# What EQ and Compression Actually Do: Spectrum, Level, and Dynamics

A mixer is listening to a four-part arrangement. The bass and the kick drum sound enormous in isolation, the guitar fills the room, and the lead line is beautifully shaped. Put them together, and the kick loses definition. The guitar seems to flatten the lead. The lead can be turned up, but then every bright note is piercing. The engineer opens an equalizer on one track and a compressor on another. What exactly are those tools doing to the samples? And why is their usefulness so dependent on the other parts that happen to be sounding?

Start with a simple volume control. If an audio sample is a number, a constant gain multiplies it. A gain of one leaves the value unchanged. A gain of one-half divides its amplitude by two. In the decibel language used on faders, gain is measured logarithmically. A drop of approximately six decibels corresponds to half the signal amplitude; a rise of six decibels corresponds to almost twice the amplitude. The formula uses twenty times the base-ten logarithm of the amplitude ratio. That factor of twenty matters because we are talking about a signal amplitude, not a power ratio.

Suppose two tracks carry exactly the same waveform, aligned and in phase, each peaking at an amplitude of point four. If we simply sum them, they make a waveform peaking at point eight: approximately six decibels higher than either individual copy. If we invert the polarity of one copy and add it to the other, they cancel exactly in ideal arithmetic. For two unrelated waveforms, the peak of the sum is not reliably three or six decibels higher. Their correlations and instantaneous phases matter. A frequent rule of thumb says uncorrelated signals increase average power by about three decibels when their powers are equal, but that is not a prediction of the combined sample peak. It is important not to mix those two measurements.

Volume alone cannot reshape the balance within a sound. If a guitar has a powerful low-midrange resonance and a delicate upper overtone structure, turning the whole guitar down reduces both together. Equalization is a way to apply different gains to different portions of its spectrum, though the precise filter also changes phase and time behavior. A common parametric equalizer provides a center frequency, gain and bandwidth or quality factor. A broad, gentle reduction centered in the lower midrange can lessen one region without removing the entire sound. A narrow reduction can target a ringing resonance, but it can also leave a strange hole or create phase behavior that matters in combination with nearby microphones.

An equalizer is not a musical understanding engine. It does not know that a particular frequency belongs to the bass player or the guitar player. It receives a signal and transforms it according to filter coefficients. One simple digital filter takes the current input sample and a weighted contribution from a previous output sample. More elaborate filters keep several delayed input and output values. Their frequency response describes how much they attenuate or amplify sinusoidal components at different frequencies. Their phase response describes how timing relationships among those components change. A fixed set of coefficients can implement useful low-pass, high-pass or peaking shapes, but the equation alone does not tell us where to put the filter in a composition.

Why does a guitar sometimes mask a lead? Suppose the guitar strums densely through the same register where a soft lead begins. The two signals may share energy in frequency regions and moments that the auditory system relies on to distinguish events. Raising the lead's gain can help, but so can leaving a gap in the guitar rhythm, changing its voicing, moving its register, shortening its sustain, or reducing part of its spectrum. In a score-based production system those arrangement changes are sometimes cheap to make. If the agent still has the MIDI or note plan, a two-beat rest may be a better fix than a complicated equalizer preset. If it has only a rendered guitar file, the options are narrower.

Consider a hypothetical four-stem session. The kick strikes at the beginning of each bar. A bass note sustains across the first two beats. The guitar strums on beats two and four. The melody enters on beat three. If the kick transient and the beginning of the bass note are simultaneous, their low-frequency energy can stack and their waveforms can partly reinforce or cancel. The problem is not solved by selecting a universal frequency at which every kick must be louder than every bass. The right move depends on the actual sounds. Perhaps the kick has a short low-frequency thump, and the bass can start a few milliseconds later without damaging the groove. Perhaps its pitch contour calls for overlap. Perhaps both are needed, but a small spectral adjustment makes their roles clearer. The mixer must listen to the passage, not merely inspect four isolated waveforms.

Compression introduces another level of behavior. Unlike a fixed gain, a compressor changes gain as a function of the signal it detects over time. Picture two parallel stories: the audio path carries what we will hear, and the detector path estimates something about its level. When the detected level exceeds a threshold, the controller computes gain reduction. In a conventional downward compressor, a ratio of four to one means that, in the idealized steady-state characteristic above threshold, an input change of four decibels produces an output change of one decibel. That does not mean the output waveform is divided by four. It is a relationship among level changes above a threshold.

Let's work one calculation carefully. Imagine a stable signal that the detector measures at minus six decibels relative to full scale. Choose a threshold of minus eighteen decibels and a four-to-one ratio. The input is twelve decibels above threshold. In the ideal hard-knee static response, the output is only three decibels above threshold, which is minus fifteen decibels. Thus the compressor applies nine decibels of gain reduction before any makeup gain. If the signal was minus twenty-four decibels and stayed below threshold, that simple model would not reduce it. But real detectors average or peak-detect audio, respond over time, and may have a soft knee, so this arithmetic is a guide to the control curve, not a promise about every individual sample.

The attack control determines how rapidly the gain reduction develops after the detector asks for it. The release determines how it relaxes. A very short attack may clamp a drum's initial strike; a slower attack may allow the transient through before compressing the sustain. A fast release can make level recover between beats, or cause audible pumping if it follows the wrong envelope. A long release can remain active while a new musical event arrives. These are time-domain choices, not cosmetic knobs. An envelope follower is itself a kind of filter: it smooths a rapidly changing measurement to make a more slowly varying control signal.

Imagine the compressor at the end of the bass track. It responds primarily to what the bass is doing, depending on its detector settings. Now put the compressor on the *whole mix*. The kick can push its detector above threshold and cause the compressor to lower the guitar, lead and bass together, even when those parts were not especially loud. That can sound like musical cohesion, or it can sound as if the arrangement inhales whenever the kick hits. Neither effect is inherent to all compressors: it depends on the threshold, detector behavior, attack, release and how the signals are routed.

A sidechain makes the detector listen to a different signal. In Ardour, a compatible compressor can receive a sidechain signal from another route. For example, the bass processor can follow the kick drum even though the bass is the sound being attenuated. A brief, controlled reduction of bass level during the kick may improve clarity. But automatic ducking is not always beneficial, and too much movement can alter the intended musical relationship. If the kick is silent for several bars, the ducking disappears; if the kick changes rhythm, the bass pumping changes with it. The dependency is musically consequential and also appears in the processing graph: a sidechain route creates a timing dependency on another track.

An equalizer can also precede the sidechain detector. Suppose the kick has a low thump and a bright click, but you want the bass ducking to respond mainly to the thump. A filtered detector feed can emphasize the frequency range of interest. It is wise to create this as an auxiliary signal not sent to the audible master bus, rather than altering the kick itself merely to make the controller work. The detector signal is control information. You do not necessarily want to hear it.

Compression can make a source *appear* louder because its range has been reduced, allowing later makeup gain to raise the average. But makeup gain raises peaks too. If the compressor reduces a transient by nine decibels and you then raise the entire result by nine, the steady-state portion might return to its former peak while quieter portions become more prominent. That is why comparing a compressed and uncompressed version at different apparent loudness can be misleading. For a fair comparison, level-match them as closely as practical. Then ask whether the altered dynamics helped the music. A compressor is not an improvement simply because the output meter stays higher.

Limiting is a related form of dynamic control, but the term covers implementations with different lookahead and transient behavior. A limiter used near the master output may prevent sudden peaks from exceeding a desired ceiling. Some limiters look ahead by delaying the audible path so the controller can act before a peak reaches the output. That delay is a source of plugin latency; in a parallel signal path it may need compensation. A true-peak limiter or analyzer considers inter-sample behavior through an oversampled or reconstructed estimate. A sample-peak meter alone does not guarantee that the continuous-time reconstruction will stay below the same number. None of this obliges you to make every piece equally loud.

Now return to CPU cost. Pure scalar gain and summation are inexpensive. A basic equalizer processes each sample through a small filter state. Several equalizers on dozens of channels are ordinarily manageable on a modern machine, though there are exceptions. A compressor estimates envelopes, computes gain and may oversample or look ahead. A sophisticated dynamics plugin may include nonlinear models or multiple bands, multiplying the work. Some processors add latency, some need sizable buffers, and some cannot be distributed across cores because the next calculation depends on the previous one. The cost is not determined by how many graphical knobs you see.

There is also a difference between four parallel compressors and a compressor on their shared bus. Four separate track processors may be independent and can potentially run in parallel. The bus compressor must wait for its inputs to be ready. If one stem needs an expensive guitar amplifier model before it reaches that bus, the bus cannot finish before the guitar branch finishes. This forms a *critical path* through the graph, which may matter more to a real-time dropout than the sum of all CPU time across tracks. Offline DawDreamer can take longer to render without missing a sound-card deadline; real-time Ardour playback cannot indefinitely postpone the next buffer.

A practical mixing exercise begins without any plugin. Balance kick, bass, guitar and melody using only gain and panning. Listen at a moderate level, and make sure no output clips. Then try three changes in isolation. First, reduce the guitar's notes during the lead entrance without touching its equalizer; if you control a symbolic arrangement, edit the notes directly. Second, restore the original part but try a modest EQ reduction that makes the lead easier to distinguish. Third, remove the EQ and let a gentle compressor on the guitar respond to the lead through a sidechain. These are not interchangeable procedures. The first changes the composition, the second changes spectral balance, and the third creates time-dependent attenuation. Compare the result at approximately matched loudness.

One more thought experiment can be done entirely on paper. Suppose the bass has a detected level of minus ten decibels and you set a threshold of minus eighteen with a two-to-one ratio. The input is eight decibels above threshold; the ideal hard-knee output is four above threshold, or minus fourteen, giving four decibels of reduction. Increase the ratio to four-to-one: the output becomes minus sixteen, giving six decibels of reduction. Turn the threshold down to minus twenty-two at the original two-to-one ratio: the input is twelve above threshold and the output is six above threshold, or minus sixteen again. Different settings can yield the same steady-state result at one level yet behave differently across the rest of the performance. This is why preserving only the output loudness number does not preserve a mix decision.

There is a deeper reason not to treat compression and equalization as interchangeable with a fader. Each process has a different relationship to time. Suppose a sustained bass tone and a short kick produce the same measured average level during a particular second. The kick may have a sharp transient occupying only a few tens of milliseconds; the bass may occupy nearly the whole second. A compressor with a fast peak detector may respond strongly to the kick, while a slower detector may mostly follow the bass. The meter you choose affects what you think you are controlling. Peak level, root-mean-square level and perceptually weighted integrated loudness are not the same mathematical object. A compressor may expose multiple detector modes, and a mastering meter may compute still another measure on the result.

Consider an idealized envelope follower that responds to a sudden step in signal level. A common exponential smoothing model reaches about sixty-three percent of the way toward its final value after one time constant. With a ten-millisecond time constant, it is about sixty-three percent settled after ten milliseconds, about eighty-six percent after twenty, and about ninety-five percent after thirty. Real compressor attack knobs are not guaranteed to use that exact definition; one developer may define attack in decibels of gain reduction, another in envelope settling time, and another may include different nonlinear behavior. That is why the same numerical attack setting can sound different across plugins. It is also why copying settings from a tutorial is not a reproducibility guarantee unless the processor and its version are identified.

Let us distinguish compressor gain from the detector's requested gain. The detector first estimates level, perhaps by rectifying the input or by measuring short-term power. A transfer curve maps that level to desired gain reduction. Smoothing then determines how quickly the applied gain approaches the desired gain. The audible output is the original path multiplied by the smoothed gain. If the detector is keyed from another track, that control path is independent of the audio being changed. The compressor may have a feedback topology instead of a feed-forward one, may work in logarithmic or linear units, and may deliberately color the signal. Our elementary threshold formula is a useful starting model, not the only possible architecture.

The order of processing also matters. Put a high-pass filter before a compressor and you may remove low-frequency energy from the signal and, depending on the compressor, from the detector. The compressor may then react less to the kick. Put the same filter after the compressor, and the bass-rich material can still trigger gain reduction before the unwanted low-frequency content is removed. These two chains can produce different loudness motion, even if they contain the same two processors with the same settings. Likewise, a compressor before a distortion effect may deliver steadier drive into the nonlinearity, while distortion before compression changes both the harmonics and the envelope presented to the compressor. A routing graph is more than a bag of plugins.

Parallel compression adds a further complication. Imagine sending a dry drum signal to one path and a heavily compressed version to another, then blending the two. The compressed path may have plugin latency. If we sum it with the unprocessed path without aligning them, their overlapping frequencies can reinforce and cancel according to delay. It can sound as though an EQ setting changed, even though we only changed alignment. Ardour can compensate reported plugin delay in its routing graph, but the end result still depends on accurate latency reporting and on any intentionally introduced delay. In an offline script we must either preserve the host's compensation or measure and align the branches ourselves. Do not assume that two stems processed separately will recombine to the same waveform as the original unprocessed source.

We can quantify why a small delay has a large effect on some sounds. Take two identical pure tones at one hundred hertz. One period lasts ten milliseconds. If the second copy is delayed five milliseconds, it is half a cycle behind. Add the two equal-amplitude tones and they cancel in the ideal, steady, noiseless case. A delay of two and a half milliseconds is one quarter cycle, producing neither perfect addition nor complete cancellation. For a real kick containing many frequencies, the cancellation pattern varies across frequency. That is the beginning of comb filtering, which we will investigate separately. A phase or timing problem can therefore masquerade as an equalization problem, but applying more EQ cannot necessarily repair the underlying alignment.

Think also about headroom inside a processor. A DAW may use floating-point processing that can represent sample amplitudes above one without immediate numerical clipping. But a modeled analog plugin may saturate when driven hard, and the final conversion to a fixed-range file or sound-card output imposes its own limits. Turning down the master after saturating a plugin earlier in the chain does not undo that distortion. Conversely, a harmless internal peak above one in floating-point arithmetic need not mean the exported mix has clipped, provided appropriate gain is applied before the fixed-range boundary. Knowing *where* a peak occurs matters as much as knowing its height.

A carefully chosen automation curve may solve a problem more transparently than compression. If the lead enters softly only in bars nine through twelve, raise its gain for those bars and return it to baseline afterward. A compressor would make its own moment-by-moment decisions according to a detector, which may or may not track musical phrases. An automation line expresses an intentional decision: this passage should be prominent. A sidechain expresses a conditional policy: whenever the triggering signal crosses a threshold, change another signal's gain. Neither is morally or technologically superior; they encode different kinds of intent. In a reproducible pipeline you can store both, but only the first is wholly independent of the actual sound of the trigger.

An engineer should also know when not to process. Start with a stereo image and a rough balance. If the melody is masked because the guitar plays constant dense chords, ask whether a thinner guitar voicing will make the score better. If the kick and bass fight over the same transient, ask whether their attacks can have complementary roles. If two microphones of one source sound hollow together, investigate alignment and polarity before buying a more elaborate equalizer. That is not a rejection of audio engineering; it is treating the arrangement and the physics of the signals as part of engineering.

The central lesson is that mixing is a negotiation between objects with musical intentions. Equalization changes spectral relationships; compression changes dynamic relationships; sidechains introduce dependencies among tracks; limiting controls peaks under an output constraint. None of them supplies an answer to whether the guitar should enter on beat two or whether the melody should change register. Keep a way back to the underlying musical parts. Use the processors when you can say what relationship you intend to change. And remember that the CPU doesn't know why a lead deserves space. It computes the transforms you ask for, even when the composition needed a different transform altogether.

## Display-only companion: exact static compression cases

Ideal hard-knee downward compressor, for detector level `L_in` in dBFS, threshold `T`, ratio `R >= 1`, before makeup gain:

\[
L_{out} = \begin{cases} L_{in} & L_{in} \leq T, \\ T + (L_{in}-T)/R & L_{in} > T. \end{cases}
\]

| Input level | Threshold | Ratio | Output (ideal) | Gain reduction |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| -6 dBFS | -18 dBFS | 4:1 | -15 dBFS | 9 dB |
| -24 dBFS | -18 dBFS | 4:1 | -24 dBFS | 0 dB |
| -10 dBFS | -18 dBFS | 2:1 | -14 dBFS | 4 dB |
| -10 dBFS | -18 dBFS | 4:1 | -16 dBFS | 6 dB |
| -10 dBFS | -22 dBFS | 2:1 | -16 dBFS | 6 dB |

Amplitude ratio for a gain change `D` decibels is `g = 10 ** (D/20)`. The example of *two identical aligned* waveforms summing from peak `0.4` to peak `0.8` is `20 log10(0.8/0.4) = 6.0206 dB`. Uncorrelated equal-average-power sources instead sum to roughly twice the power (+3.0103 dB); this does not fix peak amplitude.

A first-order illustrative filter can have `y[n] = a*x[n] + b*y[n-1]`. Actual EQ filters often use multi-coefficient sections; coefficient values, sample rate and feedback stability determine behavior. A lookahead limiter may delay the audible path and require plugin delay compensation.

### Ardour experiment: three distinct ways to make room for a lead

1. Work with a four-stem section and save a reference balance. Keep a copy of the unprocessed source.
2. Make space in the *arrangement* by removing overlapping guitar attacks during the lead, if the guitar source is still editable.
3. Restore the guitar and try a modest, broad EQ adjustment while comparing level-matched versions.
4. Restore the guitar again and try a modest lead-keyed guitar compressor where a suitable sidechain-capable plugin exists. Do not feed the sidechain tap directly to the master output.
5. Compare the results in context; document what changed and why. A plugin preset is not a substitute for the musical judgment.
