# Unequal Beats and Interlocking Cycles: Meter Beyond Four

If I ask you to tap four steady beats, you can probably do it even without music playing. One, two, three, four. Keep the taps equally spaced. Now speak the same four counts but emphasize the first and third. Without changing the timing, you have created a pattern of stronger and weaker positions. Change which counts you emphasize and the sense of grouping changes. This is a modest beginning, but it reveals an important fact: rhythm concerns not just when events occur, but how we understand those events as belonging together.

A pulse is a repeating reference for time. A subdivision is a smaller division of the pulse, often two or three equal parts in examples from Western notation. A meter is an organization of recurring strong and weak positions; it is more than the denominator of a time signature. The usual four-quarter-note bar is only one possibility. We can arrange subdivisions into groups of different lengths, or superimpose several recurring patterns. When these structures remain consistent, listeners can find their way through them even though no single melody is tapping out four equal large beats.

Start with seven equally spaced eighth-note units. Call them one through seven. If all seven are equal in duration, the entire span is simply seven times one small unit. But the seven can be grouped in different ways. Try two plus two plus three. Put an emphasis on unit one, unit three, and unit five. Speak the counts as ONE two, THREE four, FIVE six seven. The final group lasts one small unit longer than either of the first two. This gives a repeating span that is lopsided in the precise sense that the three groups do not all have the same length. There is nothing mathematically mysterious about it. The groups consume two, two, and three units, which add to seven.

Now take the same seven units and organize them as three plus two plus two. The emphasized positions become one, four, and six. Speak ONE two three, FOUR five, SIX seven. The total duration is unchanged, but the large beats arrive at different moments. The first grouping can be notated as two quarter-note durations and one dotted-quarter duration, if each unit is an eighth note. The second becomes one dotted-quarter and two quarters. This is an example of additive meter: a recurring metrical span assembled from groups whose durations are not all identical. In some contexts players might think of three larger beats, one of them longer than the others. In others, they might orient themselves by seven smaller units and a pattern of accents. Both descriptions can help, but they answer slightly different questions.

How do we avoid mistaking one accent pattern for the meter itself? First keep the sequence going for several repetitions. A single louder note does not prove that the entire piece has changed its metrical organization. A melody might place an accent against a stable underlying meter; that is a rhythmic displacement, not necessarily a new meter. The bass might articulate one grouping while a melody suggests another. Performers and listeners infer meter from a larger set of cues, including repeated accents, bass arrivals, harmonic changes, and phrase lengths. Our printed exercises make the grouping explicit so we can practice the difference, but real music can leave more room for interpretation.

Consider a separate case with eight equally spaced eighth-note units, which fit inside an ordinary bar of four quarter-note beats. We can place accents at units one, four, and seven. Those accents divide the bar into groups of three, three, and two. The total is still eight, so the four-quarter-note measure has not necessarily changed. A steady kick or handclap could keep the quarter-note pulse under that grouping, while another part emphasizes three plus three plus two. This is a common rhythmic construction in many kinds of popular and dance music. The comparison with seven eighth notes is revealing. Both patterns involve groups of two and three small units. But three plus three plus two makes eight units and can sit over a steady four-beat bar. Two plus two plus three makes seven units and commonly organizes a bar of seven eighths. Writing the sums prevents us from confusing the two ideas merely because their spoken patterns are similar.

Here is a physical exercise. Tap a steady sequence of eight small pulses on a desk. On every sequence of eight, say ONE at the first pulse, FOUR at the fourth pulse, and SEVEN at the seventh pulse, with ordinary syllables on the other units. Repeat until the grouping becomes predictable. Next keep the same eight taps, but emphasize one and five instead. You have shifted from three plus three plus two to four plus four, without altering the small-unit clock. The work of rhythm is partly the management of such expectations. We feel motion not only because time is passing, but because a pattern teaches us when the next marked event is due.

There is another kind of interaction that cannot be reduced to a single additive grouping. Imagine two parts with the same total span, but one divides that span into three equal intervals and the other into two. They begin together. The three-part line attacks at the beginning, one third of the way, and two thirds of the way. The two-part line attacks at the beginning and halfway through. After the full span both patterns repeat together. We call this a three-against-two polyrhythm in the simple model where both parts maintain their own equally spaced attacks. Neither part has to be assigned to a particular instrument. They could be two hands clapping, two spoken syllable streams, two singers on a single pitch, or two layers in a sequencer.

For an exact numerical picture, divide the whole cycle into six equal tiny units. The three-attacks part occurs at unit zero, unit two, and unit four. The two-attacks part occurs at zero and three. When the cycle reaches unit six, both start again. What do we gain by using six? It is a common multiple of two and three, so it lets us represent both equal subdivisions with integer event positions. The six-unit grid is a bookkeeping convenience; a musician need not feel six tiny beats while performing the polyrhythm. The listener might instead attend to two larger beats, three larger beats, or the composite pattern made by their interlocking attacks.

Place the events on paper: both voices attack at zero. The three-part voice attacks at two, the two-part voice at three, and the three-part voice again at four. At six they meet. The gap between successive attacks of the combined texture is two units, then one, then one, then two. This order follows directly from the grid: zero, two, three, four, six. Notice that the voices meet only at the start and end of this simple cycle. If you can follow that ordering with pencil marks, you have understood the rhythmic relationship even before attempting to coordinate the two lines physically.

A practical way to learn it is to speak a repeated phrase or tap a desk. Start with the common beginning. Make six small ticks, and mark the three-part attacks at ticks one, three, and five. Mark the two-part attacks at ticks one and four. Once that combined pattern is clear, try tapping one voice with a hand and speaking the other. You may find that the problem is no longer arithmetic but motor coordination. That is expected. The notation answers which events should occur; practice is how the timing becomes comfortable. No audio example is needed to check the arithmetic of the score, though a recorded or performed realization would be valuable for learning its feel.

These constructions open the door to larger rhythmic cycles. Suppose one repeating melody lasts five small units and another lasts four. If they both begin together and continue without changing their lengths, their starting points coincide again after twenty units. The least common multiple of five and four is twenty. We can think of this as two periods being played against one another, not necessarily as two musical lines making the same accent hierarchy. The cycle length tells us when their beginnings line up. It does not tell us what the pattern will sound like, whether the lines are audible separately, or whether anyone perceives a stable beat under them.

It is tempting to use the word polyrhythm for every case where multiple cycles overlap. Some distinctions are useful. In the elementary three-against-two example, the parts divide a common span differently. In a pattern built from five- and four-unit loops, each layer may have a different repeating period, a phenomenon often discussed under cross-rhythm, polymeter, or interlocking cycles depending on the notation and musical practice. Those words are not interchangeable across all traditions. What matters most for a composer's working description is to state which duration belongs to each layer and where the attacks fall. A clear event grid is more informative than a label applied without context.

One interesting compositional choice is to establish a regular four-beat pulse and then let a seven-unit melodic figure repeat above it. The figure's beginning moves relative to the bar line each time it repeats. If the figure lasts seven eighth notes while the underlying bar lasts eight eighth notes, the two beginnings meet again after fifty-six eighth notes. That is seven four-beat bars, or eight repetitions of the seven-unit figure. A listener might track the steady background, the moving phrase, or the moment at which their starts finally realign. By choosing a simple seven-against-eight relationship, the composer creates a long arc from two very small patterns.

Guitar can make these ideas tangible without being the foundation of the theory. One player can keep a regular low-note pulse while the picking hand accents an uneven group, or two players can divide an ostinato across different string ranges. A guitarist may need to choose an articulation that keeps the two layers distinct, but the metric arithmetic is identical for handclaps, drum sounds, plucked strings, syllables, or synthesized events. The instrument adds ergonomics and timbre, not a new definition of seven eighths or three against two.

The most important caution is cultural and musical, not numerical. Rhythm-centered practices in many parts of the world include learned timelines, cycles, gestures, dance relationships, and interactive parts that cannot be captured fully by Western bars and fractions. Some traditions intentionally resist a single abstract metrical hierarchy. Our calculations are tools for describing explicitly notated patterns, not a claim that the full meaning of a rhythmic practice is the sum of its attack positions.

To compose with these tools, begin by choosing the question you want the rhythm to pose. Should the listener learn a repeating unequal group? Should a layer create tension against an established beat? Should two cycles start together, diverge, and finally meet? Choose the smallest number of units that answers that question. Mark every onset. Keep durations consistent. Then consider whether any part needs to be sounded louder, held longer, or omitted for the structure to be perceptible. You have moved from arithmetic to musical design. The numbers tell you what can happen. Articulation, sound, and repetition help determine what the audience will actually notice.

## Reproducible grids (not spoken)

`e` means one eighth-note unit; `q` means two eighths; `dq` means dotted quarter, three eighths. A plus sign marks a grouping boundary, not an extra attack. All patterns are original and specify integer time indices.

### Two versions of a seven-eighth-note bar

| Version | Grouping | Emphasized eighth positions (1-based) | One-line events and durations | Eighth total |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| A | 2+2+3 | 1, 3, 5 | C4 q, D4 q, G4 dq | 7 |
| B | 3+2+2 | 1, 4, 6 | E4 dq, F4 q, C4 q | 7 |

Each line is a complete single-voice pattern. Its grouping can be marked by stronger attacks or simply taught as a mental count. A stronger attack on the first note of each group is **optional**, not encoded by pitch or duration alone.

### Three-plus-three-plus-two over four quarter beats

Eight eighth units indexed 1 through 8. Accents on 1, 4, 7. An optional independent pulse line marks the four quarter-note beats at positions 1, 3, 5, 7. The lines coincide on 1 and 7, but not on 4.

### Three against two with six small units

| Event line | Attacks in zero-based six-unit cycle | Equal spacing within line |
|---|---|---|
| Three part | 0, 2, 4 (and 6 as next cycle) | 2 units |
| Two part | 0, 3 (and 6 as next cycle) | 3 units |
| Combined | 0, 2, 3, 4 (then 6) | gaps 2, 1, 1, 2 |

### Exercise set

1. Write an original two-bar 5/8 melody grouping the bar as 3+2, then rewrite it as 2+3 without changing the total number of eighths.
2. Continue the 3:2 six-unit grid through three complete cycles. Verify that both voices begin together at 0, 6, 12, and 18.
3. Use least common multiples to calculate when a three-unit loop and an eight-unit loop next have simultaneous beginnings.
4. Without a musical instrument, mark eight strokes on paper and draw a vertical accent over strokes 1, 4, 7. Repeat the grid three times and compare it with accents 1 and 5.
5. On guitar, assign a quiet bass pluck to each of four quarter-note beats while the upper strings play a 3+3+2 figure over eight eighths. Write *when* each part attacks before choosing fingerings.
