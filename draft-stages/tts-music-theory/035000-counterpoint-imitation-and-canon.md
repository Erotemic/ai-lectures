# Counterpoint, Imitation, and Canon: Hearing More Than One Thought

One melody begins. Before it finishes, a second enters with what sounds like the same idea. You can follow either line, yet you also hear a third thing: the relationship between them. Sometimes they agree. Sometimes one suspends a note while the other moves. Sometimes one line seems to answer a question that the other has not finished asking. That experience is the heart of counterpoint. It cannot be reduced to naming the chord produced by each simultaneous pair of notes.

Counterpoint is a broad term with different histories and techniques. Our specific training method comes from the European tradition commonly called species counterpoint, where a composer learns to coordinate two melodic lines under a sequence of increasingly demanding constraints. Johann Joseph Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum was published in Vienna in 1725. A surviving digitized original is available from the Wienbibliothek im Rathaus. Fux did not invent the existence of independently moving voices, and his teaching method should not be mistaken for a global definition of good polyphony. It is one historically influential discipline that makes certain musical decisions legible.

A modern, accessible account of the exercise appears in the Open Music Theory chapters by Mark Gotham and Kris Shaffer. Their first-species presentation emphasizes one note against one note: two voices move at the same rate, opening and closing on selected consonant intervals, moving through other consonances, and avoiding specified parallel perfect intervals. It is a restricted design problem. Its value is that removing rhythmic complexity makes melodic independence and vertical relations easier to examine. It is not a complete model of how a piece must sound.

Consider an original eight-bar example. The lower voice, one long note per bar, moves C, D, E, F, G, F, D, C. The upper voice moves G, F, G, A, B, A, B, C an octave higher than the final lower C. At the beginning, C below G produces a fifth. Next, D below F yields a third. E below G produces another third. F below A produces a third. G below B produces a third. F below A returns to the earlier relation. D below B produces a sixth, and the final C below C is an octave. We have an explicitly inspectable sequence, not a vague instruction to write harmonious music.

Pay attention to what happens between notes. On the first change, the lower voice rises from C to D while the upper falls from G to F: contrary motion. On the next change, both voices rise, but by different intervals. Later, when the lower turns downward from G to F, the upper turns downward from B to A. Those are different degrees of independence. The voices do not always have to oppose each other. What matters is that each has a direction and the combination remains deliberate. This example contains repeated thirds; a more exacting species exercise could ask for greater interval variety. A useful model should expose its compromises rather than pretend that satisfying a few rules creates artistry.

The final step is particularly informative. The lower voice moves D to C, down by step, while the upper moves B to C, up by step. The lines approach the final octave by contrary motion. Open Music Theory describes this kind of stepwise approach in its first-species cadence rules. Notice the difference between saying the final notes are consonant and explaining why their arrival feels prepared. A chord label describes a moment; voice leading can describe a journey into it.

Now listen to the example three ways. Play only the lower line; play only the upper line; then play both together. If you can hum one part after hearing the two together, you are beginning to separate auditory streams. If the blend obscures one part, change timbre or pan rather than concluding the notes are wrong. Rendering both lines with identical sine waves at exactly matched levels can make some moments fuse more strongly than rendering them with distinct spectra. The companion uses original note events and offers separated and combined versions. They are not recordings of Fux's exercises.

First species has no rhythmic independence, which makes a second question inevitable. Can two lines remain understandable when their attacks do not coincide? In second species, one line may move more often than the other; in other exercises, the rhythm becomes more complex. A note that is not part of the instantaneous consonance may still work as a passing or neighbor tone, provided that its approach, placement, and departure fit the chosen style's conventions. An isolated inventory of simultaneous intervals cannot tell you whether the motion is coherent.

A particularly audible case is a suspension. Imagine the bass holding A while an upper voice plays F. In this register, F above A forms a sixth, ordinarily treated as consonant within the historical style. The upper F is tied across the bar line. On the next downbeat, the bass changes to C while the upper F stays. Now F above C forms a fourth, treated as dissonant above the bass in this particular two-voice counterpoint practice. On the following half-bar, the upper voice moves down to E, making a third above C. We have preparation, suspension, and resolution. The upper pitch did not become troublesome by changing itself. Its role changed because the other voice moved beneath it.

The Open Music Theory fourth-species chapter calls this sort of pattern a four-three suspension. It specifies preparation, a tied note against the new bass, and a stepwise resolution. This precise description prevents a common misunderstanding: a suspension is not merely any note that sounds tense over a chord. Its timing and origin matter. In a different musical style, the same F above C might function as a stable color rather than a dissonance demanding resolution. Our label belongs to the exercise's grammar, not a law governing all listeners.

Next comes imitation. The second line echoes a recognizable idea from the first. Imitation can preserve the exact notes, preserve intervals but start on another pitch, change the rhythm, or alter the contour. The degree of resemblance is a compositional choice. In a strict canon, a following voice performs a transformed copy of the leader under a fixed rule, usually a constant time delay and a specified pitch relation. Once the rule is chosen, later events in the follower are constrained by the leader. In freer imitation, the composer is permitted to answer the opening motif and then turn elsewhere.

Let's make an original two-bar motif. In common four-four, the leader plays C, D, E, G across the first bar, one beat each. In the second bar it plays E, D, C, and then rests for the final beat. Now delay the complete pattern by one measure and have the follower enter one octave lower. The first four beats contain only the leader. During the next four, the follower gives the first bar's pitches beneath the leader's second bar. During the following three beats, the follower completes the second bar's pitches after the leader has finished. The listener hears recognition as well as overlap. This is a strict imitation rule for the small invented pattern; it does not assert that its vertical combinations satisfy first-species counterpoint.

The overlap can create intervals the species exercise would forbid. That is a compositional fact, not proof that the canon is invalid. There are many distinct systems of counterpoint, and a short canon can be designed to privilege imitation over sustained consonance. We can revise the leader to control the resulting collisions, change the follower's interval of entry, or change its delay. Each revision alters both the melodic identity and the vertical structure. A counterpoint problem is therefore often an optimization with competing goals, but the goals must be named rather than hidden behind an abstract quality score.

This has a direct consequence for generative music. Suppose an agent emits a lead melody, another emits a bass, and a third emits a response figure. If the parts are generated independently, their phrase endings may coincide awkwardly, their registers may mask each other, and their imitative relationships may be accidental. A final process can detect some conflicts: parallel motion between two voice grids, unintended note-on collisions, or a follower that departs from its declared canon rule. But it cannot decide from those checks alone whether the texture tells the musical story you want. A planned overlap may be the point of the piece.

A second lesson is that exact pitch duplication does not guarantee perceptual similarity. Move the imitation to a register where its timbre changes significantly, and the response may be less obvious. Change the articulation and it can sound like an interruption rather than an echo. Keep pitch and rhythm identical but swap the sound of a plucked instrument for a sustained synthesizer, and the identity of the gesture may become harder to follow. For a composer using DawDreamer, this can be tested by rendering the same note events through different original synthetic voices. Ardour allows the resulting stems to be auditioned individually or together.

Consider form. If every new part enters before the old one finishes, density accumulates. At first that might create momentum; later it can exhaust the listener. A piece can make the moment of greatest density meaningful by following it with a return to one voice. Counterpoint then does double duty: it coordinates notes locally and controls attention globally. The listener may hear the opening motif most clearly after having worked to find it inside a busy passage.

What would a useful revision session look like? Begin with the eight-bar two-voice score and ask the listener to follow the lower part throughout. If they lose it in bars three through six, render the two parts with distinct sounds before rewriting the notes. Then replace the upper phrase with a new contour and verify that the bass still has direction. Next add the prepared four-three suspension in a separate short study. Ask whether the tension is heard at the moment of the bass change, or whether a timbre and register choice hides it. Finally create the delayed imitation and compare three different entry offsets. Do not change pitch, timing, and sound source all at once if you want to learn what caused a difference.

Some contrapuntal traditions teach strict control of perfect fifths and octaves; others intentionally exploit their parallel motion. Some prize smooth melodic continuity, others abrupt call-and-response. Treat the Fux-derived rules as a disciplined historical grammar, not a test of cultural sophistication. Scholars and musicians working in other traditions may analyze polyphony through concepts that this European note-against-note framework cannot capture. The most useful habit is to ask what relationship the performers are creating and what evidence supports the label we apply to it.

At the end of a counterpoint exercise, you should be able to describe more than the list of chords. You should be able to say which voice began the idea, which followed, how a held tone changed meaning while the harmony moved, what happened at the cadence, and why one part remained audible while another entered. If you can imagine changing any of those facts before hearing the new version, you have begun to compose with multiple lines as independent thoughts rather than stacking notes and hoping they cooperate.

### Original first-species-inspired study

One whole note in each bar, 4/4. Octave numbers are essential. The final lower C4 and upper C5 form P8. The first C4/G4 forms P5.

| Bar | Lower | Upper | Interval |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | C4 | G4 | P5 |
| 2 | D4 | F4 | m3 |
| 3 | E4 | G4 | m3 |
| 4 | F4 | A4 | M3 |
| 5 | G4 | B4 | M3 |
| 6 | F4 | A4 | M3 |
| 7 | D4 | B4 | M6 |
| 8 | C4 | C5 | P8 |

No parallel perfect fifths or octaves; penultimate re–ti to do–do approaches the octave by contrary step. **Pedagogical limitation:** repetitive imperfect consonances and narrow metric variation are deliberate simplifications, not a perfect model composition.

### Original 4–3 suspension

Measure A: lower A3 sustained, upper F4 is consonant (minor sixth) and held across the barline. Measure B: bass changes to C4, upper F4 forms an accented fourth, then moves to E4 (third) on beat 3. This is a **historically scoped exercise**: in some harmonic idioms a fourth above the bass is not automatically treated this way.

### Original miniature canon

Leader phrase in beats 0–7: C4 D4 E4 G4 | E4 D4 C4 rest. Follower repeats the pattern **four beats later**, an octave down. Rendering verifies the copying rule; it does not certify independent artistic success or conformity to first species.
