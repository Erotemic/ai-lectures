# How We Hear Space: Localization, Stereo, Headphones, and the Listening Room

You have two versions of the same short recording. In version one, a guitarist seems to stand at the center of the scene. In version two, the guitar seems to sit off to one side, with a long echo across the other side. The audio files contain two channels each. It is natural to say that the second file is wider. But where, precisely, is that width? It is not a distance in the file. It is a perceptual result produced by two electrical signals, two loudspeakers or headphones, an environment, and a listener.

When a real instrument plays to one side of your head, the resulting sound reaches the two ears differently. There may be a difference in arrival time, a difference in level, and differences in spectrum produced by the head, torso, and outer ears. The brain uses some combination of those cues, alongside other evidence, to form an impression of direction. John Middlebrooks and David Green's review, Sound Localization by Human Listeners, surveys this literature. Jens Blauert's Spatial Hearing offers a broader historical and experimental treatment. This is a mature research domain, but it has no one-knob equation that converts a stereo pan position into a unique physical angle for every playback condition.

Begin with interaural time difference, usually abbreviated ITD. Suppose a sound source lies toward your left. It often reaches the left ear slightly earlier than the right ear. If we approximate a path difference of seventeen centimeters and sound speed of 343 meters per second, the time difference is roughly half a millisecond. That is a back-of-the-envelope calculation based on a *chosen* path difference, not a measured anatomical constant or a universal maximum ITD. Real heads are not simple unobstructed paths, the sound wraps around them, and the available cue depends on source direction and frequency. Still, the calculation tells us why auditory spatial processing must deal with tiny timing differences.

A second cue is interaural level difference, ILD. The head can partially block sound, often giving stronger level differences at higher frequencies where the head's size matters relative to wavelength. The relative usefulness of timing and level cues changes across frequency and stimulus type. Ewan Macpherson and John Middlebrooks revisited the classic duplex theory in a 2002 experimental study of how listeners weight the cues for lateral angle. The phrase duplex theory is a useful historical organizing label, but a mixing algorithm should not treat low frequency as time-only and high frequency as level-only with an abrupt universal crossover. Their data and the broader literature argue against such a rigid switch.

We also hear elevation and front-versus-back differences, and these cannot be explained by only one left-minus-right number. The outer ear alters the spectrum of incoming sound in direction-dependent ways. The complete mapping from a sound source in space to the signal near a listener's eardrum is described using a head-related transfer function, or HRTF. HRTFs are measured as responses depending on source direction and often listener anatomy. The CIPIC HRTF Database, published by Algazi, Duda, Thompson, and Avendano in 2001, contains measured impulse responses from a set of subjects over many directions. It makes an important point tangible: a spatial filter is not a universal magical soundstage preset. Different people have different bodies and transfer functions.

An impulse response is especially convenient because we can convolve it with a source signal. Let the left-ear response be one filter and the right-ear response another. The source passes through both filters, giving a binaural pair of signals. Under suitable headphone playback, this can convey cues corresponding to a virtual direction. But the simulation depends on calibration, choice of HRTF, headphone response, room assumptions, and the listener. A renderer using a generic HRTF may produce convincing externalization for one person and front-back confusion or an inside-the-head image for another. The CIPIC database is valuable as measured material and a research basis; it is not a certificate of accurate localization for every individual. In a 1993 study of localization using nonindividualized head-related transfer functions, Elizabeth Wenzel, Marianne Arruda, Doris Kistler, and Frederic Wightman compared listeners' judgments for real sound directions and headphone-synthesized directions. Many listeners could localize relatively well in azimuth, while front-back and up-down confusions increased for virtual sources in their tested conditions. This is evidence about the tested virtual-source method, subjects, and signals, not a promise that every generic HRTF will fail. 

Now return to a conventional stereo DAW. A standard amplitude pan may reduce one channel and raise the other so an instrument shifts along a perceived line between two speakers. In the simplest case, center panning sends coherent versions of a mono signal to both speakers. The listener is not hearing a real instrument suspended at a mathematical center point; the speaker signals and room create a phantom-center impression under favorable listening geometry. Move away from that geometry, and the image may shift toward the nearer speaker. A panning control does not guarantee a stable angle independent of where the listener sits.

Different panning laws also make a significant difference. A linear amplitude pan and an equal-power pan assign different gains at the center. For two uncorrelated channels, a cosine-sine gain pair can keep the sum of channel powers constant in an idealized calculation. But the same reasoning is not equivalent to maintaining perceived loudness of coherent acoustic speaker signals at all positions. The listening room contributes phase, reflections, and frequency response. In an actual workflow, note which panner and law you used; otherwise two sessions can contain identical pan numbers yet produce different center levels.

Headphones change the experiment. With ordinary headphones, each ear receives predominantly its assigned channel, without the natural acoustic crossfeed from the opposite loudspeaker. On speakers, each ear hears both loudspeakers, filtered by the room and head. Consequently, a stereo effect that sounds beautifully separated on headphones may seem different on monitors. Conversely, some exaggerated left-right separation can sound disconnected through headphones while yielding a plausible wide stage over speakers. There is no logical contradiction: the transfer functions reaching the ears differ.

A practical trial illustrates this. Render a dry original mono melody and place it at the center of a two-channel mix. Create a second version with the same melody placed far left and a quieter delay return placed right. Compare on headphones and speakers, adjusting levels responsibly. Do you hear the instrument itself move, its echoes move, or an extended room around it? Does the result hold if you change listening position? These questions are more informative than asking only whether the left-channel peak is smaller than the right-channel peak.

Do not conflate panning with microphone placement. A microphone positioned at a different distance or angle captures a different instrument-room interaction before we even open a DAW. A close microphone may emphasize direct sound and certain timbral features; a distant microphone collects a different balance of direct and reflected energy. Moving a fader on a close-miked track cannot reconstruct every detail of what a microphone across the room would have recorded. An artificial reverb can create an impression of space, but it is a designed transformation, not a time machine for moving the original microphone.

Reflections make the geometry more complex. Sound arriving directly from a source is followed by copies reflected from walls, ceiling, floor, and objects. Under some timing and level conditions, a listener localizes toward an earlier arriving sound rather than hearing each reflection as an equally distinct new source. Wallach, Newman, and Rosenzweig published The Precedence Effect in Sound Localization in 1949. The phenomenon and its later refinements matter in rooms and stereo reproduction. However, we should not teach a universal delay, such as exactly twenty milliseconds, beyond which an echo suddenly becomes audible. Precedence behavior depends on the signal, relative levels, repetition, direction, room, and the listener.

In a mix, an early reflection can contribute to spaciousness, timbre, or localization stability. A later or stronger delay might become a separate rhythmic event. This is where sound design meets composition. A repeating guitar echo can be a musical counter-rhythm; another echo can be merely an unintended muddy tail. The distinction is not carried by the delay time alone. It depends on what else occurs in the piece and what event the listener is meant to track.

Rooms add spectral changes as well as delays. Boundaries can create modal resonances at some low frequencies, cancellations at some locations, and frequency-dependent reverberant decay. A monitor placed near a boundary may behave differently from the same monitor in the open. A listener moving a short distance can encounter a different balance of low-frequency modes. Therefore, a decision to remove bass because it booms at one workstation may be a correction for that room, a correction for the recording, or both. We need independent listening references and measurements to distinguish them.

The European Broadcasting Union's Tech 3276 document describes controlled conditions for assessing mono and stereo programme material. It is a standardization resource, not an obligation for every home composer to construct a compliant broadcast room. Its value here is methodological: controlled speaker placement, listening geometry, and room conditions make comparisons more interpretable. The same file heard through two different systems can evoke different judgments. We should record which system was used when making a claim about a mix.

Another practical concern is mono compatibility. If a stereo recording is folded into one channel, the two signals must be combined according to a defined downmix rule. Phase relationships matter. If the left channel contains a sine wave and the right channel contains the same sine wave inverted, a simple sum of the two is zero. A mix can therefore sound wide in stereo while losing an element during summation. The example is mathematically exact for those ideal signals; real music and device downmix coefficients need careful inspection. A utility that checks only stereo RMS may miss the cancellation entirely.

An original diagnostic is to record a short vocal-like melody and create a second channel by delaying the same signal by a few milliseconds. This may change the apparent spatial quality, but in mono the two versions interfere. For a sine-wave component, a delay imposes a phase shift proportional to frequency. Some frequencies reinforce and others attenuate; a series of peaks and nulls can form in the sum. This is comb filtering. If an agent proposes stereo width by adding a delayed duplicate, it should explicitly test the downmix and possible coloration, not merely announce that the stereo channels are less correlated.

In Ardour, set up a small routing experiment. Put the original mono stem on one bus. Send two copies through separate routes whose pan and delay are recorded. Create a stereo monitor bus and a distinct mono-downmix monitor with a documented coefficient rule. Compare the two while switching only one processing parameter at a time. Save a dry reference. If you're rendering with DawDreamer, use two explicitly defined channel-processing chains and preserve the impulse-response or delay files when used. Do not build a test whose interpretation depends on an undocumented plugin default.

There is also an important artistic question. Where should a musical event appear? A lead might sound strong and direct in the center, with room energy arriving from both sides. A duet might benefit from spatial separation, but separation can conflict with the intended sense of interaction. A passage may deliberately narrow from a wide ambient texture to a small, intimate center. Spatial arrangement is part of composition. Binaural engineering can help achieve a goal; it cannot decide which goal a piece ought to have.

A useful review record distinguishes three things. The first is signal geometry: channel gains, delays, filters, coherence, and measured waveforms. The second is acoustic reproduction: headphones or speakers, placement, room, and monitor levels. The third is the listener report: where the sound seemed to originate, whether sources remained distinguishable, whether the scene felt spacious, and whether it served the music. Nothing forces these descriptions into a single numerical score.

When somebody says the mix sounds narrow, they might mean high channel correlation, little source separation, insufficient contrast in arrival cues, a reverberation strategy they dislike, or a performance with no musical differentiation. Before moving the pan control, ask what perceptual change the listener wants. Hearing-space science gives us experiments to try. It does not free us from the act of listening.

## Non-spoken companion: propagation, panning, and downmix

1. **Illustrative timing:** \(\Delta t=\Delta d/c\). For an *assumed* path difference of 0.17 m and sound speed \(c=343\) m/s, \(\Delta t\approx 0.496\) ms. Neither number is a universal adult-head constant.
2. **Idealized equal-power panning:** \(g_L=\cos(\theta),\;g_R=\sin(\theta)\) for \(0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2\). Then \(g_L^2+g_R^2=1\). For correlated copies, acoustic and downmix results do **not** follow solely from this algebra.
3. **Illustrative mono sum:** if \(L[n]=s[n]\), \(R[n]=-s[n]\), then \(L[n]+R[n]=0\). Actual fold-down conventions may apply coefficients or other processing.
4. **Delayed copy:** \(y(t)=x(t)+x(t-\tau)\). At sinusoidal frequency \(f\), the magnitude relative to one component is \(2|\cos(\pi f\tau)|\) for equal-amplitude copies in the same channel. This is an ideal linear calculation, not a measured perceptual width score.
5. **Renderer metadata:** note whether the output is ordinary stereo speaker playback, binaural headphone rendering with particular HRTFs, or a multichannel speaker layout. These are not interchangeable outputs.
