# How to Listen Critically: Bias, Comparisons, and Decisions in a Mix

Suppose two agents return mixes of the same composition. The first says that it improved clarity by reducing low-midrange buildup. The second says that it improved emotional impact by increasing the vocal's apparent closeness. Both supply a stereo WAV. Which claim should you believe? They are not even asking the same question. Clarity might be assessed by the ability to follow a line or distinguish words. Emotional impact might mean a preference or an association reported by a listener. A file cannot certify its own achievement simply by being generated by a confident process.

Critical listening begins by defining the question before answering it. Can you detect a difference? Can you identify which version preserves the attack of a note? Can you follow the second melody? Which mix do you prefer for the intended arrangement? Does one translation fail on a small speaker? Each question can require different listening material, controls, and evidence. If we collapse them into a single quality number, we will confuse technical conformance, discrimination ability, and artistic choice.

This is not an argument against measurements. Digital signals are unusually amenable to exact measurement. We can measure peak level, estimate programme loudness with a specified algorithm, examine stereo correlation, count clipped samples, inspect frequency responses, and compare sample alignment. Those data tell us something real about the stored signal and the algorithm applied. They do not directly report whether a harmony feels unresolved or whether an instrumental line deserves to be prominent.

Start with the most common confound: level. If one version plays louder, listeners may perceive it as more forceful or clearer under some conditions. Even when the artistic difference is an EQ curve, the equalizer may change broadband level enough to bias a comparison. A good audition therefore records output gain and considers loudness matching. But the matching method must fit the question. If the thing under evaluation is a compressor's effect on dynamics, blindly normalizing every short phrase to the same instantaneous loudness can erase part of what we meant to compare. There is no single universal leveling operation that makes all audio comparisons fair.

Imagine an original eight-bar passage containing voice, drums, bass, and a quiet guitar answer. Version A is the dry mix. Version B has a little broad-band amplification. Version C changes the guitar timing without changing its stored gain. Before declaring a winner, match A and B at an appropriately chosen reference level and ask whether the difference remains perceptible. For C, preserve its timing relationship, align the starts of the full exports, and compare the same bars at a consistent playback level. This is an original teaching setup. No scientific paper is claimed to have tested these tracks.

There are formal methods for more precise questions. Recommendation ITU-R BS.1116-3 describes subjective assessment of small impairments in audio systems. Recommendation ITU-R BS.1534-3 describes MUSHRA, a multi-stimulus method with hidden reference and anchor for intermediate audio quality. These are specific engineering methods with assumptions, scales, listener procedures, and targets. They are valuable sources for designing controlled evaluations, but MUSHRA is not a universal grading system for whether the chorus of your original song has the right emotional intensity. An arbitrary music preference is not automatically an audio-codec impairment.

Another common task is an ABX discrimination comparison. The listener hears A and B as references and then hears X, which is one of them, with the assignment hidden. Over repeated randomized trials, their task is to say which reference matches X. If the listener performs above chance, that can support the claim that they distinguish the versions under the tested conditions. It does not establish which they prefer. A person can reliably distinguish two reverbs and prefer either, or neither. Also, the exact trial structure and probability calculation depend on the chosen protocol; one correct guess is not decisive evidence.

For example, in ten independent balanced-choice trials, random guessing has a binomial distribution with probability one half on each trial under the null model. Eight correct answers sounds persuasive, but there are forty-five ways to get exactly eight correct, ten ways to get exactly nine, and one way to get all ten. So the chance of eight or more by guessing is fifty-six out of one thousand and twenty-four, about 5.47 percent. The chance of nine or more is eleven out of one thousand and twenty-four, about 1.07 percent. The difference between these thresholds matters. This is a deliberately simple statistical calculation, assuming independent trials and no design problems, not a universal significance rule for audio quality.

Note that a discrimination experiment should not always be used. If your artistic question is which version makes the singer seem vulnerable, repeated blind identification may impose the wrong task. You might instead present both versions in randomized order, allow longer listening, and ask for a defined judgment. Record the question and the circumstances. Blinding can reduce expectations about a named processor or a supposed expert's mix, but it does not eliminate hearing variability, listener fatigue, or the influence of the musical excerpt.

Controlled comparisons need an acoustic environment. The EBU's Tech 3276 concerns listening conditions for the assessment of mono and stereo programme material. It specifies a professional evaluation context. It is not a demand that every home musician retrofit a broadcast facility. It reminds us that monitor response, geometry, room, and level are part of the experiment. Hearing a difference on headphones and not on speakers is a finding about those conditions, not proof that one set of listeners is incompetent.

The same applies to training. Sean Olive's 2003 study, Differences in Performance and Preference of Trained versus Untrained Listeners in Loudspeaker Tests: A Case Study, compared panels in controlled loudspeaker evaluations. Trained listeners were generally more discriminating and reliable in that particular research context, while broad preference patterns could be similar. This supports careful listener training for tasks where repeatability matters. It does not authorize us to disregard untrained listeners, nor does it demonstrate that a skilled mixer can assign objective artistic rankings to all music. The study compared loudspeakers, not every form of composition.

How do we actually train listening? Start with tasks for which the answer is controlled. Render two otherwise identical versions of a tone, with one version containing a modest frequency-response change. Record the exact filter coefficients, frequency range, and gain. Listen without looking at the plugin panel, then reveal the change. Move from large, obvious changes to subtler ones, and include trials in which there is no change. The unchanged trials are important. They reveal whether the listener is hearing a difference or merely expecting one whenever two file names appear.

Next practice dynamics. Render an original percussive pattern in two versions. In one, use an amplitude curve that reduces the peaks while preserving overall file length. In the other, leave the peaks and adjust the playback level so comparison is reasonable. Which version sounds more even? Which retains the attack? Can the listener predict from the signal envelope which differences should be audible? The exercise creates a bridge between measurements and hearing without claiming that one envelope is musically superior.

Then practice masking and scene separation. Create a lead phrase with a pad accompaniment. Hold the lead constant and generate four pad variants: same-level original, lower-level pad, changed rhythm, and changed register. Ask separately which variant makes the lead easiest to follow and which best serves the musical atmosphere. That separation of questions is critical. A version that improves the transcription score might make the arrangement too empty. An artistic decision may quite reasonably favor a little perceptual ambiguity.

Spatial listening supplies a further lesson. Compare a centered mono recording to a version with delayed stereo copies. Monitor once through stereo headphones and once through speakers. Fold each to mono using a defined coefficient rule and compare the result. If the widened version collapses or changes color in mono, we can analyze the phase relationship mathematically. But whether the stereo width is worth the trade-off is still a musical decision. Write down both the technical finding and the judgment.

A robust production experiment should preserve its provenance. When rendering with DawDreamer, save the source notes or PCM inputs, processing graph, plugin versions, presets, random seeds where relevant, and export settings. If a plugin produces non-deterministic output, note that exact waveform repetition may not be expected. In Ardour, create a clearly named snapshot for each candidate, preserve the route topology and automation, and export the exact comparison range. Human labels such as best_final_really_final are not useful experimental identifiers. Explicit treatment IDs, settings, and reference paths are.

We can also make comparisons fair to different agents. Suppose Agent A changes both balance and compression, while Agent B changes the arrangement. A single listening comparison cannot tell which mechanism caused a preference. One strategy is to ask each agent for a candidate and then run a separate controlled study that isolates the proposed change. Another is to acknowledge that an end-to-end preference measures the combined artistic result rather than a particular DSP improvement. Both strategies are legitimate, but the claim must match the design.

A useful review sheet has a passage identifier and time range; the intended musical target; the listening setup; the processing differences; measured levels; and three independent observations. First, what signal property changed? Second, what did the listener distinguish under the stated conditions? Third, which version was preferred and why? Separating those fields makes it possible to discover that a measurable technical difference was inaudible, or that an audible difference was not preferred. Negative results are informative. They can save us from endlessly tuning parameters that do not solve the actual problem.

What about statistical confidence? Formal listening experiments require more thought than a handful of quick votes. We must choose trial count, balance orders, guard against learning and fatigue, consider whether repeated judgments are independent, and avoid selecting whichever passage happens to produce the desired result after many tests. Confidence intervals and effect sizes can be useful, but only when the outcome and sampling procedure support them. Adding a p-value to an uncontrolled personal listening session is not scientific rigor. A well-described anecdote is more honest than a falsely precise test statistic.

Nor should the science displace the musical purpose. A melody may be intentionally buried behind distortion. The chorus may deliberately feel crowded. A solo voice may need more audible room reflections rather than fewer. Listener preference and artistic meaning cannot always be reduced to timbral similarity to a reference. The most defensible workflow combines analysis with an account of intention. It asks what the work is trying to do, identifies the signal changes that might help, and then listens under conditions that can reveal the relevant effect.

A final exercise closes the loop. Take one original musical passage and write down one criticism in a falsifiable form: for example, listeners cannot follow the four-note lead motive during bars five and six. Create two candidate edits and one unchanged control, all in the same session. Keep a record of what you changed. Ask a small number of listeners to report the motive or compare the mixes, without naming the preferred result in advance. If you only have yourself, still randomize the filenames and revisit the comparison later, acknowledging the limitations. Explain what your results actually show and what they do not.

A good producer is not someone who never doubts their ears. A good producer learns which doubts can be reduced by measurement, which can be reduced by controlled listening, and which remain questions of artistic choice. The value of a DAW is not merely that it can render a combination of stems. It gives us a reversible place to ask those questions, make one change, listen again, and preserve what we learned.

## Non-spoken companion: an exact discrimination calculation

Assume ten independent ABX trials, each with two equally likely answers under a null of guessing, and a fixed decision criterion selected before observation. Then \(K\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(10,0.5)\).

- \(P(K\geq 8)=[\binom{10}{8}+\binom{10}{9}+\binom{10}{10}]/2^{10}=(45+10+1)/1024=56/1024\approx 0.0546875\).
- \(P(K\geq 9)=(10+1)/1024\approx 0.0107422\).

These calculations are original and depend on the null assumptions; the demonstration is **not** a listening-study result. Repeated listening can violate independence, and a procedure chosen after seeing results invalidates this simple reading of a p-value.

## Comparison log, suitable for Ardour or DawDreamer

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Material | Original stems or generated note files and their checksums |
| Reference passage | Sample-accurate start/end or project bars plus tempo map |
| Candidate treatment | Exact graph, route, automation, plugin versions and parameters |
| Playback | Speaker/headphone model, level setting, room, mono/stereo mode |

## Research and claim map

- **Small-impairment listening assessment:** [ITU-R BS.1116-3 (2015), *Methods for the subjective assessment of small impairments in audio systems*](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1116-3-201502-I/en), Scope and test-method sections. This is a defined impairment protocol, not a ranking of musical arrangements.
- **Intermediate-quality MUSHRA:** [ITU-R BS.1534-3 (2015), *Method for the subjective assessment of intermediate quality level of audio systems*](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1534-3-201510-I/en), Scope and multi-stimulus method; hidden reference and anchor. Its validity does not extend automatically to subjective song-writing aesthetics.
- **General subjective evaluation:** [ITU-R BS.1284-2 (2019), *General methods for the subjective assessment of sound quality*](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1284/en), official recommendation scope. Useful framework, not a shortcut around experimental design.
- **Listening environment:** [EBU Tech 3276 (1998), *Listening Conditions for the Assessment of Mono & Stereo Sound*](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/tech3276), official summary. Requirements belong to its stated assessment context.
- **Listener training and reliability:** Sean E. Olive (2003), [“Differences in Performance and Preference of Trained versus Untrained Listeners in Loudspeaker Tests: A Case Study,” *Journal of the Audio Engineering Society* 51(9):806–825](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary/elibrary-page/?id=12206), abstract and experimental scope. Findings concern loudspeaker preference tests; not a theorem about artistic music preferences.
- **Controlled A/B/ABX exercises, binomial arithmetic, and the review sheet:** original instructional design. They are not results claimed from these sources.

## Exercises and open questions

1. Design a controlled comparison of a compressor setting. Which loudness-matching procedure could erase the difference you want to study?
2. Explain why a listener who gets 10/10 on ABX has not necessarily established that A is *better* than B.
3. Draw a processing-and-listening pipeline that keeps measurement, discrimination, and musical preference as separate outputs.
