# Why a global orthogonal transport reduces to matching

Suppose two planes in real four-dimensional space make two equal forty-five-degree principal angles. The direct rotation moves each principal plane separately. Yet an orthogonal map that couples the planes can have a smaller Schatten displacement cost when the exponent is one point one. This leaves a harder question: if an orthogonal map may mix all the principal directions at once, why should an optimal one ever be built from blocks involving at most two angles?

The real matching theorem answers that question. Its proof starts with a completely unrestricted optimizer, discovers small blocks inside it, and only then constructs a matching. We will follow that argument, including the step that excludes more complicated mixing.

## The geometry and the cost

Let K and L be equal-dimensional subspaces of a finite-dimensional real Euclidean space. We permit every orthogonal map T carrying K onto L. Orthogonal means that lengths and inner products are preserved. For an exponent p strictly between one and two, the cost is the sum of the p-th powers of the singular values of identity minus T. Minimizing this power sum is equivalent to minimizing the Schatten p norm itself.

The principal angles describe K and L independently of how we transport them. Imagine a pair of source and target lines in one principal plane, separated by an angle theta. Rotating through theta produces two displacement singular values, both equal to twice the sine of half theta. The corresponding p-th power cost is twice that chord length to power p.

A reflection across the angle bisector also carries the source line to the target. It has one displacement singular value equal to two and one equal to zero. Thus the best one-angle cost is the smaller of the rotation cost and two to power p. This is the weight of an unmatched angle, called a singleton.

For two principal angles, a four-dimensional orthogonal transporter may couple the planes. Its optimized cost is a pair weight. The optimization includes both determinant classes; restricting attention to the most familiar direct rotation would miss valid competitors. A matching selects disjoint angle pairs and leaves the other labels single. Its cost adds the appropriate pair and singleton weights.

For a numerical illustration, take two angles of forty-five degrees and p equal one point one. Two separate direct rotations cost about two point nine eight one in the p-th power objective. A coupled candidate that rotates one two-plane by ninety degrees and fixes its perpendicular plane costs about two point nine two eight. Optimizing the positive-determinant pair weight lowers it further to about two point nine two one.

The challenge is proving that the smallest such matching cost bounds an arbitrary orthogonal T. We cannot assume that T already preserves these planes.

## First choose a global optimizer

There is at least one admissible T: choose orthonormal bases of K and L and complete them to bases of the whole space. The resulting orthogonal map carries K onto L. All orthogonal matrices form a compact set in finite dimension, and the transport constraint is closed. Because the cost varies continuously, there is a globally minimizing transporter. Call it T star.

Every other admissible transporter costs at least as much as T star. We therefore need to extract a matching from T star. We do not need to decompose every possible competitor, which could be far from stationary.

To learn the structure of T star, imagine a small motion that stays admissible. Split the ambient space into K and its perpendicular complement. Apply a small orthogonal change of coordinates separately on those two parts, then apply T star. The result still sends K onto L. Such changes are generated by skew-symmetric matrices that preserve K and its complement.

At a minimum, the first derivative of the cost along every such motion is zero. The calculation creates a self-adjoint spectral-gradient operator, after using complex coordinates to record rotation phases. Because we tested actual real motions, the equation applies to our real problem. It says that the gradient has zero compression onto K and zero compression onto its perpendicular complement.

Here is what those two zero compressions mean. Write a vector as a component in K plus a perpendicular component. The gradient cannot send either component back into its own part. It crosses between the two parts. If we introduce the reflection that acts as plus one on K and minus one on its complement, conjugating the gradient by that reflection changes its sign.

This sign reversal is the structural opening. A positive gradient eigenvalue must have a corresponding negative eigenvalue, and the reflection pairs their eigenspaces. The pairing holds even when an eigenvalue is repeated.

## Why blocks have size two or four

A general real orthogonal matrix can contain many simultaneous rotations. Why can the minimizing one be divided into much smaller pieces?

The gradient is obtained from the eigenphases of the orthogonal map. A rotation through phase phi has displacement chord twice the sine of half phi. Differentiating its p-th power gives p times the chord to power p minus one, multiplied by cosine of half phi. This derivative starts at zero, rises to one maximum, and then falls to zero as the phase approaches a half-turn. The turning point occurs when the square of the tangent of half phi equals p minus one. At exponent three halves, the maximum occurs near seventy-one degrees: one lower phase and one higher phase can give the same gradient value, but no third phase can. Thus for exponents strictly between one and two, fixing a positive gradient level leaves at most two permitted phase branches. So on that level the optimizer acts through a two-phase operator. This is a restriction caused by stationarity and the scalar shape of the Schatten cost, rather than a restriction imposed at the outset.

Two phases still need not determine a preferred basis when an eigenvalue has multiplicity. The argument uses the reflection of K and complex conjugation, which remembers that the original matrix is real. Invariant complex lines and planes can be chosen compatibly with these symmetries. Pair each positive-gradient piece with its negative-gradient reflection and descend to the original real space. The resulting mutually orthogonal blocks have real dimension two or four. Each block is preserved by both the actual optimizer and the projection onto K.

There is one remaining piece: the zero-gradient space. The phase equation there makes the optimizer an involution, with eigenvalues plus or minus one. Its negative-phase projection and the projection onto K are two orthogonal projections. The elementary two-projection decomposition splits their common space into real lines or planes. Thus the zero-gradient part is also covered. We have neither discarded stationary directions nor assumed that the principal angles are distinct.

We now have a complete orthogonal decomposition of the original space into fixed directions and small reducing blocks. On a nontrivial two-dimensional block, K occupies one dimension. On a four-dimensional block, it occupies two. The same block sizes hold for the transported subspace because the optimizer preserves each block.

## Turn local blocks into globally optimal blocks

Small blocks alone do not prove the lower bound. Consider one of these blocks and suppose a different orthogonal map could carry its source intersection onto its target intersection at lower cost. Keep T star unchanged on every other block, and replace its action on this one block by the cheaper map.

The assembled map would remain orthogonal and would still carry all of K onto L. Since singular-value power sums add over orthogonal blocks, its total cost would strictly decrease. That contradicts the global minimality of T star.

Therefore every two-dimensional restriction achieves its true one-angle minimum, and every four-dimensional restriction achieves its true two-angle minimum, including the relevant determinant choices. Fixed directions contribute zero. This replacement argument turns the structural decomposition into an exact sum of optimized small-block costs.

## Recover the original angle labels

A two-dimensional block supplies one principal angle; a four-dimensional block supplies two. Because both source and target projections reduce each block, the combined list of block angles is exactly the original list of principal angles of K and L, including multiplicities. No angle can disappear or be counted twice. Assign one label to each two-dimensional block and two labels to each four-dimensional block. This is a matching on the original angle indices. For example, suppose there are three positive angles. A decomposition with one four-dimensional block and one two-dimensional block makes exactly one pair and one singleton. Three two-dimensional blocks leave all three angles unmatched. The proof has shown that some globally optimal transporter fits one of those possibilities, even though the original search allowed mixing among all three planes.

Zero angles can be removed without changing the optimal cost: directions shared by K and L need no displacement. A four-dimensional block's unrestricted pair cost is the smaller of its positive-determinant pair cost and the sum of its two singleton minima. If the second choice wins, replace that pair by two unmatched labels. We obtain a matching using positive-angle labels and the designated pair weights, with exactly the cost of T star.

Let M denote the least cost over all these finite matchings. The matching extracted from T star has cost equal to T star's cost. Hence M is no larger than that cost, and therefore no larger than the cost of any admissible T. That is the global lower bound.

For the other inequality, start from any matching. Choose minimizing real orthogonal transports in its separate two- and four-dimensional principal-angle blocks, and use the identity on the common complement. Their orthogonal direct sum is an admissible whole-space transporter, whose cost is exactly the matching cost. Choose a least-cost matching and the upper bound meets the lower bound.

Thus for equal-dimensional real subspaces, in finite dimension and for p strictly between one and two, the minimum displacement power sum equals the finite matching minimum. The key reason is the chain from real stationarity to complete small blocks, from global optimality to optimal restrictions, and from those blocks to the unchanged principal-angle labels. The proof covers repeated angles, right angles, and common fixed directions. No prior block-preserving assumption is placed on the competing orthogonal maps.

## Worked proof of the unrestricted matching lower bound

The principal issue is the inequality against **every** ambient real orthogonal transporter, without assuming any prescribed invariant principal planes. The following proof roadmap identifies the finite-dimensional steps and their Lean anchors.

### 1. Fix the optimization problem before choosing blocks

Let \(E\) be a finite-dimensional real inner-product space, let \(K,L\subseteq E\) have the same dimension \(r\), and put \(P=P_K\), \(Q=P_L\). For \(1<p<2\), set
\[
\mathcal F(K,L)=\{T\in O(E):TP T^{\mathsf T}=Q\},\qquad
C_p(T)=\operatorname{Tr}|I-T|^p=\sum_{j=1}^{\dim E}s_j(I-T)^p.
\]
Any pair of orthonormal bases for \(K,L\), extended to ambient bases, gives an element of \(\mathcal F\). The fiber is a nonempty closed subset of the compact orthogonal group, so \(C_p\) attains a global minimum at some \(T_*\). This compactness argument is essential: the subsequent stationarity argument is applied to \(T_*\), not to an arbitrary competitor.

A real skew-adjoint \(A\) commuting with \(P\) gives an admissible path
\[
T(t)=T_*e^{tA},\qquad
T(t)PT(t)^{\mathsf T}=T_*PT_*^{\mathsf T}=Q.
\]
Thus \(\left.\frac{d}{dt}C_p(T(t))\right|_{t=0}=0\). In the complexification used by `SchattenRealStationarity.lean`, the corresponding self-adjoint Schatten gradient \(G\) satisfies
\[
PGP=0,\qquad (I-P)G(I-P)=0,
\qquad JGJ=-G,\quad J=2P-I.
\]
The last identity follows by expanding \(G\) into its four \(P/(I-P)\) corners; both diagonal corners vanish. Complex conjugation \(\kappa\) also gives \(\kappa G\kappa=-G\). Both identities are consequences of *real feasible variations*. Allowing arbitrary complex variations would change the admissible problem.

### 2. Why the positive-gradient blocks have bounded dimension

Write the displacement chord for a phase \(\varphi\in[0,\pi]\) as
\[
a(\varphi)=2\sin(\varphi/2),\qquad
\frac{d}{d\varphi}a(\varphi)^p
=p\,a(\varphi)^{p-1}\cos(\varphi/2).
\]
The derivative has a single interior maximum, since its sign of change switches at
\[
\tan^2(\varphi/2)=p-1.
\]
For \(1<p<2\), each strictly positive gradient value therefore admits at most two phase branches. This is the scalar input of the two-phase spectral decomposition; repeated eigenvalues do **not** imply that the entire associated eigenspace has dimension two.

The anticommutation with \(J\) pairs the \(+g\) and \(-g\) gradient eigenspaces. The reality conjugation and its composition with \(J\) allow a complete orthogonal subdivision of a two-phase level into small compatible complex invariant blocks. Descending their reflected pairs gives real reducing subspaces of dimensions two or four, with source rank respectively one or two. The argument also covers the maximal-gradient level.

The zero-gradient kernel requires its own analysis: on that kernel, the optimal unitary has only phases \(+1\) and \(-1\), hence is a reflection. Two-projection geometry, applied to \(P\) and its negative-phase projection, produces real invariant lines or planes. The complete family of real blocks therefore covers all of \(E\) and reduces both \(P\) and the **actual** \(T_*\). See `SchattenRealGradientBlocks.lean`, `SchattenRealKernelBlocks.lean`, and `SchattenRealDecomposition.lean`.

### 3. The replacement argument upgrades a block decomposition to a lower bound

Let \(E=\bigoplus_{b\in B}E_b\) be the complete real reducing decomposition. Put \(K_b=K\cap E_b\), \(L_b=L\cap E_b\), and \(T_b=T_*|_{E_b}\). Then \(T_b(K_b)=L_b\) and
\[
C_p(T_*)=\sum_{b\in B}C_p(T_b).
\]
If some restricted transporter \(S_b:E_b\to E_b\) with \(S_b(K_b)=L_b\) had \(C_p(S_b)<C_p(T_b)\), replacing just \(T_b\) by \(S_b\) in the direct sum would yield a member of \(\mathcal F(K,L)\) with lower cost. Thus each restriction is a **global minimizer** of its unrestricted block problem. The relevant block costs are the actual two-dimensional rank-one and four-dimensional rank-two minima, not costs estimated from a conveniently selected principal-plane rotation. This step is captured by `SchattenRealRestrictions.exists_complete_globally_minimizing_blocks_real` and `SchattenRealAngleCosts.exists_real_schatten_angle_cost_decomposition`.

The principal-angle multiset is additive under reducing orthogonal sums of the two projections. Therefore the angles from the \(K_b,L_b\) restrictions reproduce the **original** principal angles of \(K,L\), including their multiplicities. This gives a matching of the original labelled angle indices: rank-one blocks become fixed points, and rank-two blocks become two-cycles. Zero-gradient lines and zero-angle labels are handled separately and may be eliminated from the positive-angle matching at unchanged cost.

### 4. The exact matching weights and the determinant-class reduction

Set \(a(\theta)=2\sin(\theta/2)\). For a singleton positive angle \(\theta\), the unrestricted two-dimensional weight is
\[
c_p(\theta)=\min\bigl\{2a(\theta)^p,\,2^p\bigr\}.
\]
The first term comes from a plane rotation; the second from a reflection, whose displacement singular values are \((2,0)\).

For two angles \(\theta\ge\psi\ge0\), define \(h=(\theta+\psi)/2\), \(d=(\theta-\psi)/2\). The positive-determinant four-dimensional class has the exact optimized weight
\[
e_p^+(\theta,\psi)
=2\min_{d\le t\le h}\Bigl(a(h+t)^p+a(h-t)^p\Bigr).
\]
The scalar phase analysis identifies the minimizer as \(t=\max\{d,\operatorname{optimalPhase}(p,h)\}\). For \(p=11/10\) and \(\theta=\psi=\pi/4\), direct evaluation gives \(e_p^+\approx2.920804\), attained near \(t=0.728592\) radians. Compare the separate direct-rotation cost \(2.980689\) and the ninety-degree coupled candidate cost \(2.928171\). These are three distinct feasible costs, with the optimized positive-class pair the least. The full unrestricted pair cost satisfies the identity
\[
e_p(\theta,\psi)
=\min\bigl\{e_p^+(\theta,\psi),\;c_p(\theta)+c_p(\psi)\bigr\}.
\]
This formula is important: a cheaper negative-determinant choice may be replaced, *at the same cost*, by two singleton labels. Consequently the global matching can be stated using only \(e_p^+\) edges and \(c_p\) singletons, without excluding either determinant class from the original transporter search.

Let \(I=\{i:\theta_i>0\}\) contain one index for **each** positive principal angle, with repeated values given separate labels. For an involution \(s:I\to I\), define
\[
 w_p(s)=\sum_{s(i)=i}c_p(\theta_i)
   +\frac12\sum_{s(i)\ne i}e_p^+(\theta_i,\theta_{s(i)}),
 \qquad M_p=\min_{s^2=\mathrm{id}}w_p(s).
\]
The half factor ensures that each pair appears once. For three positive labels, the four possible structures have weights \(c_1+c_2+c_3\), \(e_{12}^++c_3\), \(e_{13}^++c_2\), and \(e_{23}^++c_1\).

The complete-block and angle-label extraction shows that for the actual global minimizer \(T_*\), **some** such involution \(s_*\) satisfies
\[
 C_p(T_*)=w_p(s_*),\qquad
 \boxed{M_p\le C_p(T_*)\le C_p(T)\quad\text{for every }T\in\mathcal F(K,L).}
\]
This is the unrestricted lower-bound direction; the second inequality is simply the definition of \(T_*\) as a global minimizer. It does not require the arbitrary \(T\) to have any block structure.

The converse uses the actual principal-angle planes. For each matching \(s\), take the corresponding optimal two- or four-dimensional real transporter on disjoint principal-angle blocks, and use the identity on the common complement. The orthogonal direct sum \(T_s\) is admissible and satisfies \(C_p(T_s)=w_p(s)\). In particular, a least-cost matching gives
\[
\min_{T\in\mathcal F(K,L)}C_p(T)\le M_p.
\]
Combining both directions yields the global matching formula and ambient attainment.
