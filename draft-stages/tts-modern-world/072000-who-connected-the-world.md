# Who Connected the World? The Internet and Mobile Revolution, 1991–2001

## A link changes what a computer can do

In the early nineteen nineties, imagine a scientist sitting at a workstation and finding a document by following a link from another document. Neither computer networks nor electronic documents were new. What was changing was the ease with which information could move between machines, institutions and programs that had not been designed as one system. At CERN, the European physics laboratory, Tim Berners-Lee and collaborators had developed software for publishing and reading linked documents. Its components included addresses for resources, a way for programs to exchange requests, and a markup language for expressing links inside a page. The World Wide Web was not the internet itself. It was a service built using internet connectivity, and the difference mattered because an open web could spread across a network that already carried mail, file transfers and other services.

On April thirtieth, nineteen ninety-three, CERN announced that core Web software would be freely available for people to use, copy and modify. Later licensing arrangements reinforced its openness. This was a technical and institutional choice, not a guarantee that every website or online service would remain free of charge. Many separate actors helped make the Web useful: CERN engineers, university programmers, browser authors, librarians, public-network operators, standards committees and people who began publishing documents of their own. A system of links becomes valuable when people trust that the links will continue to work across different computers. Its inventors could not achieve that by creating one proprietary program that everyone was required to purchase from them.

The browser known as Mosaic, developed at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications in the United States, made the Web easier to approach in nineteen ninety-three. Other browsers already existed; Mosaic was not the first browser, and the Web did not become useful in a single afternoon. But a graphical program that could display text and images together, and that could be installed on widely used computers, changed who could comfortably explore it. In a university lab, a user could follow a link from a research note to an international database without first learning the commands of every remote machine. For an early publisher, the problem shifted from exchanging a file with known colleagues toward presenting information to people who might arrive from anywhere.

## Who paid for the network?

Much of the internet's earlier infrastructure had been financed by governments and universities. In the United States, the National Science Foundation supported NSFNET, a high-capacity backbone that connected academic networks. Other countries built their own educational and commercial systems. An early acceptable-use policy restricted certain forms of commercial traffic on the NSF backbone. As demand grew, regional networks and private carriers began providing commercial connections. The NSFNET backbone was retired in April nineteen ninety-five, as private long-distance internet infrastructure expanded. That date did not mark the birth of the internet or the instant end of public involvement. It marked a change in how one particularly influential part of its physical infrastructure was operated and financed.

Networks require expensive things that disappear from the screen: cables, switches, routers, technical workers, agreements about traffic and reliable electricity. A useful distinction is between permission to speak a common technical language and the price of reaching someone who speaks it. Internet protocols made it possible for independently owned networks to exchange data. They did not eliminate the cost of long-distance connectivity or ensure that households could afford a computer and a connection. Public research helped produce shared technical capabilities; private firms invested in building access and competing services. The resulting mixture does not fit a simple account of technology as either a public gift or the product of entrepreneurs acting alone.

The new infrastructure supported businesses that differed from earlier online services. A seller could publish a catalog that visitors reached through a browser, rather than require each customer to join a single proprietary network. A news organization could put reports on a website while still selling printed newspapers. Search, directories, advertising and online payments developed into industries. These possibilities altered the economics of distribution: producing one more copy of a digital page could cost relatively little, while finding the audience, verifying identity, processing payment and keeping a server available still required resources. Companies fought to become the first page customers visited, the search system they trusted or the platform where they kept returning.

## Standards are politics in working clothes

An open network can still become fragmented if software companies change the rules for their own advantage. The browser competition of the nineteen nineties brought new features, faster programs and easier installations, but also pages that worked in one browser and failed in another. The World Wide Web Consortium, founded in nineteen ninety-four, attempted to develop common technical standards. The work might sound remote from politics. Yet if an independent publisher must redesign every page for a dominant company's browser, the dominant company can shape who can afford to participate. Standardization helps separate the ability to publish from the commercial success of a particular software vendor. The battles over technical compatibility were also battles over competition and control.

Domain names offered another example. Human beings remember names more readily than numerical network addresses, but a name is only useful if the system can direct users to the same destination. Someone must coordinate important parts of that directory. In nineteen ninety-eight, the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers, or ICANN, was established to take on coordinating functions through a nonprofit organization with international participation, emerging from earlier U.S. government arrangements. Critics questioned who had a voice and how much influence the United States retained. Supporters emphasized the need for stable technical administration without turning every domain-name dispute into a treaty negotiation. Coordinating an address system and governing everything people say online are not the same task.

This separation of layers is central to the history. The internet is a network of interconnected networks; the Web is one information system using it; a browser is one kind of program for navigating the Web; a company that sells goods through a website owns neither the network nor the standard on which the site depends. People often speak of these things as a single invention because they encounter them through one screen. But conflicts about copyright, access, advertising, jurisdiction and privacy arise at different layers. An open standard can coexist with a highly concentrated advertising market. A country can connect universities to the internet while keeping household access expensive. Technical openness creates opportunities without deciding how power will be distributed.

## A phone becomes a means of belonging

At the same time, a different communications transformation was unfolding through mobile telephony. The International Telecommunication Union reported roughly eleven million mobile cellular subscriptions worldwide in nineteen ninety and more than three hundred million by the end of nineteen ninety-eight. Those figures count subscriptions, not necessarily distinct people. Behind them were changing technologies for handling radio signals, smaller handsets, expanding networks and new arrangements for paying for service. In countries where obtaining a fixed telephone line could take years, mobile service could offer an alternative rather than merely an accessory. The path from a phone to another person did not need to pass through the old home telephone network in exactly the same way.

Consider a market trader who needs to learn whether a shipment has arrived, or a family with a relative working in another city. A telephone call can reduce the cost of uncertainty. That does not mean every trader could suddenly afford a mobile handset. Devices, airtime, battery charging and coverage cost money; subscriptions could be shared; and urban network construction often preceded rural service. In many places, prepaid billing would become particularly important because it allowed people without a conventional monthly contract to control spending. The communications revolution therefore had several overlapping geographies. Wealthier households might use both fixed lines and mobiles, while others gained their first practical telephone access through a mobile connection.

Mobile service and internet access are often folded into the same story, but in the nineteen nineties they remained technically and socially distinct for most users. The typical mobile handset was not the later smartphone with a general-purpose browser and a fast data connection. People sent calls and, increasingly, short text messages. Internet users more often connected through institutional networks or computers attached to telephone lines. The number of subscriptions grew fast, while the ability to study online, publish a website or search a medical database remained dependent on computers, literacy, training and bandwidth. The fact that communications capacity was expanding did not imply that different services, languages or populations were benefiting equally.

## The market discovers a future before it arrives

New businesses attracted investment on a scale that became difficult to distinguish from speculation. Venture capitalists financed online retailers, search firms, portals and companies whose plans sometimes depended on the expectation that audience numbers would quickly turn into profit. Stock-market investors bought shares in companies that were building real infrastructure and in others whose business models were barely proven. In March two thousand, the technology-heavy Nasdaq index reached a peak and then began a prolonged decline. Many companies failed. The collapse did not make the protocols, fiber connections or useful online services vanish. The value of an invention and the price investors are willing to pay for a company using it are different questions.

The crash is sometimes presented as a lesson that the internet was only hype. That would be just as misleading as claiming that every company funded during the boom had a sustainable future. Overpriced shares can finance real equipment and real work, and bankruptcies can destroy jobs and retirement savings. A durable network can emerge from a period of exaggerated financial claims. Some technologies survived because they genuinely reduced the cost of communication; other products had weak demand even when their websites loaded quickly. Public history should avoid treating the fortunes of a stock index as a direct measure of the usefulness of a communications system. Investors and users were answering different questions.

## The divide is made of institutions

The phrase digital divide became common because connection was becoming economically and politically important while access remained distributed unequally. Income affected the price a family could pay for equipment and service. Education affected the confidence to use unfamiliar software. Language affected whether a useful page existed in a form one could read. Geography affected the chance that a provider would build a line to a village. Political regulation could encourage competition, restrict speech or protect incumbents. A national average could conceal inequalities between rural and urban regions, women and men, public schools and private offices. Counting internet users was an important beginning, not a complete measure of what people could do once connected.

The Web also changed whose information could travel. A small scientific group, an activist organization or a migrant community could publish documents without first purchasing printing equipment and access to international distribution. Yet publicity was never the same as guaranteed attention, and a website could spread an error as easily as a correction. Radio, television, newspapers and personal networks did not disappear. People mixed technologies: a mobile call arranged a meeting; a broadcaster reached an audience with little internet access; an online archive preserved something previously available only locally. New forms of communication entered existing institutions instead of instantly replacing them.

By the turn of the millennium, questions that looked technical had become enduring public questions. Who owns the infrastructure? Who sets the protocols? Who can register a name, reach a server, keep a service running or make a complaint? Who can speak and who is likely to be heard? The history of the commercial internet and mobile telephony is neither a tale of inevitable freedom nor one of unavoidable corporate domination. It is a history of publicly funded research, shared standards, business investment, ordinary users and political decisions. The network made new forms of connection possible. What society chose to connect, and on whose terms, was still being negotiated.
