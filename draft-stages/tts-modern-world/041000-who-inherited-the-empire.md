# Who Inherited the Empire? Independence, Partition, and Revolution, 1945–1949

## The victors did not own the meaning of freedom

In August 1945, the Second World War ended in Asia. For many people living under colonial government, the question was not simply whether the fighting had stopped. It was who would govern when the military occupiers left, and whether a distant European power could return to a territory whose people had watched its authority collapse. The war had shown that imperial states were vulnerable. It had mobilized colonized populations for military service and labor, reshaped economies, and placed competing promises about freedom into circulation. But colonial rule did not disappear automatically when Japan surrendered. Independence was claimed, organized, negotiated, and in many places fought for. The postwar world contained several meanings of liberation that could collide.

We should not mistake a series of independence dates for a single global script. The Netherlands claimed sovereignty over a colony that Indonesian nationalists declared independent in 1945. Britain negotiated a transfer of power in South Asia that divided two immense and interconnected regions. In Vietnam, a revolutionary government declared independence, but French authorities later attempted to restore colonial control and war followed. All three were shaped by World War II. Yet they differed in their local political institutions, wartime occupations, languages of legitimate rule, religious communities, outside interests, and the forms of violence accompanying state formation. Comparing them is useful only if comparison does not erase the histories within each territory.

## Indonesia declares itself a republic

On August 17, 1945, Sukarno proclaimed Indonesian independence in Jakarta, alongside Mohammad Hatta and a broader nationalist movement. A Dutch Rijksmuseum exhibit preserves the scene as an event on a porch and recalls Sukarno’s earlier role in organizing for independence. But the declaration was not merely the work of one speaker. Nationalist organizations, youth activists, local networks, political prisoners, and communities who had endured both Dutch colonial institutions and Japanese occupation helped make a republic imaginable. Sukarno had participated in politics under Japanese occupation; that relationship became one of the controversies around his leadership. The declaration asserted a sovereignty the Netherlands had not agreed to relinquish.

The date matters because it presents Indonesia as an actor rather than an object of Dutch diplomacy. The Netherlands would eventually transfer sovereignty in December 1949, after years of war and international pressure. For Indonesian national history, August 1945 is the declaration of independence; for the former colonial power, official language for many years centered the 1949 transfer. An open-access academic chapter on Indonesian historiography discusses the political meanings of these competing dates. Choosing one date without explaining the distinction risks importing a theory of legitimate power: did Indonesians become independent when they declared their republic, or only when their former ruler acknowledged it? The question reaches beyond chronology into the meaning of colonial sovereignty itself.

The Dutch government sought to restore authority, with negotiations alternating with armed campaigns. Dutch authorities called major offensives “police actions,” language that portrayed military operations against an independence movement as matters of domestic order. Indonesians had reason to name them differently: a foreign state was moving armies through territory claimed by an independent republic. An ANU Press study describes the July 1947 Dutch offensive, Operation Product, in terms of strategic infrastructure and resource-rich regions. That context matters because sovereignty was also about ports, plantation districts, roads, and revenues. A government unable to command transport and resources would struggle to make independence more than a declaration.

A large Dutch and Indonesian research program published in 2022 concluded that an earlier official Dutch claim—that extreme violence was limited to exceptional excesses by an otherwise correctly behaving military—could not be maintained. The researchers documented the broader place of extreme violence within the war of independence and examined Dutch political responsibility. That reckoning was a development in the writing of history as well as a judgment about the 1940s. It shows why official descriptions of a colonial war should not be accepted simply because they appeared in a government document. At the same time, Indonesian revolutionary violence, including attacks on Dutch, mixed-ancestry, and Chinese civilians, requires its own scrutiny rather than omission. Recognizing multiple perpetrators does not dissolve the difference between a colonial reconquest and a claim for national independence.

Indonesian leaders and factions did not agree on every question of the new state. Diplomacy, mass mobilization, armed guerrilla struggle, negotiations about federalism, and disputes over leadership were pursued simultaneously. The Netherlands’ preferred federal arrangements raised a question familiar to many postcolonial societies: could decentralization protect local autonomy, or would it preserve former imperial influence by fragmenting an independence movement? The final 1949 transfer did not settle every contested territory; the western part of New Guinea remained under Dutch rule until 1962. A flag could mark a nation’s arrival on the international stage while unresolved claims about territory and political belonging persisted.

## India and Pakistan: freedom through a border

South Asia’s passage from British rule followed another path, though its anticolonial movements had been organizing long before the war. The British Raj had relied on Indian soldiers, taxation, administration, and railway networks; Indian political organizations had contested who was entitled to speak for the population and what a representative government ought to mean. The Second World War sharpened the contradiction between Britain’s stated defense of freedom and its continued government of India. But anticolonial unity did not mean agreement about the structure of an independent country. The Indian National Congress, the All-India Muslim League, regional parties, princely rulers, Sikhs, and other communities had different fears and political commitments.

By the middle of the 1940s the challenge was not just removing British authority. It was deciding whether a single state could give adequate political security and representation to people who differed in religious affiliation, region, language, class, and historical experience. Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the Muslim League argued for Pakistan, while Congress leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru preferred a united India, although their positions evolved through failed negotiations. Religious identity did not prescribe one person’s political choice, and neither organization represented every person it claimed to defend. The fact that Muslims formed majorities in some regions and minorities in others made the proposed border an especially consequential abstraction.

In June 1947 the final British viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, announced a rapid transfer of power to two dominions. The boundary commissions were tasked with dividing Punjab in the northwest and Bengal in the east. These were not empty regions on a survey sheet: they contained farms, canals, cities, pilgrimage routes, kin networks, markets, schools, and political histories. The decisions were compressed into a matter of weeks. Pakistan came into being on August 14, and India on August 15; the boundary awards were made public on August 17. Thus some people celebrated the end of colonial rule before they could know definitively which state would govern their district. This sequence is established in both British and Indian official records.

The violence was not merely an unfortunate administrative error. Armed groups and mobs attacked people identified with other religious communities; fear, rumor, and local struggles for power accelerated flight. Trains and roads became routes of escape and sites of attack. People were separated, women were abducted and subjected to sexual violence, and many households lost property they had occupied for generations. The broad scale of migration is clear, while mortality estimates vary widely and must not be stated as precise census results. A Cambridge University Press scholarly introduction discusses the uncertainty and the distinct mechanisms of death and injury. That uncertainty in numbers does not weaken the conclusion that Partition caused mass displacement and killing.

A line on a map can become a story about religion alone, but that is too simple. A person fleeing Punjab might have been driven by a locally organized attack rather than by an abstract desire for national separation. A family in Bengal could make a different calculation about remaining or departing; some movements occurred years after the initial independence ceremonies. Nor should we assume that every person who suffered agreed with the leaders of the new state to which they moved. The British National Archives provides a useful overview of the boundary process; oral-history initiatives described by the U.S. National Endowment for the Humanities recover experiences that the commission’s papers cannot record. The sources answer different kinds of questions, and both matter.

The political achievement of independence and the human catastrophe of Partition must both remain visible. The end of British colonial rule meant that people who had been ruled by an imperial power could now build sovereign institutions. But independence did not automatically produce equal security for every resident, particularly refugees, minority communities, women whose claims were mediated by family and state authorities, or inhabitants of princely territories facing contested accession. Two new states had to build administrations while managing populations in flight, food distribution, military forces, and disputes about assets and borders. The work of making a government began amid the consequences of making the boundary.

## Vietnam: the return of an old empire into a new war

On September 2, 1945, the same date as Japan’s formal surrender ceremony, Ho Chi Minh proclaimed the independence of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in Hanoi. Japanese defeat had shattered the political and military conditions through which France had maintained its colonial claims. Vietnamese revolutionary organizations had built networks during the war and moved to establish an independent government. The declaration was a statement of national aspiration and a claim to authority. Whether that claim would be recognized by the victorious states was a different matter. France sought to restore its position in Indochina, and the interaction of Vietnamese revolutionary politics, foreign intervention, and Cold War calculations would turn independence into another long conflict.

The United States National Archives preserves a February 1946 telegram from Ho Chi Minh to President Harry Truman asking for the Viet Minh to participate in discussions of Vietnam’s postwar status. Truman did not reply. That document is small but instructive: a movement seeking independence tried to address international powers in the language of wartime legitimacy, while those powers made decisions within their own strategic priorities. A request to an American president did not mean that all Vietnamese revolutionaries had identical politics or that Washington alone determined the outcome. It reveals a diplomatic attempt to be heard and a silence in response, not a settled counterfactual about what would have happened had a reply been sent.

By December 1946, war between French forces and the Viet Minh had begun, the conflict now generally called the First Indochina War. The struggle over who could establish legitimate authority in Vietnam would become entangled with the division of the wider world into opposing Cold War blocs. Still, starting the explanation in 1950 or with later American involvement hides the earlier fight over postcolonial sovereignty. Vietnamese independence was declared in 1945; a French attempt to reassert control helped make war the immediate context of the new state’s existence. Here again the moment when a people claimed freedom and the moment when a former empire recognized it were not the same.

## Independence from what, and for whom?

These cases do not show that violence was the inevitable price of independence. They show the interaction of particular political choices with empires already structured by coercion and inequality. Indonesian leaders faced a Dutch state that used force to contest their proclaimed republic. Congress, the Muslim League, and British officials accepted a rapid divided transfer of power amid a breakdown of order in South Asia. Vietnamese revolutionaries confronted a returning French claim of colonial sovereignty. In each place, other approaches were imagined, disputed, and sometimes attempted; in each place, the people living under those decisions included more than the leaders whose names occupy official accounts.

We should also ask what independence made possible. It could create a government accountable to people who had been subordinate to a distant imperial center. It could open debates over land, language, women’s rights, education, labor, and the distribution of wealth. It could also give a new ruling coalition powers that minorities or political opponents feared. Calling a government postcolonial is a statement about a historical relationship; it does not prove that the government has solved every problem of representation. Similarly, observing that a new state struggled does not vindicate the empire that ruled before it. The relevant comparison is not between flawless independence and flawless colonial administration, but between concrete arrangements of power and their human consequences.

One of the most consequential changes after 1945 was that colonized peoples increasingly demanded to be treated as authors of the world order, not merely as territories to be administered by other states. That demand unsettled international organizations designed by the wartime victors and exposed contradictions in the language of universal rights. The era’s monuments may commemorate proclamations, transfer agreements, and national flags. The deeper history includes those ceremonies but also the borders that families crossed, the political choices they could not make, and the question that continues to outlive the paperwork: who gets to say what a nation is and who belongs to it?
