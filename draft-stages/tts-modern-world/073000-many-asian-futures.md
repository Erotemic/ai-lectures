# Many Asian Futures: Work, Reform and Politics Around 2000

## Many Asian futures

At the turn of the millennium, a traveler moving among Mumbai, Dhaka, Shanghai, Taipei, Seoul and Jakarta would not have found a single Asian model of modernization. Some governments were opening markets, others were rebuilding banks after crisis, and political movements were challenging authoritarian institutions. In one city a factory could be hiring migrant workers for export production; in another, an established company was struggling with debt; elsewhere a student campaign could change who was allowed to govern. Trade connected these places more closely, but their laws, histories and political possibilities remained different. To understand why the region mattered to the world economy, we need to examine the distinct bargains people made about ownership, work, representation and security.

In India, nineteen ninety-one was a hinge. A balance-of-payments crisis left the government struggling to finance imports and external obligations. Under Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh, authorities devalued the rupee, changed trade and investment rules and began reducing parts of a complex system of industrial licensing. The Reserve Bank of India's own chronology shows a sequence of reforms extending through the decade, not a single overnight switch from state control to laissez-faire. New opportunities appeared for private firms, while public institutions and political bargaining continued to shape the economy. The meaning of liberalization depended on whether one was an exporter, a consumer, a small producer facing competition or a worker seeking secure employment.

Reform did not eliminate the parliamentary politics of a large federal democracy. Coalition governments, state-level variation and demands for public services affected which measures were adopted and how quickly. New investment and expanding services created prominent growth centers, but much employment remained informal or agricultural. A national growth rate cannot show whether a rural household had adequate water, a reliable school or a stable market for crops. The phrase Indian economic miracle, when used without qualifications, can hide these institutional differences. A change in import duties may benefit a manufacturer that needs cheaper parts while threatening another employer exposed to new competition. The historical task is to ask which change occurred for whom and through what mechanism.

## The garment that connects households

In Bangladesh, export-oriented garment manufacturing was changing the relationship between households and wage work. Factories expanded in and around urban areas, supplying garments for international buyers. The international labor literature records large increases in factory employment through the nineteen nineties, with women accounting for a substantial majority of workers in estimates from that period. These figures are imperfect and often rely on industry associations, but the direction of change is clear. For many young women, factory employment offered paid work outside the household and a possible contribution to personal and family income. Employment could also expose them to long hours, unsafe conditions, low bargaining power and vulnerability to the decisions of distant buyers.

It would be wrong to describe this history either as the uncomplicated liberation of women by export markets or as evidence that wage labor had no value. Researchers including Naila Kabeer and Simeen Mahmud examined how women understood the opportunities and constraints of factory work. A regular wage could change a woman's say in household decisions; a factory rule or a family's expectations could still restrict her freedom. The garment could pass through design, financing, sewing, shipping and retail in different countries, while the person assembling it captured only a portion of its sale price. That difference was shaped not only by labor productivity but by buyer power, trade rules, union rights, and who controlled capital and contracts.

The growing service sector in India and the garment trade in Bangladesh were not identical forms of globalization. Software services could require higher education, English-language contracts and reliable telecommunications. Garment exports often relied on labor-intensive production under tight price and delivery deadlines. Both connected workers to customers they would never meet, and both showed that participation in world trade was mediated by local institutions. Neither automatically produced secure employment or a fair distribution of income. For a country trying to earn foreign exchange, these industries could be strategically important; for workers, the pressing questions remained the wage, the schedule, the ability to organize and whether work would still exist next month.

## China's factory and its registration book

China's reform-era growth accelerated the movement of people toward towns and coastal manufacturing centers. Factories combined domestic infrastructure with foreign orders, and local governments competed for investment. Yet moving to a city was not simply a matter of purchasing a bus ticket. The household registration system, commonly called hukou, shaped access to schooling, health care, housing and other services. Some restrictions were relaxed in the late nineteen nineties, especially in smaller towns, but access remained stratified. Rural migrants might contribute essential labor to urban production while lacking the same public entitlements as established urban residents. Their experience reveals how an export industry can depend on mobility while the welfare system still ties many rights to a place of registration.

China's entry into the World Trade Organization in December two thousand and one followed lengthy negotiations, not a sudden decision by the world's manufacturers to relocate. Tariffs, market access, rules for investors and the ability to trade under a common set of obligations all mattered. Membership could encourage investment and expand export opportunities; it also exposed domestic producers to competition and created pressures for restructuring. The WTO's accession record tells us exactly when China became a member, but it cannot tell us how a migrant worker's wage changed. For that we need evidence about factories, bargaining power, skills, labor protections and the economic regions where jobs were located.

The social bargain was complex. Workers and families could benefit from higher earnings or new urban opportunities while experiencing separation, difficult conditions and uncertainty about rights. State-owned enterprises could be reorganized or closed, placing a different set of workers at risk than those entering export factories. Urban consumers gained access to new goods while pollution and infrastructure demands grew. It is tempting to narrate China's rise as the triumph of a single economic idea. In fact, the period combined planned investments, local-government experimentation, foreign trade, market incentives, continued one-party political rule and constraints on migration. The combination, rather than any one ingredient in isolation, shaped the trajectory.

## Democracy and vulnerability after crisis

Indonesia's political change reveals another path. Suharto resigned in May nineteen ninety-eight amid economic crisis, protests and violence after decades of authoritarian rule. In June nineteen ninety-nine, Indonesians held competitive legislative elections under revised rules. Military representation had not been fully removed from the legislature, and democratization did not instantly end corruption or conflict. Nevertheless, organizations, parties and voters were reshaping political institutions. Economic insecurity and political reform were intertwined, but one did not logically guarantee the other. A currency crisis can undermine a ruler without automatically creating a just and durable replacement.

Taiwan offers a different chronology. Its political liberalization had developed through social movements, opposition organizing and reforms to institutions inherited from authoritarian Nationalist Party rule. A direct presidential election was held in nineteen ninety-six. In two thousand, Chen Shui-bian of the Democratic Progressive Party won the presidency, ending more than five decades of Nationalist Party control of that office. This was a transfer of executive power through an election rather than a coup or the collapse of the state. The transition occurred amid unresolved tensions with the People's Republic of China. The status of Taiwan and the risk of military confrontation formed part of the setting in which citizens negotiated democratic politics.

Japan, which had been a symbol of industrial growth for decades, was confronting the prolonged aftermath of a collapsed asset-price bubble. Property and equity values had fallen, banks held nonperforming loans and credit became harder to obtain. In nineteen ninety-seven and nineteen ninety-eight, serious financial distress and institutional failures exposed the costs of delaying recognition of losses. Japan's experience complicates the notion that a rich country with advanced technology must automatically enjoy rapid growth. A society can possess sophisticated industries and still face a weak banking system, falling prices and disappointing investment. The response depends on financial regulation, government action, firm balance sheets and household expectations, not simply on the skills of individual workers.

## Security is another kind of connection

Economic integration did not remove the possibility of armed confrontation. In May nineteen ninety-eight, India conducted nuclear tests, followed by tests in Pakistan. Both governments presented security arguments for their actions; the tests also had domestic political significance. The next year, fighting in the Kargil area of Kashmir demonstrated that nuclear weapons did not make limited war impossible. Rather than assuming the presence of nuclear arsenals produces either perfect deterrence or certain catastrophe, historians study the decisions commanders and politicians actually made, the risks they accepted and the diplomatic efforts undertaken to prevent wider conflict. The people of Kashmir were not a blank space on a strategic map. They lived in a region where competing sovereignty claims shaped daily security.

On the Korean peninsula, diplomacy produced a different headline. In June two thousand, leaders of North and South Korea met in Pyongyang and issued a joint declaration. The United Nations welcomed the summit as an opening for dialogue. A summit could make family reunion, cooperation and reduced tension imaginable without resolving all security disputes or guaranteeing political change. Similarly, Indonesia's electoral transition showed how political possibility could widen while conflicts about territory, military influence and civil rights persisted. The contrast matters. Neither trade alone nor international diplomacy alone predetermined peace. Institutions and citizens had to work within continuing disagreements about who belonged, who could vote and who controlled force.

## The limits of the regional label

Why do these very different histories so often appear under the heading Asian growth? Because aggregate output and trade indicators are useful: they show that production and exchange were moving. But a national statistic cannot show a factory worker's ability to refuse overtime, a rural child's access to school, a bank depositor's fear or the legitimacy of a constitutional change. The region's societies were linked by factories, cargo ships, technologies and financial markets, while each preserved its own disputes over development. Even the apparent beneficiaries of a new market could disagree about its rules. A business might want more open trade and fewer safety inspections; workers could want the new jobs and stronger protection at the same time.

By the opening of the twenty-first century, East and South Asia had demonstrated several possibilities at once. Japan's financial problems warned that prosperity could be undermined by the structure of bank balance sheets. Taiwan's elections showed a peaceful change of ruling party. China's expanding factories showed the power of large-scale production and international trade alongside restrictions on political participation and the unequal rights of migrants. India's reforms and Bangladesh's export industries revealed opportunities that were mediated by local politics, labor and public services. There was no single path followed by all of these societies, and no completed destination called modernization. There were institutions being revised, bargains being fought over and people using changing opportunities to shape their own lives.
