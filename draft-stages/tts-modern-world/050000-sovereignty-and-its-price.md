# Sovereignty and Its Price: Latin America in the Cold War

## Who decides what a country should produce?

In the middle of the twentieth century, Latin American governments and movements confronted a connected set of problems. A country might export coffee, bananas, copper or tin while importing the machines needed to process those products. Land might be concentrated in the hands of a few owners while the people cultivating it lived as tenants or poorly paid laborers. Foreign investors, local elites, trade unions and rural communities often wanted different things from economic policy. Then the Cold War made debates about land and development into questions of international security. Yet the people making those demands had histories extending well before Washington or Moscow classified them. To understand the postwar period, we should begin with their political and economic choices, not only with the responses of superpowers.

A basic distinction runs through this lecture. Economic sovereignty means more than a flag or a seat in an international organization. It includes the power to tax, regulate property, set public priorities and negotiate the terms of foreign trade. But control by a national government is not necessarily control by everyone within that nation. A policy can challenge foreign influence while strengthening a domestic ruling party. A land reform can expand opportunities for some farming families while failing to resolve the claims of others. Conversely, foreign involvement may sometimes provide resources while also limiting choices. These are empirical and political questions, not answers supplied by the words national, revolutionary or democratic.

## An economist's argument about the world economy

In 1949, the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch published a report for the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America, commonly abbreviated ECLA or, in Spanish, CEPAL. He challenged the assumption that countries specializing in primary exports could rely on free trade alone to achieve the same development opportunities as manufacturing centers. The argument placed Latin American economies in a wider system of core and periphery. Changes in productivity, trade prices and industrial capabilities did not necessarily reward the exporter of raw materials in the same way as the manufacturer of finished goods. The report became influential because it transformed dissatisfaction with an international division of labor into a debate about policy: industry, infrastructure, investment and the capacity to produce higher-value goods at home.

Prebisch did not prove that the price of every commodity would fall every year or that every tariff would promote development. His text posed a structural problem for research and public policy. If a country depended on a few export products, a downturn in their prices could reduce foreign exchange precisely when factories needed imported equipment. Governments could attempt import-substituting industrialization, encouraging domestic production of goods previously purchased abroad. But protection had costs as well as possible gains. Firms sheltered from competition might produce expensive or low-quality goods; industrial expansion needed financing, skills, electricity and customers. The argument was not simply a choice between opening and closing borders. It concerned who would build capabilities, who would bear their costs and how their success could be assessed.

The intellectual tradition that grew around CEPAL was not a single permanent program. Economists, ministers, industrialists, labor groups and agrarian reformers disputed the balance between public investment, foreign capital, private industry and redistribution. Some favored extensive state ownership; others emphasized targeted support to domestic enterprises. Rural people could have different priorities from urban manufacturers. These disagreements are easy to miss if the entire region is narrated as a struggle between a capitalist north and revolutionary south. In reality, many of the most consequential policy conflicts were among Latin Americans over how their own states should be organized, even as external economic pressure narrowed the space in which those debates occurred.

## Bolivia: a revolution made from several movements

Bolivia's 1952 revolution emerged from a history of concentrated landholding, an economy deeply dependent on tin mining, and the political exclusion of much of the Indigenous majority. Mine workers and other organized groups were central to the collapse of the old political order. The Movimiento Nacionalista Revolucionario, the MNR, became the governing force, but its reform agenda was shaped by pressure from unions, peasants and other mobilized constituencies. Universal suffrage expanded voting rights in 1952. Nationalization of the largest tin mining companies followed, and agrarian reform was enacted in August 1953. These were related changes, not the same change. Voting, control of mines and land tenure each distributed power through different institutions.

A mine could be declared national property without becoming an easy source of public prosperity. The government still had to manage equipment, workers' demands, investment and commodity prices. Likewise, dividing large estates did not automatically resolve irrigation, transport or the credit needs of small farmers. The reforms were responses to a long social struggle and to the immediate capacity of organized workers and rural communities. They also made claims about who belonged to the nation. Anthropologists and historians have asked whether the MNR's vision of a unified peasant citizenry sometimes weakened recognition of distinct Indigenous political identities. A reform can remove one form of exclusion while creating new tensions over language, custom and collective authority.

Bolivia helps us correct two historical habits. The first is to imagine that all major postwar reforms in Latin America came from communist revolutions. Bolivia's transformations began in a different ideological and organizational setting. The second is to treat agrarian reform as a purely technical exercise in dividing acreage. Land has social meanings: a livelihood, an inheritance, a place for kinship, a source of political authority and sometimes a territory governed communally. A ministry's report on hectares distributed provides one form of evidence. A mining union's demands, a peasant community's records and an Indigenous intellectual's critique provide others. We need them all before claiming to know what the revolution achieved.

## Guatemala: reform meets a covert war

In Guatemala, a popular movement overthrew the dictatorship of Jorge Ubico in 1944 and opened a period of constitutional reform. Juan José Arévalo and then Jacobo Árbenz were elected presidents during this period. Rural inequality and the legacy of coerced labor were central concerns. Árbenz backed an agrarian reform law known as Decree 900, enacted on June seventeenth, 1952. The Guatemalan Congress's legal archive preserves the law's description: it proposed to transform rural property relations, distribute eligible lands, and provide mechanisms for compensation and assistance. This was a specific legislative program, not a blanket decree abolishing all private land or an instant transfer of every plantation to agricultural laborers. It also generated conflicts over ownership, implementation and political authority.

The law targeted underused land on large holdings under defined conditions. Guatemala's U.S.-owned United Fruit Company held extensive property and faced expropriation claims. Large landowners opposed the law, while many rural workers supported access to land. Some Guatemalan communists were active in supporting the government and reform, and the political coalition was contested within Guatemala itself. It is inaccurate to dismiss the government's opponents as mere agents of foreign firms. It is equally inaccurate to treat U.S. intervention as imaginary. The later published United States diplomatic record explicitly discusses planning to remove Árbenz. A policy of land reform was recast in the language of communist threat at precisely the moment officials considered how to change another country's government.

In 1954, the covert operation known as PBSUCCESS supported forces working to overthrow Árbenz. Psychological warfare, propaganda, foreign support to armed opponents and the calculations of Guatemalan military officers all affected the crisis. An archival release notice by the U.S. National Archives identifies PBSUCCESS as the operation that supported the coup leading to Árbenz's resignation and the installation of Carlos Castillo Armas. That does not mean a handful of foreign agents personally determined every Guatemalan choice. It means an external power deliberately acted to undermine the elected government, and that its intervention entered an already contested political field. Ignoring either domestic conflict or foreign organization would flatten the history.

Árbenz announced his resignation over the radio on June twenty-seventh. His address accused the United Fruit Company and U.S. interests of helping drive the attack on his government, and defended his goal of an independent, democratic Guatemala. The speech circulated in different transcriptions and translations, and historians have checked those against surviving audio and contemporary records. As a source, it tells us how Árbenz explained his decision publicly at a moment of defeat. It cannot, by itself, settle every cause of the coup. But leaving out that Guatemalan account while relying only on U.S. security assessments would recreate the imbalance of voice that shaped the crisis. The event should be reconstructed through records made on multiple sides, with their authors and political purposes visible.

The coup reversed key elements of the reform program and reshaped the political system. It also created enduring arguments over what kind of development Guatemala might have pursued had elected reform survived. That counterfactual cannot be demonstrated by treating the government's aspirations as guaranteed results. Nor can the overthrow be defended retrospectively by assuming every reform led inevitably to dictatorship. The available record establishes an actual reform, actual domestic political conflicts and actual foreign intervention. A sound historical argument should say which of those facts it is explaining, which causal connections it can support, and where it is discussing possibilities that no archive can directly verify.

## Cuba: whose revolution?

Cuba entered the nineteen fifties with a different mix of conditions: a powerful sugar economy, close economic ties with the United States, and the authoritarian rule of Fulgencio Batista after his 1952 coup. Opposition included urban organizers, students, unions, peasants and armed rebels. Fidel Castro's Twenty-sixth of July Movement became the best known revolutionary organization, but it did not create the discontent on its own. In early January 1959, Batista left the country and the old regime collapsed. A general strike and other civic action helped determine the transfer of power. People who supported the overthrow did not necessarily agree about the form of government that should follow. A revolution may unite participants around what they oppose before revealing deep differences about what they want to build.

Agrarian reform became an early and defining issue. A law adopted in May 1959 placed limits on large holdings and foreign landownership. This was not a question debated only in Havana. Historian Sara Kozameh's research emphasizes Black peasant organizers and earlier agrarian campaigns, including policy experiments in territories held by rebels in 1958. The people who cultivated land could shape revolutionary demands rather than merely receive decrees issued by famous leaders. The new law sought to change who controlled agricultural production and rural property, but subsequent changes, including state management and collectivization in parts of the sector, altered the relationship between farmers and institutions. It would be misleading to assume that every rural household experienced the same degree of control or benefit.

One of the revolution's best-documented mass campaigns was educational. In 1961, Cuba mobilized students, teachers and other volunteers for a national literacy drive. The UNESCO Memory of the World account describes an archive containing teaching manuals, learners' letters and other campaign materials. Those records show how the state turned literacy into a large organizational project and how learners and young instructors became participants in it. Literacy meant practical possibilities for a person who wanted to read a wage statement, a letter or a newspaper. At the same time, the teaching materials and mobilization expressed revolutionary political ideas. We do not have to choose between acknowledging the achievement of expanding literacy and examining the political messages of a government-directed campaign.

Individual voices can help prevent a mass campaign from becoming a statistic. UNESCO has published the recollection of Cuban literacy educator Lilavatti Díaz de Villalvilla Carbó, who described participating as one of many young volunteers and remembered the public celebration of the campaign's completion. Her account is a first-person retrospective made in a commemorative setting. It tells us how one participant understood the experience; it does not prove that every volunteer felt the same or that later educational problems disappeared. The historical question reaches beyond whether a campaign was declared successful. Who was trained to teach? How were rural families contacted? What did adults learn, and which supports helped them continue reading afterward? Those questions connect popular participation to the durability of policy.

Revolutionary government also brought sharp conflicts over political power. Trials and executions followed Batista's overthrow. Political opposition and independent media faced expanding restrictions as Castro and his allies consolidated authority. Property nationalization and a growing relationship with the Soviet Union intensified conflict with the United States. In April 1961, anti-Castro Cuban exiles supported by the U.S. government attempted an invasion at the Bay of Pigs and were defeated. Calling that a U.S.-supported invasion is historically supported; describing every Cuban opponent as a foreign puppet is not. Some exiles and domestic opponents had their own political commitments and grievances. Likewise, acknowledging foreign intervention does not answer the separate question of how Cubans could express dissent inside their country.

By the time of the missile crisis in October 1962, Cuba had become deeply entangled in Soviet-American strategic confrontation. Yet the island was not simply a piece on a board controlled by two superpowers. Cuban leaders sought security against another invasion and pursued their own revolutionary program, even while their dependence on Soviet support limited their leverage. U.S. leaders feared Soviet missiles near American territory, but that perspective alone cannot describe the choices Cubans confronted after the Bay of Pigs. A full explanation needs the decisions of Moscow, Washington and Havana, as well as the effect of confrontation on civilians. The crisis also made plain how an argument about sovereignty could become an argument about survival in a nuclear age.

## Development aid as a political choice

The United States and Latin American governments also negotiated reform programs that were neither the Guatemalan coup nor the Cuban Revolution. At Punta del Este in Uruguay in August 1961, members of the Organization of American States, except Cuba, endorsed the Alliance for Progress. Its charter called for economic growth, social justice, representative institutions, improved education and health, and agrarian reform. The language is worth reading rather than caricaturing. Officials understood that poverty and unequal landholding could not be addressed solely with military aid or anticommunist declarations. But a charter sets objectives. Whether money reached local communities, whether governments pursued redistribution, and whether aid strengthened or weakened political accountability are separate questions demanding evidence from implementation.

The Alliance for Progress was also part of Cold War strategy. United States officials sought to prevent the appeal of Cuba's revolutionary path, while Latin American governments attempted to secure investment and resources for plans of their own. That mixture made the program politically contentious from the start. Some national leaders welcomed external capital but rejected foreign conditions; some demanded land reform that domestic elites resisted; others preferred projects that reinforced existing institutions. There was no single Latin American recipient waiting for an American solution. As with Bandung's economic proposals, the hardest problem lay between a shared statement and the local balance of interests that determined what could actually be built.

## A connected but not uniform history

These stories should not be forced into an argument that all land reform leads to one result. Bolivia nationalized major mines and widened political participation while facing conflicts over Indigenous identity and state control. Guatemala enacted an agrarian reform in a constitutional setting before a covertly supported overthrow. Cuba redistributed rural property and expanded literacy while consolidating one-party power and confronting U.S. intervention. Prebisch and CEPAL helped provide an intellectual framework for governments seeking greater industrial capacity, while the Alliance for Progress offered a different mixture of development and international strategy. The events are connected by problems of sovereignty and inequality. Their institutions, participants and outcomes were not interchangeable.

The material evidence we would need to judge them is varied. A reform law identifies what legislators authorized. A government production series offers estimates of output. A peasant petition may reveal who sought land and why. An archived covert-operation document can establish that a foreign state deliberately tried to remove a government. A teacher's recollection describes what education felt like for one participant. None can stand in for all the others. Historical investigation improves when we ask a source to support only the claims it can genuinely bear, then compare evidence produced by actors with different interests.

For people living through these transformations, the great argument was not simply capitalism versus communism. It was whether a family would own or rent its land; who would decide how a mine operated; whether a child could learn to read; whether a government could be removed at an election; and whether outside governments could change the result by force. Those were concrete forms in which sovereignty and social justice were experienced. No single revolution, development program or international alliance settled them all. The region's history after 1945 is therefore a history of many actors trying to change the terms of life, sometimes cooperating and sometimes struggling against one another over the meaning of freedom itself.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Each source is attributed to its author or institution. Diplomatic, archival, and legal texts document what officials recorded or enacted, not universal consent or direct proof of implementation. Publisher abstracts and catalog descriptions are identified as such and are not misrepresented as books or archival files read in full. An institutional description of an oral-history collection is not used to invent, quote, or merge individual testimonies. Historical debates are distinguished from settled document chronology. All source locators and known limits are retained in the ledger and metadata. No arbitrary lecture length requirement is used.
