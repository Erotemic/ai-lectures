# The Ballot After Apartheid: South Africa’s Difficult Transition

## The vote and the country that preceded it

In April nineteen ninety-four, South Africans queued to vote in a national election that included citizens whom the country's previous government had deliberately excluded from equal political power. Photographs of the lines can make the result look like a sudden awakening: a democracy appears when people put ballots into boxes. But the lines were made possible by decades of strikes, boycotts, schools and churches turned into meeting places, armed resistance, clandestine organizing, international pressure, and negotiations that came close to breaking down. Many voters had spent much of their lives under laws that defined where they could live, which schools they could attend, and whether they could exercise political rights. The election was a victory over those laws. It was also the first test of what a government elected by everyone could do about the inequalities the laws had helped create.

Apartheid is often translated simply as separateness, but that word alone understates the system. After the National Party came to power in nineteen forty-eight, it expanded and codified a racial hierarchy through laws governing population classification, residence, political representation, employment, and everyday movement. Earlier South African segregation and dispossession provided foundations for that order. Its administration was practical and coercive: a permit to be in a city, forced removal from a neighborhood, land allocated by racial classification, a school with unequal resources, police empowered to punish someone crossing an official boundary. It was not a society of separate communities choosing to live apart. It was a state that assigned unequal rights and then enforced the assignment.

Resistance preceded the final decade by a very long time. The African National Congress organized campaigns for political rights; the Freedom Charter of nineteen fifty-five articulated a vision in which South Africa belonged to all who lived in it. The state responded to demonstrations with repression, including the killing of protesters at Sharpeville in nineteen sixty. The ANC and the Pan Africanist Congress were banned. Some opposition organizations turned to armed struggle, while others continued civic work under severe restrictions. The youth uprising that began in Soweto in nineteen seventy-six, protesting school language policy among other grievances, showed again how educational policy and political authority were linked. Its repression galvanized a generation. The history of the transition cannot begin in the office of a reforming president.

## A movement with many front doors

During the nineteen eighties, anti-apartheid resistance increasingly connected organizations that were active in ordinary places: civic associations, congregations, schools, student organizations, unions, neighborhood committees, and women's groups. The United Democratic Front, launched at Mitchell's Plain in August nineteen eighty-three, was a coalition rather than a single centralized party. Its immediate target included a proposed tricameral parliamentary system that would provide limited representation for people officially classified as Indian or Coloured while continuing to exclude the African majority. Many activists recognized the plan as an attempt to reorganize minority rule, not end it. The UDF helped different local struggles identify a common political opponent without requiring every group to share one ideology or method.

Women were central to organizing these structures. Albertina Sisulu was among the movement's leaders, but the work also occurred through local campaigns over rent, transport, schools, food, childcare, and the treatment of detainees. A woman's political work might be classified as community welfare even when it challenged a system that determined whether her family could remain together. A worker could join a strike over wages and also help sustain a boycott against an unpopular township authority. These were not lesser struggles outside political history. They were ways people made the authority of an unequal state harder to exercise in the places where it affected daily life. An account focused only on speeches by nationally known men would miss a large part of the mechanism of change.

Independent unions also brought power that could not be measured merely by attendance at a rally. Factories, mines, transportation, and services depended on labor, and organized workers could interrupt production. The Congress of South African Trade Unions, known as COSATU, formed in nineteen eighty-five and became a major part of the political landscape. Collective bargaining, workplace safety, and democratic rights became connected without being identical. A union member might support the ANC while criticizing a proposed economic policy, or might put an urgent workplace issue first. The labor movement's capacity for organization would remain important after majority rule, when employees would face a government associated with their former allies.

The state used emergency regulations, detention without ordinary legal safeguards, censorship, and force against activists. The risks were neither distributed equally nor limited to famous prisoners. Students could be detained; newspapers suppressed; families lose income when wage earners went into hiding; communities be drawn into violent struggles over local authority. At the same time, South African opposition was not a single disciplined movement with universally accepted tactics. The ANC's armed wing attacked state targets and some operations caused civilian deaths. Rival organizations debated nationalism, socialism, nonracialism, and methods of struggle. Local violence sometimes involved opposition factions as well as security forces. A credible history can recognize the immense asymmetry of state power without erasing conflicts within resistance.

## Pressure inside and beyond South Africa

International campaigns against apartheid grew through churches, students, unions, exiles, sports boycotts, financial measures, and sanctions. Some governments defended engagement or resisted broad restrictions, arguing that contact could encourage reform or that sanctions would hurt vulnerable workers. Others treated economic and cultural isolation as a way to raise the cost of maintaining racial rule. These were contested strategies with unequal effects. It would be too simple to say that an overseas boycott by itself ended apartheid. It would be equally misleading to treat opposition within South Africa as independent of international networks. Activists depended on supplies, attention, and political alliances that crossed borders, while government and business leaders had to weigh diplomatic and economic costs.

By the end of the nineteen eighties, the government confronted deepening crisis: political resistance remained widespread, its legitimacy was damaged, the economy faced strains, and global conditions were changing. The Cold War's end altered one argument the authorities had used to portray the ANC primarily as a communist threat. President F. W. de Klerk, who took office in nineteen eighty-nine, concluded that the old arrangements could not be sustained as before. His decision was consequential, but calling him a solitary liberator would invert the causal story. He acted in a field that opponents of apartheid had changed at immense cost. Neither reform from above nor resistance from below alone explains why the political door opened when it did.

On February second, nineteen ninety, de Klerk announced the unbanning of major liberation organizations and the intention to release Nelson Mandela. Mandela walked out of prison on February eleventh after twenty-seven years in captivity. The event gave a face and voice to possibilities people had discussed for decades. In a press conference after release, Mandela emphasized his commitment to peace while rejecting the notion that a demand to end oppression was incompatible with continued organized struggle. The distinction mattered: the opening of negotiations did not require the opposition to pretend that the state had suddenly become just. It created an arena in which the conditions for a new system could be argued rather than simply imposed.

## Negotiation while the killing continued

The negotiations were neither a friendly conversation nor a straight path to elections. The ANC and the National Party government debated the status of political prisoners, security forces, armed groups, constitutional guarantees, the rights of minorities, regional powers, and how the country would be governed while talks proceeded. Formal meetings included the Convention for a Democratic South Africa, called CODESA, beginning in December nineteen ninety-one. The ANC did not represent every opponent of apartheid, and the National Party did not control everyone claiming to defend the old order. Parties had incentives to use the prospect of disorder as leverage. They also had to convince their own supporters that compromise would not squander what generations of struggle had sought.

The violence of the early nineteen nineties makes any description of a smooth peaceful transition inadequate. There were deadly clashes involving ANC and Inkatha Freedom Party supporters, and violence in which security-force conduct and alleged collusion came under investigation. The Boipatong massacre in June nineteen ninety-two intensified mistrust and helped derail negotiations. In September, security forces in the Ciskei homeland killed protesters near Bisho. Hundreds of thousands faced the possibility that political change might be postponed or drowned in violence. Negotiators returned to talks because the alternatives were also frightening. It is more precise to say that a settlement emerged amid violence than that South Africa simply chose peaceful change.

The assassination of Chris Hani on April tenth, nineteen ninety-three, brought the danger of wider catastrophe into focus. Hani was a prominent communist and former leader of the ANC's armed wing; many people saw him as a principled and forceful figure who could speak to those impatient with compromise. His murder by a far-right extremist provoked fury. Mandela appealed publicly for restraint while political leaders worked to sustain negotiations. The moment revealed both the fragility of the process and the authority Mandela had acquired beyond a formal state office. His words could not undo the killing; they helped signal that the future would not be decided by the assassin's intention to provoke conflict.

The interim constitutional settlement was a negotiation about the rules by which future disagreements would be handled. Universal adult suffrage, an elected legislature, a government of national unity for the transition, and basic rights protections provided an alternative to permanent minority veto and to winner-take-all coercion. Constitutional principles would constrain the drafting of a final document. The arrangements were not free of compromise or disagreement, especially over regional authority, protection for civil servants, and the pace of social change. But they addressed an important problem: an election without credible guarantees about the law and security institutions might leave losing parties willing to overturn it by force.

## Counting everyone, confronting the inheritance

South Africa's first universal national elections took place in April nineteen ninety-four. The ANC won a clear majority; Nelson Mandela became president in May. The act of voting had a particular meaning for people long excluded from choosing national rulers. Yet an election cannot instantly repair differences in landownership, housing, education, health care, or accumulated household wealth. Families displaced under apartheid did not automatically regain homes, and equal legal citizenship did not guarantee equal means of earning a living. The government faced choices about redistribution, public services, property rights, and economic stability. Those choices would be debated by some of the same workers, church members, and community organizers who had helped bring it to power.

A final constitution was drafted through the Constitutional Assembly, with the Constitutional Court required to certify compliance with the agreed constitutional principles. The constitution was adopted in nineteen ninety-six and came into effect in February nineteen ninety-seven. It set out enforceable rights and defined a democratic order based on equality, nonracialism, nonsexism, and universal suffrage. Its design was part of the transition, not a decorative summary of national reconciliation. Rights become consequential when institutions can be asked to protect them against officials, parliamentary majorities, or powerful private actors. Whether they are fulfilled in daily life is another question. Written guarantees supply standards through which citizens can demand better performance; they do not perform the work on their own.

South Africa also had to decide how to confront political violence committed across decades. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission was established with a mandate concerning gross human-rights violations between nineteen sixty and nineteen ninety-four. Its committees collected statements, held public hearings, considered reparations, and reviewed applications for amnesty under specified conditions. Survivors could speak publicly about experiences the previous state had denied or hidden. Some perpetrators sought amnesty in exchange for full disclosure and demonstration of a political objective. This arrangement was not identical to prosecution in court. It reflected a contentious attempt to combine truth-seeking, accountability, and an achievable transition within a society where perpetrators and victims would continue to live alongside one another.

The commission's hearings did not bring every victim complete answers or equal reparations. People could disagree about who deserved forgiveness, whether amnesty sacrificed justice, or whether documenting a past atrocity did enough to change the institutions that permitted it. Its remit concentrated on defined categories of gross violations; the ordinary, systemic harms of apartheid—land dispossession, labor exclusion, unequal schooling—could be harder to fit into testimony about a specific episode of torture or killing. Public truth and material repair are related, but not the same. To preserve that distinction is not to dismiss the value of the hearings. It is to understand why the end of a discriminatory legal order leaves an unfinished argument about justice.

## Why the settlement mattered

The South African transition is sometimes presented as a contest between two leaders who learned to shake hands. Mandela and de Klerk did matter. They helped make agreements possible, and their choices carried risks. Yet that frame gives too little credit to people who built political capacity without a seat at the negotiating table. Students had protested an imposed curriculum, residents had organized against rent and transport rules, union members had stopped work, women's groups had sustained communities under repression, and exiles had carried the struggle across borders. The settlement depended on a society that had developed ways of demanding change, not only on leaders reaching a personal understanding.

It is also necessary to resist a different simplification: that violence would inevitably have ended either through civil war or a miracle of forgiveness. The actual settlement consisted of enforceable procedures, bargains over political inclusion, the hard work of containing armed factions, and people willing to accept an election whose outcome they could not dictate. Violence did continue during negotiation, and the new democracy inherited institutions that required reconstruction. What made nineteen ninety-four historic was not that every social conflict ended. It was that the claim of one racial minority to govern the majority by law was formally displaced by a claim that all adult citizens could help authorize the state.

The long line of voters is therefore a beginning as well as a conclusion. It records the defeat of a political exclusion that had structured lives for generations. At the same time, it asks whether the new institutions will be able to make equal citizenship meaningful across economic and social differences. Constitutional law can name dignity and equality; ordinary people must be able to invoke those rights in schools, workplaces, hospitals, courts, and neighborhoods. The story of apartheid's end is not best remembered as a national problem solved forever. It is the history of a society changing the rules of political membership—and accepting responsibility for the difficult questions that became possible to argue in public afterward.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Official state, court, UN and medical agency materials establish specific decisions and records, not the entire lived history or intent behind them. Archival collection descriptions and article abstracts are labeled and not passed off as complete interviews or full-text scholarship. Where casualties or responsibility remain disputed, the lecture distinguishes verified events from uncertainty. Expand with Baltic and Polish oral histories, Uganda and Brazil community records, South African local organizing files, and preserved Bosnian and Rwandan survivor testimony before claiming comprehensive regional coverage. Lecture length is dictated by the material, not a word or time limit.
