# Whose Work Counts? Families, Migration, and Labor After 1945

## A household is part of the economy

Imagine a household after nineteen forty-five in which a parent leaves early for a factory, another adult prepares food for several people, a child carries water, and a relative looks after a baby. The factory worker may appear in payroll statistics; the others may not. Yet the paid shift cannot happen in the same way without meals, washing, care, and places to sleep. Economists often call this work social reproduction: the labor that sustains people, households, and the capacity to work again tomorrow. The phrase can sound abstract, but its meaning is ordinary. Families were not merely spectators of postwar growth. They furnished the time, skills, and emotional effort on which institutions and industries relied.

In many societies after the war, industrial and service employment expanded, cities grew, and governments promised greater access to housing, schooling, health care, or wages. But the meaning of a better life was never uniform. One person measured opportunity by a dependable job. Another wanted to own land rather than work somebody else's farm. A third wanted to migrate, while a fourth wanted the legal right to remain with family in a city where residence was restricted. The same policy could produce new opportunities and new dependencies. To study the social history of these decades, we have to follow not only national production totals but also the routes by which money, food, children, and people moved among households.

The distinction between a formal worker and an informal worker often concealed that dependence. A factory employee might have a wage slip, a recognized union, and rights established in law. A street vendor, home-based seamstress, small trader or agricultural pieceworker might be excluded from those systems while performing necessary work. Families frequently combined incomes from several kinds of labor. Many women supported households through informal work, yet policies designed around the standard male factory employee could fail to recognize them as workers. This was a major political problem rather than a vocabulary problem: who could bargain, borrow money, claim benefits, or request a safer workplace depended partly on whose labor the state chose to count.

Postwar histories sometimes describe a single transition from agriculture to industry and then to comfortable consumer life. That picture fits some places better than others, and no national average tells us what happened inside every household. Rural-to-urban migration could enlarge earnings while adding housing costs and separation. A factory could offer wages to a young woman while maintaining lower-paid job categories and punishing organizing. A state could expand schools but leave unpaid childcare almost entirely to mothers. Even when opportunities improved, they were shaped by race, class, legal status, and access to political power. We need several histories, not one staircase on which every society occupied a different step.

## When migration was called temporary

In Britain, workers from Caribbean colonies and Commonwealth countries helped rebuild the economy and sustain public services after the war. The ship Empire Windrush arrived in nineteen forty-eight and became a symbol of these migrations, though British archival records document earlier arrivals on ships such as the Ormonde and Almanzora. Migrants brought different professions, ambitions, and family situations. Some came to study, others to work or join relatives. Their movement was bound up with the legal status of empire and citizenship. They had not simply appeared from a foreign world to which Britain had no relationship. Their labor and political identities were already part of an imperial history that Britain itself had constructed.

Arriving did not guarantee a room, a fair wage, or a welcome. British National Archives material records severe shortages of accommodation, racial exclusion, and the use of temporary shelter near Clapham Common. Oral-history projects held by Black Cultural Archives and the University of London preserve the perspectives of people who sought employment, established social networks, and sometimes endured racist treatment while building lives. One interview summary concerns Alift Harewood, who moved from British Guiana to England and worked for decades as a nurse and midwife. That one life demonstrates the kind of professional and familial history the broad term Windrush generation can hide. It does not make her experience identical to every arrival.

As political debate about immigration hardened, the British government imposed new entry restrictions on Commonwealth citizens, including the Commonwealth Immigrants Act of nineteen sixty-two. The change revealed a contradiction between inherited language about belonging to a Commonwealth and the selective way the state controlled settlement. A person could be recruited for needed labor while being treated as socially disposable or legally precarious. Such distinctions shaped the prospects of spouses and children too. Whether a household could live together depended on wage levels, housing discrimination, and rules defining who could enter or stay. Migration policy was therefore family policy, whether the government called it that or not.

West Germany developed a different labor-recruitment system. Agreements with Italy, Greece, Spain, Turkey and other countries supplied workers during a period of industrial growth. A nineteen sixty-one agreement with Turkey was framed as a temporary arrangement; the German word Gastarbeiter, or guest worker, carried that expectation in its name. Yet people who worked for years, formed relationships and raised children were not simply visitors whose social lives could be switched off when demand changed. Government policy and employers' labor needs often imagined rotation. Migrants made plans of their own. Germany's Federal Agency for Civic Education traces how the employment agreements led to long-term settlement even after recruitment was halted in nineteen seventy-three.

## A border can be part of a wage contract

Across the United States–Mexico border, the Bracero Program operated from nineteen forty-two to nineteen sixty-four. It organized contracts for Mexican labor, particularly in agriculture, and came to involve millions of work contracts over its lifetime. A contract was not an individual person; a worker might return on multiple contracts, so we should not confuse the total contracts with the number of workers. The Smithsonian's Bittersweet Harvest exhibition draws on photographs and recorded accounts to show the dual realities of the program. Workers could gain income and opportunities, while also encountering dangerous conditions, dependency on employers, and unequal treatment. The bargain was not identical for every family, region, or year.

In a seasonal migration system, labor can move while many costs remain at home. A spouse may manage the farm, household, finances, and children during the worker's absence. Money sent back can provide education or housing, yet separation can expose family members to hardship and insecurity. The Bracero History Archive includes recorded experiences of relatives as well as workers. That archive exists because a migration ledger showing arrival dates and contracts cannot tell us what a marriage or childhood felt like. At the same time, a few vivid interviews do not provide a statistical answer to how the entire system affected families. We need both human narratives and careful records to understand how gains and burdens were distributed.

The three migration systems were not interchangeable. British Commonwealth migrants entered a country with legal and imperial links to places they or their parents had lived. Turkish workers entered West Germany under agreements explicitly framed around temporary employment. Braceros moved through a contract regime directly tied to employers and seasonal agriculture. But they faced a common political issue: states and businesses often valued labor without extending equally secure forms of belonging. Workers answered by forming communities, using unions, preserving languages, building institutions, and making claims on the places where their labor was needed. Migrants were not merely an input in an economic growth model; they were people who changed the political character of the receiving societies.

There is another danger in telling migration history only through those who reached a destination. The people who stayed behind may have depended on remittances, suffered separation, or gained new responsibilities. Children might learn two sets of social expectations, one from grandparents and another from a distant parent. Some travelers returned, some settled, and some were forced to move again when laws or jobs changed. We should avoid turning these possibilities into one invented family story. They are questions for oral histories, household budgets, correspondence, and local records. When a country announces that it has recruited a certain number of workers, it has measured a policy, not the complete social world created by that policy.

## Work and family under a system of internal passes

South Africa shows how a state could control labor movement even when it did not have to cross an international border. Apartheid pass laws restricted where Black South Africans could live, travel, and seek employment. These rules made the labor market inseparable from policing. Employers needed workers; the state sought to restrict Black residence in urban areas and preserve a racial order. Families were separated by administrative categories, and residence permits could determine whether a parent lived with children. In this setting, a campaign against a pass document was also a campaign about household survival and the right to build a life where work could be found.

Women organized against the extension of the pass system. The Federation of South African Women, founded in the nineteen fifties, issued a Women's Charter that demanded political equality, fair employment, maternity protections, childcare and education. In August nineteen fifty-six, thousands of women marched to Pretoria's Union Buildings. The march is remembered as a central event in the anti-apartheid movement, but it was also a debate about how the state regulated time and family. Dora Tamana, one of the women organizing resistance, linked pass restrictions with unemployment, housing shortages, children, and imprisonment. Her words are preserved in historical accounts. Here the right to work could not be separated from the right to live with one's family.

The march did not immediately dismantle apartheid or all pass laws. The state continued to use detention and administrative restrictions, and women continued organizing locally. It would be a mistake to tell the story as though a single remarkable day settled the conflict. It would also be a mistake to treat the march as ceremonial because it did not secure immediate repeal. It helped create networks, an organizing tradition, and a public argument that the restrictions were illegitimate. In many political histories, laws are central because they can be dated precisely. Social change often begins much earlier, in the slower construction of solidarity and the repeated decision to resist a rule that remains in force.

South Africa's experience can illuminate the broader problem of counting women's labor. When an official document labels a woman a dependent, it may conceal her income, agricultural work, childcare, political activity, and contribution to a household's survival. Pass regulations further separated the right to work from the right to reside with loved ones. These were not simply private matters beneath national history. Who raised the next generation, who paid rent, and who cared for the ill were basic questions about the organization of society. Political movements understood this when they tied labor rights to schools, family housing, and the dignity of their members. The public and private spheres were linked all along.

## Who is recognized as a worker?

The International Labour Organization adopted an Equal Remuneration Convention in nineteen fifty-one. It called on ratifying governments to promote equal pay for men and women performing work of equal value. That phrase is significant. Equal value can include jobs that are different in title or tasks but comparable when skills and responsibilities are assessed fairly. A standard does not change every workplace as soon as it is adopted; ratification, enforcement, bargaining power and discrimination all affect what happens. But the convention gave advocates a language for a problem that employers could otherwise hide by assigning different kinds of work to men and women, then saying their wages were incomparable.

In Gujarat, India, women who earned money as vendors, home-based producers and other informal workers organized a different kind of response. The Self-Employed Women's Association, SEWA, emerged in nineteen seventy-two under the guidance of Ela Bhatt and women working through labor and cooperative movements. The organization challenged the assumption that trade unions belonged only to factory employees with a standard employer. If a woman bought materials, carried goods, made products and negotiated a price in the market, she was working, even if no factory manager signed a wage slip. Collective organization could then address credit, market access, recognition, health and childcare, alongside bargaining and legal rights.

SEWA's own history emphasizes organizing by women workers and the development of member-based institutions, including banking and cooperatives. Its account is valuable as a statement of a movement's aims and experiences, although claims about long-term effects deserve independent evaluation. The conceptual shift is clear without relying on a later success statistic. Rather than asking informal workers to first become factory employees so that their demands could be recognized, organizers argued for rights from the realities of the work women already did. This also changes the question of family life. A vendor who brings a child to work may need childcare just as urgently as a factory worker, even if neither the employer nor the state recognizes responsibility for providing it.

We should not imagine every woman wanted the same form of liberation. Some prioritized access to wages, others control over farming, freedom to migrate, education, an end to racial policing, or a larger voice in family decisions. Paid employment could enlarge bargaining power while adding a second working shift at home. Some movements sought state services; others built mutual-support institutions where the state was absent or hostile. Across Britain, South Africa, India, and the United States–Mexico border, the demand was often not for an abstract identity but for practical authority over time, earnings, children, mobility and safety. Those similarities matter, but the different political settings should remain visible.

## International norms and unresolved household politics

In nineteen seventy-five, the United Nations convened the first World Conference on Women in Mexico City as part of International Women's Year. Delegates debated equality, development and peace, and the meeting adopted a World Plan of Action. The conference did not produce agreement about every priority or immediately change national law. But it institutionalized a claim that work, education, health, political participation and the condition of women belonged in international discussions of development. What had sometimes been relegated to private life was now part of the agenda of states and global institutions. The move also raised a practical question: when governments adopted grand declarations, who would monitor the ordinary places in which inequality was produced?

An index or a treaty may tell us how a government classified women's work. A union record may show an organized demand. A migrant's interview may show what a job and a neighborhood meant to a person who actually arrived. None of these sources contains every truth. A state census can miss unpaid labor; a movement manifesto tells us what activists sought, not whether they all obtained it; an archival interview is shaped by the questions asked and the later moment of remembrance. The appropriate response is not to discard them but to read their different strengths together. This lets us distinguish a right written on paper, a workplace practice, and the lived experience of the person to whom the right supposedly belongs.

The postwar decades brought more jobs, migration, schooling and public services to many populations, but also systems designed to supply labor without accepting workers as full members of society. The everyday effort of keeping a household alive gave shape to each of these changes. A wage made one kind of contribution visible. A meal prepared at night, a sick child cared for, a transfer of money across an ocean, or a collective demand for a place to live made other contributions visible. Once these activities enter the history, the family ceases to be background scenery. It becomes a place where global economic systems were negotiated, resisted and rebuilt every day.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Official state, military and organizational records establish what their makers stated, ordered and publicized, but must not be mistaken for comprehensive records of social outcomes. Participant accounts are situated voices rather than statistical samples. Where only an abstract, excerpt, or archive index was accessible, the limitation is explicit. Interpretations and contested causal accounts are distinguished from documentary chronology. No fixed length or duration rule applies to this lecture.
