# Who Made the World's Goods? China, Factories, and Global Trade in the 2000s

## From a workshop floor

Picture a production line in the Pearl River Delta in the early two thousands. A plastic housing arrives from one supplier. Circuit boards arrive from another. A worker checks a connector, a colleague tests the finished device, and a truck takes cartons toward a port. The finished product may be sold under the brand of a foreign company, but the work behind it crosses several borders. Materials, component designs, licenses, credit, shipping insurance, and retail contracts all influence what happens on that factory floor. Chinese labor and investment were central to this system, but the words made in China could conceal the international organization of production. A country of enormous domestic variety was also becoming a place where decisions made by distant buyers could affect the timing of overtime on an assembly line.

China's rise as a manufacturing exporter did not begin on the day it joined the World Trade Organization. Coastal special economic zones had been developed from the nineteen eighties, including Shenzhen near Hong Kong. Local officials offered infrastructure and arrangements meant to attract investment, while firms from Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, elsewhere in East Asia, Europe, and North America participated in production. Chinese domestic enterprises and state-owned firms also changed their roles. By the start of the new century, large industrial clusters linked suppliers, workers, ports, and investors. The long buildup matters because it keeps the history from collapsing into a single treaty. The treaty later changed the prospects and rules for activity already underway.

## Entering a rule-bound marketplace

On December eleventh, two thousand and one, China became the one hundred and forty-third member of the World Trade Organization. Its accession followed lengthy negotiations over tariffs, services, trading rights, and other rules. Membership gave exporters a more predictable framework for reaching many markets, while China undertook commitments that required changes at home. None of these promises guaranteed that every firm would prosper or every worker would benefit. Governments and businesses had to decide how to adapt. For some factories, participation in global trade meant easier access to buyers. For some domestic producers, it meant sharper competition. WTO rules affected goods crossing borders, but they did not settle how income from production should be shared within China.

The chain of ownership was especially complicated in electronics. United Nations trade research published in two thousand and two already documented the strong presence of foreign affiliates in some Chinese electronics exports. A computer or mobile phone could be assembled in China from high-value components and technology purchased elsewhere. The gross value recorded as an export from China was therefore not the same as the value created entirely by Chinese workers or companies. A manufacturer's profit, an assembler's wage, a supplier's margin, and a retailer's revenue were distributed across the chain. This accounting distinction matters when countries later argue about trade imbalances. A label on a shipping container reports the declared location of trade; it does not tell the whole story of who designed, financed, produced, or earned from the product.

Export factories were not all alike. Garment making and toy assembly could depend heavily on repetitive work, flexible contracts, and narrow margins. Electronics production involved more complex systems of subcontractors and specialized suppliers. Heavy industry, construction materials, machinery, and steel required different forms of capital, land, fuel, and transport. Some firms were state controlled, some domestic private businesses, some joint ventures, and some foreign owned. Their interests in wages, regulation, currency policy, and reliable electricity did not necessarily coincide. There were also regional differences: a coastal industrial district connected to a port operated in a different economy from a farm community far inland. Speaking of a single Chinese factory worker obscures these differences, just as a national export total obscures the many industries behind it.

## The journey of a worker

For millions of rural residents, industrial growth made migration to a town or city a serious possibility. Some young people entered factories directly, while others tried construction, services, market work, or a succession of temporary jobs. Their decisions were not made from a blank slate. Farming households needed cash for expenses, some regions had fewer local opportunities, and family members often pooled resources across locations. The household registration system, usually called hukou, distinguished people's registered places and status, influencing access to public schools, insurance, and other urban services. People could be essential to an industrial city without receiving all the public benefits available to locally registered residents. That gap made migration an issue of citizenship and municipal finance as well as labor supply.

A worker's pay slip was one part of the household economy. Earnings could be sent home to support parents, children, siblings, or a family business. Housing might mean crowded shared accommodation near the factory. Long shifts could be attractive to someone trying to save quickly and exhausting to someone unable to refuse overtime. A family might gain income and lose time together. Rural remittances and urban construction linked distant places, so industrial growth cannot be understood by treating the countryside as an empty reservoir of labor. Villages supplied workers, food, family care, and sometimes the land-use rights that made temporary migration less dangerous. The question was not simply whether migration improved an average wage. It was who could choose it, under what conditions, and with what options for return.

Researchers and policy observers reported significant gaps between the rights written in Chinese labor law and actual workplace conditions. Problems included wage arrears, excessive hours, lack of written contracts, and difficulties securing representation. The experience differed widely across factories and regions, so no single episode can stand in for the whole manufacturing sector. Some employers offered improving pay and training; others avoided obligations or relied on subcontracting that made responsibility difficult to establish. Workers filed complaints, brought disputes, and protested unpaid wages. Their efforts were part of the industrial history, not an afterthought to changes in trade statistics. An account that depicts working people only as victims would miss the bargaining, planning, mobility, and organizing through which they tried to alter their situations.

In June two thousand and seven, China's national legislature adopted a Labor Contract Law that took effect on January first, two thousand and eight. Its provisions addressed written employment contracts, termination, employer obligations, and forms of employment that had expanded with market reform. The law was a response to problems already visible in workplaces; it was not proof those problems disappeared. Foreign companies, domestic employers, trade unions, local officials, and workers all had stakes in how the law would be enforced. Some employers raised concerns about higher costs, while workers' advocates pressed for rights that could be made real. Legal text establishes a claim a worker may assert; courts, administrative agencies, collective organization, and the balance of power determine whether asserting it succeeds.

## Ports, households, and policy

Cities changed rapidly around industrial expansion. Roads and container terminals enlarged the scale at which a factory could operate. Electricity networks, logistics firms, financial institutions, technical schools, and industrial parks created the infrastructure for manufacturing. Urban consumers bought more appliances and phones, and firms increasingly served the Chinese domestic market as well as foreign buyers. But the geography of investment was unequal. Coastal provinces with strong transport links often attracted far more foreign-oriented production than interior regions. Local officials were rewarded for growth, while infrastructure costs and labor services pressed on public budgets. Economic geography was something governments helped produce, not just the result of private entrepreneurs independently choosing efficient sites.

Governments also faced the legacy of state-owned enterprise reform. In earlier decades, work in many state enterprises had been connected to housing, social insurance, and a relatively secure employment identity. Restructuring changed that arrangement. New private and foreign-invested jobs could create opportunities while closures or layoffs unsettled older industrial communities. An older worker leaving a state factory and a younger migrant entering an export plant did not experience the same reform. Across the country, families tried to combine savings, education, elderly care, and the possibility of moving. Economic change became visible in the timing of marriage, the price of urban housing, and whether a parent could bring a child to a school near work. A factory expansion was not confined to its production statistics.

## Energy and the hidden bill

Manufacturing at great scale demanded power, water, transport, and places to dispose of industrial waste. Coal supplied much of China's expanding electricity demand. Emissions harmed air quality, while wastewater and competition for water created local conflicts. Public agencies within China acknowledged serious pollution problems; environmental advocates and residents also challenged harmful industrial practices. We should not imagine that pollution began with export assembly or that foreign demand was solely responsible. Domestic construction and consumption grew rapidly, and local development strategies had their own incentives. But the international distribution of costs was often unequal. Goods used abroad could be produced where workers and nearby residents bore emissions and pollution that were not included in the retail price.

Consider how a product's environmental history differs from its trade history. A shipment might be counted when it leaves a port, even though its components required mining, chemical treatment, electricity, and transport elsewhere. A firm may report success because the final product passes quality inspection, while a community reports contaminated water or lost farmland near a supplier. These are not two descriptions of separate economies. They are descriptions of different costs attached to the same production network. Consumers, regulators, companies, and residents have different amounts of information and power to demand changes. Historians therefore need more than national output graphs: they need location-specific evidence about exposure, regulation, employment, and the distribution of public goods.

## A shock from overseas

In two thousand and eight, a financial crisis that began in United States mortgage markets spread through global banking and trade. Orders from some overseas buyers weakened. The risk for an export-oriented factory was immediate: fewer contracts could mean reduced shifts, closures, or a return journey for workers who had expected steady wages. The Chinese government responded with credit easing and an announced investment program worth four trillion renminbi over two years, directing attention to transport, housing, rural infrastructure, reconstruction, and other priorities. The headline amount did not mean the central government newly spent that sum in cash; it combined proposed investments with roles for local authorities and other financing. The policy also reveals something about China's economy before the crisis: export production was important, but governments could mobilize domestic infrastructure investment on a vast scale.

Investment-led stabilization raised its own questions. A new road could connect a previously isolated place to jobs and markets. A construction project could also expand local government debt or use land on contested terms. More bank credit could support a struggling enterprise and increase exposure to risky loans. Some businesses pivoted toward domestic customers; others disappeared. The financial crisis was not one external event that left China unchanged. It tested the relationship between exporters, banks, governments, and households, and it accelerated debates about the balance between foreign demand, domestic consumption, and investment. The arguments were made by people who benefited differently from each strategy. It matters which problem a policy was designed to solve and whose problem it actually solved.

## Who earns the value?

The history also extends beyond Chinese borders. Buyers in Europe, North America, and elsewhere gained access to a wider range of manufactured goods, often at lower prices. Firms reorganized supply chains. Businesses in other producing countries faced competition; some lost orders, while others became suppliers of parts, raw materials, or specialized services. Workers in different countries could experience the same restructuring as opportunity or threat. A trade deficit measured between two countries cannot, by itself, identify the wage and profit relationships that produced it. Political arguments over Chinese exports often compressed an international chain into the image of two nations trading finished goods, leaving out investors and intermediate producers in third countries.

China's own debates were equally complex. Officials and business owners argued about productivity, household spending, regional inequality, education, industrial upgrading, and social provision. Migrant workers wanted secure wages and better access to urban life; some employers wanted predictable labor costs and more freedom to adjust hiring. Rural families weighed earnings against separation. The state could build a highway but still had to decide whether people arriving to work nearby would have equal access to schools and insurance. In that sense, the problem of industrialization was not merely how to make more goods. It was how a society distributed the obligations that came with producing them.

Around the turn of the century China became an indispensable part of international manufacturing, but it did not do so as a single factory controlled by one hand. Local governments, workers, investors, foreign buyers, state enterprises, private workshops, and transport networks made different contributions. Global trade rules helped shape expectations; longstanding reforms supplied capacity; labor law and environmental politics exposed questions that output alone could not answer. The most important correction to the familiar label world's factory is to restore the people and relationships that a label leaves out. A good can be assembled in one place, designed in another, sold in a third, and paid for—financially and socially—in many more.
