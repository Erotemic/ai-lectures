# The Ballot and the Bomb: Kosovo and East Timor, 1999–2002

## Two places, two questions

In nineteen ninety-nine, people in two places on opposite sides of the world confronted a question that international law could not answer by reading a map alone: who should govern a territory, and who could protect its inhabitants when those claiming authority over them used force? Kosovo lay inside the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, following the breakup of the larger Yugoslav federation. East Timor had been occupied by Indonesia after the end of Portuguese colonial rule. Both became sites of international military deployment and United Nations administration. Yet the histories were not the same. One involved a NATO air war without prior explicit Security Council authorization. The other culminated in a Security Council-authorized multinational force after Indonesia accepted international assistance. Understanding that distinction is the beginning of the story, not its conclusion.

International intervention is sometimes narrated as an argument among diplomats, generals and lawyers. Those arguments matter, but they can overshadow the people whose lives were being decided. A displaced family needs to know whether the road home is safe. A voter needs assurance that a ballot will not bring punishment. Someone whose relatives have disappeared needs records, protection and the possibility of accountability. Terms such as sovereignty, self-determination and territorial integrity name genuine political principles. They can also be invoked by states seeking to preserve control and by movements seeking freedom from coercion. The history becomes clearer when we ask what each authority actually did and how civilians experienced its power.

## Kosovo before the bombing

Kosovo's population was predominantly ethnic Albanian, while Serbian political and religious claims to the territory were powerful. After the reduction of Kosovo's autonomy under Serbian leadership in nineteen eighty-nine, many Albanian residents faced discrimination and exclusion from public institutions. Political leader Ibrahim Rugova and others developed a largely nonviolent strategy involving parallel institutions and demands for self-determination. Later, the Kosovo Liberation Army pursued armed resistance, and attacks by militants and state security forces contributed to escalating violence. The resulting struggle was not merely a timeless hatred between two communities. It emerged from concrete changes to constitutional status, police power, political representation and the possibilities for peaceful protest. The choices of different groups within Kosovo also diverged.

During nineteen ninety-eight, violence and displacement escalated. The government in Belgrade, led by Slobodan Milošević, treated the armed insurgency as a security threat, but military and police operations also targeted civilians and communities far beyond the combatants. International observers and governments debated sanctions, verification and diplomacy. The talks at Rambouillet in early nineteen ninety-nine failed to produce a settlement accepted by Belgrade. NATO governments argued that a military campaign was necessary to stop a humanitarian catastrophe and compel a change in the conduct of Serbian and Yugoslav forces. Their decision did not erase the legal issue: the Security Council had not explicitly authorized the use of force before the bombing began.

NATO launched air strikes against the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia on March twenty-fourth, nineteen ninety-nine. The campaign continued for seventy-eight days. Serbian and Yugoslav forces intensified expulsion and violence against Kosovo Albanians during the war. Human Rights Watch documented systematic forced displacement, killing, intimidation, destruction of homes and efforts to strip refugees of identifying documents. Many fled across borders to Albania and Macedonia; others were displaced within Kosovo. The fact that NATO said it sought to protect civilians cannot replace the record of what civilians suffered on the ground. The timing of violence, chains of command and the means of forced expulsion are questions for documentary investigation, not assumptions to be settled by the proclaimed intentions of any belligerent.

## The bombings and the law

Air war caused its own civilian harm. Human Rights Watch documented approximately five hundred civilian deaths in incidents associated with NATO bombing, while recognizing the limits of available evidence. Some attacks struck transportation links, moving vehicles or facilities whose military character and civilian risks were debated. NATO described efforts to select militarily justified targets and minimize casualties. A committee reviewing the bombing for the prosecutor of the international tribunal examined incidents and did not treat every civilian death as automatic evidence of a prosecutable war crime. These are different kinds of evidence: an incident count, an assessment of compliance with the laws of war, and an argument about whether starting the campaign was legally justified. They should not be collapsed into one verdict.

On June tenth, nineteen ninety-nine, the Security Council adopted Resolution twelve forty-four, providing for an international civil and security presence and a political process in Kosovo. Serbian and Yugoslav forces withdrew, and NATO-led troops entered as part of the new security arrangements. The resolution referred to the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia while also envisioning substantial self-government for Kosovo. It did not settle Kosovo's final status. Just as important, it was adopted after the air campaign. A later UN mandate for peacekeeping and administration must not be described as though it had already authorized the air strikes in March. That chronological error obscures the core controversy over humanitarian force and the Security Council.

Supporters of NATO's decision argued that the danger to Kosovo Albanians and the failure of diplomacy required urgent action, even without explicit Council authorization. Critics argued that allowing military alliances to decide for themselves when force was humanitarian risked weakening the UN Charter's restrictions on war. Governments also worried about the precedent for interventions justified by selective sympathy or geopolitical advantage. These disagreements involved genuine concerns on both sides: the obligation to protect people from grave crimes, and the need for legal limits on the use of armed force. The history does not become more rigorous by treating all positions as morally identical. It becomes more rigorous by making clear which factual and legal questions each position addresses.

## The aftermath belongs in the story

The entry of international troops did not make Kosovo safe for everyone. Large numbers of displaced Kosovo Albanians returned, but minorities, particularly Serbs and Roma, faced harassment, attacks, arson, abductions and displacement. Human Rights Watch documented abuses after the withdrawal of Yugoslav forces. These later crimes should not be presented as equivalent in scale or origin to the previous state campaign of persecution, but they were real failures of civilian protection. An intervention justified in part by the safety of civilians must be evaluated by its ability to protect those who are vulnerable after power changes hands as well as before. The designation of a peacekeeping force did not itself guarantee equal security.

International civil administration introduced difficult questions about accountability. Who could write the rules for policing, courts, housing and return of property? How were Kosovar Albanians, Serbs, Roma and other residents represented in decisions affecting them? A territory can be placed under international supervision while its residents still argue about political status and democratic legitimacy. Resolution twelve forty-four established institutions for an interim settlement, not an agreed final answer. Kosovo's experience challenges the simple notion that defeating one army is the same as establishing political consent. Protection requires long-term institutions and some way to address crimes, missing persons and rights without allowing retaliatory violence to become the next order.

## The long history of East Timor

East Timor's struggle had a different beginning. Portugal had ruled the territory as a colony. After Portugal's political transformation in the nineteen seventies, the future of the colony became contested among East Timorese political movements. Indonesia invaded in December nineteen seventy-five and annexed the territory, a claim not accepted by the United Nations as a valid act of decolonization. Occupation, armed resistance, forced displacement and violence persisted over many years. The Timor-Leste Commission for Reception, Truth and Reconciliation later documented deaths and abuses during the conflict, including an enormous toll from hunger and illness as well as direct killing. The commission's estimates are historical findings with methods and limits; they are not numbers to repeat without attribution.

For East Timorese communities, the future was never only a negotiation between Jakarta and Lisbon. Local independence movements, families, religious organizations, clandestine organizers and the diaspora sustained political demands, even while disagreeing about strategy. Indonesian authorities and their allies also had constituencies who favored integration or autonomy, but the existence of disagreement did not justify coercion against civilians. International support for self-determination developed unevenly, and the Cold War had shaped how foreign governments treated Indonesian rule. By the late nineteen nineties, Indonesia was itself undergoing political change after President Suharto resigned. That change opened space for talks about a UN-supervised decision on the territory's future.

Agreements signed in May nineteen ninety-nine established a process in which voters would accept or reject a special autonomy arrangement within Indonesia. The United Nations Assistance Mission in East Timor, called UNAMET, organized the consultation. On August thirtieth, despite intimidation, East Timorese people lined up at polling sites. Surviving photographs show queues, desks and election workers in particular villages; they document the practical work of voting, not the private thoughts of everyone photographed. The result was decisive. Approximately seventy-eight and a half percent rejected the autonomy proposal, leading toward independence. Strictly speaking, the ballot asked about autonomy under Indonesia, rather than presenting two simply named options called independence and integration. The distinction explains the subsequent constitutional process.

## A result does not protect the voter

When the result was announced in September, pro-integration militias, often with the support or participation of elements of the Indonesian security forces, committed widespread violence, destruction and forced displacement. Buildings were burned, communities uprooted and people were driven across the border into West Timor or into hiding. The reaction exposed a central weakness of a ballot conducted in a territory where the state responsible for security was entangled with forces threatening the voters. A free choice requires more than a carefully printed paper. People must be able to cast a vote, learn the result and survive its consequences. The UN mission could organize a consultation; it did not command an army capable of stopping the post-result violence by itself.

Indonesia agreed on September twelfth to accept international assistance. On September fifteenth, the UN Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution twelve sixty-four, authorizing a multinational force to restore security, protect UN personnel and facilitate humanitarian operations. The force, known as INTERFET, was led by Australia. This sequence differed materially from the Kosovo intervention: the Council authorized the force before its deployment, following Indonesia's acceptance. Neither fact means the response came soon enough for all civilians or that outside military power was free of its own political interests. It does identify the legal and diplomatic basis on which the international troops entered East Timor.

On October twenty-fifth, nineteen ninety-nine, the Security Council established the United Nations Transitional Administration in East Timor, or UNTAET. The mission received broad legislative and executive authority during the transition. It faced the tasks of restoring services, rebuilding institutions and helping the territory prepare for self-government. The very breadth of that mandate created a dilemma. Foreign administrators might supply resources and expertise while also holding decisions that East Timorese citizens expected to make for themselves. Consultation and local participation therefore mattered as much as legal authority. International administration could help create the conditions for independence, but it was not itself the democratic self-government for which many East Timorese had struggled.

East Timor achieved formal independence as Timor-Leste on May twentieth, two thousand and two. This date marks a political achievement, not a claim that the legacy of the preceding occupation had been repaired. Families still sought missing relatives, survivors carried trauma, and the country faced difficult questions about land, public services, justice and relations with Indonesia. It mattered that independence was not a gift handed down by an international mission. East Timorese campaigns had sustained the claim to self-determination for decades. The UN operation became consequential because it intersected with that long struggle, and because citizens continued demanding that authority pass to institutions they could hold accountable.

## Which principle won?

Comparing Kosovo and East Timor brings several principles into focus. Sovereignty gives states claims against external intervention. Self-determination gives peoples claims about political membership and the right to shape their future. Human rights law and the laws of war limit what officials and armed groups may do to civilians. Security Council mandates supply specific international authority, but their wording and timing matter. In Kosovo, NATO launched an air campaign without prior explicit Council authorization and Resolution twelve forty-four subsequently established an interim international presence. In East Timor, a UN-supervised vote led to post-election violence, then a Council-authorized force and an international transitional administration. The events cannot be merged into one generic precedent.

Nor can one outcome measure the experiences of every resident. Military success, a ceasefire, an independence ballot and the creation of police or courts are different milestones. A returnee may find a burned house. A minority family may fear revenge. A survivor may need a record of what was done to a relative, not only a promise that the border will be stable. These human realities do not make international rules irrelevant. They explain why it is dangerous to reduce the debate to a contest between the abstract rights of states and the abstract compassion of interveners. The legitimacy of authority must eventually be tested through protection, representation and accountability.

By the turn of the millennium, the international order had neither discovered a universal formula for humanitarian intervention nor abandoned the idea of rules. It had demonstrated the limits of both unilateral moral claims and legal procedures that arrive too late to prevent violence. Kosovo and East Timor showed different routes through crisis, one deeply disputed from its opening military action and the other grounded in a supervised vote and subsequent Security Council decisions. Both left difficult work after the uniformed forces arrived. The historical lesson is not that institutions always succeed or invariably fail. It is that the people whose lives an institution governs must remain visible long after the cameras turn away from the diplomatic ceremony.
