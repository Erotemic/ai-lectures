# Who Speaks for a Nation? Associations, Constitutions, and the Politics of Representation

## A country is not a single voice

A petition reaches an imperial government. At the bottom are signatures, and at the top is a claim: we speak on behalf of our people. What does that mean? A petition might represent a neighborhood, a trade association, a group of educated reformers, or tens of thousands of people mobilized to resist a policy. It is possible for petitioners to speak sincerely and effectively without speaking for everyone. In the late nineteenth century, around the world, people challenged rulers by founding associations, building newspapers, convening congresses, staging demonstrations, and sometimes organizing revolutions. A central question was not simply whether a nation deserved freedom. It was who could claim to be that nation, and by what means others could challenge the claim.

We should distinguish at least four political ideas that often appear together in these struggles. Nationalism claims that a people share a political identity and perhaps deserve collective self-government. Constitutionalism seeks to limit power through publicly established rules, whether or not a state is independent. Representative government asks who authorizes officials to speak and make decisions. Anti-imperialism challenges domination by another power. A reformer could embrace all four, or only some. One might demand elected representatives while remaining loyal to a monarch. Another might seek independence but hesitate to give the vote to women or workers. One might want an empire reformed into a community of equal citizens rather than broken into separate national states.

This is why the familiar story in which nationalism begins as a European idea and travels outward is too simple. Political activists engaged with imported legal languages and institutions, but they also drew on local histories, ethical commitments, religious movements, agrarian grievances, and already existing forms of assembly. The task is to see the choices in their own settings. The merchant, the student, the religious scholar, the peasant, and the army officer need not imagine identical futures. Movements that later become national histories were sometimes coalitions of people who agreed on a particular injustice and little else.

## Indian organizations and the widening meaning of politics

Consider British India in the decades after the revolt of 1857. Local and regional political associations developed, and in December 1885 an all-India gathering established the Indian National Congress in Bombay, now Mumbai. Its early activities included resolutions, requests for administrative reform, and the argument that Indians deserved a greater share in decisions about their own society. The Congress was a concrete institution with delegates, leaders, meetings, and disagreements. It was not in 1885 an already assembled electorate of every person in India. Its prominent early participants came disproportionately from educated and relatively privileged backgrounds. Their political activity nevertheless helped create a common forum in a territory where colonial government divided authority among provinces and princely states.

Even the word representative acquired a different meaning depending on the setting. A Congress leader might claim to represent Indian interests before a colonial authority, while the government could answer that the delegates had never been elected by most Indians. That objection could be self-serving: the government itself did not rest on universal consent. Yet the problem of representation was real. Which languages were used at meetings? Who could afford to travel? Which communities were treated as central? Were women included in shaping agendas? An institution can create national politics while still reproducing class and gender exclusions. The history becomes more interesting when we ask how those boundaries were contested from within and without.

Bengal's partition in 1905 altered the scale and methods of opposition. British officials defended the division in administrative terms, while many critics understood it as an effort to divide political society. Protesters encouraged swadeshi, the use and production of goods associated with one's own country, and boycotts of imported goods. These were not merely symbolic gestures. They linked household purchasing, business decisions, schooling, public demonstrations, and arguments over political independence. The movement challenged the assumption that politics happened only inside government chambers. A person who could not vote might still refuse a product, join a rally, publish a pamphlet, or support a school.

But a national boycott also raises an uncomfortable question: who has the means to participate? Imported and local goods differed in price and availability, and economic campaigns could carry religious or social symbols that did not resonate with everyone. Bengal's inhabitants did not have one uniform view of partition or one unified interest in its reversal. The organizations that mobilized nationalism also competed over the direction of reform, methods of protest, and relations among religious communities. It would be inaccurate to project the later Indian state backward onto all participants in 1905. The outcome of twentieth-century independence struggles was not already fixed in the first Congress meetings.

## A constitution inside an empire

Shift west to the late Ottoman Empire, where nationalism was only one of several ways to reorganize political life. An Ottoman constitution was promulgated in 1876, and an elected parliament operated briefly before Sultan Abdülhamid the Second suspended parliamentary life in 1878. Opponents later campaigned for the constitution's restoration, and the Young Turk Revolution of 1908 brought a second constitutional period. The key aspiration of many reformers was not initially the disappearance of the entire Ottoman state. It was a different relationship between rulers and governed: public laws, parliamentary deliberation, and accountability across a politically diverse empire.

The empire included people of different languages, religions, and local political traditions. Some reformers pursued an inclusive Ottoman citizenship. Others favored stronger central institutions or increasingly defined political belonging in narrower national terms. An elected parliament did not eliminate those arguments. If representatives speak in the name of an empire's population, what happens when its people disagree about whether they are one political community? If a constitution gives everyone rights in principle, can its promises be realized while imperial administrators, military officers, landed elites, and local notables still hold unequal power? Constitutionalism created an arena for these conflicts. It did not solve them simply by becoming law.

Historian Yuval Ben-Bassat studies petitions from Ottoman Palestine after 1908. His research describes a significant change in the destination and language of appeals: petitioners increasingly addressed representative and administrative institutions and expressed claims in terms of rights and civil equality, rather than relying only on the sultan's personal favor. This is an example of political transformation observable in paperwork. A change in the addressee of a petition might look trivial. But it can indicate a change in what people expect authority to be. Instead of asking the ruler to be generous, the petitioner asks an institution to honor a rule. We should not infer from one regional collection that every petitioner everywhere thought the same way.

The second constitutional period also teaches caution about progress narratives. Restoring a parliament did not guarantee stable pluralism. Competing programs of centralization, ethnic and religious belonging, security, and territorial survival intensified under pressure from internal struggles and foreign intervention. The story cannot end with the satisfying moment when a constitution is proclaimed. A constitution needs institutions capable of enforcing its promises, and political rights need people able to defend them. The Ottoman experience makes nationalism visible not as a single inevitable destination but as one answer among several to the question of how diverse people might live under a common political order.

## Egypt: the nation and the army

Egypt supplies another test. In 1881 and 1882, Ahmad Urabi and his allies challenged the dominance of Anglo-French financial interests, privileged military hierarchies, and the power of Khedive Tawfiq. The movement included officers but was not reducible to a soldiers' quarrel. Historian Donald Malcolm Reid emphasizes civilian participation, public political demands, and the breadth of the movement. Contemporaries could speak of Egypt for the Egyptians while continuing to profess loyalty to the Ottoman sultan. Their political identities did not need to fit a later map of fully independent nation-states.

British military intervention in 1882 ended this attempt at political reorganization and inaugurated occupation. We should be precise about what was at stake. An anti-foreign movement could oppose European control of finances and offices while also demanding change in who wielded authority within Egypt. Arguments about local elite privilege, taxation, military hierarchy, and constitutional representation were connected, not interchangeable. A simple label such as nationalist revolt might conceal that social breadth. A label such as military mutiny might conceal the political challenge altogether. Historical names are themselves arguments about what deserves to be remembered.

## The Philippines: several futures at once

In the Philippines, demands for reform under Spanish rule were followed by an armed independence movement. José Rizal and La Liga Filipina represented one approach to political change; Andrés Bonifacio and the Katipunan, organized in 1892, developed a revolutionary one. The Katipunan mobilized through networks of secrecy, mutual commitment, and increasingly open rebellion from 1896. Independence was not a single speech delivered by one uncontested representative. Revolutionaries debated how to organize authority and fought over leadership. A movement could oppose imperial hierarchy and still face deep disagreements over its own government.

The sequence became even more complex when war between Spain and the United States reached the archipelago in 1898. Filipino revolutionaries sought independence, but the Treaty of Paris transferred Spanish claims over the Philippines to the United States without granting Filipino representatives control of the settlement. Philippine constitutional organization advanced at Malolos, and in 1899 a republic faced war with the United States. We need two distinct observations here. First, Filipinos had organized political claims and institutions of their own; they were not passive spectators waiting for imperial states to determine the future. Second, having an independence proclamation and a constitution did not ensure that a stronger foreign power would recognize sovereignty.

The Philippines also shows why it is risky to write the history of a revolution only as the biography of its most famous leaders. Bonifacio, Aguinaldo, Rizal, and others held different positions and made different political choices. Behind the names were local networks, printers, fighters, families, towns, and provincial political disputes. The survival of certain speeches or official records cannot tell us everything ordinary participants believed. The historian should distinguish decisions documented in a congress or proclamation from the varied motives of people who risked their lives. Political community had to be made amid inequality, violence, language differences, and changing military conditions.

## Who is missing from the petition?

Set these cases beside each other. The Indian National Congress gathered delegates; Ottoman petitioners redirected appeals toward institutions; Egyptian civilians joined soldiers in challenging authority; Filipino revolutionaries organized their own government. These are not equivalent actions, but they share a problem: how does a political claim become authoritative? One answer is procedure, such as election or a constitution. Another is participation, such as protest and collective organization. Another is the ability to sustain an institution against coercion. Each answer exposes weaknesses in the others. A perfectly drafted constitution may be imposed from above. A crowded demonstration may not include every affected community. An organization may be well disciplined and still exclude dissenters.

The term nation can describe a solidarity people choose, a culture they inherit, a program advocated by leaders, or a state that claims their loyalty. These meanings overlap without being identical. It matters whether a nationalist organization speaks of a single history that erases internal differences, or creates a political process in which those differences can be expressed. It matters whether women, the poor, and religious minorities can join the public conversation on terms they help determine. To notice exclusions is not to declare the movements fraudulent. It is to see their unfinished work and the conflicts that shaped them.

Finally, historians need to read political declarations with two questions in mind. What did these authors want their audience to believe? And what can the document actually establish about the world? A constitution is excellent evidence of a constitutional claim; it is incomplete evidence of everyday obedience to that constitution. A British report on Egyptian disorder establishes an imperial official's framing; it is not automatically a fair account of Egyptian political objectives. A proclamation of independence tells us who asserted authority, not that sovereignty was uncontested. Recovering this distance between the document and the lived world is not cynicism. It is how we respect the political struggle enough to study it honestly.

When someone says, we speak for the nation, the productive historical response is neither automatic agreement nor automatic dismissal. Ask who organized the meeting, who wrote the words, who paid for the press, who entered the room, who could safely disagree, and who had the power to reject the petition. Then ask how those answers changed over time. Nationalism and representative government did not arrive ready-made. They were built through decisions over membership, law, labor, language, armed force, and the reach of political imagination. That process was part of making the modern world, and it remains visible wherever someone claims to speak for people whose voices are more numerous than any single organization can contain.
