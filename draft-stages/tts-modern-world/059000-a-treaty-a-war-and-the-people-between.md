# A Treaty, a War, and the People Between: The Late Cold War, 1975–1989

## The promise and the warning

A treaty can be signed in a quiet room, but its effects are tested in crowded streets, military headquarters, and homes close to a border. In the late nineteen seventies, Europeans could read diplomatic assurances about security while watching governments prepare new missiles. Across the Soviet Union, people copied lists of rights promised by their own state and asked why those promises were not being honored. In Afghanistan, the question of what sort of government should rule was becoming a struggle in which outside powers would intervene with immense force. These histories belong together, but they are not interchangeable. Nuclear negotiations were about the terrible power of states to destroy one another. Dissidents were asking what that power meant for the people governed by those states. Afghans would experience a war that diplomacy in Europe did not prevent.

Start in Helsinki, in August nineteen seventy-five. Thirty-five states participating in the Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe approved a Final Act. The Soviet government valued the diplomatic acknowledgement of existing European frontiers; Western governments spoke of greater stability and contact. The agreement also included provisions concerning human rights, freedom of information, family reunification, and exchanges between societies. It was not an enforceable world constitution, nor did it abolish conflicting interpretations of borders and sovereignty. Its importance was partly that governments publicly committed to principles that citizens could now quote back to them. The same document could be read by a foreign ministry as an arrangement among states and by a writer under surveillance as a claim that the government had promised more than it delivered.

Monitoring groups emerged in the Soviet Union and other European countries to document whether the commitments were observed. Their methods mattered. They did not need an army to reveal an inconsistency. They assembled names, dates, and particular cases: someone denied permission to emigrate, someone prosecuted for a publication, a family kept apart, a meeting prevented. Officials could repress these people, and often did. But the act of documenting created a portable record with an address beyond the state's own institutions. It also tested the line between domestic and international politics. If human rights were mentioned in a document signed by governments, could authorities still insist that every complaint was only an internal affair? The Helsinki process did not answer this question by itself. It supplied words and procedures through which an increasingly difficult argument could be carried.

At almost the same moment, a different calculation was being made in defense ministries. A nuclear missile stationed in Europe was not just a weapon pointed toward an enemy. It could shorten warning times, unsettle alliances, and create pressure to decide before reliable information was available. The Soviet Union was deploying the mobile SS-twenty missile; NATO governments debated whether the United States should station new ground-launched systems in Western Europe. Each alliance described its decisions in the vocabulary of security. That vocabulary did not make the systems symmetrical, and it could not dissolve the fears of people expected to live near them. In December nineteen seventy-nine NATO adopted a two-track policy: it planned new deployments while also offering negotiations about intermediate-range forces. The proposed military buildup and the proposed talks were therefore parts of the same decision, not successive eras of aggression and peace.

People who favored deterrence believed that refusing to answer Soviet deployments could leave NATO vulnerable to coercion. Opponents of new missiles believed that introducing more weapons on either side could make a devastating mistake more likely. Within both camps there were disagreements about whether an agreement could be verified and about what should count as a balanced reduction. These are not simply two opinions to be averaged. They reveal different theories of what prevents war. One theory stresses the costs an opponent expects to suffer if it attacks. Another stresses the possibility that machines, warning systems, rigid plans, and misunderstanding can defeat even leaders who intend to avoid war. Neither theory gave ordinary citizens control over the launch decision. The issue was not merely how many warheads existed but what institutions were authorized to decide their use.

## Afghanistan was already in crisis

To understand why Soviet troops crossed into Afghanistan at the end of nineteen seventy-nine, begin before they arrived. Afghanistan had not been a passive square on a superpower chessboard. The monarchy was overthrown in nineteen seventy-three, and in April nineteen seventy-eight the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan seized power. The party was divided into factions. Its leaders pursued rapid changes in landholding, social relations, and political authority while using arrests and coercion against opponents. Rural communities, religious leaders, officials, and others resisted for varied reasons. Some welcomed aspects of reform; others rejected its manner, its content, or the state's violence. By the following year a rebellion and violent government repression had widened the conflict. The Afghan state was already in a civil war before the Soviet army entered it.

The Soviet Union had long been involved in Afghan military and political affairs, but direct intervention was a separate and consequential choice. Declassified Soviet leadership materials reveal alarm about the stability and loyalty of Afghan ruler Hafizullah Amin. Soviet officials feared the loss of an allied government and imagined that rival powers might gain influence near Soviet territory. Those fears were not proof that an American takeover was imminent. They were part of what Soviet decision makers believed or claimed. Some Soviet military officials warned that troops could not easily settle Afghanistan's political crisis and might become entangled in war. In December nineteen seventy-nine the leadership nonetheless chose intervention. Soviet forces entered, Amin was killed, and Babrak Karmal was installed as leader. Moscow could change the individual at the top; it could not manufacture consent throughout the country.

It is tempting to tell this as a neat story in which a distant empire moved a pawn and met resistance. That picture leaves out how Afghans were arguing with one another. The communist leadership was not one undifferentiated group. The armed opposition was not one movement with a single program. Rival commanders, religious authorities, local networks, ethnic and regional loyalties, and political organizations pursued goals that sometimes overlapped and sometimes collided. The intervention altered those conflicts because the government now depended on foreign military support, while opponents could receive assistance across the borders. Fighting became bound to international rivalry, but it did not cease to be Afghan. A useful history must be able to recognize imperial intervention and internal disagreement without using one to erase the other.

The United States condemned the invasion and increased support for forces opposing the Soviet-backed government. Pakistan became crucial to supply networks and refugee movements; other governments, including Saudi Arabia, had their own interests in the conflict. This was not an alliance created by a single command center that could shape every village uprising. External sponsors could provide resources and influence which groups received them, but they could not determine the entire politics of the resistance. Nor did a fighter's opposition to Soviet occupation establish agreement with liberal democracy, equality among Afghan communities, or any particular postwar constitution. The political question of what should replace the Soviet-supported government remained open during the war, even while foreign governments treated the military struggle as a contest of geopolitical influence.

The most important geography of the conflict cannot be drawn as arrows between capitals. It must also show the movement of families out of homes and across borders. By the end of nineteen seventy-nine the United Nations refugee agency later estimated that hundreds of thousands of Afghans had already reached Pakistan and Iran. As violence continued during the nineteen eighties, the number of Afghan refugees in those neighboring countries reached millions. People had to seek shelter, negotiate legal status, find food and work, and decide whether children would grow up in exile. Host communities and governments faced new responsibilities and tensions. The war's social consequences were not postponed until after its military end. They accumulated while armies and diplomats still argued over victory.

## Détente unravels; negotiations return

The Soviet intervention contributed to a collapse of confidence between the superpowers. In Washington, President Jimmy Carter withdrew the signed SALT Two agreement from Senate consideration, restricted some forms of trade, and announced that the United States would not send a team to the Moscow Olympics in nineteen eighty. None of these acts halted the war. After Ronald Reagan became president in nineteen eighty-one, American military spending rose and confrontational rhetoric sharpened. Yet the language of a second Cold War can conceal an important continuity: officials still understood that managing nuclear danger required communication with the adversary. Policymakers argued over whether strength was a prerequisite for an agreement or a barrier to one. They also disagreed among themselves about what concessions were acceptable. Arms control was never simply the opposite of strategic competition; negotiations took place inside it.

In nineteen eighty-three NATO began the planned deployment of new intermediate-range missiles after negotiations failed to produce an agreement. Anti-nuclear demonstrators organized in European countries and elsewhere, warning that they might be asked to bear the consequences of decisions they could not control. The size and program of each movement differed, and peace activists were not all united on what should replace nuclear deterrence. Their political presence nevertheless mattered. The arms race was argued over in legislatures, town squares, trade unions, churches, newspapers, and households, not only at summit tables. Some protesters aimed to freeze arsenals; others called for unilateral disarmament or for negotiable reductions on both sides. The state documents tell us why governments said they deployed missiles. They do not by themselves tell us how all the people living with the missiles understood them.

A new Soviet leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, came to power in March nineteen eighty-five. His reforms were responses to pressures within Soviet society and the economy, not simply favors offered to the West. He sought to reorganize an economy burdened by inefficiencies and military commitments, and his approach to foreign affairs became more willing to pursue negotiated reductions. American officials did not agree immediately on how to respond. Reagan himself had combined hardline rhetoric with an interest in bargaining, while some advisers remained skeptical of Soviet intentions. At Geneva in nineteen eighty-five and Reykjavik in nineteen eighty-six the leaders discussed arms questions of extraordinary scope. At Reykjavik they contemplated reductions so sweeping that observers could see the possibility of a different nuclear order, even though disputes, including over strategic defense, prevented a final settlement there.

The breakthrough most visible in the official record came in December nineteen eighty-seven. Reagan and Gorbachev signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty in Washington. It required the elimination of an entire category of ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles within specified range limits, along with relevant launchers. The agreement was about particular delivery systems, not a complete abolition of nuclear weapons. It also established detailed procedures for exchanging information and inspecting declared facilities. Inspectors from the two former adversaries could visit sites to test whether promised eliminations were occurring. This is one reason verification was central rather than a decorative appendix. A treaty whose success depends only on trust is different from one that builds ways to observe compliance. By nineteen ninety-one the parties had eliminated more than two thousand six hundred covered missiles.

Notice the parallel between the missile inspectors and the Helsinki monitors, though their authority was profoundly different. Official arms inspectors entered facilities under a negotiated interstate regime. Human-rights monitors gathered evidence without the security protection of such a treaty and could be prosecuted by their own governments. Both were engaged in a struggle over whether a promise could be checked. In one case, the objects were missile launchers and declarations. In the other, they were the lives and freedoms of people. The political effects were not identical, but the methodological question is worth holding together: who is permitted to measure the difference between official language and observed conduct? Negotiation could produce real constraints on military systems without guaranteeing a society's rights. Rights language could help citizens organize without giving them the means to force rulers to comply.

## The war continues beyond agreements

In April nineteen eighty-eight, after years of indirect negotiations, the Geneva Accords established a framework connected to the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Afghanistan. United Nations diplomacy played an important role. The last Soviet troops left in February nineteen eighty-nine. This was a clear military and diplomatic event, but it was not an agreed peace among all the Afghan forces. The communist-led government remained for several more years, and conflict among Afghan groups continued. Many refugees could not simply reverse their journeys and return to their former lives. Landmines, destroyed infrastructure, fear of political enemies, and changing local authority shaped what return meant. An international agreement can mark the departure of one army while leaving the dispute about the country's future unresolved.

The costs are often described in the language of superpower credibility: the Soviet Union failed to secure its objective, and the United States helped an opponent bear a large burden. Those statements capture something real while obscuring the people who did most of the dying, fleeing, and rebuilding. Afghan civilians lived through attacks by more than one side, contested authority, and the destruction of livelihoods. Afghan women were neither a single group with one political view nor mere symbols in foreign arguments about modernization and tradition. Families who supported different factions could all suffer from a war prolonged by intervention and the supply of weapons. A refugee history asks questions that a military chronology tends to miss: who could cross the border, who received aid, how communities held together, and what was lost when a child spent a decade away from home?

By the end of the nineteen eighties, political changes within the Soviet Union and across Eastern Europe were also challenging the structures of the Cold War. It would be a mistake to assign their causes to one president, one treaty, or one defeat. Economic strains, public movements, leadership choices, national aspirations, and the weakening of coercive arrangements all mattered. Gorbachev's willingness to negotiate and reluctance to use force in the old manner helped open possibilities. But what people did with those possibilities varied by country. When we say the Cold War was ending, we should ask which Cold War we mean: the threat of direct war between nuclear powers, the rule of one-party states over their own populations, or conflicts in places whose futures had been treated as prizes of rivalry. These processes did not end on the same day.

Think of three records left by these years: a declaration of rights, a logbook recording a missile inspection, and a register of a family seeking refuge. Each was an effort to make an enormous historical process legible. None contained the whole story. A declaration does not prove that the rights were honored. An inspection does not eliminate every route to war. A refugee register cannot express all the choices and grief hidden inside a statistical category. Together they resist a story in which history is made only by two leaders shaking hands. The late Cold War was an age of dangerous weapons and negotiated restraints, but also of dissent, displacement, and unfinished struggles about government. The central question is not merely why one rivalry softened. It is how people tried, with unequal power, to hold institutions accountable for what those institutions promised to do.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Records from state departments and international agencies are evidence about institutional policies and how they are represented, not independent confirmation of motives or social outcomes. Web excerpts and bibliographic abstracts are labeled and not passed off as fully read archival works. Where counts or causal explanations are uncertain, the spoken narrative preserves that uncertainty. Expand this pack with Afghan and Soviet participant oral histories, Chinese local and worker archives, African independent policy evaluations, and non-U.S. histories of technology before claiming comprehensive regional coverage. Lecture length is dictated by the material, not a word or time limit.
