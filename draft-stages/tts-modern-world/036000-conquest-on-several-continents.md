# Conquest on Several Continents: How a War Became Global

## A map made of more than armies

The phrase world war can make an enormous conflict sound like one event. In fact, it was a chain of invasions, political decisions, alliances, occupations, and struggles over resources, fought by people whose reasons for entering the war were not the same. Some states invaded their neighbors to construct empires. Others fought after being attacked. Some colonial subjects fought in armies whose governments did not grant them equal political rights. And civilians did not simply wait behind the lines for soldiers to return. They faced bombing, deportation, disrupted agriculture, and government demands for their labor. Understanding this war means connecting military chronology to the systems of power that sent people into danger and made survival unequally possible.

The conventional European date is September 1939. Japanese conquest and Chinese resistance had already produced years of warfare in East Asia. This lecture does not collapse the histories into a single supposed beginning. Instead, it asks how separate wars became one global struggle and why that connection did not make them identical. The fighting that devastated Warsaw was not the same as a Burmese village's encounter with a moving front, although their histories became entangled in a common military and economic conflict. Differences in regime ideology, goals, occupation policies, and racial hierarchy are part of the explanation. A timeline alone cannot carry the historical argument.

## Poland: conquest and the destruction of sovereignty

Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939. Britain and France declared war on Germany two days later, honoring the guarantee they had made concerning Poland's independence. But Poland's position had already been endangered by the agreement between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union reached in August. Its secret protocol allocated spheres of influence in Eastern Europe. Soviet forces invaded eastern Poland on September 17. This was not simply a neutral country caught between accidental movements of armies. German and Soviet governments made political arrangements that enabled Poland's partition. Distinguishing these decisions from Polish resistance matters because Poland did not freely choose to become the battlefield on which they acted.

Nazi Germany deployed concentrated air, armored, and ground forces and defeated Polish armies in weeks. The speed of defeat has sometimes been retold as though Polish soldiers barely fought, but the Holocaust Museum's account emphasizes resistance despite severe disadvantages in equipment and deployment. German bombing and artillery devastated Warsaw before its surrender in late September. Military terms such as rapid maneuver may help explain operations, but they can conceal the effects of fire on crowded streets, hospitals, water supplies, and dwellings. A state can lose an army's defensive campaign and still retain a population whose rights, property, language, and political organizations the occupier intends to remake.

Occupation changed that problem from military defense to a struggle over the future of society. Germany annexed parts of western Poland and administered other territories through the General Government. German policies targeted Jews for segregation, forced labor, and escalating persecution; Polish civilians also faced expulsions, imprisonment, killing, and coercive reorganization. Soviet occupation in the east brought its own state violence and suppression. A map colored in two occupiers' shades would not show the different institutions acting on people living under them. Nor would a military surrender mean civilian acceptance. Occupying forces had to confront underground organizations, local networks, and the ordinary impossibility of making a conquered population disappear from history.

## The expansion of European fighting

Germany attacked Denmark and Norway in April 1940, then the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, and France in May. The collapse of the French government and the armistice of June transformed European politics. Britain continued to fight, and air warfare became central in the summer of 1940. These dates describe changing military conditions, but they also describe changing opportunities for occupation, collaboration, refusal, and resistance. Under conquest, people did not always have clear categories of choice. Following an order could be a way to feed children, a form of accommodation, an expression of enthusiasm, or an act compelled by threats. Historians must not turn every behavior under occupation into a referendum on political allegiance.

Nazi policies in occupied Europe did not stop at extracting taxes or requiring obedience. Political and racial programs accompanied conquest. German offices and military formations participated in forced labor, property seizures, expulsions, and persecution. The machinery of occupation involved police, civil administrators, soldiers, transport offices, and private enterprises, each with distinct responsibilities. This matters when we ask who committed a crime. Orders made in a capital still required people to write lists, guard roads, drive transports, assign work, and enforce restrictions in local communities. A plan does not execute itself. The history of genocide belongs in its own careful account, but the war's expanding geography provided conditions in which the perpetrators exercised increasing power over civilians.

Italy, allied with Nazi Germany, fought in the Mediterranean and North Africa, while several European states faced invasion or territorial pressure. British imperial forces and their allies fought in these regions as well as in Europe. These fronts were not merely side stages on which to mark time before a famous landing in western France. Control of ports, shipping routes, fuel, and access to territories mattered to each government. The war depended on moving supplies over oceans while opponents attempted to sink them or deny access to bases. Some military accounts emphasize decisive battles, but battles were made possible by days and months of production and transport that are harder to place on a commemorative calendar.

## The invasion of the Soviet Union

In June 1941 Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union, breaking the agreement that had helped divide Poland less than two years earlier. This was not merely an attempt to win territory by ordinary diplomatic coercion. Nazi planners intended to destroy Soviet power and transform vast eastern regions into land under German domination. Their racial ideology treated many of the people living there as disposable or fit for enslavement and removal. The Holocaust Museum calls the conflict a war of annihilation. Those words matter. They direct attention to policies aimed not only at armed enemies but at entire populations whose lives the invaders judged expendable.

German forces initially advanced rapidly, surrounded large Soviet formations, and captured enormous numbers of prisoners. Nazi treatment of Soviet prisoners of war was catastrophic and deliberately murderous. The Holocaust Museum documents systematic starvation, exposure, killings, and forced labor under German military and SS authority. This was not the unavoidable consequence of there being many prisoners. Soldiers from different enemy countries were treated on sharply unequal terms. When historians compare mortality in captivity, they are examining policies and racial hierarchies, not simply differences in climate. The institution holding a prisoner matters as much as the fact that a prisoner has been captured.

German army units also cooperated with police and SS formations carrying out mass murder in occupied Soviet territory. This is a central historical correction to the comforting idea that the regular army fought an ordinary war while a wholly separate organization committed the crimes. The degree and form of involvement varied, but the surviving documents show logistical support, coordination, and direct participation in persecution and killing. It is essential to make such claims precisely and attribute them to recorded cases. Collective responsibility for policy is not a claim that every individual behaved identically. It is an argument about institutions that enabled violence on a massive scale.

Soviet resistance, extensive civilian mobilization, and enormous losses transformed the military balance. German expectations of quick victory failed. Over time Soviet armies regained territory and pushed westward. The Eastern Front was not an episode inserted between the fall of France and the Allied landing in Normandy. It was the largest and most destructive European land theater of the conflict, involving millions of soldiers and civilians under existential pressure. Recognizing that scale does not require praising Stalin's dictatorship or ignoring Soviet political repression. It requires distinguishing the regime's internal history from the experiences of the many Soviet peoples who fought, were displaced, starved, or died during the German invasion.

## An alliance does not make every front the same

Meanwhile, Japanese forces had fought to dominate China and increasingly challenged European colonial holdings and the United States in the Pacific. U.S. restrictions on oil and other strategic goods became part of a deteriorating confrontation. The Office of the Historian at the U.S. State Department traces negotiations, sanctions, and Japanese leaders' refusal to retreat from conquest in China. An explanation of diplomatic pressures is not a justification of the decision to attack. On December 7, 1941, Japanese aircraft struck Pearl Harbor; Japanese attacks elsewhere in the region helped turn the Asia-Pacific war into an even broader coalition conflict. The United States entered the war against Japan, and Germany declared war on the United States.

The expansion brought the fate of British, Dutch, American, and other colonial territories directly into Japanese campaigns. But their inhabitants were not blank spaces on the maps of imperial governments. The British Library's history of the Burma fighting points to a multinational army involving British, Indian, Nepalese, East African, and West African personnel. It even records the difficulty of communication among soldiers speaking many different languages. Uniforms bearing imperial insignia did not make all the wearers citizens of the same political order. Some served voluntarily for pay or advancement, others under pressure or through institutions shaped by colonial rule. Their motivations cannot be inferred from the flag under which they fought.

To campaign in Burma required airfields, supply depots, roads through difficult terrain, medical care, and the ability to replace equipment lost in the field. A battle map cannot show the full scale of labor needed to build and maintain these systems. It also cannot show how local food production and civilian movement were disrupted by marching armies. Allied claims to be fighting for freedom acquired a harder meaning when the army included colonized people whose own movements for self-government remained under imperial control. That tension is not an argument that Japanese occupation promised liberation. The expansion of Japanese imperial power brought violent occupation and forced labor. Rather, it means that both the defense of territory and the legitimacy of colonial rule require distinct questions.

## Production, transport, and information

War production linked mines, farms, factories, rail networks, ships, and airfields across continents. Oil and metal mattered because modern armies could not operate without them. Food mattered because soldiers, industrial workers, and displaced civilians could not live on proclamations. Industrial capacity mattered, but only when raw materials and machines could be turned into usable supplies and moved where they were needed. In this sense the difference between a fast advance and a stalled offensive may depend partly on what happened far from the front. A government that controls a factory in theory does not necessarily control the fuel, labor, or railway cars required for it to function.

Military leaders needed intelligence and public support. Governments censored news, made propaganda, classified casualties, and attempted to frame losses as temporary sacrifice or evidence of heroic endurance. Civilians had to interpret incomplete information while making immediate decisions about evacuating, hiding, working, or resisting. The historian's knowledge of an eventual Allied victory is a dangerous luxury when reconstructing those decisions. A person in occupied Europe in 1941 could not see a future map of 1945. An official in a besieged Soviet city, a farmer in Bengal, and a merchant in Shanghai faced different information and risks. The proper question is what each actor plausibly knew at the moment, not what we can now place in chronological order.

War also redistributed authority. Military administrations could dictate where people traveled, which crops they grew, what work they performed, and whether they were detained. A person under occupation might encounter the war first as the closing of a market, then as a compulsory work order, then as the disappearance of a neighbor. These experiences are not secondary because they lie outside a battlefield. They reveal how governments and armies turned military goals into material constraints on daily life. The documentary record must be examined carefully: a requisition order establishes a demand made by officials, not how consistently it was enforced; a survivor account establishes a particular experience, not automatically the average of a whole region.

## Reversal and the meaning of victory

By 1943 the balance was turning in several theaters. Soviet offensives rolled back German gains in the east. Allied forces advanced in North Africa and entered Italy. In June 1944 Allied armies landed in Normandy while the Soviet Union launched major offensives farther east. These operations were connected through pressure on German resources and choices, but they were not interchangeable. Geography, losses, industrial production, and political goals all shaped how different coalitions fought. The history of the war should resist treating one famous operation as if it single-handedly settled a conflict that had drawn in so many armies and peoples.

In spring 1945 Germany was defeated. The Soviet army reached Berlin, and German surrender in Europe took effect in early May. Victory was not simply the reversal of map colors. Vast numbers of people had been displaced, countries occupied, cities destroyed, and entire Jewish communities annihilated. Political decisions about reconstruction, borders, prosecutions, and the rights of survivors lay ahead. Japan continued fighting until its surrender later in 1945. Even then, the end of organized warfare did not return colonial subjects, refugees, or former prisoners to a world free of conflicting claims over sovereignty. The end of a war is a date; the end of its consequences is not.

A global account therefore has to hold several truths at once. Nazi Germany and imperial Japan pursued aggressive conquests with distinct ideologies and practices. Their opponents coordinated to defeat them, but the opposing coalition contained democracies, a Soviet dictatorship, and empires governing colonized peoples. Military victory prevented further Axis expansion and brought occupation to an end in many places. It did not remove every inequality within the victorious alliance. Nor should the collaboration necessary to defeat the Axis be mistaken for universal agreement about the peace that would follow. Distinguishing military alliance from political consent is essential to understanding the postwar world.

The most helpful way to remember this war is not as a list of generals with arrows pointing toward Berlin and Tokyo. It is to see that wars of conquest create enormous institutions of coercion, and that resistance requires institutions of production, supply, mutual aid, and political commitment in response. Each place has its own history. But once we understand how these histories became linked, the word world becomes meaningful. It tells us about connections among decisions made in capitals, labor demanded in distant territories, and the people whose lives were transformed by movements they could neither predict nor fully control.

## Source method and open research issues (display only)

- No personal testimony, speech, diary extract, dialogue, or private motive has been invented. Analytical comparisons are presented as interpretation, not disguised as documented episodes.
- Documented atrocities and state policies are described with direct institutional and scholarly references; uncertain totals are either attributed and qualified or omitted.
- Archival inventories establish the presence and provenance of documents, not the unexamined contents of those documents. Publisher abstracts establish an author’s stated thesis, not complete access to the work.
- Official statements and legal orders document what authorities said and demanded; testimony and independent inquiry are needed to establish how affected people experienced them.
- Narration and citations are separate so the lectures work in TTS. No word-count or lecture-duration rule has been used.
