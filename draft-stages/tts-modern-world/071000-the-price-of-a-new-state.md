# The Price of a New State: Russia in the 1990s

## A price changes overnight

Imagine arriving at a grocery counter in a Russian city early in nineteen ninety-two. The label on a loaf of bread still has a number. But the meaning of that number has changed. Prices once fixed by state offices are being released into a market in which sellers can change them, wholesalers must find supplies, and consumers must somehow make wages and savings keep pace. Nobody at the counter needs a lecture in macroeconomics to discover that the same cash buys less than yesterday. Yet to understand the events, we must look beyond the price itself. The collapse of the Soviet Union had broken networks of production, supply and payment across fifteen new states. The Russian government was attempting to replace one economic system while the institutions of the next were still being made.

Russia's leaders inherited factories, military laboratories, collective and state farms, apartment buildings, banks and a planning bureaucracy. They also inherited shortages, fiscal pressures, distorted relative prices and trade relationships built for a single union. Boris Yeltsin and reformers associated with Yegor Gaidar argued that keeping the old system of price controls and government support would prolong shortages and delay production responsive to demand. Critics argued that a sudden release of prices, with inadequate financial protection, would destroy household savings and expose people to a market they had little capacity to navigate. These were disagreements over speed, sequence and who would bear the losses, not simply a confrontation between people who desired freedom and people who opposed it.

In January nineteen ninety-two, the government liberalized most prices, not all of them, and began a wider program of trade and currency reform. An open price can tell buyers and sellers something about scarcity; it cannot by itself create an efficient bank, a functioning bankruptcy court or a fair system for enforcing contracts. Russia's monetary conditions made the transition more destructive. Inflation eroded ruble savings and fixed incomes, while firms were struggling to acquire materials and receive payment. For a pensioner who had carefully saved for retirement, the loss was not an abstract revision to an economic indicator. It could mean that years of accumulated purchasing power had disappeared faster than the state could redesign pensions.

## Ownership on paper

Next came the question of who owned enterprises previously held by the state. The voucher-privatization program distributed claims that citizens could exchange, sell or place in investment funds. In principle, this offered a way for millions of citizens to participate in changing ownership. In practice, a voucher was only as valuable as the information and bargaining position of its holder. People who needed cash immediately could sell cheaply; managers knew more about the condition of their plants; and controlling a block of shares mattered more than possessing a single share. A factory could be listed as privatized while its old directors still made most important decisions. Changing legal title was not the same thing as creating accountable ownership.

The first privatization wave is often remembered for images of citizens exchanging vouchers and managers acquiring enterprises. The later loans-for-shares auctions became still more controversial. In nineteen ninety-five, banks supplied loans to a cash-strapped government, with stakes in valuable state enterprises pledged as security. When the government did not redeem these stakes, connected financiers acquired substantial positions in major natural-resource companies. Official and retrospective inquiries describe limited competition, conflicts of interest and weak transparency. The arrangement helped concentrate economic and political influence. It is important to state the mechanism rather than merely use the word oligarch, which can become a label that explains nothing about how power was obtained.

Historians and economists continue to disagree about how much of later inequality and industrial performance the loans-for-shares transactions themselves caused. Daniel Treisman's later reassessment accepts that the auctions appeared corrupt while questioning some claims about their aggregate economic effects. Other accounts emphasize the decisive political and legal advantages of insiders. These perspectives are not reconciled by pretending the auctions were competitive, and they need not be reconciled by assuming every enterprise acquired by an insider subsequently performed badly. The strongest conclusion is narrower: ownership became highly concentrated through a process that many citizens could reasonably perceive as denying them equal participation in the national assets they had once been told belonged to everyone.

## A constitution in the streets

Economic transformation collided with an unresolved constitutional order. Russia did not begin the nineteen nineties with a settled division of powers between the presidency, parliament and courts. Yeltsin and the legislature disputed who could authorize reforms, appoint governments and define the rules under which the state itself would operate. Parliamentary opponents included communists, nationalists and other critics of executive power; their differences mattered. Some defended social protections or opposed the pace of reform, while others sought a different arrangement of national authority. A conflict about prices and privatization thus became a conflict about the legitimacy of the institutions making the decisions.

On the twenty-first of September nineteen ninety-three, Yeltsin issued a decree dissolving the existing legislature. Deputies refused to accept it and asserted that Yeltsin had exceeded his constitutional authority. For days, opposing authorities claimed to speak for Russia. The confrontation turned violent in October. Armed clashes took place in Moscow, and on the fourth of October, tanks fired at the parliament building known as the White House. The sight of artillery aimed at the seat of an elected legislature should not be polished into an ordinary procedural milestone. People died. The precise meaning of the events remains disputed in Russian political memory, but the use of force is not in dispute.

A new constitution was approved by referendum in December nineteen ninety-three, alongside parliamentary elections. It created a powerful presidency and a new legislature, the State Duma and Federation Council. Russia still had competitive elections and a lively, though unequal, media environment in the nineteen nineties. Yet the circumstances of the constitution's birth shaped what followed: the presidency acquired considerable formal authority while much of the state's administrative capacity and its independence from private money remained contested. Elections could determine who occupied offices without resolving who controlled television channels, regional patronage networks or the enforcement of contracts. Democracy depends on more than holding a ballot after a violent institutional showdown.

## Work without pay

For many working households the most characteristic labor-market experience was not a formal notice of dismissal. It was staying employed but not being paid on time. In some places a factory continued production, kept people on its books and delayed wages for weeks or months. Workers could be offered goods in place of cash or told that the enterprise itself had not been paid by its customers. The economists Hartmut Lehmann, Jonathan Wadsworth and Alessandro Acquisti used household surveys to show the scale of delayed pay across private firms, public enterprises and government-funded workplaces. Regional labor markets mattered: a worker could not simply change jobs if every potential employer nearby was also short of money.

This makes wage arrears more than a financial curiosity. Wages are a promise about time: work performed this month will support rent, food and travel next month. When payment is postponed, employers transfer uncertainty to employees. Pensions suffered similar interruptions, and the problem differed widely by region. A family might cultivate vegetables, exchange favors with relatives, sell personal possessions or take a second job. Those possibilities were not equally available. A person with land or relatives nearby had options another person did not. An economic reform that increases potential efficiency in the long term can still impose severe immediate burdens when social insurance, competition and the rules of payment are weak.

The social picture resists a simple claim that all Russians were made poor or that everyone was free to prosper. New retail businesses, local entrepreneurs and expanding consumer choice were real. So were regional unemployment, collapsing industrial towns, rising inequality and uncertainty about whether a public service or a pension would arrive. Urban professionals with access to foreign currency or new private firms often faced different opportunities from elderly people whose income was fixed in rubles. In villages and industrial regions, memories of the Soviet system could include both coercion and real expectations of paid work, schooling and guaranteed services. Historical analysis has to keep these mixed facts together, rather than use hardship as proof that no reform was needed.

## Two wars at once

Russia's transition was also a question of borders and sovereignty. Chechnya, a republic within the Russian Federation, was the site of an independence struggle and of military campaigns fought in the midst of the new state's political instability. Russian forces entered in December nineteen ninety-four, and the assault on Grozny brought severe civilian suffering. Human Rights Watch documented indiscriminate shelling, abuse and displacement. Chechen fighters and armed groups also committed violations. A ceasefire and the Khasavyurt agreements of August nineteen ninety-six ended that phase of large-scale fighting without producing a durable settlement of Chechnya's political status. The conflict was not an economic footnote. It exposed how military coercion and contested national membership could coexist with talk of democratic renewal.

In nineteen ninety-nine violence escalated again. Armed incursions from Chechnya into Dagestan and a series of apartment bombings blamed by the Russian government on Chechen terrorists preceded another Russian military campaign. Responsibility for some events remains politically disputed; historical evidence about a stated justification must be separated from proof of every allegation. Once fighting resumed, civilians faced bombardment, displacement and grave violations of humanitarian law. The new war also helped change public politics at a moment when Yeltsin's government was already losing authority. Any story of Russia's market transition that leaves out the wars misses a central part of the argument about what the new Russian state would be allowed to do.

## The August crisis

By nineteen ninety-eight the government had become heavily dependent on short-term borrowing. Russia issued treasury bills called G K O s, using new borrowing to finance its obligations while trying to maintain the ruble's exchange-rate band. Investors demanded increasingly high returns as doubts grew about the state's ability to raise taxes, service debts and hold the currency's value. Falling commodity prices, banking weaknesses and the aftershocks of financial crisis in Asia added pressure. These were connected problems: banks holding government debt could lose assets if the government restructured, while banks promising foreign currency to creditors could be overwhelmed if the ruble fell. A public debt problem and a currency problem were becoming a banking problem together.

On August seventeenth, nineteen ninety-eight, Russian authorities announced emergency measures including a restructuring of certain ruble-denominated government debts and changes to the exchange-rate system. The ruble depreciated sharply after attempts to defend the new band failed. Financial institutions could not honor many obligations, prices of imported goods rose, and households and businesses experienced another shock to their savings and payments. A government announcement described in the language of maturities and moratoria became a problem of whether a shop could obtain inventory or whether a depositor could withdraw money. The official account of the crisis is valuable for its dates and mechanisms, but it does not tell us every household's experience.

International lenders and Russian officials disputed what could have prevented the crash. Some emphasized persistent budget deficits and weak tax collection. Others stressed the risks of a fragile banking system, political incentives, short-term capital movements, the defense of an exchange rate and the limited legitimacy of reform. The International Monetary Fund participated in financing programs and later assessed their failure; it was not a disinterested observer. After the collapse, a weaker currency eventually helped some domestic producers compete with imports, and growth resumed. Neither that recovery nor the deep losses of the preceding years proves that a single alternative policy would have solved every constraint. We should keep mechanisms, judgments and counterfactuals distinct.

## A handover without a settled order

In August nineteen ninety-nine Yeltsin selected Vladimir Putin as prime minister and indicated that he preferred him as successor. Putin had directed the Federal Security Service, and his political ascent coincided with the renewed war in Chechnya. On December thirty-first, nineteen ninety-nine, Yeltsin resigned, making Putin acting president under the constitutional succession rules. The handover had a legal form, but it occurred inside a political environment already shaped by extraordinary presidential authority, oligarchic influence, war and repeated economic emergencies. Later events did not follow mechanically from one privatization auction or a single constitutional provision. Still, the path into the new century had already narrowed the choices available to Russian citizens.

The most revealing question may be not simply whether the Soviet command economy should have ended, but how a society determines the terms on which a replacement is built. Who may rewrite the constitution? Who has enforceable rights over public property? Who gets paid during a transition, and who can sue when a promise is broken? Can a region contest the center without being subjected to overwhelming force? Those questions link the price on a shop shelf to the authority of a state. They also reveal why the word transition can mislead. It sounds like the journey from one fixed point to another. For the people living through Russia's nineteen nineties, the destination was fiercely contested, and the road was shaped by choices that could have been made differently.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Further work should include independent Russian household oral histories, the direct Russian-language legal record of 1993, regional factory archives and Chechen testimony. Institutional assessments of 1998 cannot alone settle policy counterfactuals. Dates and disputed causal interpretations are attributed or qualified.
