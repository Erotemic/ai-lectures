# No Single African Century: Politics, Health, and Economic Choices After 2000

## A handover and a question

In Ghana at the turn of the millennium, an election presented voters with a choice about government, not merely a ceremony confirming power already held. The governing party faced a strong opposition, and the presidential contest went to a runoff. John Kufuor's victory marked a transfer of national executive power to the opposition after Jerry Rawlings completed the terms allowed by Ghana's constitution. The change was important because it demonstrated that political competition could produce a new government under agreed rules. But one election did not answer how services would be funded, whether every voter would feel represented, or whether political parties would remain accountable between elections. A durable democracy has to do more than hold a credible handover. It must also give the people who lost the vote a reason to believe they can win another one.

Ghana's experience was not a report card for the continent. At nearly the same moment, other African states were moving through civil wars, contested elections, multiparty negotiations, authoritarian consolidation, economic recovery, and local organizing. We need to resist the habit of calling Africa one story, whether the story is perpetual crisis or a sudden economic miracle. The continent contains many political systems, languages, and histories. States differ in resource wealth, infrastructure, debt obligations, and relations with their neighbors. Even within one country, a capital city and an agricultural district can inhabit quite different economies. The shared history of colonial boundary-making and postcolonial development matters, but it does not erase the distinct choices being made by citizens and governments.

## A new continental organization

In July two thousand and two, African heads of state officially launched the African Union in Durban, South Africa. The new organization succeeded the Organisation of African Unity, founded in nineteen sixty-three during the struggle to end colonial rule. Leaders proposed a more ambitious agenda for integration, peace and security, rights, and development. The AU's Constitutive Act, adopted in two thousand, gave the organization institutions and principles on paper. Those principles were not self-enforcing. They raised difficult questions about whether regional bodies could intervene when states failed to protect people, and how far governments would accept scrutiny from their neighbors. Creating an institution is an act of politics; making it work requires money, legitimacy, and members willing to accept obligations when it is inconvenient.

Around the same time, African leaders advanced the New Partnership for Africa's Development, known as NEPAD. Its original framework, formalized in two thousand and one, argued for African-directed development, investment in human capacities, stronger economic institutions, and a different relationship with global capital and aid. It combined a call for greater national responsibility with a demand for more equitable international arrangements. This was neither a rejection of international cooperation nor simple endorsement of donor-led programs. The plan's authors were attempting to influence the terms on which African economies would participate in world markets. Some critics questioned whether its reliance on investment and market integration could deliver broad prosperity. The interesting historical fact is not that the leaders agreed on a magic formula. It is that African policymakers proposed their own framework and had to confront disagreement about its content and implementation.

## Democracy is tested between elections

Nigeria returned to civilian government in May nineteen ninety-nine after years of military rule. Olusegun Obasanjo, himself a former military ruler, became president under a new constitutional order. The transition enlarged space for political life, but observers documented serious electoral irregularities and continuing weaknesses in the protection of rights. Nigeria's federal system distributed authority across states and local governments, while oil revenue connected national budgets to international markets. The political question was not simply whether a military officer had left the presidential residence. It was whether courts, elections, civic organizations, and the press could limit arbitrary power and whether citizens could demand public services in return for the resources extracted from their regions.

In the Niger Delta, communities lived near oil installations whose revenues flowed through national and international networks. Residents had long raised complaints about pollution, land, security, and the distribution of benefits. The return to civilian rule did not erase those demands, and conflict over resources continued. A national budget could show revenue from petroleum, while a village asked about a damaged waterway, a job, or police conduct. These were connected views of an economy, but they were not interchangeable. Some local actors advocated nonviolent protest and negotiation; others entered armed conflict, and state security responses also affected civilian lives. Reading only the account of the oil company or only the statements of central government would miss the range of local perspectives. A democratic constitution does not by itself settle who governs valuable land and water.

The contrast with Ghana becomes sharper when we consider Kenya's elections of December two thousand and seven. Disputed results sparked violence that included political attacks, displacement, and killings. The Kenyan Commission of Inquiry into Post-Election Violence later documented one thousand one hundred and thirty-three deaths, while examining responsibility across political actors and security institutions. A negotiated power-sharing arrangement in two thousand and eight helped end the immediate crisis, but it did not erase the harm or the issues that had made the electoral contest combustible. The lesson is not that elections cause violence. It is that competitive votes can become dangerous when trust in counting, security, and lawful remedies is weak. People need a legitimate way to challenge outcomes without being forced into confrontation or punished for where they live.

## Care as political work

In South Africa, another kind of political contest unfolded around HIV treatment. Effective antiretroviral combinations had transformed the prognosis of many patients in wealthier countries, but access in much of Africa remained severely constrained by price, infrastructure, and political choices. Community organizations, clinicians, scientists, and people living with HIV pushed for testing, treatment, and dignity. The Treatment Action Campaign combined public demonstrations, education, legal arguments, and alliance building. Its demands concerned the practical meaning of constitutional health rights: a right written in law could be empty for someone who could not obtain a medicine. Activists rejected the assumption that Africans should accept a different standard of treatment because national budgets were strained or systems were difficult to organize.

In July two thousand and two, South Africa's Constitutional Court ruled in a case brought by the Treatment Action Campaign concerning nevirapine and prevention of HIV transmission from pregnant women to infants. The Court found the government's restrictions unreasonable and ordered the removal of barriers to wider access within public services. It did not claim that a judgment alone would build clinics, train staff, or solve HIV. Those tasks required follow-through and continued advocacy. The legal case illustrates how citizens used institutions built during South Africa's democratic transition to contest government policy. Patients and families were not only subjects of a public-health emergency; they were political actors who argued about the evidence, demanded accountability, and changed what the state was obliged to attempt.

Clinics in Khayelitsha, near Cape Town, helped demonstrate the possibility of delivering antiretroviral treatment through public-health services facing difficult conditions. Médecins Sans Frontières worked with local partners as treatment initiatives developed, while organizers pressed for broader access. The work involved more than distributing pills. People needed ongoing clinical care, reliable supplies, information about treatment, and protection against stigma. Public confidence depended on the ability of nurses, counselors, patients, and families to sustain relationships over time. Across African countries, the HIV epidemic followed different patterns and public-health responses. Even where the same drug was available, the outcome could depend on local transportation, public budgets, legal rules, and trust. Disease has biological mechanisms; access to treatment is also a history of institutions and politics.

## Debt, markets, and investment

Debt remained another central question. The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries Initiative, launched by the World Bank and International Monetary Fund in nineteen ninety-six and expanded in nineteen ninety-nine, offered a process for reducing some countries' unsustainable obligations. A further multilateral debt-relief initiative was agreed in two thousand and five and implemented by the IMF beginning in two thousand and six. Relief could free resources previously needed for debt service, but eligibility and policy conditions mattered. Writing off debt did not automatically create productive investment, repair a health system, or eliminate dependence on volatile exports. Governments still had to decide which public services to fund, how to tax, and what to do when commodity prices fell. Debt relief was a policy mechanism with possible benefits and limits, not a certificate that a development problem had been solved.

Commodity prices and demand from expanding economies, including China, altered trade opportunities for some African producers during the two thousands. Oil, metals, and other raw materials could bring foreign exchange and investment, but those revenues were concentrated in particular countries and industries. Landlocked economies, agricultural producers, and urban service sectors faced different opportunities. A new road to a mine might generate wages and public revenue while also raising questions about land rights and the distribution of profits. A high national growth rate could coexist with unemployment, weak schools, or expensive food. The appropriate question is not whether growth is good or bad in the abstract. It is which activities grew, how workers and households participated, and whether institutions could convert revenues into lasting services.

## Phones, distance, and new business

Kenya also supplied a striking example of innovation in the financial system. In March two thousand and seven, Safaricom launched M-PESA, a service that let customers transfer small amounts of money using ordinary mobile phones and a network of agents. A person did not need a nearby bank branch to send money to a relative. Cash could be deposited and withdrawn through participating outlets, with a digital balance moving between registered accounts. The system grew rapidly, aided by demand for domestic remittances and by its fit with existing mobile networks. Its launch was not proof that every Kenyan suddenly gained financial security. Access to a phone, agent liquidity, fees, and trust still mattered. But the example shows innovation emerging in response to local financial needs, not simply being imported from wealthier markets.

A mobile payment service changes the meaning of distance. For a migrant working in Nairobi, transferring money to a rural household could become quicker and less costly than sending cash through an informal courier. For a shopkeeper, an agent network could help circulate payments without traditional bank infrastructure. That development required technology, but it also required regulation, commercial judgment, and confidence in how customer funds would be safeguarded. Many African economies were experimenting with such arrangements rather than waiting for a single future of modernization to arrive. It would be just as misleading to treat the Kenyan example as a complete continental model as it would be to ignore it because it does not match the familiar picture of a factory-led industrial revolution.

## What political categories hide

The early twenty-first century contained several political vocabularies at once: democratization, regional integration, human rights, security, and development. These words could point in compatible directions, but they could also conflict. A government might invoke national unity while limiting an opposition party. A regional organization might endorse accountability but lack the authority or resources to enforce its commitments. An election could transfer power peacefully in one country and become a struggle over recognition in another. Citizens' movements made demands within and across these institutions. It is through their work, as well as presidents' signatures, that politics becomes a lived process. History should not call an entire continent democratic or authoritarian based on a small sample of its countries.

The economic vocabularies also overlapped. A finance ministry might measure debt-service savings, a clinic might count treatment appointments, a farmer might worry about transport costs, and a family might rely on small remittances. All four belong to development, yet no one measure can stand in for the others. In a large country, change may reach people at different rates; in a small country, a political or climatic shock can reverse earlier gains. International organizations provide useful comparable data, but their categories have limits. The most revealing sources sometimes come from a lawsuit, a local election, a health committee, or the founding of a payment service. They show which questions people regarded as urgent when broad statistical summaries were being written.

The story of Africa after two thousand is therefore not a continent waiting for history to happen to it. African states and citizens helped establish new regional institutions, contested the legitimacy of governments, developed legal claims to lifesaving treatment, negotiated debt arrangements, and invented services shaped by the problems of daily life. None of these achievements erased conflict or inequality. Together they challenge the idea that political and economic change has a single track from failure to success. The better question is always more specific: in which place, under whose authority, with whose participation, and at whose expense did change occur? Only then can continental institutions and global trends be connected to the choices made by people who lived through them.
