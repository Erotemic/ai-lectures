# A War With More Than One Front: Empire, Carriers, and the Global First World War

## A world war is not the same thing as a European war

Suppose a history book opens the First World War on a map of France and Belgium. It shows trenches, artillery, and a front moving only a little despite appalling losses. That map describes something real and essential. But it leaves out why a farmer in East Africa might be seized to carry ammunition; why a student from northern China might become a laborer in France; why Australians began their campaign in a German colony in the Pacific; and why an Armenian family inside the Ottoman Empire could be threatened by the wartime government itself. The name world war should direct our attention to several overlapping histories, not merely to fighting in Europe with international participants.

The crucial mechanism was empire. Britain and France could draw upon colonial armies, recruits, shipping, taxes, and labor across continents. Germany held colonies in Africa and the Pacific. The Ottoman Empire, its own multiethnic state with territory across parts of Europe and Asia, entered on Germany's side. Japan fought against Germany and seized opportunities in East Asia and the Pacific. An event that began within European diplomatic and military alliances therefore changed routes of trade, the allocation of food, the demands imposed on communities, and the local balance of political power in places far from the Western Front. Those consequences cannot be evaluated solely by counting battles.

There is a further distinction between geography and agency. A territory on a war map may be colored to indicate an empire. That does not mean its inhabitants agreed with the empire's war policy. People volunteered, were conscripted, found employment, resisted recruitment, moved to protect family members, sought political concessions, or pursued existing local conflicts under changed conditions. The evidence often records administrative decisions more fully than the choices of those subject to them. We will therefore ask not only where the armies moved but who ordered the movement, who supplied it, who could refuse, and what the war meant to people whose names rarely appear in national commemorations.

## How Europe drew on the lives of an empire

Indian soldiers served in France, Flanders, and several other theatres of war. The National Army Museum records Indian infantry and cavalry divisions arriving on the Western Front by the end of 1914; the imperial war effort also depended on medical and logistical service. To understand participation, distinguish a regiment's deployment from an individual's reasons for service. A military order can show when a unit moved. It does not tell us whether a soldier enlisted for wages, family tradition, ambition, or loyalty, nor whether those motives changed under fire. Wartime letters and military papers offer partial routes into the experience, but they require handling as mediated records.

Consider the Reports of the Censor of Indian Mails in France. A British Library discussion explains how soldiers' letters were sometimes dictated to scribes, translated, extracted into reports, and screened by officials anxious about military secrecy and political unrest. Each step changes what the historian can recover. A sentence appearing in an English censor's report is not an unfiltered window into a soldier's thoughts. Yet the existence of this record shows that servicemen communicated across imperial distances and that administrators regarded those communications as politically significant. We can read the letters for what they convey while also asking which voices never reached the archive, and which statements never escaped censorship.

The British West Indies Regiment provides another case. Recruits came from across Caribbean colonies to serve in Africa, Europe, and the Middle East, often assigned labor tasks close to military danger. The division between combat and labor was not a division between dangerous and safe work. Racist assumptions also affected training, duties, and treatment. The National Army Museum traces the regiment's later confrontation with unequal treatment at Taranto, which we will return to when discussing demobilization. The sequence challenges the idea that mobilization automatically produced equal citizenship: governments could demand sacrifice without offering equal status to the people who served.

Indigenous participation further complicates the imperial picture. The Australian War Memorial notes that Aboriginal men enlisted despite laws and recruitment practices that restricted Indigenous service. It estimates their participation while acknowledging that the documentary count is incomplete. It would be a mistake to supply a single patriotic motive for all these men. Their decisions occurred within a settler state that had not recognized Indigenous sovereignty through treaties and that did not offer equal rights. Enlistment and exclusion could coexist. The war mobilized individuals inside a political order whose inequalities it did not simply suspend.

## East Africa: the war carried on human backs

In East Africa, German and Allied forces fought a mobile campaign lasting through 1918. German colonial troops included African askaris; the opposing forces also included African soldiers and men from several parts of the British Empire. The National Army Museum describes a campaign involving extended movement over regions with inadequate roads and railways for military transport. Here the word logistics means something tangible: food, guns, ammunition, and medical materials had to travel where motor transport often could not. That work fell heavily on carriers, many recruited through colonial authority. A battle map may show an army arrow. For those carrying supplies, the same arrow represented prolonged journeys, exhaustion, exposure, and danger.

Michael Crowder's chapter in UNESCO's General History of Africa stresses that carriers, soldiers, women, and children experienced the campaign's demands and that compulsory recruitment provoked resistance. Donald Savage and J. Forbes Munro describe how British East Africa's 1915 recruitment legislation clarified the colonial government's ability to compel men into the Carrier Corps. Their published abstract specifically notes evasion and complaints. These sources establish that carriers were not simply a voluntary auxiliary force. They do not, by themselves, identify the experiences of particular households. To recover those, one would need oral histories, local records, and accounts gathered beyond the military headquarters.

The human cost was not distributed in proportion to formal military rank. Colonial soldiers faced combat, but communities also lost agricultural labor and household support when men were taken to serve as carriers. Armies requisitioned food and relied on territories already subject to unequal rule. Disease and malnutrition mattered alongside bullets. A government tally of battlefield casualties consequently captures only one part of the harm. Crowder's emphasis on social and economic disruption invites a broader question: after the army moved on, what happened to planting, harvest, livestock, markets, and the people waiting for workers to return? The answers varied, but that variation is part of the war's history.

We should not collapse African wartime politics into reactions to European orders alone. Rival colonial administrations fought over territories, and existing African political relationships altered the scope of mobilization. Some leaders sought accommodation; others organized resistance or attempted to exploit the crisis. Crowder discusses how recruitment pressures helped provoke opposition and how the European presence appeared less secure when rival colonial forces fought each other. The lesson is not that all African societies adopted one common position. It is that the war exposed contradictions in imperial authority while placing severe demands on people with little say over the decision to fight.

## From the Pacific to the Dardanelles

Early in the war, Australian forces occupied German possessions in New Guinea. Japan fought Germany in East Asia, including the campaign against the German position at Qingdao in Shandong, and acquired strategic influence in the Pacific. These operations matter because they changed which powers controlled ports, islands, and communications routes. Qingdao was not empty territory waiting to change flags. It lay in China, whose own sovereignty and diplomatic claims would become central in 1919. The fighting in 1914 therefore created questions that no European armistice could settle merely by stopping gunfire on the Western Front.

The Dardanelles campaign shows how the Ottoman front drew forces across the world. In 1915 British and French forces, together with Australians and New Zealanders, attempted to force the straits and threaten the Ottoman capital. The campaign became associated in Australian and New Zealand memory with the April landings at Gallipoli. But the people defending the peninsula were not incidental scenery for someone else's national awakening. Ottoman troops confronted an invasion of territory under their state's control. The effort failed, followed by Allied evacuation. Placing both sides in the account changes the story from a rite of passage for one nation's soldiers into an encounter among states and societies with incompatible aims.

Gallipoli and East Africa look militarily different. One involved amphibious landings, entrenched positions, and evacuation by sea. The other depended on long-distance movement and enormous burdens on carriers. Yet both depended on global transport and imperial manpower. Both generated histories remembered differently by governments, descendants, veterans, and people whose labor was requisitioned. The comparative lesson is methodological. A single measure such as territory gained, losses counted, or campaigns won is too narrow to capture the consequences. Ask also who had the authority to start the operation and who was required to sustain it.

## China and the enormous machinery of labor

China's participation reveals another way a society might enter a world war. While China declared war on Germany in 1917, Chinese laborers had already begun traveling under arrangements with French recruiters. British recruitment followed. Historian Steven E. Rowe discusses Chinese republican officials and elites who saw labor service as a way to build international standing, while employers and military authorities needed workers for transport, repair, construction, and support. Chinese laborers were not a uniform mass: conditions, contracts, places of work, and the institutions supervising them differed. The important point is that diplomatic strategy and physical labor intersected across continents.

Workers crossed oceans and continents to reach France. Some served with the British Chinese Labour Corps; others worked under French arrangements in factories, ports, or supply services. Their labor included roadwork, trench support, equipment repair, and dangerous post-battle tasks. Rowe describes grievances about pay, food, and living conditions, as well as worker strikes. That is a more accurate picture than reducing Chinese participation to silent manpower. Laborers had interests of their own. They negotiated and protested within unequal systems in which the governments recruiting them also controlled their movement, accommodation, and discipline.

This episode changes how we think about wartime citizenship. Chinese officials regarded the labor program partly as a route toward being taken seriously at the eventual peace conference. Workers, meanwhile, bore the risks and constraints of overseas employment. A state's diplomatic ambition and an individual's practical calculation are not the same thing. Later, when the Paris settlement assigned former German rights in Shandong to Japan rather than returning them to Chinese control, many Chinese students and activists interpreted it as a betrayal. The workers' contribution did not translate automatically into the political settlement some had hoped for. We will examine the precise treaty clauses in the postwar lecture.

## The Ottoman war, civilians, and the boundaries of violence

The Ottoman Empire fought on multiple fronts and experienced profound civilian suffering. It is important not to write the Ottoman side exclusively as the opposing army at Gallipoli, nor to imply that every Ottoman population faced identical dangers. During the war, the Committee of Union and Progress government directed mass deportations and killings of Armenian civilians. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents the arrests of Armenian leaders in April 1915 and the subsequent expansion of deportations into areas far from active fighting. Wartime claims of security were used by perpetrators, but such claims do not establish that the persecution was a legitimate military necessity.

The Armenian genocide is a central part of the war's history, not a separate event to be added only after campaigns and treaties have been described. Ottoman officials targeted a population within their own state's territory; civilians were driven on marches, dispossessed, and killed or made to die in conditions of starvation and disease. Contemporary documentation includes diplomatic, relief, and witness reports. The scale and mechanism of the crime have been studied by historians and genocide researchers. One can recognize the distinction between battlefield deaths and intentional mass persecution without claiming that every participant in the Ottoman armed forces or every resident of the empire held the same beliefs.

The comparison with East Africa is not a claim that all forms of violence were identical. Rather, it tests the adequacy of a history built exclusively around troop movements. Coercive recruitment, shortages, epidemic disease, deliberate deportation, and genocidal violence had different causes and degrees of intention. They require different evidence. The fact that each occurred in connection with the same world conflict shows why a comprehensive history must keep the categories separate. It is possible to be attentive to the war's logistical systems without losing the moral and historical specificity of persecution.

## What the armistice did not end

On 11 November 1918 an armistice ended fighting between Germany and the Allied powers on the Western Front. It did not bring every soldier, worker, or carrier safely home, restore food supplies, resolve colonial grievances, or decide what would happen to the former colonies of defeated powers. The British West Indies Regiment would face discriminatory treatment while awaiting transport home. Chinese laborers still had work to do and decisions to make about return. Former German territories in Africa and the Pacific would be redistributed within an international system that continued to treat their inhabitants as people to be governed by others.

The evidence we have used is intentionally heterogeneous. A museum account can establish a campaign sequence. An academic chapter can compare recruitment systems. A military censor's file records a letter only after it has passed through administrative controls. A study of colonial labor can reveal structures of coercion without identifying every affected person. None can substitute for all the others. That is why the demand for careful attribution matters: the historian should state what a particular document can support and resist presenting a general source as proof of someone's private thoughts.

The war deserves to be called global not because every society experienced it in the same way, but because military, economic, and political decisions became entangled over long distances. Different peoples encountered the conflict as combatants, carriers, migrants, civilians under occupation, victims of state persecution, or political activists. Some wartime choices would reach forward into the peace settlements, anticolonial movements, and new institutions of international law. Next we will turn away from commanders and look more closely at the households, workplaces, hospitals, public finances, and returning soldiers for whom the end of battle was not the end of crisis.

- Historical examples are documentary or explicitly analytical; no fabricated testimony or invented dialogue has been attributed to historical participants.

- Statistical estimates, especially of influenza mortality and wartime deaths, carry model and source uncertainty and should not be presented as exact individual histories.
