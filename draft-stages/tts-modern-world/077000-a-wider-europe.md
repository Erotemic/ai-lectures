# A Wider Europe: Enlargement and Unequal Mobility, 1993–2007

## A border that changes without moving

On May first, two thousand and four, an enormous political border changed without an army crossing it. Ten states became members of the European Union in a single day: Cyprus, the Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia. The Union grew from fifteen members to twenty-five, incorporating more than seventy-four million people. A truck driver crossing from Poland into Germany might still encounter a border post. A worker might still need permission to take a job in another member state. But the legal and economic relationship between governments had changed. Decisions about trade, environmental rules, public procurement, and the movement of people would increasingly be made within shared European institutions. The event joined together countries with very different recent histories and left many of their differences intact.

The date can make enlargement look inevitable, a diplomatic ceremony after Europe had simply reunited. The process was neither automatic nor free of political conflict. Candidate governments spent years negotiating rules and changing domestic institutions. In several countries, citizens voted in referendums; governments also had to persuade parliaments, businesses, farmers, and workers that membership was worth its costs. Some saw accession as security against future domination or as formal recognition of the democratic transformations after nineteen eighty-nine. Others feared that domestic industries and agricultural communities would struggle against firms from wealthier countries. And the governments already inside the Union had their own concerns about budgets, wages, borders, and the operation of institutions designed for fewer members. The negotiation was a bargain among unequal economies, not a ceremony in which every participant entered from the same starting point.

## What joining required

In nineteen ninety-three, European leaders adopted what became known as the Copenhagen criteria. A candidate needed stable democratic institutions, respect for the rule of law and human rights, protection of minorities, a functioning market economy, and the capacity to carry out the obligations of membership. These obligations included a vast body of European law and administrative practice, often referred to by the French expression, the acquis. The label is abstract; the work is concrete. A food inspector must be able to enforce common product standards. A court must be able to apply relevant rules. A customs office has to exchange information with other states. A government procurement system needs procedures that firms can challenge. Preparing for membership was therefore partly a political and economic restructuring of everyday administrative life.

Membership criteria and actual governance should be kept separate. A country could formally adopt a law to protect minorities while leaving serious inequalities in schools or housing. It could create a competition agency before that agency had enough trained investigators. It could privatize a state enterprise and still struggle to establish effective regulators. European institutions examined candidates' progress, but admission was also shaped by political judgments about stability and strategic commitment. For some citizens, those judgments were reassuring because they offered external pressure for reforms their own governments might otherwise avoid. For others, they raised a democratic problem: why should rules with profound local consequences be negotiated with distant institutions whose leverage was greatest before accession? This tension between common standards and political self-government did not disappear once the accession treaty took effect.

## More than one route to Europe

The eight countries joining from central and eastern Europe had experienced socialist state systems or Soviet domination in different forms. Poland's postcommunist transformation was not the same as Estonia's reassertion of independence or Slovenia's departure from Yugoslavia. The Baltic states, occupied and incorporated into the Soviet Union during the Second World War, regarded restored statehood and European membership as connected questions of sovereignty. The Czech Republic and Slovakia had separated peacefully in nineteen ninety-three, then followed their own political and economic paths. Hungary and Poland confronted reforms of factories, banks, farmland, and municipal services as they prepared to compete in a wider market. Enlargement gathered these histories under one treaty; it did not make them copies of one another.

Cyprus and Malta remind us that the accession was not only an eastward movement across former Cold War boundaries. Malta was a Mediterranean island state with its own institutions, trade relationships, and contested arguments about the costs of EU membership. Cyprus joined while the island remained divided. The government of the Republic of Cyprus did not exercise effective control over the northern area, where Turkish Cypriots lived under a separate de facto administration. European law was suspended in that territory under the terms of accession, even though Cyprus as a whole joined the Union. Citizenship and legal application did not line up neatly on the map. Here was a direct test of the idea that joining a common legal order could itself settle disputes about territory or identity. It could not. The border question entered the Union with the new member.

## A market is made of rules

The single market promised that goods and many services could circulate under common rules across a much larger area. For a manufacturer, this might mean that a product meeting the relevant standard could be sold into many national markets. For a small business, it could mean facing new competitors with better equipment, cheaper financing, or established distribution networks. Investors could plan production across borders. Firms might relocate operations toward lower wages or closer to customers. Prices and jobs would not respond identically everywhere. The gains from greater trade could coexist with the closure of a particular factory or with pressure on a rural community whose local market had been protected. Treating aggregate economic growth as a complete judgment on enlargement would leave out the geography of its costs.

European funding programs offered another mechanism of integration. Transport links, municipal infrastructure, training programs, and environmental projects could receive support to help less wealthy regions develop and adapt to common standards. Support did not mean money arrived automatically whenever a town needed a bridge. Projects had to be proposed, administered, audited, and often co-financed under detailed rules. Stronger local administrations could sometimes make better use of funds than communities that needed capacity most urgently. The larger historical question was who could convert membership into tangible opportunities. A legal right to compete in the single market is most valuable to a firm that can finance production and meet standards. A university exchange is more accessible to a student who can afford travel and housing. Distribution matters even when membership creates genuine benefits overall.

## The right to move and the right to work

An essential promise of European citizenship was mobility, but labor mobility in two thousand and four was not immediately unrestricted everywhere. Many of the older member states imposed transitional limits on workers from the eight central and eastern European entrants. Britain, Ireland, and Sweden opened their labor markets at accession; others retained restrictions for periods allowed under the treaty. The result was a patchwork. A Polish engineer could find different rules depending on whether she sought a job in London, Dublin, Berlin, or Vienna. Travel, residence, employment, social benefits, and professional recognition were related rights with different administrative conditions. Saying that borders disappeared conceals this legal complexity. Saying nothing changed conceals the scale of the new possibilities that migration created.

Migration brought workers to construction sites, farms, hospitals, universities, transport companies, hotels, and private households. Sending communities could gain through wages earned abroad and through returning workers' skills, while losing nurses, engineers, young parents, or skilled tradespeople for years. Receiving communities gained labor and new enterprises, but also faced questions about housing, schools, recognition of qualifications, and enforcement of labor standards. Public debate often reduced people to a choice between being useful labor and being an economic threat. The human reality was more varied. A person could send money home while raising children across borders; another could discover that a degree was not recognized in the country where jobs were available. The common market and the family geography of Europe were changing together, not always at the same pace.

## Membership is not the euro

One of the most persistent confusions about this moment is that joining the European Union meant immediately using the euro. It did not. The euro was already circulating in several older member states, but the ten countries of the two thousand and four enlargement did not all adopt it upon entry. New members were expected to meet separate monetary convergence criteria before joining the euro area; there was no automatic timetable that turned accession into a common currency on the same day. Slovenia became the first of the two thousand and four entrants to use the euro in January two thousand and seven. The distinction matters because a shared market and a shared currency involve different kinds of policy constraints. Governments can trade under common standards while retaining national monetary instruments, at least until they join a currency union.

It is just as important not to confuse European Union membership with participation in every European border arrangement. Passport-free travel under Schengen, the free movement of workers under EU law, and adopting the euro are different legal projects with different histories. They often interact, but their membership maps do not automatically coincide. A citizen deciding where to live or work must attend to the actual rules in force at the time, not a popular picture of Europe as a territory without any barriers. For historians, this is a wider lesson about regional integration: apparently simple maps conceal different layers of authority over goods, people, money, policing, and welfare. Those layers may expand at different speeds, and their differences can become politically contentious.

## Democracy and a larger table

A Union of twenty-five governments also had to decide how it would make collective decisions. Treaties apportioned votes and powers among the Council, Parliament, Commission, and courts. New states gained representation and a voice in legislation, but many decisions still required compromise with wealthier and more populous partners. This was a form of shared sovereignty, not the abolition of national governments. Member states continued to choose schools, health services, tax policies, and many social arrangements while accepting binding obligations in other fields. Some citizens welcomed common institutions because they offered checks on arbitrary national rule. Others worried that decisions about local affairs would become too remote for voters to contest. Both concerns could be present even in countries where accession itself had broad popular support.

The uneven application of membership rules was especially apparent in difficult questions about minority rights and borders. Accession criteria promised respect for minorities, yet the protection of Roma communities, speakers of minority languages, and migrants depended on national institutions as well as European law. In Cyprus, the Green Line continued to divide communities despite the island's membership in the Union. Integration could create forums, legal tools, and incentives for reconciliation, but it did not dissolve historical claims or guarantee equal treatment. The claim that Europe had overcome its divisions was therefore partly an aspiration. New members brought their experiences of occupation, nationalism, war, and democratic change into shared institutions, where they could both challenge and reinforce inherited assumptions.

## Enlargement as an unfinished political choice

Bulgaria and Romania joined on January first, two thousand and seven, extending the enlargement process rather than closing it. Membership in the Union was increasingly linked to debates about where Europe began and ended, which states could satisfy the criteria, and what obligations existing members owed to candidates and neighbors. The previous westward and eastward lines of the Cold War no longer defined the membership map. But that did not mean all European states could or wished to join, or that their citizens agreed about what the Union should become. The legal ideal of equal membership met economic differences, national political conflicts, and new arguments about democracy and accountability. Each enlargement changed both sides of an accession negotiation: the candidate and the institution admitting it.

The two thousand and four enlargement is easiest to understand by resisting two complete stories. It was not simply the triumphant end of division, because wages, mobility rights, and territorial conflicts remained unequal. Nor was it merely a market project imposed on passive populations, because citizens, parties, governments, and civic movements actively fought over its terms. It made new routes for trade, study, family life, and political cooperation while producing new questions about who belonged and who carried the costs. The political border changed on one date; Europe's lived borders shifted over years, sometimes in contradictory ways. That is what enlargement means when seen from a workplace, a school, a parliamentary debate, and a disputed line across an island rather than only from a map colored in one shade.
