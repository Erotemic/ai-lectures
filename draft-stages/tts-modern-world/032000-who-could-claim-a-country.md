# Who Could Claim a Country? Anticolonial Organizing Between the Wars

## The promise of representation meets its limits

After the First World War, several movements began to ask not merely for better terms under foreign rule but for a new definition of who could govern. Others demanded reform within an existing empire or a voice in institutions that had previously excluded them. Those aims were not identical. A Congress seeking constitutional representation, students challenging a cultural inheritance, women contesting colonial taxation, and people deliberately breaking a salt law did not all use the same language of independence. The years between the wars are often narrated as preparation for the independence movements after 1945. But that story risks ignoring the immediate arguments, achievements, disagreements, and failures that mattered to participants then.

It helps to distinguish three kinds of political authority. A colonial administration claimed the authority to make and enforce rules. An organization might claim the authority to speak for a population, which was a different claim and might itself be disputed. And a community might assert the authority to refuse a particular tax, law, or local office. None of these necessarily implied a single future state or a common ideology. The National Congress of British West Africa pursued a regional political program that reflected the organizing capacity of an educated leadership. Gandhi and his colleagues in India used mass civil disobedience to contest colonial law. Both raised questions of representation, but they did so with different constituencies and tools.

Diplomatic recognition could coexist with constrained sovereignty. A 1936 debate in the British House of Commons recalled Britain's unilateral declaration of Egyptian independence in 1922 while defending continued British reservations concerning imperial communications, defence, foreign interests, and Sudan. This is especially revealing because the record states the imperial government's position in its own words. It does not tell us that Egyptians accepted the arrangement, or that the terms of the treaty later satisfied everyone. An independence announcement is therefore not a substitute for examining who commanded troops, controlled resources, or decided what could be negotiated. Sovereignty could be formal, limited, disputed, and lived all at once.

## Salt was not a small subject

In India, the Civil Disobedience Movement beginning in 1930 made an everyday necessity into a political instrument. The Gandhi Heritage Portal's chronology records Gandhi's March 2 letter to Viceroy Lord Irwin and the march beginning at Sabarmati on March 12. Jawaharlal Nehru's March 5 circular to provincial Congress committees is an unusually direct window into the organizers' preparations. He expected the attack on the salt monopoly to spread and urged local committees to be ready. Together, these documents establish the strategic choice and the leadership's stated intentions. They do not, by themselves, prove that participation was identical in every province or that all participants shared the same goals.

Why salt? A tax on a basic commodity can make the state visible where people cook, work, and buy necessities. The colonial monopoly connected a general political claim to a concrete legal act. Making or collecting salt in defiance of government rules did not require a participant to understand a complicated treaty; it required understanding that an everyday activity had been made subject to imperial authority. Gandhi's strategy aimed to turn a law into a public test of obedience. Yet the movement was never simply a laboratory illustration for one leader's ideas. People brought local grievances, social positions, and their own political expectations to the campaign, which makes those beyond the leadership important subjects for further archival work.

The distinction between historical documents matters here. Gandhi's well-known March 2 letter preceded the march. A second letter, dated May 4 and preserved in a transcription at Mani Bhavan, set out his intention to march to the Dharasana salt works. The later letter cannot be cited as though it had initiated the earlier march. By May, the conflict had already moved beyond its first demonstration. Gandhi explained the next step in terms of challenging government control of salt and anticipated possible arrest. We can reconstruct his argument from a direct primary text without confusing an organizer's tactical intentions with the experiences of the people who carried the movement forward.

The British Library catalog of a colonial file on the campaign lists material about the salt law, censorship, the Garhwal Rifles, and proposals concerning Gandhi's deportation. That catalog does not expose every document's contents, but its scope shows the administrative questions British authorities considered. Comparing such a record with Nehru's circular helps distinguish two projects. Congress organizers were trying to widen noncompliance and coordination. The administration considered how to preserve authority and manage dissent. Neither record is a neutral transcription of the whole movement. Reading them together produces a more reliable picture than allowing either side to define both the conflict and its meaning.

It would be misleading to say the Salt March won Indian independence in 1930. It challenged legitimacy, produced new forms of participation, and made repression more visible, but the colonial state remained. Nor should every region's struggle be reduced to the same kind of nonviolence or the same relation to Congress. To understand a mass campaign, distinguish the initial symbolic act, the organizations spreading it, the local decisions to participate or refuse, the coercive response, and the eventual negotiating position. The salt law was an entry point into a larger argument about consent to political rule. That argument continued long after the original walkers reached the sea.

## The new culture arguments in China

In China, political and intellectual movements pursued another set of answers. Historians place the May Fourth protests of 1919 within the larger New Culture movement of the preceding and following years. The Paris peace settlement's treatment of Chinese claims helped provoke the protests, but debates about political authority, language, education, and the inherited social order had already been developing. Edward Q. Wang's bibliographic survey emphasizes the range of ideas circulating in these debates: liberalism, anarchism, pragmatism, Marxism, and other competing approaches. They were not all steps toward one predetermined party victory. To describe May Fourth only as the birthplace of a later government would erase arguments participants did not yet know how history would resolve.

The primary-source teaching collection prepared by Columbia University's Asia for Educators includes a 1916 argument by Chen Duxiu and a 1934 speech by Chiang Kai-shek concerning the New Life Movement. Those documents belong to different moments and political positions. Putting them beside one another shows that reform could mean the rejection of inherited authority for one writer and the disciplined moral reorganization of society for another. Even the words modern and national do not settle what form of social life their advocates wanted. A good historian asks who wrote the program, which institutions could enforce it, and whose everyday practices were to be changed.

After the May Fourth era, students, workers, intellectuals, the Nationalist movement, and the emerging Communist movement did not remain one political coalition. Competing ideas about liberation could be aligned against foreign domination and divided over property, labor, political rights, and party power. The Chinese Communist Party was formed in 1921; Nationalist and Communist organizations also underwent changing alliances and conflicts. None of this should be described as if one timeless Chinese nationalism marched steadily from a single student demonstration to a final outcome. The diversity of political arguments is documented in Wang's survey and in the contrasting primary texts chosen for Columbia's collection.

A comparison with India's civil disobedience can clarify the differences. Both histories involved campaigns against domination and arguments about national political life. Yet the Chinese debates described here included broad disputes over written language, family authority, and intellectual method, as well as over government. The Indian salt campaign attached an explicit act of disobedience to a particular monopoly law. In neither case should a famous leader become the whole population. The question is not whether the two movements match a model called awakening. It is what social, legal, and cultural institutions the participants tried to transform, and by what means.

## West African constitutional demands and local resistance

In British West Africa, the National Congress of British West Africa brought another scale of organizing into view. Historian G. I. C. Eluwa stresses that this movement was distinctive partly because its organizers imagined a political program embracing four British West African colonies rather than treating each colony in isolation. He also notes the movement's educated-elite leadership. Both points matter. The Congress demonstrated that imperial administrative borders did not completely determine political imagination. But it would be a mistake to treat its delegates as if they could speak automatically for farmers, workers, market traders, and every local political institution across those territories.

There is a temptation to divide anticolonial history into elite politicians who wrote petitions and ordinary people who occasionally rebelled. The division is too simple. Petitioning could involve real risks, sustained networks, and arguments about constitutional rights. Local resistance could be organized through sophisticated institutions of its own. A historian can investigate the former through organizational papers and the latter through oral traditions, colonial investigations, market rules, and scholarly reconstruction. Comparing the two does not mean deciding in advance which is more authentic. They addressed different sites of power and often different immediate problems. The 1929 women's mobilizations in southeastern Nigeria make the point sharply.

The events commonly called the Aba Women's Riots of 1929 have been studied as a major women's rebellion or Women's War. Nigerian historian Abiodun S. Afolabi places the outbreak at Oloko rather than treating the town of Aba as the sole origin. His research identifies fears about taxation, but also grievances against warrant chiefs, colonial courts, falling prices for palm produce, high imported-goods prices, and changes in market transactions. An account that says simply that women rioted because of a new tax misses the institutional and commercial grievances already present. It also risks reproducing a colonial term that frames organized political confrontation chiefly as disorder.

The women involved could use networks connecting markets, kin, and communities. Their action directly challenged officials and institutions that the British system of indirect rule had elevated or reshaped. Afolabi's article brings together administrative records and other historical material to argue that the revolt cannot be understood as a spontaneous reaction to a single rumor. The wider study describes a complex relationship between colonial courts, commercial practices, and women's political agency. That is the kind of context lost when history records only the moment a crowd appears before an administrative building. Political organization may have existed long before an official archive decided that an event was worthy of reporting.

The American Historical Association reproduces an earlier colonial account and sets it beside a later framing by African historian A. Adu Boahen. That juxtaposition is a lesson in source criticism. A colonial official writing about a riot may describe crowd behavior and the threat to administration with considerable detail, yet say much less about why the institutions themselves were contested. A later historian has different questions and may emphasize rebellion, not disorder. Neither label should be adopted without examination. A primary source is not an eyewitness account of everybody's intentions. It is evidence made by a particular observer for a particular purpose.

The West African examples therefore prevent an easy story in which organized politics grows only from committees into political parties. The National Congress had a cross-colony constitutional vision and an elite organizational base. The women of the Owerri Province confrontation acted against local taxation fears, courts, commercial grievances, and imposed authority. Both illuminate governance under empire; neither is a minor version of the other. To explain their significance we need to attribute each argument to the people who made it, and each historical interpretation to the scholars who reconstructed it. A regional political history becomes more accurate when it does not pretend one representative can stand for all.

## Independence could be conditional

Egypt adds another test for the words independence and representation. In 1922 Britain declared the protectorate ended, but retained decisive reservations on several subjects. In a parliamentary debate about the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty, British ministers summarized those reserved interests: imperial communications, external defence, foreign interests and minorities, and Sudan. From their perspective negotiation could complete an arrangement protecting British power. From the perspective of Egyptian nationalists, the fact that such questions remained reserved was itself central to the demand for meaningful sovereignty. The British parliamentary document proves the British government's position; it cannot be cited as direct evidence that Egyptian political groups accepted its logic.

This example also helps distinguish constitutional form from the ability to determine a country's future. An elected legislature or a recognized national flag could coexist with foreign military presence and unequal treaty obligations. Different Egyptian organizations could disagree about the trade-offs of negotiation, confrontation, or party politics. The same question appears in other colonial settings but with different institutional details: which decisions were reserved, who could veto, what protections were enforceable, and who counted as a representative? A history of independence movements that stops at the date of formal recognition misses the substance of those questions. It also risks treating sovereignty as an object bestowed by an empire rather than contested by people subject to it.

## How should we recognize an anticolonial movement?

These cases share no single strategy. A famous march disputed a law by breaking it publicly. Chinese student and intellectual movements debated the foundations of social authority and organized around anti-imperial protest. West African congress delegates asserted a political claim across colonial borders. Women in southeastern Nigeria attacked local arrangements of authority embedded in colonial administration. Egyptian leaders negotiated and contested the terms of sovereignty. Each example has a different unit of action: a march, a text, a market network, a congress, a treaty. Comparing them requires more precision than grouping all of them under the word nationalism.

The sources also differ. Nehru's correspondence reveals the plans of Congress organizers. A colonial administrator's account of a women's rebellion reveals both reported events and an official's categories. A parliamentary speech reveals how a government defended its treaty. A scholar's later work supplies an interpretation of patterns invisible or unacknowledged in some contemporary documents. We must never silently turn one into another. Readers and listeners should be able to find the author, date, and relevant passage for an important claim. That discipline is not a burden added after the history is written. It is what allows multiple political voices to remain visible without pretending they all said the same thing.

The deeper question is what it means to say that a people governed itself. Who could decide law, land, schooling, labor, taxation, and foreign relations? Who claimed to represent the people, and could that claim be challenged by the people concerned? Did a constitutional victory change the market, village, or household where power was actually encountered? Many interwar campaigns left these questions unresolved. Their significance does not depend on our knowing how decolonization later unfolded. It lies in their deliberate efforts to make previously settled forms of authority contestable. Economic crisis in the next decade would intensify some of those disputes, without resolving them for everyone.

## Source method and unresolved issues (display only)

- Historical testimony and dialogue have not been invented. Illustrative comparisons in the spoken prose are identified as analytical and are not assigned to real people.

- Government documents show institutional policy and language; they do not automatically establish that affected communities consented or experienced the events as the government claimed.

- Published research summaries, abstracts, and archive finding aids are labeled by review scope. They are not treated as though the full source were checked.

- Historical causation and policy motivation are interpreted cautiously. Differences between legal enactment, implementation, outcomes, and subsequent memory remain explicit.

- The manuscripts are written to fit the subject; there is no lecture-duration or word-count requirement.
