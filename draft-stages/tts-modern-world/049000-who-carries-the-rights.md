# Who Carries the Rights? Civil Rights and Anti-Apartheid Organizing

## Rights written down, rights withheld

In the decades after 1945, governments throughout the world claimed to stand for freedom while many people within their borders were denied basic civil and political rights. In the United States, Black citizens faced legally enforced segregation, violence and systematic barriers to voting. In South Africa, a white-minority government consolidated apartheid, a system that used statutes and policing to classify people and control where they could live, travel, work and organize. The political systems were not identical: the United States was a constitutional democracy with entrenched racial exclusions, whereas South Africa's political structure explicitly reserved national power for a racial minority. But organizers on both sides of the Atlantic confronted the gap between the language of rights and the institutions enforcing inequality. Their campaigns made that gap a public political crisis.

This is often told through famous speeches or the decisions of presidents and prime ministers. Yet the architecture of a mass movement is largely built below that level. Someone writes a letter to a city official, gathers signatures, prints notices overnight, arranges rides, keeps accounts, provides legal support or opens a house to a meeting. These actions matter because a legal rule survives through routine compliance, and challenging it requires sustained coordination. Without the ordinary work, even a compelling public symbol may change little. The evidence for that work appears in organizational minutes, campaign documents, participants' letters and later recollections. We should distinguish a movement's stated goals from what its tactics achieved, and avoid confusing one widely remembered leader with everyone who made the movement possible.

## Montgomery: organizing before the famous arrest

In Montgomery, Alabama, Black passengers were subjected to racist treatment on city buses long before December 1955. The Women's Political Council, formed in 1949 under Mary Fair Burks, campaigned against discriminatory service. When Jo Ann Robinson became president of the council, she made the bus system a priority. In May 1954 she warned the mayor that Black residents might organize a boycott unless their complaints were addressed. The demands included fair treatment when paying fares and boarding, not only the arrangement of seats. This was not a spontaneous grievance discovered when one passenger refused an order. The political work had been developing for years through Black women's networks and local institutions.

On December first, 1955, police arrested Rosa Parks after she refused to surrender her seat to a white passenger. Parks was already a seasoned activist, and the community response was neither an accident nor a singular act of defiance by someone outside politics. Robinson and other organizers rapidly prepared and distributed boycott leaflets. Churches, civic groups and local leaders coordinated a mass refusal to use the buses on December fifth. The Montgomery Improvement Association was formed to sustain the campaign, and Martin Luther King Junior became its president. His public role mattered. So did the work of people whose names were not on every newspaper front page: women who planned meetings, people who drove car pools and workers who walked long distances for months.

The boycott continued for more than a year. The city and state attempted to punish organizers through legal action, while demonstrators had to keep getting to work, school and family obligations. The eventual ruling against segregation in the city's buses rested on constitutional litigation as well as collective pressure. A court judgment did not retroactively make the hardship disappear, and a boycott was not the same as a judicial opinion. The two strategies interacted. The campaign gave a local problem national visibility and created institutions capable of sustaining action over time. The legal result changed a particular rule; the organizers' experience changed their expectations about what they could do together.

## From schools to voting rights

A year before the bus boycott, the United States Supreme Court had decided Brown versus Board of Education. The unanimous 1954 ruling held that racial segregation in public schools violated the equal-protection guarantee of the Fourteenth Amendment. Its legal reasoning addressed the inequality produced by state-enforced separation, not simply differences in books or buildings. But the 1955 follow-up decision called for implementation with a phrase that allowed delay, and desegregation met organized resistance. A court can state a constitutional principle; it cannot itself provide every bus ride to a newly integrated school, protect every student from threats or resolve every district's funding decisions. The years afterward demonstrated the distance between the legal holding and local compliance.

Young people helped transform that distance into a field of political action. In February 1960, students began sit-ins at a segregated lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina. Similar actions spread. Ella Baker convened student activists at Shaw University that April, where they formed what became the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, or SNCC. Baker argued that the struggle reached far beyond access to one meal. She emphasized the ability of people affected by injustice to act collectively and guide their own movement. The students' founding statement committed them to nonviolent direct action; it also recognized that local groups would have to determine how those principles applied in practice. The movement was a network of deliberating organizers, not a disciplined audience following orders from a distant center.

Those distinctions became urgent in Mississippi, where many Black citizens were blocked from registering to vote through tests, intimidation, employer retaliation and violence. Fannie Lou Hamer, a former sharecropper and a central organizer of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, testified before the Democratic National Convention's credentials committee in August 1964. She described her attempt to register and the punishments imposed afterward. Her testimony was not an abstract claim that voting was important; it was a participant's account of the costs of seeking a right the Constitution purported to protect. It also exposed the conflict between national party leadership, which worried about electoral coalitions, and grassroots activists demanding political representation. We can learn a great deal from the testimony without pretending that Hamer's experience was the only Black experience in Mississippi.

Federal legislation changed the legal landscape. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 restricted discrimination in public accommodations and employment, among other provisions. The Voting Rights Act of August 1965 attacked specific devices used to suppress Black voting, including literacy tests. Neither law can be understood as a favor delivered to a passive population. They emerged amid organizing, litigation, demonstrations, violence and political conflict. Nor did enactment instantly eliminate discrimination. Legal authority created new grounds for action; it also provoked arguments over enforcement, jurisdiction and what material equality would require beyond a formal right to enter a restaurant or cast a ballot. The history of rights includes winning laws and making those laws real.

## South Africa: segregation as an instrument of government

South Africa's National Party came to power in 1948 and expanded a legal structure of apartheid. Racial classification, residential restrictions and passes tied the movement of African people to their jobs and places of residence. Pass laws were more than an inconvenience requiring identification. They could determine whether a person could lawfully remain near a workplace or family, and expose people to arrest for violating administrative rules. Urban planning and policing reinforced a political system in which most people had no equal share in choosing the national government. To understand the resistance, we should begin with the daily power of these laws. A demand for freedom of movement was also a demand to keep households together and to live without arbitrary police interruption.

In 1952 the African National Congress and the South African Indian Congress helped organize the Defiance Campaign, which challenged discriminatory laws through deliberate noncompliance. Participants put their bodies at risk of arrest, and the campaign attracted people across racial and political lines. In 1955, the Congress of the People adopted the Freedom Charter at Kliptown. The charter demanded universal political participation, equal rights, and substantial changes in land, work and public resources. This was not simply a request for slightly kinder administration of racial separation. It claimed that political authority required the participation of everyone living in South Africa. The document's authors and supporters were making a political program; the state did not accept their authority to set one.

The Freedom Charter also became a source of disagreement among opponents of apartheid. Some organizers embraced a broad multiracial alliance and a program connecting voting rights to economic redistribution. Others argued that African leadership and a specifically Africanist understanding of liberation should take priority. These distinctions became sharper when the Pan Africanist Congress, or PAC, separated from the ANC in 1959 under leaders including Robert Sobukwe. It is too crude to say that all opponents of apartheid shared one ideology until police repression forced them apart. Their differences concerned strategy, political membership and visions of the state that might replace minority rule. A movement can oppose the same injustice and still disagree about the society it hopes to create.

South African women were not spectators to these disputes. The Federation of South African Women, established in 1954, adopted a Women's Charter demanding voting rights, equal opportunity, and equality in pay, marriage and property. Organizers opposed the extension of passes to African women because the documents threatened work, family and freedom of movement. On August ninth, 1956, thousands of women traveled to the Union Buildings in Pretoria to deliver petitions. Accounts describe a crowd of roughly ten to twenty thousand; estimates differ, and the precise count is less important than the scope of organization. Leaders included Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophia Williams-De Bruyn, among many others. Their achievement was a national campaign built across numerous local meetings and journeys.

The march did not make the pass system disappear. That distinction is essential. Protest can show what thousands of people reject without having the immediate power to repeal the law they oppose. Yet the organization, the petitions and the way women carried the issue into public view mattered. The historian Elizabeth Schmidt traced African women's resistance to the pass laws in the nineteen fifties and showed that the 1956 march belonged to a longer sequence of campaigns and arrests, not a single isolated day. The Federation's program also reached beyond passes to work and family law. Reducing these women to supporters of famous male activists would miss their own political agenda.

## Sharpeville and the choices after repression

On March twenty-first, 1960, the PAC called for a campaign against passes. Protesters assembled at Sharpeville near Johannesburg. Police opened fire, killing sixty-nine people and injuring many more. The event became a turning point in domestic and international opposition to apartheid. The National Party government declared a state of emergency and banned the ANC and PAC. The government presented opposition as a threat to public order; the violence exposed the state's readiness to kill people challenging its racial restrictions. In the years afterward, opponents debated underground organization, exile, armed resistance and the possibilities of continued nonviolent action. The change in tactics should be placed against the escalating constraints under which organizers acted, not described as a sudden disappearance of earlier commitments.

The killings were reported and protested abroad, including at the United Nations. International opinion did not itself determine South African policy, but it altered the diplomatic environment in which the government defended apartheid. Anticolonial leaders and African governments connected the struggle in South Africa to broader arguments about racial equality and political sovereignty. Yet even international solidarity had limits. Governments differed over sanctions, trade and the use of force, and public condemnation was often stronger than practical pressure. The people directly confronting police and pass offices carried risks that distant sympathizers did not. An international resolution and a local organizer's arrest record are different kinds of historical evidence, each necessary for a complete account.

## Similar demands, different political fields

The Montgomery boycott and the anti-pass campaigns reveal how routine systems of racial hierarchy become vulnerable when people refuse to treat them as natural. In Montgomery, a city bus had rules about seats and payment that asserted unequal citizenship. In South Africa, a passbook helped a state control who could remain in a city or keep a job. Neither object looks like a grand theory of racial government. Both could decide the course of a working day. Organizers turned those routine points of control into public political questions. They did not merely demand respectful language. They contested who could impose a rule and whose experience should count in deciding whether it was just.

But it would be wrong to assume that American civil rights organizations and South African liberation organizations operated under the same constitutional possibilities. Black American activists contested exclusions within a political system that offered judicial and electoral avenues, however obstructed and dangerous they were. Under apartheid, the white-minority state's architecture denied the majority ordinary national electoral power and outlawed major liberation organizations. Activists in both countries used petitions, public protest, legal claims and international appeals, but they faced different institutions and made different strategic choices. A useful comparison keeps the shared mechanism of organized resistance while preserving the particular coercive structures being challenged.

Nor should we imagine one unified civil rights movement within the United States. The organizers who pressed for immediate access to public accommodations were sometimes pursuing different priorities from those emphasizing political power, community safety, employment, education or rural land and income. Those demands often overlapped. SNCC's insistence on student autonomy and the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party's challenge to the national party show that the movement also contested representation within organizations claiming to speak for reform. The question of who gets to make decisions did not disappear when activists agreed that racism was wrong. Sometimes the same commitment to equality required disagreements about tactics and power inside the struggle itself.

A final caution concerns famous victories. When an apartheid law or an American segregation rule was repealed, historians gained a clear date. Daily life seldom changed on a single date. Workers might still face discriminatory hiring, families might remain excluded from secure housing, and police might continue practices that people had protested. A rights movement therefore has several possible measures of success: a law's repeal, the ability to organize, actual access to a school or workplace, the security of a family, or the political power to prevent a violation from returning. No single measure tells the whole story. It is an error to replace the narrative of denied rights with a narrative of legal triumph and stop there.

## How movements alter what is possible

Jo Ann Robinson's letters, Ella Baker's article, Fannie Lou Hamer's testimony, the Freedom Charter, and the records of South African women's campaigns show a progression. People identify a routine injustice, argue about the rule that sustains it, form organizations able to survive pressure, and demand the authority to help write different rules. We do not need to make these individuals agree across time and place. Their differences are part of what makes the comparison useful. One helped prepare a bus boycott; another defended independent student organizing; another demanded representation in a political party; others attacked a system of movement controls and minority rule. What joined them was the insistence that affected people were not merely petitioners waiting for permission to be counted. They were makers of political history.

## Source method and unresolved questions (display only)

Each source is attributed to its author or institution. Diplomatic, archival, and legal texts document what officials recorded or enacted, not universal consent or direct proof of implementation. Publisher abstracts and catalog descriptions are identified as such and are not misrepresented as books or archival files read in full. An institutional description of an oral-history collection is not used to invent, quote, or merge individual testimonies. Historical debates are distinguished from settled document chronology. All source locators and known limits are retained in the ledger and metadata. No arbitrary lecture length requirement is used.
