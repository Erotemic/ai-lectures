# Whose Restoration? Meiji Japan from Village Taxes to Imperial Power

## A restoration with several audiences

An announcement changes who holds power in a capital. It does not immediately change the soil, debts, duties, or loyalties of every farm. That distinction matters for Japan after the fall of Tokugawa rule. The Meiji Restoration of 1868 has often been narrated as a national decision to modernize. Yet it was a contested political transformation built upon earlier institutions, international pressure, regional conflicts, and negotiations over land and taxation. When the new government claimed to restore imperial authority, people throughout the country still had to discover what that meant in their own lives. A scholar, a landowner, an indebted tenant, a samurai retainer, and a young silk worker did not encounter the same restoration.

Japan had not been sealed off from knowledge or commerce before 1853. Tokugawa institutions regulated foreign relations and exchanges, but merchants, domain officials and scholars already drew on networks of learning and production. The American naval expedition led by Commodore Perry in 1853 intensified arguments about treaties, defense, and domestic political authority. The National Diet Library’s history of the constitutional state traces the language of public debate back to this late-Tokugawa crisis. That is important because it means deliberation and reform were not simply gifts bestowed by a new imperial bureaucracy in 1868.

In 1867 political authority was formally returned toward the imperial court, and the new regime consolidated itself in the following year. The Charter Oath of 1868 promised deliberative assemblies and attention to public opinion. Those words were politically consequential, but should not be mistaken for an immediate establishment of universal electoral government. Who would deliberate? Which interests could be heard? Which people remained subjects of officials who spoke on their behalf? The National Diet Library displays the oath as part of the evolution toward constitutional rule, while its later collections show how long that institutional argument continued.

## Domains, taxes, and the power to build a state

One reform reorganized the political map within Japan. In 1871 the government abolished the remaining domain administrations and moved toward prefectural control. That did not merely replace one title with another. Domains had been fiscal and political authorities, able to demand resources and sustain retainer households. Centralization shifted control toward the new state, which needed a more predictable stream of revenue. An army, schools, diplomatic missions, and infrastructure cannot be built from slogans. They must be financed, and the question of who pays is itself a story of political power.

The land-tax reform beginning in 1873 made formal claims to land and monetary assessment central to the new fiscal order. The article “Reforming States, Agricultural Transformation, and Economic Development in Russia and Japan, 1853–1913” explains that this changed the balance between proprietors and tenants, including older village arrangements protecting some tenant claims. This is a specific scholarly argument, not a claim that land rights were uniform in every district. Land that was once managed through several overlapping customary obligations was increasingly recorded through documents naming owners and their obligations to the state. A receipt or a title could offer security for one person and exposure to eviction for another.

The tax was assessed in money, which made agricultural households more exposed to market prices and to the need to obtain cash. How severe the burden was varied with crop yields, tenure, and economic conditions. Historians point in particular to hardship during the deflation of the early 1880s. Notice the double effect of a stable fiscal system. A predictable tax might make national borrowing and infrastructure easier for the state. For a family facing a poor harvest or a falling crop price, it could raise the risk that a farm or tenancy would no longer be sustainable. We cannot infer either prosperity or ruin from the existence of a modern tax ledger alone.

This is also why the Meiji state should not be understood as an abstract engine separate from village society. Rural households supplied taxes, recruits, food, domestic manufactures and laborers who could move toward growing industries. Some families benefited from new opportunities to sell, rent, and invest; others faced debt or loss of tenure. The outcome often depended on distinctions within the countryside, not a simple opposition between a modern city and a backward village. The government sought new power by creating institutions through which it could reach households. Those institutions, in turn, altered what households could demand from landlords and the government.

## Silk, skill, and whose work counted

Consider a silk-reeling operation. A cocoon has been raised by many acts of feeding, cleaning, timing, and handling before its thread can enter a machine. The product travels through merchants and export channels, but household and factory labor are not easily separated. UNESCO describes the Tomioka Silk Mill and its related sites as an interconnected system: a large reeling plant, a farm for experimental production, a training institution, and facilities for storing silkworm eggs. This is more informative than calling Tomioka simply a symbol of Western technology. The technology had to be adapted to a biological material, a workforce, and Japanese institutions.

Women’s labor made this industrial history possible. Research on Meiji textile work describes young women leaving rural households for silk-reeling and cotton-spinning employment. Aditi Dixit and Elise van Nederveen Meerkerk compare why the gender balance of textile labor differed between Japan and India; their evidence reaches into the early twentieth century, so it should not be projected unchanged onto every factory in the 1870s. Tim Liao’s study of Nagano examines the regional ecology and household pressures that made recruitment of female workers feasible. The point is not that women happened to be present at the margins of an industrial revolution; their labor was central to parts of it.

Historians also question how freedom should be measured for those workers. A wage could provide a contribution to family income and some individual possibilities; contracts, dormitory rules, health hazards, and unequal bargaining power could constrain those possibilities. Elyssa Faison’s research, whose chapter abstract is publicly visible, studies the relationship between industrial labor management and the ideology of the “good wife, wise mother.” That abstract establishes the topic and argument, but not the details of any individual worker’s testimony. We should not invent such testimony. If we want a real voice, we need to go to a documented interview or surviving letter and report its provenance.

The gendered division of labor also reveals something about “technology transfer.” Machines did not walk into factories and operate themselves. Japanese producers and workers learned, repaired, adapted, and standardized processes. Female operators’ skill and endurance rarely appear in a story told only through ministry decisions and foreign experts. Even when imported machinery mattered, Japanese institutions of training and commercial organization mattered too. A comparison with India should not become a ranking of societies: differences in rural tenure, household labor, and business organization help explain different patterns of industrial employment, not different capacities for intelligence or creativity.

## Constitutional demands are not the same as constitutional equality

The new government’s leaders disagreed with other political actors over who could shape state decisions. The Freedom and People’s Rights movement demanded representation, while officials drafted constitutions through a carefully managed process. An imperial rescript in April 1875 announced steps toward constitutional institutions. The National Diet Library preserves the document context and identifies the Chamber of Elders, Supreme Court and prefectural governor assembly envisioned at the time. Read that source as evidence that public representation was the subject of struggle. A government promise does not make all the people equal participants in writing the rules.

The 1889 constitution established an imperial constitutional order; the first election for the lower house occurred in July 1890, and the first Imperial Diet opened in November of that year. The National Diet Library provides both the constitutional text and a chronology of parliamentary steps. The text locates sovereign authority in the emperor and outlines the institutions through which state decisions were made. This was not the later democratic constitution of postwar Japan. Nor should the existence of a legislature be used as a substitute for asking who could vote, under what tax qualifications, and who controlled ministers, the military, and budgets.

The People’s Rights movement itself was not a single program with a single constituency. Some advocates wanted elected institutions primarily to restrain bureaucratic arbitrariness; others pressed for wider participation or relief from taxes. The National Diet Library documents the late-century Constitutional Liberal Party’s agenda, including reduction of land taxes and changes to political and press regulations. These demands link a rural household’s tax bill to arguments about national representation. The ability to vote and the burden of paying could be connected even when a particular farm family had no voice in the Diet.

## Japan as an imperial state

There is a moral danger in ending this story at the factory and the constitution, as though institutional modernization automatically produced peaceful equality. Meiji leaders also pursued military and diplomatic expansion. Japan defeated Qing China in the war of 1894–95 and obtained major concessions in the settlement. Its later imperial control over Korea culminated in annexation in 1910. The National Diet Library’s foreign-relations exhibit records these developments, but its materials necessarily emphasize Japanese state documentation. To understand Korean experiences of domination we would also need Korean sources. A responsible lecture can state the imperial expansion clearly without pretending this one archive contains every perspective.

Those overseas ambitions were connected to domestic state-building. A fiscal apparatus that could collect money, schools that spread national education, and a military that recruited and trained personnel could also project power beyond the islands. But neither empire nor reform followed inevitably from a single decree, and individual people contested each. Japan’s leaders worked in a world where other states had used coercion to demand access to markets. That does not absolve Japanese imperial policy of its consequences. It helps us see how the language of national protection can be attached to projects of domination over other peoples.

A good comparison with Qing China now becomes possible. Both societies faced coercive foreign demands; both had political actors who advocated changes to law, schooling and military organization. But their social and administrative structures, geopolitical positions, fiscal capacities and internal conflicts were not identical. It is not sufficient to say that one embraced modernity and the other rejected it. Comparing two governments requires examining who paid taxes, who commanded soldiers, who translated knowledge, and who could force reforms through opposition. Comparison should increase precision, not turn a diverse history into a competition with one winner.

We began with several possible witnesses to a restoration. Their accounts remain different. A landlord may notice more transferable property rights. A tenant may notice a less secure bargain. A factory worker may notice her pay, dormitory rules, or homesickness. A political organizer may notice a legislature that is real but limited. A Korean subject may notice the extension of another state’s power. None of these positions alone captures Meiji Japan. Together, with the documentary and scholarly sources named here, they let us see state-building as a redistribution of capabilities and constraints, rather than a single spectacular leap from old to new.
