# The Sea Has More Than One Clock: The Indian Ocean Tsunami and Reconstruction, 2004–2013

## A morning on several coasts

On Sunday, December twenty-sixth, two thousand and four, the earth ruptured beneath the sea west of northern Sumatra. The earthquake, approximately magnitude nine point one, was among the strongest recorded by modern instruments. The seafloor moved abruptly along a long zone where one tectonic plate descends beneath another. That movement displaced an enormous volume of water. The resulting tsunami was not one towering breaker racing intact across the sea. It was a train of long waves, which could be hard to notice in deep water yet rise and surge inland as powerful currents when the seabed became shallow. The closest Indonesian coasts were struck very quickly. Farther shores had more time before the waves arrived, but time is useful only when warning information reaches people who know what to do.

From Aceh to Sri Lanka, southern India, Thailand, the Maldives, and the coast of East Africa, different communities experienced the same geophysical event at different hours. The sea crossed national boundaries without asking whose holiday it was. In some places people felt a prolonged earthquake; in others the ground never shook. On some beaches the water withdrew dramatically, exposing the seabed. Elsewhere the first sign was a sudden surge. Fishermen, market vendors, hotel staff, parents, and children met danger through the details of ordinary life: where the road climbed, who could run, which doors opened, who was at sea. A historical account that begins and ends with the wave height misses the distribution of risk. Geography mattered; so did housing, mobility, local knowledge, and access to a route out.

Later tallies placed those killed or missing at roughly two hundred and twenty-eight thousand across fourteen countries, although counts and categories vary between agencies and over time. Indonesia suffered the largest loss, followed by Sri Lanka, India, and Thailand. This figure is not a precise census of every death. Entire families and records disappeared. In some communities a missing person could never be formally identified. A count of the dead is necessary for understanding scale, but a disaster also destroys fishing boats, wells, tools, schools, clinics, religious buildings, and the evidence by which households establish ownership. A surviving resident might lose both a spouse and the document needed to claim a plot of land. Recovery begins with lives, but it quickly enters the ordinary institutions that make living possible.

## What a warning must accomplish

Earth scientists had long understood that undersea earthquakes could generate tsunamis. The Pacific basin had developed an international warning network after earlier disasters. The Indian Ocean did not have an equivalent region-wide mechanism operating in December two thousand and four. Instruments detected the earthquake; the missing link was not simply a sensor capable of feeling the earth move. Officials needed a reliable way to assess a possible tsunami, contact regional authorities, reach villages and beaches, and communicate a practical instruction. Some exposed coasts were too close to the source for a distant scientific bulletin to arrive in time. There, knowledge of natural warnings and a practiced path to higher ground mattered most. On more distant coasts, faster international communication could have made a substantial difference. These are related problems, but not interchangeable ones.

Imagine a message that says a large earthquake has happened offshore. It is an observation, not yet a useful warning to a family on a low-lying road. The family needs to know that severe flooding may follow, where the danger zone is, and where safe ground lies. Authorities need to know who has permission to issue an evacuation order; telecommunications networks must work; visitors and residents must understand the language and the signal. A warning chain can fail between any two of these steps. The chain also has an ethical dimension. An official may hesitate over the cost of an unnecessary evacuation. A resident may fear leaving work, a market stall, or a dependent relative. The point of preparedness is to make the safer choice practicable before uncertainty becomes an emergency.

People who recognized an abnormally retreating sea or the shaking of a major coastal earthquake sometimes moved uphill before receiving an official instruction. But it would be wrong to turn these accounts into a claim that everyone with a particular cultural identity knew what was coming. Experience and warnings differed between households and islands. Nor could rapid local evacuation alone protect every community. The first waves arrived within minutes on parts of northern Sumatra, where infrastructure and geography sharply limited escape. The lesson was not that traditional knowledge would replace instrumentation, or that instruments would replace communities. Physical observations, remembered hazards, public education, accessible shelters, and functioning institutions had to reinforce one another.

## Emergency help and the people already there

In the first hours and days, neighbors, surviving local officials, religious organizations, medical workers, and domestic volunteers provided much of the immediate assistance. International aircraft and aid agencies later arrived on a scale that drew enormous global attention. Delivering food, water, medical supplies, shelter material, and transport mattered. So did the less visible work of locating relatives, recording the dead, clearing routes, repairing wells, and restoring medicines for conditions unrelated to the tsunami. The Tsunami Evaluation Coalition, formed by aid organizations to examine the response, concluded that international actors often failed to recognize and support local capacities adequately. The people present before a foreign relief team landed had knowledge that outsiders could not buy on arrival. Some also needed help themselves, because they too had lost homes and family members.

A relief operation has to decide what counts as a need. A helicopter can photograph a destroyed fishing harbor. It cannot so easily identify the woman who cleaned fish, repaired nets, traded at a stall, or lent small sums to boat crews. A replacement boat may restore one visible occupation while leaving that surrounding economy without income. Needs assessment becomes political whenever the aid category is narrower than the local chain of work. International agencies faced genuine logistical uncertainty; not every error was indifference. Yet a standardized package, ordered quickly for a huge operation, could overlook distinctions that a neighborhood committee already knew. The evaluation literature describes both major achievements in emergency relief and failures of consultation and coordination. These conclusions should be held together, not forced into a verdict that aid was either wholly successful or wholly harmful.

On India's Tamil Nadu coast, Human Rights Watch interviewed families whose earnings depended on the fishing industry without owning boats. In one account, women described the collapse of work for boat repairers after the wave destroyed the fleet. The report also documented problems for people excluded from official relief categories. That observation changes how reconstruction is imagined. A port is a workplace with many occupations, not just a collection of vessels. Discrimination can be embedded in documents, social hierarchy, and the rules for eligibility even when no official announces an intention to discriminate. Identifying the person who receives assistance, the property considered lost, and the evidence required to prove that loss is therefore part of the history of the disaster, not an administrative footnote.

## The land under the rubble

In Aceh, where coastal towns were swept apart, rebuilding houses required something that photographs could not supply: agreement about whose land a house belonged on. Many deeds and boundary markers had vanished, and surviving residents might lack formal title even when neighbors had long known the boundaries. A World Bank reconstruction review discussed hundreds of thousands of affected land parcels and the limited proportion supported by certificates. Officials could not simply print a new map and assume its lines would be accepted. Communities needed ways to establish claims, address inheritance after the loss of relatives, prevent opportunistic seizure, and protect people who had used land without owning it in a conventional legal sense. Fast construction could become a source of new conflict if it preceded legitimate settlement of these questions.

Reconstruction agencies also had to decide where people should live. Moving households farther from a dangerous shore could reduce one hazard while severing access to fishing grounds, markets, relatives, and places of worship. Keeping people near their former neighborhoods could preserve social relationships but require stronger escape routes and risk planning. An empty rectangle on an engineer's map was not necessarily an available home site. The preferred solution depended on livelihood, title, transport, and residents' own decisions. The larger the promised reconstruction budget, the greater the temptation to measure success by buildings delivered. A completed house is valuable, but its usefulness cannot be assessed without asking who lives in it, how people work, and whether the settlement can maintain water, schools, and roads after foreign agencies leave.

The tsunami entered Aceh during a long armed conflict between the Indonesian state and the Free Aceh Movement. In August two thousand and five, the parties signed a peace agreement in Helsinki. Disaster relief and international attention helped create an opening, but researchers warn against reducing a complex peace process to the arrival of the waves. The sides had their own histories, bargaining positions, and political goals. Research based on interviews in post-conflict Aceh also found a divide between assistance aimed at tsunami survivors and assistance reaching people harmed by war. A household could be devastated by both. Peace and reconstruction did not always use the same eligibility rules. The presence of a successful peace agreement therefore did not mean every family could return easily to a stable livelihood.

## Several countries, several recoveries

In Sri Lanka, the waves hit communities already divided by decades of civil war. A June two thousand and five agreement between the government and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam proposed a shared structure for managing tsunami reconstruction in six northern and eastern districts. Its text explicitly recognized the needs of Sinhala, Tamil, Muslim, and other communities. The proposal was controversial and did not resolve the war. Here aid distribution was entangled with the question of who could govern territory and speak for residents. A clinic, road, or housing program could appear to strengthen one authority over another. Relief workers needed access to people; communities needed supplies; political actors disputed the authority through which those supplies should pass. Treating the coast as a politically blank space would have misunderstood the emergency.

Southern India showed a different set of problems. The waves devastated fishing villages and damaged islands in the Bay of Bengal, while the wider Indian state had substantial domestic institutions able to organize relief. The impact still fell unevenly on people whose work was informal, whose homes lacked secure tenure, or whose social position limited access to officials. Different districts used different rules for compensation, replacement housing, and access to the shore. The wider lesson is that a national capacity to send supplies does not guarantee equal delivery to every eligible person. Survivors could be active petitioners and organizers, not passive recipients. Their complaints offer evidence about the reconstruction system that cannot be inferred from the total volume of relief funds disbursed.

Thailand had to respond to losses among fishing families, local businesses, migrant workers, and foreign visitors. In the Maldives, entire islands faced damaged freshwater supplies, harbors, and buildings; there was often no nearby high ground within the island itself. Across East Africa, the waves arrived after traveling a much greater distance, catching people who had felt no earthquake. The international geography of the disaster could be plotted by wave travel time, but reconstruction did not follow a single timetable. Insurance, the tourism economy, fisheries, and public budgets differed. Even within one country, a family dependent on an employer, a household with an intact boat, and a tenant who had lost everything might recover at different speeds. Comparisons should ask what each indicator actually measures.

## Turning catastrophe into preparation

After the disaster, governments asked UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission to help establish an Indian Ocean tsunami warning and mitigation system. Coordination began in two thousand and five. Regional capabilities took years to mature; the system's regional tsunami service providers took on an operational advisory role in March two thousand and thirteen. This was not the same as the instant construction of a fully functioning system in two thousand and five. India, Indonesia, Australia, and other members built and adapted monitoring centers, gauges, buoys, communication arrangements, and response procedures on different schedules. Early warning is an institution assembled over time, not a single machine installed after a disaster.

India, for example, began interim tsunami information services in two thousand and five and developed a round-the-clock national warning center that became fully operational in October two thousand and seven. Regional advisory systems and national warning services perform related but different tasks. An advisory can estimate whether a coast faces danger. It still must reach an authorized local decision maker, who must have a way to tell people to move. Communities need exercises, routes, and shelters, including plans for people with limited mobility. Maintaining those capacities requires budgets when there is no emergency on the news. The historical test of institutional learning is not the number of instruments purchased in the first year, but whether knowledge and action remain connected years afterward.

International philanthropy after the tsunami also raised an uncomfortable question: why could a spectacular disaster mobilize resources so rapidly while quieter disasters and longstanding poverty attracted less? This is not a reason to regret generous aid. It is a reason to investigate how public attention, television images, diplomatic relationships, and donor procedures distribute support. Reconstruction could sometimes work with large grants, new engineering skills, and new opportunities for public health. But donors and governments needed to ask how much responsibility shifted to local institutions once outside personnel departed. The best comparison is not between an ideal aid operation and a flawed one. It is between different mechanisms for getting tools and decision-making power to people who must use them.

## What survived the water

A social history of the tsunami has many kinds of evidence. Geologists record rupture and seafloor displacement. Hydrologists and engineers reconstruct runup and inundation. Rescue agencies list missing persons and shipments. Officials preserve maps of land claims. People record which road was passable, which neighbor sheltered a family, which committee answered a petition, and which promised house was never finished. Those kinds of evidence answer different questions. Scientific reconstruction can explain where the water went, but not whether a tenant qualified for replacement housing. A reconstruction report can show that a school was built, but not whether a child could get there. Explaining catastrophe requires both mechanisms and social experience.

The Indian Ocean tsunami was not a punishment from nature, and the loss of life was not explained by a single failure. Earthquakes are physical events. Exposure and response are shaped by decisions about settlement, roads, warnings, employment, law, and the organization of public help. The waves traveled at different speeds across a common ocean; recovery followed different clocks in every affected community. The lasting achievement of the response was not simply a warning network, although that mattered. It was an expanded understanding that the last step of any warning or reconstruction plan belongs to a person who must be able to act on it. When the sea next gives a warning, the question will be not merely who heard the signal, but who could reach safety.
