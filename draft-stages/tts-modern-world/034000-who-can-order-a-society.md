# Who Can Order a Society? Dictatorship, Planning, and Democratic Reform

## Crisis did not write its own constitution

Imagine a visitor in the early 1930s collecting political promises. One government announces that national unity requires a leader above parties. Another invokes the needs of industrial modernization to compel a radical reorganization of agriculture. A third claims that an emergency requires wider public insurance and legal protection for workers. A military leadership asserts that security requires control of neighboring territory. These are not four versions of the same policy. They answer different political questions, employ different kinds of coercion, and grant different rights. The historical problem is to understand why some people supported each project, why others resisted, and what the governments actually did once they possessed power.

The Depression intensified instability, but it did not invent fascism, and it did not force every society toward dictatorship. Mussolini had already come to power in Italy in 1922, years before the 1929 crash. German National Socialism built a different movement around a racial worldview, political violence, territorial ambitions, and a particular crisis of parliamentary government. Elsewhere, political actors sought enlarged social insurance, collective bargaining, debt relief, or redistribution of land. A comparison is useful only if it can explain the differences. We should not describe fascism as mere government involvement in the economy, or democracy as simply the absence of economic planning.

One tool for the comparison is to ask four questions in every case. Who could criticize or remove the people exercising power? Who controlled the police, prisons, and armed forces? Who possessed legal and practical protection against discrimination or confiscation? And how could workers, farmers, minorities, and political opponents organize? These questions connect institutions to people's daily experience. They also make clear why political ideology is not only a collection of slogans. A regime's program becomes historically consequential when it is translated into laws, enforcement practices, and material changes in who is protected or punished.

## Italian Fascism: destroying the restraints on power

Benito Mussolini was appointed prime minister of Italy by King Victor Emmanuel the Third in October 1922. The fact of appointment matters because the transfer of authority was not simply a direct victory in an unconstrained national election. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum traces a further transformation through the electoral and legal changes of 1924 and 1925. Mussolini's position was consolidated into dictatorship, with parliament's ability to control the government sharply weakened. The process was not merely an expansion of executive convenience. It dismantled political alternatives and enforced the fascist claim that organized opposition was illegitimate.

Italy's dictatorship developed through intimidation, party organization, and altered institutions, not through a single switch thrown at one moment. Mussolini's government demanded loyalty and presented national unity as an answer to class conflict and parliamentary bargaining. Workers' independent representation and party pluralism became targets. When studying this history, it helps to separate what the regime promised—discipline, unity, greatness—from the means it used to achieve control. The people being silenced did not lose their political rights because they had agreed that unity mattered more. They lost them through the accumulation of power and the narrowing of lawful opposition.

Italian Fascism's racial politics also changed over time. The Holocaust Museum's account notes that the regime enacted antisemitic measures in 1938, restricting Jewish Italians' jobs, marriages, positions, and public lives. That date does not imply there had been no colonial racism or coercion before it; it marks a specific expansion of persecution directed against Jews. Nor can Italy's earlier history be flattened into an exact copy of Nazi Germany. Regimes can share authoritarian and fascist characteristics while differing in chronology, institutions, and the groups they persecute. The appropriate lesson is to compare documented policies, rather than assume that a label proves every detail.

Fascist ideology presented conflict as a problem to be ended by a powerful national state, but its practices generated new conflicts and imposed new hierarchies. Opposition was treated as a threat to the nation rather than as an ordinary part of political life. The monarchy's continued institutional existence did not restore competitive parliamentary government. The question for listeners is concrete: could a person publish criticism, form an independent political organization, or seek legal protection against the ruling party without risking repression? Examining those powers tells us more about the dictatorship than treating its public ceremonies as evidence of freely shared national enthusiasm.

## Germany: an appointment followed by the destruction of politics

The German case requires especially careful chronology. Adolf Hitler was appointed chancellor on January 30, 1933, but did not at that moment possess the unlimited authority of the later Nazi state. The Holocaust Museum distinguishes appointment from subsequent dictatorship. Following the Reichstag fire, emergency measures suspended civil liberties and enabled arrests and the intimidation of political opponents. In the March 1933 election the Nazi Party did not win an absolute majority of votes on its own, and the campaign took place under extraordinary coercion. It is therefore misleading to summarize the dictatorship as a government freely and fully chosen by a unified electorate.

On March 23 the Reichstag approved the Enabling Act under conditions of intimidation, suppression, and arrests. A translation of the enacted law is available through the Holocaust Museum. Its articles allowed the government to issue laws without ordinary parliamentary approval, including measures inconsistent with the existing constitution. This primary text shows the legal form of the transfer of power. The museum's contextual account shows why the formal vote cannot be understood without the violence and exclusions surrounding it. Reading law and context together prevents us from mistaking the appearance of a legislative procedure for the survival of meaningful constitutional restraint.

The Nazi regime then moved against independent parties, trade unions, and civic organizations while expanding police power and persecution. Antisemitism was central to the Nazi worldview, not an optional decoration added after economic problems had been solved. The movement offered racial belonging to those it included while making other people targets of exclusion and violence. This is one reason comparisons with more general authoritarianism must be precise. A state that curtails opposition is already threatening freedom, but the racial and genocidal projects of Nazism require their own historical account. We should not reduce them to an economic-policy preference or to a generic desire for order.

Historians compare Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy because they shared practices of leadership cult, anti-parliamentary politics, repression, and mobilized nationalism. Yet the comparison only works if we also examine what differed: German racial doctrine, Italian political institutions, the chronology of antisemitic laws, military alliances, and expansionist goals. They were not identical governments under different names. More importantly, neither was merely a technical alternative to a democratic fiscal program. The destruction of organized opposition and protection under law was itself a defining political action, not a side effect of choosing a different plan for industry or public spending.

## Soviet industrialization and the violence of forced transformation

In the Soviet Union, Stalin's leadership pursued rapid industrialization and the forcible collectivization of agriculture. The state attempted to reorganize rural production and the movement of grain through coercive institutions. Historians of the famine in Ukraine describe food requisitions, punitive measures, and catastrophic hunger during 1932 and 1933. Stanislav Kulchytsky's study emphasizes the destructive role of collectivization and Kremlin decisions in the Holodomor. Natalya Naumenko's economic-history research tests several explanations, including weather and policies, and finds policy effects central. These are named scholarly analyses; they should not be presented as if a single accessible abstract settled every historical debate.

The word collectivization can sound like a change in accounting. For rural families it concerned who could hold land, keep animals, decide what to grow, retain produce, or leave a community. Orders about procurement and punishment could determine whether a harvest fed the people who raised it. The catastrophe cannot be understood by looking only at industrial production targets. The state claimed a right to reshape society in the name of a future collective good, while people experienced coercion in the present. Distinguishing official goals from the effects of policy is essential: the existence of an industrial plan does not explain away violence against those ordered to comply.

The Ukrainian famine is also a case where source attribution must be explicit. Naumenko's 2021 article estimates the relative contributions of weather and economic policies using a particular dataset and model. Kulchytsky's book offers a broader historical interpretation emphasizing punitive measures and state intent. Their approaches can illuminate different aspects of the event; neither should be silently converted into an exact universal death count or a shortcut through questions of intent and legal classification. The mass mortality and policy violence are central historical facts. The explanation of their mechanisms and the classification of the crime require precise evidence, scholarly context, and attention to survivors' records.

Comparing this with the western Depression requires care. Both settings involved crises of production, social security, and state authority, but their institutions were not equivalent. In one case the economy was being transformed by a party-state using coercive rural policies and centralized planning. In others, banking collapses occurred within different private-credit and parliamentary systems. That contrast does not excuse failures in any setting. It prevents a more basic error: calling everything that happened in the 1930s the result of one global crash, even when major programs had distinct origins and motives. The question is what the authorities sought to control, by which means, and at whose expense.

## Japan: financial recovery and territorial aggression

Japan's interwar history adds another complication. In September 1931 Japanese forces expanded control in Manchuria, and the creation of Manchukuo challenged China's sovereignty. The U.S. Department of State's historical account traces the Mukden Incident, the League of Nations' Lytton investigation, and the international dispute over recognition. The League's response did not reverse Japan's territorial control. This was not an economic recovery plan equivalent to a domestic banking intervention. It was military aggression and an attempt to change political authority over territory and people, involving institutions and decisions distinct from the European fascist parties.

Japan's economic and imperial stories unfolded together without being reducible to one another. Masato Shizume's economic research describes recovery after the monetary and fiscal changes associated with Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo. Diplomatic history describes the simultaneous erosion of international restraints on Japanese expansion. An improved growth rate is not evidence that a state had become more peaceful or politically accountable. Nor should we assume every Japanese citizen, soldier, official, or business leader held the same view of empire. To understand the changing balance of power, we need institutional history, local histories of occupation, and records from Chinese people subjected to aggression.

The conflict over Manchuria also matters for understanding Chinese politics. Chinese nationalist groups, local communities, and rival political organizations confronted foreign encroachment amid internal divisions. A translated 1934 speech by Chiang Kai-shek on the New Life Movement shows another kind of official response: an effort to prescribe disciplined social conduct as part of a broader state-building project. The speech is evidence of an announced program, not proof that the entire society endorsed it. Again we must avoid collapsing very different political movements into a single ideology merely because they stressed order, national renewal, or discipline. The means of governing and the treatment of opposition remain crucial.

## Reform without abolishing every political alternative

The United States offers a different comparison. Franklin Roosevelt's administration and Congress expanded the federal government's social and economic role during the 1930s. The Social Security Act of 1935 established national structures for old-age benefits and other forms of support, while the National Labor Relations Act of the same year created new federal protections for private-sector collective bargaining. Both laws are preserved and explained by the U.S. National Archives. They did not abolish elections or party competition. They sought to change the rules by which people were supported or could bargain within a constitutional system that remained contested.

The labor law addressed a genuine struggle. Employers had used blacklists, coercion, and dismissals against organizing workers, while unions and other worker groups sought effective bargaining rights. The National Archives' account notes both the statute's creation of the National Labor Relations Board and the labor disputes that preceded it. The passage of a law did not guarantee that workers everywhere were equally protected, and the new rules did not make workplace conflict vanish. They did change the recognized legal avenues for pursuing claims. The distinction between reforming institutions through contestable legislation and silencing competitors by decree is not a superficial one; it is part of what those reforms were about.

Social insurance likewise had boundaries. Agricultural and domestic workers were outside the original old-age insurance coverage, with disproportionately severe consequences for Black workers and others concentrated in those jobs. What caused those exclusions is a more specific historical question than who suffered them. Social Security historian Larry DeWitt has challenged the familiar claim that the exclusion can be explained entirely as a deliberate racial bargain, arguing from administrative records for the importance of collection and implementation concerns. Other scholars emphasize the racialized political context. We can state the unequal coverage and its effects without pretending the evidence gives a single uncontested account of legislators' motives.

Mexico supplies another path that a story confined to Europe and the United States would miss. Agrarian reform had been contested since the Mexican Revolution, and under President Lázaro Cárdenas in the 1930s the redistribution of land became a major state project. Mexican historian Alejandro Tortolero Villaseñor situates these changes within the longer history of land concentration and reform. The article's accessible abstract supports the broad chronology and interpretation; it does not authorize an exact inventory of which villages benefited or how every community understood redistribution. Here the state could intervene to alter property relations, while the historical question remained whether people gained durable local control and improved livelihoods.

The contrast is not between governments that acted and governments that did nothing. All of the systems in this lecture involved state power. The comparison is instead about authorization, accountability, coercion, and the distribution of rights. A social-insurance statute could establish enforceable claims while leaving exclusions. Agrarian reform could redistribute property while provoking disputes over land and local authority. A fascist dictatorship could suppress organization in the name of national unity. Treating these as politically interchangeable because each used legislation or bureaucracy would obscure the central questions of who could contest decisions and who was protected against state violence.

## Keep the differences visible

The 1930s cannot be explained by saying that hard economic times automatically produced hard rulers. Fascism existed before the crash, Soviet collectivization had its own revolutionary and institutional origins, and Japanese territorial aggression followed a particular history of imperial expansion and military power. The Depression changed pressures and opportunities but did not erase those distinct origins. People and organizations made choices, often under severe constraints. Some consolidated repression; others expanded social rights; still others sought independence or control over land. Historians can judge those choices more accurately when they identify the actual institutions and acts involved instead of substituting a general mood of crisis.

Nor should the existence of elections or reform legislation make us assume everyone experienced political equality. The American New Deal operated amid racial discrimination, unequal access to social protection, and conflicts between employers and organized workers. Legal expansion of rights was significant precisely because it could be contested, limited, and later revised. The challenge for a comparative history is to hold two ideas at once: constitutional reform is meaningfully different from political terror, and reforming states can still exclude or mistreat groups of people. One distinction does not erase the other. Both are needed for a faithful account of how social change was experienced.

We also need to listen for the limits of a ruler's claim to represent the nation. In Mexico, the question might concern whose land could be redistributed and who controlled communal institutions. In China, a state-building campaign claimed authority over behavior and social discipline. In Italy, the state eventually wrote antisemitic discrimination into law. These are different histories, not interchangeable examples. Each asks whether the people affected could challenge a decision and what legal or political remedies existed. A slogan about national renewal may conceal disagreement over who belongs to the nation at all.

The world approached another war through this contested political landscape, not through a single inevitable march from the stock-market crash to battlefield. We need to know what happened in Germany, Italy, the Soviet Union, Japan, China, Mexico, and the United States on their own historical terms. The next inquiry is how these different state projects, imperial ambitions, economic constraints, and popular movements interacted as international violence widened. There is no explanation adequate to the scale of that history that begins by erasing the differences among them. The task is to connect the histories without making them identical.

## Source method and unresolved issues (display only)

- Historical testimony and dialogue have not been invented. Illustrative comparisons in the spoken prose are identified as analytical and are not assigned to real people.

- Government documents show institutional policy and language; they do not automatically establish that affected communities consented or experienced the events as the government claimed.

- Published research summaries, abstracts, and archive finding aids are labeled by review scope. They are not treated as though the full source were checked.

- Historical causation and policy motivation are interpreted cautiously. Differences between legal enactment, implementation, outcomes, and subsequent memory remain explicit.

- The manuscripts are written to fit the subject; there is no lecture-duration or word-count requirement.
