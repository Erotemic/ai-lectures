# Faith in Motion: Reform, Empire, and Religious Communities after 1500

Imagine being asked to identify the religious turning point of the sixteenth century. A familiar European history course might answer: the Protestant Reformation. That response points to a movement of enormous consequence. But imagine asking the same question in Safavid Iran, in a port on the Malay Peninsula, in the Punjab, or in a newly colonized Andean community. The answer would change. The religious institutions that shaped those places were changing too, under pressures that did not begin in Germany in 1517.

We need more than a list of reforms. A religion is not a single ruler making a decree and a population obeying. Traditions live through practices, texts, teaching, pilgrimage, festivals, households, communities, and arguments about authority. Some changes happen by force. Others emerge through vernacular preaching, music, debate, everyday relationships, and the gradual establishment of new institutions. Those paths may cross without becoming identical.

The central problem for this lecture is how to describe change while preserving distinctions. It is possible to compare Catholic and Protestant reform in Europe with the Safavid reshaping of Iranian religious life, the growth of Sikh institutions, Muslim networks in Southeast Asia, and Christian transformations in the Americas. It is not defensible to call all of these one worldwide Reformation. Their chronologies, actors, and theological stakes differ. Comparison works when we name what is being compared.

## A movement before it became a label

In 1517, Martin Luther challenged the sale of indulgences and aspects of church authority in the Latin Christian world. The disputes that followed involved salvation, scripture, priestly authority, sacramental practice, and the relationship between church and political power. Luther did not launch a uniform movement whose later factions had already been planned. The reformations developed through disputation, printing, preaching, political decisions, and conflict.

A striking feature of the movement was the desire to place authoritative teaching within reach of wider audiences. Translations and printed texts could circulate beyond the people who knew Latin. Yet printing was not a guarantee that everyone could read or that literacy alone determined belief. Sermons, songs, images, family relationships, schools, and local clergy also mattered. The printed page existed within a social setting that could make the words persuasive, controversial, or dangerous.

The movement did not remain one Protestant position. Reformed traditions, Lutheran churches, radical Anabaptist communities, and other groups came to disagree on doctrine and practice. Some disputes concerned baptism, the Lord's Supper, legitimate religious authority, and the proper relationship between rulers and congregations. Rulers and city councils could sponsor one form of reform and persecute another. It is therefore inaccurate to present the Reformation as an uncomplicated victory for freedom of individual conscience. Some reformers challenged existing hierarchies while building new ones.

The Catholic Church also undertook major renewal and reform. Religious orders, pastoral initiatives, teaching programs, scholarship, and the Council of Trent, which met during several periods between 1545 and 1563, helped reshape Catholic institutions. Calling this only a reaction to Protestants misses older reform currents within Catholicism. Yet the sharpening of confessional boundaries did alter how Catholics and Protestants described one another and how political authorities disciplined religious practice. Confessional identity could become closely tied to law, education, and the state.

What changed for someone outside the learned elite? A parish might gain a different style of sermon, revised devotional expectations, new requirements of conformity, or new religious images—or the removal of familiar images. A family might face pressure from authorities, find its local church contested, or move because of persecution. What began as disputes over theology could reorganize the practices of ordinary life.

## Safavid Iran and the making of a confessional state

In Iran a different process began near the same time. Shah Ismail's ascent in 1501 marked the beginning of Safavid dynastic rule. Ismail and his followers brought together military force, claims to sacred legitimacy, and a developing state. The Safavid promotion of Twelver Shi'a Islam became a defining feature of Iranian history, especially in relation to Sunni Ottoman rivals.

A schematic account might say that the ruler changed the official religion and the country followed. That is much too simple. Communities already had distinct devotional practices and scholarly traditions. The growth of Shi'a institutions involved patronage for religious scholars, ritual life, learning, and the reorganization of authority over generations. Coercion and persecution were also important elements of confessional consolidation. The state could use religious difference to identify allies and enemies, but the lived results of its policies varied across geography and time.

The Safavids did not merely imitate a European struggle between confessions. The genealogy of the Safaviyya order, the role of Qizilbash followers, traditions of Shi'a learning, and Persianate political culture gave the new state its own history. Confessional conflict with Ottoman rulers mattered, but the rivalry also involved territory, diplomacy, trade, and dynastic legitimacy. Calling it purely a religious war would ignore the other reasons rulers fought.

Shah Abbas's later reforms, and patronage at Isfahan, show the combination of religion, court culture, and state formation. Mosques, shrines, and civic architecture participated in a vision of imperial authority; merchants and artisans helped sustain the cities that expressed it. Even within this powerful court-centered story, we should avoid confusing a ruler's favored doctrine with every household's private experience. A state may establish norms more quickly than people change convictions or practices.

## Islam in Southeast Asia: no single moment of conversion

Across Southeast Asia, Islam's spread cannot be reduced to a sea voyage undertaken by one missionary or a ruler's single decision. Coastal towns and maritime kingdoms encountered merchants, scholars, teachers, and travelers linked to South Asia, Arabia, Iran, and other centers. In several places Muslim communities developed over long periods before or alongside the conversion of rulers. Courts could adopt Muslim forms of legitimacy while retaining local political and cultural practices.

The historian Khairudin Aljunied describes Malay Islamization as entwined with connected societies. Muslim traders and travelers could settle near neighbors of other faiths; later religious teaching and political patronage enlarged communities. This process was neither always peaceful nor always imposed from above. There were direct appeals to faith, advantages in commercial and diplomatic relationships, inherited family affiliations, pressure from rulers, and disagreements about proper practice.

The historian Michael Laffan cautions that later stories about earlier Islamization sometimes reflect the concerns of the periods in which those stories were composed. A narrative produced in the seventeenth century, for example, may describe the religious origins of an earlier court in terms that legitimate later scholars and leaders. We must distinguish a claim about the past from independent evidence that the claimed event occurred in that form.

Along Java, Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and elsewhere, different communities integrated Islamic teaching with their local languages, customs, and preexisting religious worlds in different ways. Some later reform movements pressed for stronger separation from practices that they considered improper; others supported broader accommodation. We should not call one of these positions the authentic voice of the whole region. Religious traditions were interpreted and debated by their participants.

The ocean made contact possible, but shipping did not make belief automatic. A sailor could carry a text, introduce a teacher, establish a household, or fund a mosque; a local community still had to decide what those changes meant. The same sea that moved spices and cloth could sustain intellectual links among distant centers of learning, while every port remained a place with its own politics.

## The Sikh tradition and a community taking shape

In the Punjab, Guru Nanak, traditionally dated 1469 to 1539, taught in a religious and linguistic environment marked by multiple devotional traditions. His teachings emphasized devotion to one divine reality, ethical responsibility, and criticism of empty ritual or status claims. The emergence of the Sikh tradition was not simply the blending of two complete religions into a third. It developed distinctive teachings and institutions through the Gurus and their communities.

The process did not end with one life. The community associated with Guru Nanak was shaped by successors, gatherings, practices of shared service, and the preservation and performance of sacred verse. The compilation of scripture under Guru Arjan in the early seventeenth century became a decisive moment in the community's institutional history. Reading later Sikh self-understandings backward into every feature of the 1520s would conceal that development. Yet treating the early community as merely a prelude to later events would be equally misleading.

Here we see why religious history cannot be a table of founding dates. A tradition may establish itself through generations of teaching and relationship, not through the creation of a state. Verses that are sung, remembered, and interpreted can bind communities together across time. A ruler's tolerance or hostility may affect those communities, but the state is not the only institution capable of sustaining a religious world.

Mughal rulers, whose power expanded across northern India during the sixteenth century, encountered many such traditions. Akbar's court developed a distinctive practice of discussion across religious communities and an imperial vision sometimes associated with sulh-i kul, or peace with all. These discussions involved political sovereignty as well as sincere intellectual interests. Akbar's policies should not be projected onto every Mughal emperor or described as a modern secular constitution. His court was one powerful actor in a region whose religious institutions had lives of their own.

## The Americas and the violence of conversion

Now cross the Atlantic. In the Americas, Spanish and Portuguese colonial projects brought Christian missions into direct connection with conquest, settlement, disease, dispossession, and coerced labor. Religious authorities and colonizers often sought the destruction or suppression of Indigenous ritual traditions. Churches were built within new systems of rule, and conversion could be demanded or made advantageous under unequal conditions.

What happened afterward cannot be described as one people losing religion and receiving another intact. In Andean communities, local histories shaped how Christian images, saints, ritual practices, and institutions were understood. The historian Kenneth Mills examines the development of plural “Andean Christianities,” asking how local communities made sacred meanings out of Catholic institutions and images. These practices were not always accepted by church authorities, and colonial persecution was real. Yet to imagine Indigenous people as passive recipients of European belief is to erase their interpretations and strategies.

We must also resist romanticizing every local adaptation as resistance. A community might embrace parts of Christian teaching, preserve older observances, navigate a colonial court, seek protection through a religious brotherhood, or experience deep conflict over the demands of missionaries. Individuals within a village could disagree. What looked like outward compliance in one source might conceal diverse convictions; conversely, a historian cannot simply assume that every convert was pretending.

The archive presents a problem. Colonial officials preserved descriptions of Indigenous worship partly when they wished to regulate or suppress it. Their labels may distort what people were doing. Archaeology, local-language texts, oral traditions, visual culture, and careful comparative work can expand our view. Even then, the evidence often resists easy statements about what a particular person privately believed.

This gives us a contrast with the European Reformations. In both settings rulers and institutions sought to regulate belief and conduct. But colonized Indigenous peoples faced radically unequal political power and attempts to reorder social life under conquest. The parallel is not identity. A comparison must account for those differences instead of smoothing them into the general phrase “religious reform.”

## Traditions that do not fit the same storyline

Our survey has emphasized movements that are easily described as reforms or changes of official religious affiliation. That emphasis risks leaving out the histories of Buddhist and Hindu institutions, local and regional sacred practices, and other traditions that remained deeply influential across early modern Asia and beyond. They were not static while dynasties fought and missionaries traveled.

In South Asia, devotional communities, teachers, pilgrimage, temple patronage, and vernacular literary traditions continued to develop through changing political circumstances. To describe those lives only as the setting in which a Muslim ruler governed would misplace the center of the story. Across mainland Southeast Asia, Buddhist monastic communities, kingship, and local ritual practices helped organize social relations and political legitimacy. Elsewhere, forms of Buddhism and other religious traditions developed within different political and cultural settings. There was no single worldwide Buddhist institution whose policies can be plotted against a European Reformation calendar.

Comparison is still useful. We can ask how a sacred text becomes authoritative, how ritual practice changes across languages, how rulers seek religious legitimacy, how communities preserve institutions through conquest, and what happens when a new teacher challenges established authority. But no answer should assume that religion everywhere means an institution resembling a European church, or that change must take the form of one dramatic schism.

This is also why words like tolerance and syncretism need care. Tolerance can describe a relationship of unequal authority: one ruler permits others to worship under conditions the ruler sets. Syncretism can identify borrowings and recombinations, but it can imply that traditions were pure and sealed before contact. Historical actors often understood themselves as preserving, recovering, purifying, or correctly interpreting inherited truths. Our categories should help reveal their arguments, not replace them.

## A question about who makes a religious world

Imagine four different objects: a printed Protestant pamphlet, a Safavid state commission, a Sikh devotional hymn, and a Christian image used in an Andean community. Each can move beyond the setting in which it was created. Each depends on interpreters, audiences, and institutions. But they are not interchangeable expressions of one process.

The pamphlet may be bound up with a public argument about scriptural authority and church practices. The commission may express imperial patronage and a policy of confessional consolidation. The hymn sustains a community through remembered and performed teaching. The Andean image may become the subject of locally specific devotion under colonial inequality. Historians can trace some of these uses through books, inventories, paintings, legal records, and surviving practices. We must never claim that an object tells us the private conviction of everyone who encountered it.

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries saw extraordinary religious movement and contestation, but not one synchronized religious revolution. Political authority mattered, and so did texts, teachers, households, merchants, scholars, artists, and worshippers. Religious ideas helped rulers make claims on populations; religious communities could also constrain rulers, resist persecution, and create forms of belonging beyond states.

A world history that treats religion only as an instrument of empires is incomplete. A history that treats religious change only as the inevitable unfolding of doctrines is incomplete too. The difficult, more interesting task is to examine where lived faith, institutional power, and human choice intersected—and to preserve the differences among the people who lived that history.
