# Who Pays for the Adjustment? African Societies in the 1980s

## A budget is a record of power

Picture the finance minister of an African country at the beginning of the nineteen eighties. The country has schools to operate, clinics to supply, imports to purchase, and loans whose payments are coming due. Export earnings arrive in foreign currency, but the price of the main export has fallen. Interest rates abroad have risen. A creditor asks for a plan before agreeing to new lending. The minister cannot satisfy every demand at once. Someone will be told to accept a smaller allocation, higher prices, a lower wage in real terms, or fewer services. The apparent technical problem of balancing accounts is a political question: who is asked to adjust, and who can refuse? This is one way to enter the history of African societies during the nineteen eighties, an era frequently described through a single phrase, structural adjustment.

There was no single African economy awaiting a single rescue. Countries differed in climate, mineral resources, agriculture, recent wars, public institutions, and connections to international markets. Some exported copper, some cocoa, some oil, and some depended heavily on agricultural goods whose prices they did not control. Governments had made their own policy choices after independence: building public enterprises, promoting industries, maintaining food subsidies, and expanding education. Such choices could provide valuable services and opportunities, but some projects were inefficient, politically protected, or difficult to finance. External forces mattered too. Higher global interest rates, changes in commodity prices, imported fuel costs, and borrowing contracts shaped the room for maneuver. To say that domestic decisions mattered is not to erase international constraints. To say that international conditions mattered is not to absolve political leaders of responsibility.

African governments were not silent about the kind of development they wanted. In nineteen eighty the Organization of African Unity adopted the Lagos Plan of Action and a related final act, calling for greater economic cooperation and an effort to build more self-reliant, diversified economies. Its proposals placed importance on agriculture, industry, science, and regional exchange. A plan is not proof that its authors could finance or implement everything in it. Still, its existence matters because it challenges a familiar story in which international financial institutions invented the entire debate about Africa's economic future and African governments merely received instructions. African leaders and planners had articulated a program, and later African Union archival records show continued attempts to assess implementation. The dispute was partly about who should set the objectives of development, not only which budget line should be cut.

In nineteen eighty-one the World Bank published a report commonly called the Berg Report, officially titled Accelerated Development in Sub-Saharan Africa: An Agenda for Action. It argued that many government policies had discouraged agriculture and exports and that economic incentives needed to change. Its recommendations emphasized more realistic prices, different roles for state and private enterprise, and changes in aid and public investment. The Organization of African Unity responded to the report, leaving a documentary trace of a real policy debate. The reports did not have equal power: a lender's preferred framework could shape access to resources, while African governments had to weigh creditor demands against domestic political claims. But neither side should be represented as speaking with one unanimous voice. The intellectual conflict was about diagnosis, priorities, and what kind of dependency new policies might reduce or reproduce.

## What structural adjustment meant in practice

Structural adjustment was not one law enacted across the continent. It was a family of programs, negotiated country by country, usually alongside assistance from the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, and other lenders. Common measures included reducing fiscal deficits, changing exchange rates, liberalizing trade, reforming state enterprises, revising subsidies, and altering the prices paid to producers. A currency devaluation could make an export more competitive abroad but raise the local price of imported medicine, fuel, or machinery. A food subsidy might protect urban consumers while imposing large fiscal costs or discouraging some domestic production. Ending it might help a budget and harm families immediately. Whether a specific change worked depended on sequencing, institutions, markets, and access to other protections. There was no meaningful average household that felt all these effects equally.

Ghana provides one important case, though it cannot stand for the continent. By the early nineteen eighties, its economy had endured serious decline, inflation, damaged infrastructure, shortages of imports, and sharp difficulty earning foreign exchange. Cocoa remained central to export revenue; falling prices and other disturbances compounded the problem. Under Jerry Rawlings's government, Ghana launched an Economic Recovery Programme in April nineteen eighty-three, supported by international lenders and donors. Its measures included exchange-rate changes, fiscal stabilization, and efforts to restore production and exports. Subsequent International Monetary Fund assessments reported substantial economic recovery and lower inflation. That is evidence that policies and associated financing coincided with important macroeconomic improvements. It does not by itself tell us how every Ghanaian household fared, or which parts of the recovery were due to rainfall, aid, policy, or a rebound from an exceptionally deep collapse.

Consider what a devaluation does. If a cocoa exporter receives foreign currency, a lower value of the domestic currency can increase the local return from the sale, though the relationship depends on taxation and purchasing arrangements. But a hospital importing equipment may find the same foreign purchase more expensive in local money. An urban employee whose wages rise slowly may discover that a basket of goods costs more. In the long run, successful exports might support investment, public revenue, and imports. In the short run, the losses and gains reach different people. A national statistic reporting higher growth is therefore a starting point, not a complete social history. The fuller account asks who faced new prices, who gained market access, and which services the government had resources to maintain.

Zambia offers a different view of the relationship between prices and legitimacy. The country depended heavily on copper, and declining export earnings put pressure on public finances and the supply of foreign currency. Zambia adopted adjustment measures during the nineteen eighties, including changes to exchange-rate policy and food subsidies. In December nineteen eighty-six, price increases and shortages of maize meal helped provoke riots in the Copperbelt and elsewhere. The government reversed parts of the policy. In May nineteen eighty-seven President Kenneth Kaunda announced a break from the program being pursued with international financial institutions. This was not a simple morality play in which one institution issued an order and a president helplessly obeyed. Zambian officials negotiated policies, domestic groups challenged them, and the government made and remade choices under serious constraints.

A dispute about maize meal sounds local, even modest, when compared with an international debt crisis. But staple food is where broad policy becomes a daily calculation. People cannot wait for the long-term adjustment of an economy before eating dinner. A government can claim that subsidies are financially unsustainable; consumers can answer that the new price is socially unsustainable. Both claims must be examined rather than assumed. Nor should the deaths and repression associated with unrest be treated as an accounting adjustment. The historical record records contested estimates and different explanations of how policy changes caused shortages. What is clear is that people organized and protested, and their actions changed the immediate course of government policy. They were not merely passive recipients of a global economic decision.

## The social cost, and the argument over evidence

By the middle of the decade, criticism of adjustment was no longer confined to politicians who opposed specific loans. Researchers, health workers, and international agencies were asking how stabilization policies affected child nutrition, education, and access to care. The study Adjustment with a Human Face, associated with UNICEF and published in nineteen eighty-seven and eighty-eight under the editorship of Giovanni Andrea Cornia, Richard Jolly, and Frances Stewart, argued that protecting vulnerable people had to be part of policy design. The title was pointed: it implied that a program might improve some financial measurements while failing people whose losses were difficult to see in national accounts. It did not follow that every program caused identical harm. It followed that indicators chosen to judge a program could conceal who bore the transition costs.

Suppose a government reports that inflation has fallen, exports have improved, and a budget deficit has narrowed. These are meaningful changes. A parent might still report that a clinic has no medicine, or that school costs now force a child to work. Such observations can coexist. To decide whether adjustment caused the problem, we would need records of spending and services before and after reform, evidence about drought or war, differences among communities, and comparisons with plausible alternatives. This is demanding work. It is easier to announce that adjustment either saved or destroyed an economy. But the historical importance lies in what those formulas hide: the distribution of risks and resources, the time horizon of reforms, and the weakness or strength of institutions meant to protect those who cannot absorb a shock.

African economists and policy institutions developed alternatives. In nineteen eighty-nine the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa prepared an African Alternative Framework to Structural Adjustment Programmes for Socio-Economic Recovery and Transformation. It placed more emphasis on longer-term productive capacity, distribution, human needs, and the mobilization of domestic resources. The framework was adopted by African ministers and received consideration at the United Nations. That did not make it a ready-made solution. The hard questions remained: how to finance services during a foreign-exchange crisis, how to reform public enterprises without destroying useful capabilities, and how to improve agriculture while protecting those who buy food. The existence of this alternative shows that criticism did not always amount to a wish to return unchanged to the past. It could be a debate over the priorities and ownership of reform.

## War, organization, and competing futures

Debt programs were not the only pressures shaping Africa in the nineteen eighties. In southern Africa, armed conflicts, cross-border interventions, and displacement made economic planning inseparable from violence. Mozambique, independent since nineteen seventy-five, experienced a devastating civil war that uprooted communities and damaged rural life. Refugees crossed into neighboring countries including Malawi, while those who remained faced hazards to farming and transport. The international refugee agency's later account of its operations shows how difficult repatriation became while fighting continued. To explain a country's economic condition solely through its borrowing or budget policy during such years would be to omit war itself. Destruction of a road or clinic is not a market incentive. It is a loss of physical and social capacity that can shape economic choices for years.

In South Africa the decade was marked by another kind of mass organizing. The apartheid regime tried to preserve white minority rule through repression and limited institutional reform. In nineteen eighty-three the United Democratic Front brought together a broad network of civic, religious, trade-union, student, youth, and women's organizations. Boycotts, neighborhood campaigns, labor action, and other forms of resistance changed the political possibilities inside the country. These movements had different goals and strategies, and the government answered many forms of dissent with force. Their history belongs in a survey of African societies in the eighties because economic questions cannot be separated from who has political rights. A factory wage, a residence permit, a local tax, and the right to organize were entwined under apartheid. Calls for democracy were also arguments about labor and the distribution of power.

Women's participation in community and labor organizations is particularly important to the story because household survival work is easily omitted from national economic debates. A rise in food prices can mean more time spent finding cheaper supplies, more unpaid care, and difficult choices about which family member continues school. The costs may be recorded nowhere as wages or spending. Women organized around these pressures in different national settings, including through South African women's groups within the anti-apartheid coalition. We should not infer identical experiences for all African women or reduce their politics to a single burden of care. Activists also demanded representation and rights, challenged state violence, and argued over the kind of society they wanted. A history that sees only ministers and creditors misses this political labor.

By the end of the decade, the most productive question was no longer whether the state or the market was always good. It was what states were able to do, what markets actually delivered, and who could correct errors. Some policies tried to protect inefficient monopolies; others removed protections without creating effective alternatives. Export earnings could finance public goods, yet extreme dependence on one export could expose a country to every fluctuation in world prices. Public investment could build roads and schools, but corruption or weak administration could waste it. External lenders could provide critical finance and impose conditions that narrowed democratic choice. These tensions were not failures to discover a universal economic formula. They were the substantive political problems that the formula was supposed to solve.

## What a decade leaves behind

When looking back, resist calling the nineteen eighties simply a lost decade for Africa. For many people the period brought grave economic hardship, violent conflict, or weakened public services. For others it involved new organizing, debates about democratic accountability, recovery from previous economic breakdowns, or efforts to build different institutions. The word lost can flatten those differences and make Africans disappear from the explanation. The Lagos Plan of Action, the arguments with the World Bank, the Ghanaian recovery program, Zambia's food protests, and the African Alternative Framework demonstrate that local and regional choices mattered. These decisions were made within an international system whose power was highly unequal. Agency does not mean unlimited freedom; it means that we ask which choices were available, who made them, and how their effects were contested.

There is a useful exercise in reading any account of structural adjustment. Find the indicators in its first paragraph. If it mentions inflation, ask about wages. If it mentions exports, ask which producers and traders earned the revenue. If it mentions public savings, ask what happened to clinics and schools. If it mentions riots, ask what price changed and how police responded. If it mentions declining welfare, ask what data identify the effect of policy rather than war, drought, or preceding crisis. These are not instructions to choose a side before investigating. They are a way to keep the human unit of analysis visible while preserving economic reasoning. A serious history can acknowledge real recoveries and real suffering without making either disappear.

The lasting issue is accountability. A government promises that a sacrifice today will buy stability tomorrow. A lender promises that new rules will produce growth. A popular movement promises that organization can make the state answer to those it governs. Each claim invites evidence. The African debates of the nineteen eighties were about institutions that could generate wealth and institutions that could be challenged when they failed. The people most affected were farmers and workers, traders and students, patients and taxpayers, displaced families and organizers. Their lives were not details added to an economic story after the calculations were finished. They were the reason the calculations mattered in the first place.

## Source method and remaining gaps (display only)

Records from state departments and international agencies are evidence about institutional policies and how they are represented, not independent confirmation of motives or social outcomes. Web excerpts and bibliographic abstracts are labeled and not passed off as fully read archival works. Where counts or causal explanations are uncertain, the spoken narrative preserves that uncertainty. Expand this pack with Afghan and Soviet participant oral histories, Chinese local and worker archives, African independent policy evaluations, and non-U.S. histories of technology before claiming comprehensive regional coverage. Lecture length is dictated by the material, not a word or time limit.
