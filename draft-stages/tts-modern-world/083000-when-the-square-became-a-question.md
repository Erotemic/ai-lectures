# When the Square Became a Question: Uprisings Across the Arab World, 2010–2013

## The street vendor and the state

On December seventeenth, two thousand and ten, a young street vendor named Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire in Sidi Bouzid, a town in Tunisia's interior. Accounts of the confrontation that preceded his act differ in their details, and we should not turn it into a perfectly scripted legend. What is clear is that his death became a focus for anger about police humiliation, precarious work, corruption, and political exclusion. A protest did not spread because one person had the right symbol. It spread because many people recognized the conditions around that person. Demonstrators in smaller inland towns confronted security forces before the world's cameras began treating Tunis as the center of the story.

Tunisia's president, Zine el-Abidine Ben Ali, had ruled since nineteen eighty-seven. His government had expanded schooling and certain economic opportunities, but it also restricted opposition parties, controlled public expression, and gave the ruling circle advantages unavailable to ordinary citizens. Growth figures could not tell a graduate whether a job would be available or whether a police official could destroy a livelihood. Protesters demanded work and dignity, and increasingly the departure of the president. These were political demands and economic demands at once. Their strength came from the connection: the person denied a job or fair treatment had few effective ways to hold officials responsible.

As demonstrations widened, police fired on crowds. Human rights investigators documented deaths, injuries, and the experiences of families who sought explanations. Workers, lawyers, local activists, students, and established professional and labor networks helped protests survive repeated repression. On January fourteenth, two thousand and eleven, Ben Ali left Tunisia. The departure of the president was a turning point, not proof that a democratic constitution had already been achieved. Police institutions, ministries, courts, and the economy did not disappear with him. A revolution can remove the person at the top while leaving the deeper question of who is allowed to govern unanswered.

## Cairo's many publics

News from Tunisia traveled rapidly through satellite television, newspapers, relatives, phone calls, and online networks. Egyptian activists had organized long before January twenty-fifth, two thousand and eleven. Workers had struck, lawyers challenged emergency powers, and families protested police brutality. The death of Khaled Said after a police encounter became a prominent rallying point. When demonstrations spread from Cairo's Tahrir Square to Alexandria, Suez, and other cities, participants did not all imagine the same future. Some called for elections; others sought freedom from abusive police; workers wanted wages and protections; young organizers demanded an end to a system that treated them as subjects rather than citizens.

On January twenty-eighth, security forces attacked demonstrators and the authorities disrupted internet and mobile connections. The attempt to cut communication did not make the crowds vanish. People found other routes to meeting places through neighborhoods, mosques, workplaces, trusted acquaintances, and broadcast media. The fact that digital networks helped some groups coordinate does not mean a website made the revolution. A protest movement requires people who will risk arrest, people who supply food or medical care, and institutions that can withdraw consent or enforcement. The street is not a screen. It is a place where a claim about citizenship meets the physical power of the state.

Hosni Mubarak resigned on February eleventh after nearly three decades in office. Senior officers on the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces took control of the transition. The army's refusal to preserve Mubarak in office was decisive, but it did not mean generals had surrendered their own authority. Elections and constitutional amendments followed amid continuing demonstrations, clashes, and arguments about the place of the military and religion in public law. The early celebration of a dictator's departure concealed a difficult institutional question: if a military establishment controls coercive power and much of the state's machinery, what can an elected government actually change?

Mohamed Morsi won the presidential election in two thousand and twelve as the candidate of a movement associated with the Muslim Brotherhood. His supporters described an electoral mandate and a break with military rule. Critics objected to his efforts to concentrate power and to the constitutional process; large demonstrations demanded his departure. In July two thousand and thirteen the military removed him. That sequence includes both genuine public opposition to an elected president and a military overthrow of that president. Neither fact cancels the other. Security forces then carried out mass killings when dispersing pro-Morsi sit-ins in August. The revolutionary demand for dignity had not produced a settled agreement about who could legitimately command the state.

## Tunisia's longer negotiation

Tunisia followed another path, but not an effortless one. Its first elections after Ben Ali chose a constituent assembly to write a constitution. The Islamist Ennahda movement entered government, while other political currents contested how religion, individual rights, and executive authority should be described. Old state agencies remained important. Social demands from the interior persisted. Women had been central participants in the uprising and in debates over legal rights; they were not simply beneficiaries waiting for elites to negotiate. Political assassinations and mass demonstrations in two thousand and thirteen raised the possibility that compromise would fail.

Four Tunisian organizations then offered a route through the impasse: the General Labour Union, the employers' confederation, the Human Rights League, and the national lawyers' order. Together they became known as the National Dialogue Quartet. It mattered that these organizations had membership, bargaining experience, and reputations outside parliament. They could bring factions into a process in which no side needed to pretend it had won every dispute. A new constitution was adopted in two thousand and fourteen, followed by elections. The Quartet later received the Nobel Peace Prize. That recognition did not settle Tunisia's unemployment, regional inequality, or future constitutional struggles. It marked a specific achievement: negotiated political rules under immediate pressure.

These Tunisian and Egyptian histories illuminate an important difference between a crowd and an institution. A crowd can make continued rule costly for a president. Building accountable government then requires courts, police, representative organizations, elections, and procedures by which defeated rivals can remain in public life. These institutions may have participated in the old regime, or they may not yet exist with sufficient independence. Neither a street protest nor a ballot automatically answers who controls security forces. Political openings make the question visible, but they do not supply the answer by themselves.

## Libya: protecting civilians and deciding a war

In Libya, demonstrations against Muammar Qadhafi's long rule began in February two thousand and eleven, with Benghazi an early center. Security forces used violence against protesters, and the confrontation rapidly turned into armed rebellion. The country did not have the same network of autonomous national labor and party organizations that could negotiate a settlement like Tunisia's. Towns, military units, tribal and regional networks, defectors, and armed groups made different choices. The balance of force was immediately central. The regime controlled substantial military equipment; opponents feared that government advances would be catastrophic for civilian populations.

On March seventeenth, the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution nineteen seventy-three. It demanded a ceasefire and authorized member states to take necessary measures to protect civilians, while excluding a foreign occupation force. The text also established a no-fly zone. These provisions were a legal authorization for a particular purpose, not a blank statement that foreign governments could choose Libya's political future. Military action began through a multinational coalition; NATO subsequently took command of the operation. Its air campaign helped prevent the regime from defeating armed opponents and contributed to the circumstances in which Qadhafi was overthrown and killed in October.

A dispute about Libya soon became a dispute about the meaning of protection. Supporters of the intervention argued that state violence and the threat to Benghazi made external force necessary. Critics questioned whether the campaign's conduct exceeded civilian protection and moved toward regime change. The Security Council vote and the subsequent military operations are documented events; the judgment about alternatives and proportionality is a separate historical argument. After the old leadership fell, rival militias and political authorities struggled over weapons, ministries, and territory. Removing a dictatorship had not created a monopoly on legitimate force. For civilians, the end of one danger could open a period of new insecurity.

## Syria: a protest becomes a war

Syria's first large protests in two thousand and eleven emerged in a state already shaped by decades of emergency powers, security surveillance, and rule by the Assad family. In Daraa, reports of children detained and tortured after writing anti-government slogans became a flashpoint. Demonstrators demanded their release and an end to corruption and repression. Security forces opened fire. Human rights investigators documented interviews, video evidence, arrests, and the violent suppression of gatherings. Syrian protesters also created local coordination committees, organized strikes, and tried to share accounts of events in towns that outsiders could not freely visit. The first months cannot accurately be described as simply a war between two comparable armies.

As killings and detention continued, parts of the opposition armed themselves, defecting soldiers joined insurgent groups, and the conflict acquired multiple fronts. Opposition forces differed in political purpose, external support, and treatment of civilians. By two thousand and twelve the country was in a sustained armed conflict. Government units and allied forces attacked populated areas; armed opposition groups also committed serious abuses. By two thousand and thirteen, mass displacement and the use of increasingly destructive weapons transformed daily life. A political movement seeking change had become entangled in a war over territory and survival, with people unable to choose a safe side.

The fragmentation matters for history. A Syrian family might fear government arrest, shelling from rival forces, a checkpoint run by an armed group, or the loss of food and medicine, sometimes all in one journey. Foreign powers were not merely watching. Regional governments supported different armed and political actors, while international diplomacy repeatedly failed to stop the violence. None of that means Syrians had no agency, and none means a single foreign decision explains the whole war. The institutions that could have mediated disputes were weak or subordinated to coercive power. By the time negotiations were attempted, many actors could continue fighting without trusting a settlement.

## Bahrain and the region's uneven map

Bahrain adds a contrast sometimes left outside accounts that move only between Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, and Syria. In February two thousand and eleven, demonstrators occupied Pearl Roundabout in Manama and demanded political reform, representation, and an end to discrimination. Many were Shia Muslims in a state ruled by a Sunni monarchy, but reducing their demands to religious identity misses their constitutional claims and the participation of people with different political views. Security forces attacked protesters; neighboring Gulf forces entered Bahrain in March at the government's request. Arrests, dismissals, and attacks on medical personnel followed. The monarchy remained in power. External security relationships and the configuration of domestic institutions shaped the outcome.

Elsewhere across the region, demonstrations also appeared in Yemen and other states, but with different histories of military organization, regional division, patronage, and foreign alliances. The phrase Arab Spring was convenient for newspapers because it gave simultaneous events one season and one name. Historians need to ask what the label conceals. Tunisia's labor organizations were not Egypt's military establishment. Libya's armed fragmentation was not the same political process as a Syrian protest movement being met with escalating state violence. Shared slogans did not imply shared paths, and a common language did not make all governments, social classes, or institutions alike.

## What a revolution changes

Images of large crowds are persuasive because they show people acting together. But a famous photograph cannot reveal whether demonstrators agree on a constitution, whether officers will accept civilian control, or whether the new authorities can provide jobs. Nor can a social media post tell us who distributed leaflets, organized transport, fed strikers, or protected a neighborhood during a curfew. Digital evidence is valuable, especially where officials tried to suppress reporting. It is incomplete evidence about collective action. Researchers must compare it with oral testimony, local organizational records, legal documents, and the actions of security institutions.

By the end of two thousand and thirteen, the region had not converged on a single outcome. Tunisia had a precarious but real negotiation over democratic institutions. Egypt had returned to military-led rule after a turbulent electoral interlude. Libya had removed its ruler without establishing a stable security settlement. Syria was being torn apart by war. Bahrain's monarchy had survived repression and foreign security assistance. These results cannot be explained by measuring enthusiasm, courage, or internet access. They require histories of how power was held, organized, challenged, and transferred—or not transferred.

The enduring question of these uprisings is therefore not whether the people wanted freedom. Many did, and they said so in different words and political traditions. It is how an old ruling coalition can be dismantled without replacing it with unchecked force, how rivals can govern together after a victory, and how economic promises can be fulfilled when the treasury, police, courts, and workplaces still reflect old inequalities. A revolution announces that the present arrangement is not inevitable. Building another arrangement is a distinct and demanding kind of politics.
