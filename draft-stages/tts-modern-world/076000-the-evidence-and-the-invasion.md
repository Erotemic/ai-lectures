# The Evidence and the Invasion: Iraq, 2002–2005

## A war argued before it was fought

In early two thousand and three, governments argued over whether Iraq possessed prohibited weapons, whether inspections could verify disarmament, and whether an invasion would make the world safer. The questions were connected, but they were not the same. A state might have violated disarmament obligations without possessing the stockpiles another government claimed it held. An intelligence assessment might sincerely warn of danger yet be wrong. A government might have reasons to seek regime change that exceeded its declared concern about weapons. And even genuine misconduct by a state does not automatically answer whether another state is legally entitled to invade it. These distinctions matter because the United States and Britain presented military action as a response to an urgent threat, while many governments wanted inspectors to continue their work.

Iraq had already endured several transformations before the invasion. Saddam Hussein's Baathist dictatorship had pursued wars against Iran and Kuwait, violently suppressed opponents, and used chemical weapons during the nineteen eighties. After Iraq invaded Kuwait in nineteen ninety, a United States-led coalition expelled its forces the following year. A United Nations disarmament regime and far-reaching sanctions followed. Iraqi households lived amid damaged infrastructure, shortages, and a state that distributed resources while repressing dissent. The regime's previous crimes were real; they do not have to be exaggerated to understand why many Iraqis feared it. But the historical record must also account for the harm suffered by people who did not choose their rulers. Sanctions, state repression, and the prospect of renewed war shaped what any proposed liberation could mean.

## From reports to certainty

In November two thousand and two, the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution fourteen forty-one. It required Iraq to meet disarmament obligations, submit declarations, and cooperate with renewed inspections by the United Nations monitoring commission and the International Atomic Energy Agency. It warned of serious consequences for continued violations. What it did not do was plainly state that a new invasion could begin without another decision by the Council. Governments disagreed over the legal significance of earlier resolutions and of fourteen forty-one itself. France and other governments argued that the Council had not given an automatic authorization to use force. The United States and Britain maintained that existing resolutions supplied a legal basis. To present that legal disagreement accurately, we have to distinguish the text of a resolution from the different theories governments used to interpret it.

Inspectors returned to Iraq in late November two thousand and two. On March seventh, two thousand and three, chief inspector Hans Blix described a complicated picture to the Security Council. Inspectors were gaining access to sites with fewer difficulties than they had sometimes encountered in the nineteen nineties, while major unresolved questions remained about declarations and past programs. Nuclear inspectors separately examined claims about an active nuclear weapons effort. The process was not a simple demonstration that Iraq had fulfilled every requirement, but neither did an unresolved inventory prove that huge active stocks were waiting to be found. The inspectors' work was a method: inspect buildings, compare documents, interview personnel, pursue discrepancies, and decide which conclusions the evidence warranted. That method takes time, while political leaders were increasingly speaking as though the important conclusions had already been established.

One year later, a United States Senate intelligence investigation found that major judgments in the prewar assessment of Iraqi weapons programs had overstated or lacked support from underlying intelligence. The Iraq Survey Group, sent after the invasion to investigate weapons activities, did not find the stockpiles of active chemical and biological weapons central to prewar public claims. It documented an older history of Iraqi weapons programs and intentions, a more complex record than either the picture of an imminent arsenal or a claim that no prohibited activity had ever occurred. The sequence matters. An intelligence warning issued before a war is an account of what officials believed or claimed at that time; an investigation after the war can test those beliefs against places, records, and people that were not previously accessible. The later findings do not travel backward in time to become what leaders already knew, but they do allow us to judge the reliability of their assertions.

## The invasion and the people beneath it

The United States, Britain, and other coalition partners began the invasion in March two thousand and three. Coalition forces moved toward Baghdad while striking military and government targets from the air. Iraqi regular forces, Republican Guard units, and irregular fighters resisted in different ways and places. By early April the government had lost control of the capital. The fall of Saddam Hussein's regime was a major political rupture, but an invading army's ability to take a city could not by itself establish a functioning successor state. The civilian tasks remained enormous: keeping hospitals supplied, protecting water and electrical systems, stopping looting, preserving records, and providing security to neighborhoods that had lived for decades under coercive rule. Public images of victory captured real changes in military control and only a fraction of this wider process.

Human Rights Watch investigated civilian deaths during the opening campaign. It found that coalition forces took precautions in some forms of targeting, but that ground-launched cluster munitions in populated areas caused extensive civilian harm. In al-Hilla, doctors and residents described casualties after weapons scattered small explosive units across residential streets. Some devices remained unexploded, threatening civilians after combat passed. The report also described violations by Iraqi forces and other attacks on civilians. A serious account has to attribute each documented action to the force responsible rather than assigning all violence to one side or collapsing it into an impersonal cost of war. It must also admit what a field investigation cannot do: count every person killed across the country or settle every disagreement about the legality of an individual strike.

The destruction of a dictatorship posed another question: which parts of the Iraqi state should survive its removal? The ministries had employed engineers, doctors, administrators, teachers, clerks, and security officers; some had served a coercive regime, some had joined the ruling party to keep a job, and some had actively committed grave abuses. Removing leaders responsible for oppression was a legitimate concern for Iraqis harmed by the regime. Destroying the administrative networks through which water, salaries, maintenance, and policing operated could also leave those same Iraqis unprotected. A state is not identical to its ruling family, yet neither can a state built around a dictatorship be assumed to serve everyone fairly once its president has fled. Reconstruction required carefully sorting people and institutions. Decisions made in that sorting would shape the occupation.

## An occupation changes institutions

In May two thousand and three, the Coalition Provisional Authority issued Order Number One on de-Baathification and Order Number Two dissolving specified institutions, including the Iraqi army and several security organizations. The first sought to dismantle the leadership structures of the ruling party and remove senior cadres from public responsibilities. The second declared designated state bodies dissolved and suspended their financial obligations pending new arrangements. These were written decisions, not simply a mood in the occupying administration. They affected chains of command, professional identities, employment, and expectations of salary. Historians and officials have debated how much the orders contributed to subsequent armed resistance. We should resist a single-cause explanation: insecurity, humiliation, revenge, regional pressures, militant mobilization, and the destruction of older political institutions all interacted, and not all individuals affected by the orders chose violence.

An occupation also faced the question of authority. Who could appoint officials, make laws, collect revenue, and speak for Iraqis? The Security Council's Resolution fifteen eleven, adopted in October, recognized the need for a political transition and authorized a multinational force in the post-invasion security situation. Its adoption did not retroactively settle the contested legality of the invasion itself. Iraqi politicians and communities had differing plans for the future, and foreign authorities had enormous influence over the institutions being designed. Some Iraqis saw the end of Baathist rule as an opening for political and religious expression; others feared that occupation and armed conflict were replacing one form of coercion with another. Both reactions could exist within the same city and even within the same household.

Violence increasingly came from multiple directions. Insurgent groups attacked foreign troops, Iraqi police and officials, infrastructure, aid workers, and civilians. Coalition and Iraqi security operations also caused civilian harm, arbitrary detention, and allegations of abuse. Armed groups sometimes claimed to be defending Iraq from occupation while killing people they categorized as collaborators or enemies. Sunni and Shia identities mattered to political organizing, but they did not predetermine whether a particular person supported an armed group, a religious party, or a secular political future. Kurdish parties and communities in the north carried different memories of Baathist repression and different demands for autonomy. To describe the war merely as ancient sectarian hostility would erase the recent institutional decisions and political strategies through which sectarian categories became instruments of mobilization and fear.

## A prison and a broken promise

In April two thousand and four, photographs from Abu Ghraib prison showed U.S. personnel humiliating and abusing Iraqi detainees. Investigations followed into torture, cruel treatment, command decisions, and the wider detention system. The public scandal was about far more than the existence of shocking images. Detainees were vulnerable to coercion because armed authorities controlled their movement, contact with family, and ability to challenge accusations. A government claiming to bring legality to a country damaged by dictatorship had to be judged by what happened to people in its custody. Human Rights Watch argued that official explanations focused too narrowly on a few individuals while ignoring the policies and failures of oversight that made abuse possible. Determining individual legal responsibility still required evidence; institutional responsibility could not be dismissed simply by punishing the people visible in a photograph.

By two thousand and four, ordinary political participation was taking place amid insecurity. Some people organized parties or newspapers, joined local councils, negotiated with religious leaders, or worked to restore schools and clinics. Others had reason to fear an election office, a police station, or a convoy on the road. Women seeking political and professional roles faced threats from armed actors as well as older constraints. The issue was not whether Iraqis were capable of governing themselves. It was whether they could safely dispute their own future while an occupying power, provisional state agencies, insurgencies, and militias competed to control territory. Elections are essential to representative government, but a ballot alone cannot prevent coercion when armed organizations can decide who is allowed to reach the polling place.

## Counting causes and consequences

The Iraq War made a lasting example of the gap between a publicly stated threat and the evidentiary basis for acting against it. Before the war, leaders spoke about chemical, biological, and nuclear danger and interpreted intelligence in light of Saddam Hussein's record of deception and aggression. Afterward, investigators found that central claims about active weapons stockpiles had been wrong. This does not mean the old dictatorship was harmless; it means its very real abuses must be kept analytically distinct from the different, unsubstantiated claim used to justify a new war. The distinction protects the meaning of evidence. A correct condemnation of a regime's historical crimes cannot prove any proposition about the location of weapons today, and a mistaken weapons claim cannot be repaired by pointing out other crimes once it has failed.

The second enduring problem was what happens when military victory eliminates an existing state structure without a broadly legitimate replacement. The occupation's orders reorganized employment and security; fighting damaged cities and services; new armed groups sought power; and civilians attempted to maintain lives amid uncertainties they had not created. Responsibility is not indivisible. The previous regime was responsible for its repression and wars, coalition governments for their decisions to invade and occupy, and insurgents and militias for their own attacks on civilians. Those actions also interacted and changed incentives over time. Asking which decision mattered most requires careful counterfactual history, not confidence gained from a single quotation or photograph. The violence of later years was not a law of nature, but neither can it be explained by only one administrative mistake.

The lesson is not that leaders must wait for perfect certainty before acting. The world rarely supplies it. It is that the scale and irreversibility of force make evidentiary discipline especially important. What is known? What is inferred? Which institutions can check a claim? What would happen to the people who must live under the result? In Iraq, these questions were asked by diplomats, soldiers, inspectors, Iraqis opposing Saddam, Iraqis opposing occupation, journalists, and families trying to survive. Their answers differed, and some of their predictions proved disastrously wrong. The history is more useful when we preserve those differences rather than compressing them into a story in which one event inevitably produced all the rest.
