# 020000 — Measuring Duct Static Pressure and Room Air Delivery

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A room is colder than the others. You can feel air coming out of its register, so the duct must be fine. Or perhaps not. A little airflow at the grille does not establish the quantity of air reaching the room. Conversely, a loud grille may be evidence of a restriction, not a sign of generous air delivery.

To diagnose a ducted heating or cooling system, we need two different kinds of measurement. Pressure tells us something about the resistance faced by the fan. Airflow tells us how much air the system actually moves. Neither one automatically gives the other without knowing the equipment and the measurement conditions.

## What static pressure means

Imagine a blower drawing air through a filter and return duct, then pushing it through a coil and supply ducts. On the suction side, the pressure relative to the room is often negative. On the discharge side it is positive. Static pressure describes pressure not associated with the air's directional kinetic energy. Technicians use a manometer and properly placed pressure probes to sample it.

For a specified equipment configuration, total external static pressure refers to the pressure difference the blower must overcome across the external components defined by the manufacturer's rating. That definition matters: a filter or coil may be included or excluded depending on how the equipment and test locations are defined. A number without a test-point diagram can be meaningless. The result is usually expressed in inches of water column or pascals.

A fan has a performance relationship between pressure and flow at a given speed. Increase the resistance and many fans deliver less air. An electronically controlled variable-speed blower may attempt to hold a target airflow by increasing speed and power, up to its limits. Thus a dirty filter may produce lower flow on one system and higher blower effort on another. Pressure data must be interpreted with the manufacturer's fan performance information and the active operating mode.

## Pressure is not flow

Picture two duct systems with the same measured supply pressure. One has a short wide duct, and the other a long restrictive duct with several bends. They need not have the same flow. A pressure measurement can indicate excessive resistance, but it does not directly measure the air volume moved unless paired with a suitable calibrated method or performance relation.

Airflow at room registers can be measured with an appropriate flow hood or other validated technique. An anemometer reading at one point on a grille is not automatically representative of the entire opening: velocity varies over the face, and the grille itself disturbs the profile. A proper test may need area correction or a specific capture method. Duct leak tests, fan airflow measurements and register delivery measurements are distinct procedures answering distinct questions.

Now consider an imaginary three-room system. The measured delivery is 110 cubic feet per minute to the first room, 85 to the second, and 55 to the third. Those figures add to 250 cubic feet per minute at the room registers. That total may be smaller than airflow through the fan because some air leaks to an attic or crawlspace, or because other registers were not included. It cannot by itself prove a fan rated at 300 is underperforming. You would need the design flows, complete room inventory and operating condition.

## A disciplined test sequence

Record the filter model and condition, equipment settings, mode of operation, fan speed command, open dampers, outdoor conditions and which doors are closed. Measure the return and supply static pressures at correct equipment-specific locations. Compare with the maker's published limits or fan tables. Then measure delivered room flows and compare them with design expectations. Document duct leakage and return pathways separately when indicated.

The sequence matters because the system can change while you test. A smart thermostat may change fan speed. A heat pump may enter defrost. A variable-speed system may ramp after several minutes. A closed bedroom door can alter the room's pressure relative to the hall and affect the return path. Numbers from different modes cannot be meaningfully combined as if they described one state.

One room's poor comfort might be caused by inadequate supply flow, excessive heat loss, solar exposure, a blocked return path, thermostat location, zoning decisions or a duct leak. Start by comparing how the symptom changes with doors, filter state, blower mode and outside conditions. Do not 'balance' a room by randomly closing other registers before you know the design intent. Increasing local noise while starving the equipment of total flow can make the system worse.

## A useful diagnostic distinction

Suppose total external static pressure is high and all registers deliver less air than expected. System-wide restriction deserves attention: a clogged filter, restricted return, duct geometry, blocked coil or closed damper. Suppose total pressure is within the equipment's expected range but one room is consistently low. Local duct or balancing defects rise on the list. Suppose airflow is adequate but the room still overheats in afternoon sun. The dominant problem may lie in solar gain rather than in the supply duct.

The measurements are useful because they divide possibilities. Good commissioning records identify exact test locations, operating conditions, instrument method and comparison criteria. A repair should be followed by the same measurements, under comparable conditions. Static pressure shows the cost of forcing air through a network. Airflow shows what the network actually delivers. Comfort depends on those quantities and on the building around them.

## Non-spoken technical companion

**Equations and units.** For a defined test boundary, external static pressure across the air handler is the signed difference between downstream and upstream pressure taps; with negative return and positive supply gauge readings, the magnitudes add. An inch water gauge is approximately 249 pascals. Manufacturer placement diagrams and component inclusion rules determine which pressures to use.

**Illustrative data record.** Mode / blower speed setting / filter state / return tap / supply tap / equipment limit / room-hood readings / door positions / outdoor and indoor dry-bulb temperatures. Never compare a measured pressure against a generic universal maximum without confirming the equipment's approved range.

**Common interpretation errors.** A face-velocity spot reading is not duct volume flow; grille capture requires a validated technique. Register sum is not necessarily total fan flow. Duct pressurization leakage testing is not the same as operating airflow. A single static-pressure reading cannot establish a precise flow without an applicable fan curve and test method.

**References.** DOE Building America Solution Center, *Heat Pump Quality Installation and Commissioning*; ENERGY STAR HVAC Contractor guidance and airflow measurements; ASHRAE, *Testing, Adjusting, and Balancing* (applicable edition). Invasive pressure-tap location and energized-equipment work require correct equipment procedures and qualified assessment.
