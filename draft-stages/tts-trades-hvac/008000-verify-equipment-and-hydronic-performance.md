# Verify equipment and hydronic performance

## Equipment Sizing, Commissioning, and Performance Checks

A homeowner replaces an air conditioner with a newer unit that has an impressive efficiency rating. The house is still uncomfortable. Certain rooms remain hot, others become cold, the equipment cycles frequently, and the humidity stays high. Nothing in that story yet proves the unit is defective. The important distinction is between the rated performance of a product and the actual performance of a system after it has been installed in a building.

The building has a heating and cooling load that depends on weather, enclosure, internal gains, sunlight, ventilation, infiltration, and occupant behavior. The equipment has a capacity that changes with operating conditions. The distribution system must deliver that capacity to rooms. Controls decide when the equipment operates. Commissioning is the process of checking that all of those pieces work together according to a specified design, rather than merely observing that the fan turns on.

### First find the load

A load calculation estimates the rate of heating or cooling required at selected conditions. In the United States, residential designers commonly use ACCA Manual J or an accepted equivalent. Inputs include building geometry and orientation, envelope performance, glass areas, infiltration assumptions, and indoor and outdoor design conditions. A rule of thumb based only on square footage throws away much of the information that determines real demand.

Suppose a building has a calculated design heating load of twenty thousand British thermal units per hour at a particular outdoor temperature. That does not mean it requires a machine sold as 'twenty thousand Btu per hour' in every climate or at every operating point. A heat pump may deliver less capacity as outdoor temperature falls, and defrost and controls affect the system. Selection should use equipment performance data at the relevant conditions and account for the intended backup strategy. Likewise, cooling involves both sensible heat, which changes air temperature, and latent heat, which removes water vapor.

A very large unit is not automatically a better unit. Oversizing can produce short cycles, uneven comfort, and weak humidity control. An undersized unit may run continually and still fail to maintain the intended indoor condition during design weather. Variable-capacity equipment gives more flexibility, but does not erase distribution constraints or make flawed sizing irrelevant.

### Airflow is part of capacity

A fan must move air against the resistance of ducts, grilles, coils, and filters. This resistance is often summarized using external static pressure, but a pressure measurement alone is not a complete measurement of airflow. Fan curves and operating settings determine the relationship. A duct that is undersized or has multiple tight bends may starve a coil of air even though the compressor can move refrigerant perfectly well.

Low airflow can lead to poor temperature control, freezing in some cooling conditions, and ineffective delivery to distant rooms. Excessive airflow may reduce moisture removal or create noise and drafts. The correct target comes from the equipment manufacturer's performance requirements and the distribution design, not a universal airflow value copied from another system.

Duct leakage is an additional loss mechanism. Air supplied into an attic or drawn from a dusty crawlspace changes both efficiency and indoor-air conditions. A balanced-looking register layout does not prove that the network is sealed or that every room receives the design flow. Measurement at accessible points and comparison with specified values are essential.

### Refrigerant and controls complete the circuit

Refrigerant charge affects compressor and heat-exchanger performance. The commissioning process can include manufacturer-defined temperature, pressure, superheat, subcooling, or other tests as appropriate for the equipment and conditions. No single refrigerant-pressure reading establishes correct charge across all technologies or outdoor temperatures. Modern equipment may offer built-in diagnostic modes, but they still need interpretation in context.

Controls require similar care. A thermostat might be set correctly while its sensor is exposed to a hot wall or sunlight. An auxiliary heater might run due to staging settings rather than an equipment failure. A heat-pump defrost cycle can temporarily change the delivered air temperature without indicating a problem. A system diagnosis therefore records operating mode, outdoor and indoor conditions, measured airflow and temperatures, and whether the machine is at steady state.

### What does successful commissioning look like?

A useful handoff includes the design load and equipment selection rationale, manufacturer performance tables, duct and airflow verification, refrigerant commissioning records, control settings, and a demonstration of major modes. The homeowner should know where filters are, how to interpret ordinary changes such as defrost, what warning indicators mean, and which conditions should prompt service.

Imagine someone complaining that the main bedroom is too warm. Swapping the thermostat may have no effect if a long duct has insufficient flow or the room has a strong west-facing solar load. Ask which physical path is inadequate. Compare the calculated room load, measured delivery, duct loss, and control behavior. Correcting one component without testing the rest can move the symptom rather than resolve it.

An efficient piece of equipment is only a component of an efficient HVAC system. Commissioning makes that statement operational: we define expected performance, collect evidence under specified conditions, compare against the design, correct deviations, and leave a record that later technicians can use. Installation is not complete when the equipment starts. It is complete when its intended functions have been demonstrated.

## Combustion, Flues, and Carbon Monoxide

Performance verification must also respect interactions with combustion and the air paths that support it.

Imagine looking at a forced-air gas furnace as an exchange between two fluids that must not mix. On one side, fuel and combustion air react inside a burner and combustion chamber. On the other side, indoor return air passes across a heat exchanger and is delivered to rooms by a blower. Heat crosses the exchanger wall. Combustion products should follow their own vent path to the outdoors. This separation is central to the system's purpose.

If the building becomes warm, we know that energy is entering the living space. We do not yet know whether the combustion stream is contained, whether the vent works correctly, or whether the furnace is operating within its specified limits. Comfort, efficiency, and safety require different evidence.

### Burning fuel is a controlled chemical reaction

A gas burner mixes fuel and oxygen in proportions and patterns suited to its design. With favorable conditions, combustion releases heat primarily while producing carbon dioxide and water vapor. Departures from proper mixing or temperature can generate carbon monoxide and other products. Carbon monoxide interferes with the body's use of oxygen, and a person cannot detect it by smell. Appliances using oil, gas, wood, coal, or other fuels all need appropriate combustion and exhaust management.

A heating appliance must receive sufficient combustion air. Traditional room-air designs depend partly on surrounding indoor air, while sealed-combustion equipment can provide dedicated air intake and exhaust paths. Induced-draft or power-vented equipment adds fans and controls, but a fan does not make the vent immune to obstruction or incorrect routing. Each design has manufacturer-defined requirements for vent material, termination, condensate handling, clearances, and operational checks.

### The heat exchanger is a boundary

A furnace heat exchanger has to transfer substantial heat without allowing the furnace's combustion gases to mix with distributed household air. Its materials repeatedly expand and contract as the furnace cycles. Poor installation conditions, corrosion, overheating, age, and mechanical stress can damage a heat exchanger. A crack cannot reliably be judged from the fact that a furnace still heats well or from a single casual glance at the visible exterior.

A blocked filter can also matter for combustion-related integrity through a different route. With insufficient circulating airflow, the heat exchanger may operate at excessive temperature, leading to limit-control operation and added thermal stress. The correct fix is not to bypass a limit switch. A high-temperature limit is evidence that the machine has reached a protective threshold, and the airflow and heat-input conditions need assessment.

When inspecting a heating problem, separate the airside components from the fuel-and-flue components. A blower that runs does not prove flame establishment. A burner that ignites does not prove safe exhaust. A temperature rise across the furnace measures something about energy transfer to supply air, not the concentration of carbon monoxide inside the combustion stream.

### The sequence of operation makes faults interpretable

Many modern furnaces use a controlled start-up sequence: receive a heating call, establish required draft or combustion airflow, prove safety conditions, initiate ignition, prove flame, and then run the heating and circulation cycle. Actual sequences and sensors differ across equipment. A pressure switch, flame sensor, high-temperature limit, or rollout device must be understood as part of the protective control system rather than as an obstacle to making the appliance start.

Suppose a furnace repeatedly tries to ignite, then stops. Several mechanisms are possible: an ignition problem, a flame-proving failure, an obstruction affecting draft, a fuel-supply problem, or another protective condition. The sound of one attempted start does not identify which. The control's recorded error information and qualified measurements matter. Defeating or jumpering a safety switch to force continued operation destroys the diagnostic and protective function.

A vent can also be wrong while the burner fires. Natural draft depends on the buoyancy of warm gases, the geometry and condition of the vent, and the pressures surrounding the appliance. Other exhaust devices in the building can lower indoor pressure and disrupt it. Condensing appliances have different vent-gas temperatures and condensate requirements. A vent system acceptable for one furnace technology cannot automatically be reused with another.

### Carbon monoxide is not a maintenance indicator to wait for

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and U.S. Fire Administration emphasize correctly installed and maintained fuel-burning equipment and working carbon monoxide alarms. A CO alarm is a warning device, not a measurement system for demonstrating that an appliance is safe. Low concentrations, intermittent events, or a malfunctioning alarm can complicate detection. The absence of an alarm is not proof that all combustion and venting conditions are correct.

Symptoms of possible exposure, an activated CO alarm, or signs of an exhaust failure call for immediate protective action and emergency guidance, not continued operation to gather better data. A qualified technician can evaluate combustion, venting, and building-pressure interactions using appropriate instruments and prescribed procedures. The objective is not merely to get the flame to stay lit; it is to confirm that the appliance operates correctly as an integrated system.

### Think in independent failure paths

Consider three different service calls. In the first, the house is cold because a circulation blower has failed; the control system may prevent or terminate heat production. In the second, the blower works but a vent obstruction prevents normal draft, so the machine shuts down. In the third, the appliance appears to heat normally, but a pressure interaction raises the risk of exhaust spillage. The first may look like an energy-delivery problem, the second like a control-sequence problem, and the third may have no obvious comfort symptom at all.

That is why HVAC diagnosis should draw a block diagram of fuel, combustion air, venting, heat transfer, circulation air, and controls. It should list what evidence establishes each function separately. A complete repair restores all relevant functions and verifies them, rather than making a single symptom disappear.

## Hydronic Heating: Pumps, Loops, Radiators, and Balancing

In a house heated by hot-water radiators, one room is too warm and another never reaches its set temperature. The boiler is running. The supply pipe feels hot. Does that mean the boiler is too small? Not necessarily. The problem may lie in how heat is being moved from the heat source to the rooms.

Hydronic heating uses liquid, usually water or a treated water mixture, as a transport medium. A heat source warms the water. A circulator drives it through pipes and heat emitters. The water releases energy to indoor air and surfaces, then returns cooler to be reheated. Radiators, baseboards, and embedded radiant floors are different ways of delivering that energy. Their operating temperatures and response times may differ markedly.

### Water carries energy by changing temperature

A hydronic loop transfers useful heat at a rate controlled by mass flow and temperature difference. In simplified steady operation, heat transfer equals mass flow times the water's specific heat capacity times the temperature drop through the emitters. Increase flow while preserving the same temperature difference, and more heat would be transported; in a real system, the temperature difference itself usually changes with flow and room conditions. The point is to think in terms of water moving energy, not merely whether the water feels hot.

Imagine two identical rooms with identical radiators, except that one loop receives little flow. The inlet to that radiator may be hot while much of its surface stays relatively cool. A large temperature difference across a radiator can indicate low flow, but it also depends on load and emitter design. Conversely, a very small supply-return temperature difference does not automatically mean the system is healthy; there could be high flow with inadequate heat extraction or limited temperature difference at the heat source.

### The circulator works against a hydraulic system

A circulator supplies pressure difference to drive flow through the resistance of pipes, elbows, valves, heat exchangers, and emitters. In a closed, filled loop, the pump does not ordinarily have to continuously lift the full building-height column of water; static head largely balances around the loop. It must overcome frictional and component pressure losses and sustain stable operation. In an open or improperly filled system, or one with air and unsuitable pressure, the picture changes.

Pumps have performance curves. The operating point emerges from the pump curve and the system resistance curve. A larger pump is not automatically better: unnecessary high flow can create noise, erosion concerns, control instability, and electrical consumption. Too little flow can prevent heat delivery or cause temperature limits to be reached. Pump and system selection requires actual design data.

### Parallel branches and balancing

Suppose a manifold supplies three radiator branches. If one branch is short and unobstructed while another is long with many fittings, the branches do not necessarily receive equal flow. Flow tends to favor lower-resistance paths. Balancing valves add controlled resistance where needed so design flow can be distributed among emitters. Closing every easy branch at random may appear to warm a cold room but can cause other problems. Commissioning is based on the intended heat load, emitter capacity, pipe layout, and available measurements.

Zone valves and individual circulators serve different arrangements. A zone valve may open or close one branch while a common pump serves several branches; multiple pumps may serve circuits with different characteristics. Controls need to handle what happens when only one zone calls, when all zones close, and when a heat source requires minimum flow. There are hydraulic design patterns such as bypasses, variable-speed circulators, or hydraulic separation that address these issues, but they are not interchangeable parts chosen by guesswork.

### Pressure, air, and expansion matter

As water heats, it expands. A closed hydronic system uses an appropriate expansion device and pressure-control components so temperature changes do not produce uncontrolled pressure rise. A gauge reading should be interpreted in the context of the specific boiler and system, not compared blindly with somebody else's home. Air can collect at high points or in poorly vented emitters, interrupting flow, making noise, or reducing effective heat transfer. Air elimination, fill pressure, and circulation interact.

A technician facing a cold radiator might investigate whether its supply and return connections are functioning, whether a valve is closed, whether air is present, whether the pump is moving water, or whether another branch is taking most of the flow. The evidence should drive the next measurement. A hot boiler and a cold room do not by themselves identify the failed component.

### Radiant systems have different dynamics

An embedded floor has thermal mass. It may take longer to change room temperature than a finned baseboard, and its surface temperature must be controlled within the assembly's design limits. Mixing valves and outdoor-reset controls are ways to manage water temperature when a heat source and emitter require different operating conditions. A boiler may use higher water temperatures than a low-temperature radiant circuit; supplying one circuit directly from the other without understanding the design can create comfort or material problems.

Diagnostic reasoning is most effective when it separates heat generation, heat transport, and heat release. Is the source delivering energy? Is the water flowing through the intended paths? Are the emitters releasing enough heat to match each room's loss? Then examine controls, measurements, and system states that distinguish these stages.

Hydronic heat is not complicated because the water has mysterious properties. It is complicated because heat transfer, pump behavior, branch resistance, expansion, and controls are coupled. Once those parts are separated conceptually, room-by-room differences become tractable engineering questions rather than invitations to replace the boiler.

## Heating System Commissioning: Supply, Controls, and Documentation

A new heating system warms the thermostat to its setpoint. Is it commissioned? Not necessarily. The thermostat reports conditions at one location. The system might be delivering too little heat to distant rooms, circulating water inefficiently, short cycling, or operating with unsafe combustion conditions. Installation establishes that equipment is connected. Commissioning establishes, with recorded evidence, that it works as intended in the building where it was installed.

Commissioning is often confused with startup. Startup turns equipment on, checks that obvious faults are absent, and verifies basic operation. Commissioning relates observed performance to specified design intent: capacity, airflow or water flow, temperature differences, control sequences, and safety devices. It also produces the information that a future technician will need. A system can appear comfortable on a mild day and perform poorly under winter design conditions.

### Define the design before testing

A heating load is the rate at which a building loses heat under specified indoor and outdoor conditions. Heat moves through the enclosure and is carried away by ventilation and infiltration. If equipment is chosen without an estimate of those losses, the installation may be oversized, undersized, or mismatched to distribution capacity. An oversized burner may hit the room setpoint rapidly and then stop, but frequent starts and stops are not automatically evidence of good heating.

The commissioning plan should name the intended temperature conditions, equipment type, distribution system, and control sequence. With forced air, airflow through the heat exchanger or coil matters. With hydronic heating, water flow, supply and return temperature, pressure, air removal, and terminal-unit performance matter. The targets come from design calculations and manufacturer documentation, not a universal temperature difference copied from another home.

### Heat carried by air or water

Consider a hydronic loop. Warm water leaves the heat source, gives up energy to radiators or tubing, and returns cooler. The rate of heat transfer depends on both flow and temperature drop. A very high temperature difference can indicate strong heat delivery at the intended flow, but it can also indicate inadequate flow in a poorly balanced loop. A small difference may mean a room is already warm, an oversized flow rate, or an exchanger that is not transferring expected heat. Temperature alone is ambiguous.

The same principle holds for air. Supply temperature does not reveal heat delivery unless airflow is known. A duct may deliver hot air at the register yet move too little of it to satisfy the room load. Closing registers to force more air elsewhere can change system static pressure and produce consequences at the fan or other branches. We want measurements of both transport quantity and temperature, followed by reasoning about the full network.

Balancing is the process of distributing flow where needed. It begins with a plan for target room loads and branch flows, not with endlessly adjusting dampers until someone says the house feels better. A distant radiator may be cool because the pump lacks useful head, air is trapped, a valve is closed, or the terminal has little temperature difference from the room. These mechanisms require different corrections.

### Controls are a sequence, not a box

A modern heating system includes sensors and decisions: thermostat demand, operating mode, boiler or compressor start conditions, circulation commands, safety limits, and minimum run times. A heat pump in cold weather may temporarily enter defrost. A boiler may prioritize domestic hot water before space heating. Two zones may call at once and change the available flow. Commissioning should observe meaningful combinations of these states, not only one full-heat command.

A simple functional test can start from a satisfied thermostat, raise the call for heat, record the sequence in which equipment and circulators respond, and then observe how the system ends the call. For a zoned system, repeat with each zone and appropriate combinations. Confirm that a loss of sensor signal or a limit trip produces the intended documented response. Safety devices are part of the system's design; do not defeat them to make equipment run.

Combustion appliances demand special treatment. Flue integrity, adequate combustion air, venting, gas connections, and carbon-monoxide risk cannot be inferred from warm registers or a steady flame. The commissioning plan must include the manufacturer's required combustion and safety checks performed with suitable instruments and qualified practice. Ventilation and pressure interactions with the building are relevant because exhaust fans and tight envelopes can affect some installations.

### Record baseline performance

A useful commissioning record names the make and model, equipment settings, filter or strainer conditions, measured temperatures, pressures, electrical readings where applicable, zone behavior, and relevant control parameters. Include the date and outdoor conditions; a heating system's readings change with the weather and load. Record what could not be tested in the season of installation and how it will be evaluated later.

Suppose a complaint arises months afterward: the north bedroom is cold. The technician can compare current register flow, supply temperature, damper position, and thermostat behavior with the commissioning baseline. Without those numbers, diagnosis starts from memory and guesswork. Documentation converts a one-time installation into a maintainable system.

Commissioning is not a claim that every room will always have identical temperature. Solar gains, occupancy, open doors, and weather create legitimate variations. It is a demonstration that the system's controls, distribution, and heat source respond coherently under the conditions examined. A warm house is the outcome people notice. Commissioning is how we learn why it is warm, whether the system is performing as intended, and what evidence remains when it no longer does.

## Refrigerant-System Measurements and Diagnostic Boundaries

Refrigerant measurements are another form of evidence whose meaning depends on the equipment state and on the limits of the measurement.

A room that never quite cools down is often blamed on low refrigerant. That diagnosis may be correct, but room temperature alone does not tell us the charge in a sealed refrigeration system. Airflow may be restricted. The outdoor coil may be dirty. A control may be cycling incorrectly. The building load may exceed what the system can deliver. A refrigeration diagnosis becomes reliable only when we trace heat through the complete machine, including the air or water that exchanges heat with the refrigerant.

A heat pump is a thermal transport machine. Its compressor raises refrigerant pressure and temperature. A heat exchanger rejects heat as refrigerant condenses; an expansion device creates a pressure drop; and another heat exchanger absorbs heat as refrigerant evaporates. A reversing valve changes the flow arrangement in many heating-and-cooling systems. Those names identify functions, not a universal layout or operating pressure. Refrigerant properties, metering design, compressor control, and outdoor weather all change the expected measurements.

### Three temperature differences

Start with a cooling system. Room air returns to the indoor unit warmer than the supply air that leaves it. The temperature difference can help assess sensible cooling, but it is only one piece of evidence. If airflow is too low, the supply air may become very cold while the total useful heat delivered to rooms is inadequate. If humidity removal is high, some cooling capacity goes into condensing water vapor, so the dry-bulb temperature difference alone understates total cooling. A well-designed measurement therefore records airflow and moisture conditions as well as temperatures.

The outdoor heat exchanger is also exchanging energy with moving air. In cooling mode it must reject indoor heat plus work supplied to the compressor. A hot condenser coil may be consistent with healthy operation under load, but an abnormally high condensing temperature can also indicate poor airflow, high outdoor temperature, or a refrigeration fault. The room-side and outdoor-side measurements should make physical sense together.

The third difference is between the refrigerant's saturation temperature at a measured pressure and the actual pipe temperature at the corresponding point. That comparison leads to superheat and subcooling. These are not decorative numbers on a gauge set: they describe how far the refrigerant's actual state is from a saturation boundary.

### Superheat and subcooling are state descriptions

At a known pressure, a pure refrigerant has a corresponding saturation temperature, subject to the relevant thermodynamic conventions. If vapor leaving an evaporator is warmer than its saturation temperature at that pressure, that temperature excess is superheat. If liquid leaving a condenser is cooler than the saturation temperature at its pressure, that difference is subcooling. For refrigerant blends with temperature glide, bubble-point and dew-point conventions matter; using the wrong side of a chart can create a misleading result.

A large superheat does not prove that refrigerant is missing. It can result from inadequate evaporator feed, restricted flow, control behavior, or other conditions. Low superheat can be associated with risks of liquid reaching equipment intended to handle vapor, but the acceptable value depends on system design and measurement location. Subcooling likewise responds to charge and system configuration, but cannot diagnose charge by itself across all designs.

The correct target is not 'the number my neighbor saw on a similar unit.' It comes from the manufacturer's service procedure, refrigerant data, specific outdoor and indoor conditions, operating mode, and stable equipment state. In variable-speed equipment, controls may deliberately shift pressures and temperatures as operating demand changes. Capturing a reading during a transition and interpreting it as steady-state evidence is a basic diagnostic error.

### Establish air-side and electrical evidence first

Before attributing a fault to the sealed circuit, examine the easier parts of the energy path. Is the filter loading excessive? Are the return and supply paths unobstructed? Is the indoor fan actually operating at the commanded speed? Are outdoor heat-exchanger fins blocked by dirt, vegetation, or ice? Are sensors measuring plausible temperatures? Is the thermostat or defrost controller commanding the expected mode?

A compressor may be starting and stopping because of a control or protective input. A weak capacitor, poor connection, faulty fan, or voltage problem can change operating behavior without a refrigerant leak. The goal is not to assume an electrical diagnosis either, but to collect enough observations to locate the problem at a system boundary. A refrigerant reading that seems odd can be a symptom of inadequate air or water flow rather than the origin of the defect.

Condensation is another useful observation. In cooling mode, water collects when indoor coil surfaces are below the air's dew point. A clogged drain can create a water problem even if cooling is otherwise normal. Frost forming on an indoor coil can be associated with poor airflow, inappropriate refrigerant behavior, or control faults. It is not an instruction to add refrigerant.

### Test hypotheses with measurements under known conditions

Suppose a system cools poorly on hot afternoons. Record indoor return temperature and humidity, supply temperature, outdoor temperature, operating mode, fan state, and whether the system has settled into that mode. If the airflow is weak, inspect filters, ducts, and fan behavior before inferring anything from refrigerant conditions. If airflow is normal but the equipment is cycling, record which control or protective device initiates the cycle. If the building gains solar heat rapidly, the load may be the missing part of the explanation. These distinctions make a service conversation much more productive.

Working on a refrigerant circuit has an additional boundary. In the United States, EPA Section 608 rules generally require appropriate technician certification for activities that may release refrigerant. The EPA explicitly identifies attaching and detaching pressure-gauge hoses as technician activities under the rule. Refrigerants also present equipment-specific pressure, thermal, environmental, and sometimes flammability concerns. Knowing what superheat means does not authorize opening the system or improvising pressure readings. A homeowner can record observable air-side evidence and the nameplate data; qualified service work should follow the applicable rules and manufacturer procedures.

### A diagnostic conclusion is a chain, not a single number

A strong report states the operating mode, stabilized conditions, measured air-side performance, relevant system control states, the refrigerant used, observed pressures and temperatures if legitimately measured, and how the findings compare with equipment-specific requirements. It identifies competing causes and the check that excluded each one. After repair, it demonstrates performance under comparable load and records the result.

The refrigeration cycle is elegant precisely because energy and matter must agree. A convincing diagnosis respects that balance. It connects room conditions, heat-exchanger flow, compressor work, control state, and refrigerant thermodynamics instead of treating one gauge value as a verdict.

**Track:** HVAC and Refrigeration. **Background:** heating and cooling loads, airflow, humidity, and controls. **Purpose:** distinguish a correctly selected machine from a correctly performing installed system.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Simplified selection thought experiment

A calculated heating load of `20,000 Btu/h` at a chosen outdoor design temperature is a **requirement**, not a model number. If manufacturer data give `15,000 Btu/h` at that temperature, there is a `5,000 Btu/h` shortfall to address through equipment selection, envelope work, or a designed supplemental heat strategy. Do not use a nominal capacity measured under different conditions as an equivalent value.

##### Evidence table

| Design question | Record |
|---|---|
| Required heating/cooling output | Building/room load calculation and design conditions |
| Equipment can meet requirement | Capacity and efficiency tables at specified conditions |
| Air reaches rooms | Fan settings, duct layout, measured static pressure/airflow as applicable |
| Refrigeration circuit correct | OEM-defined commissioning data under valid test conditions |
| Control behavior correct | Mode/stage transition and sensor observations |

**Review boundary:** Refrigerant handling, combustion safety, electrical connections, and system commissioning are equipment-specific regulated technical work. The lecture teaches selection and evidence reasoning, not procedures for accessing charged circuits.

### Combustion, Flues, and Carbon Monoxide

**Track:** HVAC and Refrigeration. **Background:** airflow, heat exchangers, ventilation, and control sequences. **Purpose:** trace combustion energy and exhaust through a heating system and distinguish heat-delivery faults from combustion-safety faults.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Six interfaces of a combustion heating system

| Interface | Normal function | Failure evidence may involve |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel → burner | Controlled input and mixing | Supply, pressure, ignition, flame proving |
| Air → combustion chamber | Oxygen and required airflow | Intake restriction, draft fault, pressure interaction |
| Combustion gas → vent | Continuous exhaust route | Obstruction, leakage, wrong materials, condensate |
| Heat exchanger | Heat crosses; streams stay separated | Corrosion, cracks, overheating |
| Return air → blower → rooms | Appropriate circulation and temperature rise | Filter, blower, duct resistance |
| Controls and limits | Sequence and protect operation | Fault codes, protective lockouts, limit trips |

##### Safety and scope

Do not bypass flame, draft, pressure, rollout, or temperature-protection devices. CO alarm placement and replacement must follow applicable local requirements and manufacturer instructions. Apparent flame color, absence of smell, or warm supply air do not certify combustion safety. Professional combustion analysis and appliance-specific commissioning are required for consequential decisions.

### Heating System Commissioning: Supply, Controls, and Documentation

### 013000 — Heating System Commissioning: Supply, Controls, and Documentation

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Heat-transfer relationship

For a liquid loop, `Q_dot = m_dot × c_p × (T_supply - T_return)`, where flow is mass per time and `c_p` is specific heat. A representative water-flow relationship in common US hydronics units is `BTU/h ≈ 500 × GPM × ΔT(°F)` near typical water properties; glycol solutions require different properties. This expression does **not** independently diagnose poor flow without design targets.

##### Commissioning record fields

Design load and distribution plan; manufacturer setpoints; filter/strainer status; external static pressure and flow (air), or pump head/flow and air purge status (hydronic); supply/return temperatures; zones and functional states; control response; safety test records; weather at test; unresolved seasonal tests.

**Editorial boundary:** Combustion testing, refrigerant adjustment, electrical checks, and pressure-vessel safety require qualified procedures. Manufacturer and adopted code requirements supersede conceptual examples.

### Refrigerant-System Measurements and Diagnostic Boundaries

### 014000 — Refrigerant-System Measurements and Diagnostic Boundaries

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### State comparisons

- Superheat (for relevant vapor state): `T_actual_vapor − T_dew_at_pressure`, with blend and location conventions checked.
- Subcooling (for relevant liquid state): `T_bubble_at_pressure − T_actual_liquid` where the bubble-point convention applies.
- Sensible air-side rate: approximately `Q_sensible = m_dot_air × c_p_air × (T_return − T_supply)`; this omits latent cooling and requires independently justified airflow.
- Cooling-mode balance: outdoor heat rejection = indoor heat absorption + compressor/electrical work, subject to equipment boundaries and losses.

##### Decision worksheet

Record model and refrigerant, setpoint, mode, outdoor temperature, return/supply humidity and temperature, airflow evidence, outdoor coil condition, condensate drainage, fault codes, control phase, and measured operating values by an appropriately qualified technician. Do not infer charge from a single symptom.

**Review boundary:** Refrigerant circuit access, recovery, recharging, and gauge connections are subject to applicable law and equipment-specific service requirements. U.S. EPA Section 608 certification scope is documented in the original source record; do not generalize across jurisdictions or refrigerant classes.
