# 019000 — Commissioning Balanced Ventilation and Finding Fan-Flow Imbalance

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A balanced ventilation system can be installed, powered on, and apparently operating while the building receives much less fresh air than the owner expects. One fan may be working against a blocked outdoor grille. Another may be moving air through a cleaner, shorter duct. The control panel still says the fans are on. To know whether the system performs its job, we must measure the air it actually moves.

An energy-recovery ventilator, or ERV, transfers some heat and often some moisture between outgoing exhaust air and incoming outdoor air through its core, without intentionally mixing the two air streams. A heat-recovery ventilator, or HRV, primarily transfers sensible heat. Both have a supply path and an exhaust path. Their nominal flow settings are starting points. Delivered airflow depends on fan performance, filter loading, duct resistance, installation details and operating mode.

## What does 'balanced' describe?

In its simplest sense, balanced mechanical ventilation delivers approximately as much outdoor air to the building as it exhausts through the device over a chosen operating condition. If the supply measures 80 cubic feet per minute and exhaust measures 80, the device is balanced at that test point. If supply measures 50 and exhaust 90, the unit has a 40-cubic-foot-per-minute exhaust excess at that condition.

That arithmetic does not mean the whole house has exactly a 40-cubic-foot-per-minute infiltration flow at every moment. Other fans, fireplaces, wind, stack effect and duct leakage affect pressure and exchange. Nor does equal flow automatically guarantee that each occupied room receives sufficient outside air. The required total flow and distribution must come from the building's ventilation design and applicable standard, not a convenient round number.

Airflow is often inferred incorrectly from noise. A loud supply fan may be laboring at high static pressure while delivering less air. The same fan can sound different when a filter loads. A nominal label such as '100 CFM' may refer to performance at specified test conditions rather than the delivered flow of an installed duct system.

## Separate air paths before reaching for controls

Trace the outdoor-air intake from its weather hood to its filter and heat-exchange core. Follow the supply through its fan and ducts into the building. Now follow stale indoor air through the extract grilles, exhaust fan, core, and outdoor discharge. At each step, note where a blockage, leak, damper, reversed connection or pressure loss could occur. A duct that leaks into an attic may move significant air at the fan but not deliver all of it to the intended rooms.

Outdoor intakes can be obstructed by debris, snow or ice. Exhaust hoods may be blocked or poorly located. Filters and heat exchangers can accumulate material, and defrost modes can temporarily change the normal flows. A clean-looking filter does not prove low resistance, and an unfamiliar fan speed does not prove a controls fault. The first step is to identify the actual operating mode and the physical state of the paths.

Duct layout matters. A long run with many tight bends offers more resistance than a shorter, properly sized run, although the exact loss depends on diameter, roughness and flow. Replacing a duct with a smaller one because it is easier to route can move the fan to a very different point on its pressure-flow curve. Simply increasing fan speed might compensate at a cost in noise and energy, or may fail to reach the needed flow at all.

## Commissioning is a measurement sequence

Before measuring, establish what design flow the system is supposed to provide in its normal modes, where the terminals are, and which filters and dampers should be in place. Confirm outdoor hoods are clear and that the system is operating in the specified mode. Then measure the flow with suitable, calibrated equipment at the designated test points or terminals using a method compatible with the installation. For some systems the manufacturer provides calibrated pressure taps or diagnostic procedures; for others a professional commissioning method uses flow hoods or traverse measurements. Measurement location and instrument uncertainty matter.

Imagine an installation where measured supply is 70 and exhaust is 105 cubic feet per minute. Closing an exhaust damper until the displays say 'equal' is not adequate if the fan is in an unexpected high-speed boost mode, the supply filter is blocked, or the controls require a different commissioning sequence. First find the cause of the mismatch. Then adjust approved balancing dampers or fan settings according to the equipment instructions, and remeasure both sides at the same operating condition.

Test more than one mode if the system has continuous background ventilation and boost. Verify control response, condensate drainage where provided, frost protection, filter access, and outdoor openings. Ask how the device behaves when a kitchen exhaust hood or dryer runs. A balanced ERV by itself cannot cancel every other exhaust appliance in the house.

## Indoor conditions reveal a second layer

After airflow commissioning, indoor humidity, odors, comfort and pressure observations help reveal what the numbers do not. High indoor humidity might mean insufficient ventilation, but it can also reflect a wet basement, excessive indoor moisture production, poor heating, or an unsealed envelope. Very dry winter air can result from high outdoor-air exchange, but the correct response is not automatically to throttle ventilation below design. The system must maintain its indoor-air-quality purpose.

A practical record includes the filter type and condition, device mode, supply/exhaust flow, pressure or fan setting as applicable, outdoor conditions, noise, control changes and maintenance dates. Repeating measurements after a filter change or seasonal service exposes drift. A device that was balanced when clean can become unbalanced as filters load at different rates.

Ventilation commissioning teaches an essential distinction: operating is not the same as performing. A pair of spinning fans is evidence of motion. Measured supply and exhaust flows under documented conditions are evidence of ventilation performance. The goal is not symmetry for its own sake; it is reliable delivery of the designed air exchange with the expected pressure, energy and comfort consequences.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Worked comparison.** In a fixed operating mode, measured supply of 70 CFM and extract of 105 CFM give a 35 CFM device imbalance toward exhaust. That is not a measurement of whole-building infiltration; other fans and uncontrolled leakage affect mass balance.

**Commissioning record.** Equipment/model, design outdoor-air and exhaust rates, chosen operating mode, filter type and condition, outdoor-hood condition, fan speed, duct damper positions, measured terminal/device flows, instrument and uncertainty, outdoor temperature, defrost state, condensate and frost-system function, dates, and inspector.

**Boundaries.** Consult the manufacturer and locally adopted ventilation standards for required rates and balancing tolerances. Do not obstruct required ventilation to improve one symptom, and do not guess at combustion appliance interactions. Heat exchanger bypass, recovery efficiency, balanced flow and distribution are separate performance quantities.

**References.** DOE Energy Saver, *Energy Recovery Ventilation Systems* and *Whole-House Ventilation*; ENERGY STAR, *HVAC Quality Installation*; applicable local ventilation program commissioning documents.
