# Fubini and Tonelli: When Two Integrals May Trade Places

Suppose a painter applies a varying thickness of paint across a rectangular wall. You could compute the total volume by adding paint along every horizontal line and then adding the lines. Or you could work along vertical lines first. The physical quantity cannot depend on which direction you choose to organize the calculation. Yet in analysis, the statement that two repeated integrations give the same result is a theorem, not a permission slip.

The issue becomes especially important when the integrand is unbounded, the region is infinite, or positive and negative contributions compete. If you switch the order of summation in a conditionally convergent series, you may change the answer. Double integration has an analogous danger: cancellation can mask an infinite amount of absolute mass. The theorems of Tonelli and Fubini explain the safe cases and expose the dividing line.

To see that line, we need to understand what a product measure is, why nonnegative functions are special, and why an integrable *absolute value* is the correct requirement for signed functions. We will also calculate one concrete example where both repeated integrals exist but give opposite answers.

## Building area from two notions of length

Take two measure spaces. The first describes possible values of an x coordinate, and the second describes possible values of a y coordinate. A pair of coordinates belongs to their Cartesian product. We want a measure of subsets of that product whose value on a rectangle is the product of the measures of its two sides.

For example, a horizontal interval of length two crossed with a vertical interval of length three forms a rectangle of area six. That is familiar geometry. The general construction treats a measurable rectangle as the product of two measurable sets, even when neither space resembles a line. The measure of the rectangle is the product of the two measures, with the usual conventions for nonnegative extended values.

There is a small caveat at infinity. The product of zero and infinity is assigned the measure-theoretic value zero for rectangles having a null side. This is consistent with the idea that a null set crossed with an enormous space should remain null in the product, under the standard product-measure construction for sigma-finite spaces. It avoids treating a zero-area strip as an undefined shape.

The measurable sets of the product are generated by measurable rectangles. In the most familiar setting, Lebesgue measure on a Euclidean plane is the completion of the product of one-dimensional Lebesgue measures. Completion matters: a subset of a product-null set can be measurable in the completed plane even when its fibers behave badly at exceptional coordinates. The clean abstract theorems are stated on the product sigma-algebra; their completed-space versions are understood with almost-everywhere qualifications.

Why does the phrase *sigma-finite* appear? A measure space is sigma-finite if its entire domain can be covered by countably many measurable sets of finite measure. The real line has infinite total length, but it is sigma-finite because countably many finite intervals cover it. Under sigma-finiteness, the product measure exists in the standard construction and is uniquely specified by its values on measurable rectangles. Without such hypotheses, product-measure uniqueness and familiar section formulas require more care.

## First calculate a triangular region

On the unit square, let the density be one below the diagonal and zero above it. More precisely, the paint is present at a point when its y coordinate is between zero and its x coordinate. The total painted area is a half.

Let's compute it in two ways. Fix x first. The allowed y values run from zero to x, so their length is x. Add these vertical lengths as x moves from zero to one. You obtain the integral of x, which is one half.

Now fix y first. The allowed x values run from y to one, so their length is one minus y. Add these horizontal lengths and again get one half. Both methods describe the same region. Neither needed a geometrically sophisticated argument; the repeated integrals arose naturally from counting each vertical or horizontal slice.

This example also points toward a proof strategy. If the function is the indicator of a measurable rectangle, the two orders agree because each order gives the product of two side measures. The result extends to a finite nonnegative sum of rectangle indicators. Then, if we can approximate a general nonnegative measurable function from below by such simple building blocks and pass to the limit, we should be able to extend the equality to that function.

There are technical steps in proving that a product-measurable function has measurable sections and that the iterated section integral is measurable. Those steps use a monotone-class argument or an equivalent generating-class theorem. Once measurability is secured, monotone convergence supplies the extension from elementary functions to general nonnegative ones. The idea is simple, but the measurable-class construction is not decorative: it ensures we are allowed to integrate the slices in the first place.

## Tonelli: nonnegativity allows infinity

Tonelli's theorem states that, for a nonnegative product-measurable function on the product of sigma-finite measure spaces, integrating over the product gives either repeated integral, no matter which variable goes first. The shared value may be infinite.

That final allowance is important. A nonnegative function cannot hide a positive mass behind an equally large negative mass. When all contributions are nonnegative, extending the region or adding terms can only increase the integral. This is exactly the setting where monotone convergence excels.

Take a density on the positive quadrant formed by multiplying e to the minus x by e to the minus y. Each factor has integral one on the positive half-line. Integrate first in y: you get e to the minus x. Integrate that in x and obtain one. Reverse the order and the same calculation works. This example is pleasant because we can do the arithmetic explicitly, but Tonelli applies even if the integrals cannot be evaluated by elementary antiderivatives.

Consider instead the function one everywhere on the positive quadrant. Each inner integral is infinite, and the outer integral remains infinite. Tonelli still says the two orders agree: both answer infinity. The theorem is not a trick for turning every integral into a finite number. It is a logically sound rule for exchanging the order even when the correct answer is infinite.

The practical use is often to show that an object is integrable. For a nonnegative function, you may compute the repeated integral in whichever direction is easier. If it comes out finite, you know the product integral is finite. If you were studying an arbitrary signed function, however, you would have to apply this calculation to its absolute value before invoking the next theorem.

## Fubini: cancellation requires absolute integrability

Fubini's theorem handles real or complex functions, including functions with changing signs. Its central hypothesis is that the absolute value is integrable on the product. In other words, the total unsigned mass must be finite.

With that assumption, both the positive and negative parts of a real function are separately integrable. Tonelli applies to each. Subtract their finite integrals, and the two iterated signed integrals agree with the integral over the product. For a complex function, do the same with its real and imaginary parts. The result is Fubini's theorem.

There is an important almost-everywhere qualification. For an integrable function, the individual sections are integrable for almost every fixed x, and likewise for almost every fixed y. One may define the exceptional sections arbitrarily when forming the outer integral, because they occupy null sets in the outer variable. Demanding that every single section be integrable would be stronger than the theorem promises and is not needed.

Fubini is therefore not the statement that any two formally written repeated integrals commute. It is the statement that if the absolute integral is finite, then the two repeated integrals exist in the measure-theoretic sense almost everywhere and have the same finite value.

This theorem has a useful contrapositive diagnostic. If you manage to compute two genuinely different repeated integrals for one measurable function, something in the hypotheses must fail. In the examples of interest, it is usually absolute integrability.

## A function with two different answers

Here is an example that deserves to be remembered. Work on the open unit square, with coordinates x and y strictly between zero and one. Define a function whose numerator is x squared minus y squared and whose denominator is the square of x squared plus y squared. The expression is positive in one part of the square and negative in the other. Near the origin it is roughly of size one over the square of the distance, with a sign that depends on direction.

Fix an x greater than zero and integrate the function with respect to y from zero to one. A direct derivative calculation shows that its antiderivative, as a function of y, is y divided by x squared plus y squared. Evaluate at the endpoints. You get one divided by one plus x squared. Integrating this from zero to one gives pi over four.

Now fix y and integrate first with respect to x. The antiderivative is minus x divided by x squared plus y squared. Evaluating from zero to one gives minus one over one plus y squared. Its integral from zero to one is minus pi over four.

The first order yields positive pi over four; the second yields negative pi over four. Both inner integrals and both outer integrals are perfectly ordinary finite one-variable integrals for their respective positive outer coordinates. If desired, we can assign the function value zero on the coordinate axes so there is no question about exceptional boundary slices. The disagreement does not disappear.

What failed? Near the origin, the integral of the absolute value of this function diverges. Its magnitude behaves like an angular factor divided by the square of the radius, while planar area in polar coordinates contributes a factor of the radius. Over angular regions where the numerator is not almost zero, the absolute integral therefore contains the divergent integral of one over radius near zero.

Signed contributions from different parts of the domain cancel differently when we organize the computation in different orders. Absolute integrability would prevent precisely this ambiguity. There is no contradiction with Fubini, because its crucial hypothesis was absent.

## Sum, integral, expectation

The same two theorems clarify infinite sums. Counting measure on the natural numbers turns integration into summation. If a family of terms is nonnegative, Tonelli says that a double series can be summed in either order, even if both answers are infinite. If the sum of absolute values is finite, Fubini says we can change the order of a signed or complex double series.

Probability offers another interpretation. A joint distribution describes the combined behavior of two random variables. When a nonnegative quantity depends on both variables, Tonelli justifies adding its contributions one variable at a time. For an integrable quantity that can take both signs, Fubini justifies the same exchange. Independence can simplify the resulting formulas, but independence is not required by either theorem.

The theorems also connect to convolution, where a function is defined by integrating a shifted product of two functions. Interchanging a convolution integral with another integral is common in harmonic analysis and partial differential equations. When done correctly, the proof always identifies a nonnegative or absolutely integrable joint integrand and checks the applicable hypotheses rather than relying on a picture of two integrals as interchangeable symbols.

## Choosing the correct theorem

A useful order of operations is this. First identify the two measure spaces and their product. If the function is nonnegative, consider Tonelli, including the possibility that the result is infinite. If the function changes sign or is complex, calculate or estimate the repeated integral of its *absolute value*. If that is finite, Fubini gives the exchange. If neither condition holds, stop. You might still be able to compute a particular iterated integral, but you cannot conclude that reversing the order preserves it.

What began as a question about painting a wall becomes a principle about the order in which infinite information is aggregated. Finite sums can be rearranged freely. Infinite sums and integrals need control. Nonnegativity is one route to that control. Absolute integrability is the other. The distinction between them will return when we study spaces of functions whose absolute powers have finite integrals.

### Product measures and sections

Let \((X,\Sigma,\mu)\), \((Y,\mathcal T,\nu)\) be **sigma-finite** measure spaces. The product sigma-algebra \(\Sigma\otimes\mathcal T\) is generated by measurable rectangles. Its (unique) product measure is characterized by

\[
(\mu\times\nu)(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)
\]

for measurable rectangles, with the standard convention \(0\cdot\infty=0\) for nonnegative extended measure values.

If \(f:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]\) is product-measurable, Tonelli asserts

\[
\int_{X\times Y}f\,d(\mu\times\nu)
=\int_X\!\left(\int_Yf(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)
=\int_Y\!\left(\int_Xf(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y),
\]

including infinite values. **Fubini** asserts the same equalities with finite answers when \(f\) is integrable: \(\int|f|\,d(\mu\times\nu)<\infty\). Sections and inner integrals are well-defined a.e. in the signed case. For the *completed* product measure, exceptional sections may not be measurable; the a.e. formulation remains the right one.

### Why a rectangle computation extends

1. For \(f=1_{A\times B}\), both repeated integrals give \(\mu(A)\nu(B)\).
2. Finite positive linear combinations of rectangle indicators inherit the equality.
3. A monotone-class/generating-class argument proves section measurability and extends the formula first to indicators of sets in \(\Sigma\otimes\mathcal T\), then to general nonnegative simple functions.
4. Approximate nonnegative measurable \(f\) by increasing simple functions and apply monotone convergence twice to obtain Tonelli.
5. For \(f\in L^1\), apply Tonelli to \(f^+\), \(f^-\), or to real and imaginary parts; all required integrals are finite.

### A sharp order-of-integration failure

On \((0,1)^2\), define

\[
f(x,y)=\frac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
\]

For each fixed \(x>0\),

\[
\int_0^1f(x,y)\,dy
=\left[\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right]_{y=0}^{y=1}
=\frac1{1+x^2},
\quad
\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\frac\pi4.
\]

For each fixed \(y>0\),

\[
\int_0^1f(x,y)\,dx
=\left[-\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right]_{x=0}^{x=1}
=-\frac1{1+y^2},
\quad
-\int_0^1\frac{dy}{1+y^2}=-\frac\pi4.
\]

In polar coordinates \(f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)=\cos(2\theta)/r^2\). On a fixed angular sector with \(|\cos(2\theta)|\ge c>0\), its absolute planar integral dominates a positive constant times \(\int_0^\epsilon dr/r=\infty\); hence \(f\notin L^1((0,1)^2)\).

### Exercises with answers

1. **Triangle:** Compute both orders for \(1_{\{0<y<x<1\}}\). **Answer:** \(\int_0^1 x\,dx=\int_0^1(1-y)\,dy=1/2\).
2. **Nonnegative unbounded function:** Compute \(\int_{(0,1)^2}(xy)^{-1/2}\,dx\,dy\). **Answer:** Tonelli: \((\int_0^1x^{-1/2}\,dx)^2=4\).
3. **Infinite but valid:** For \(f=1\) on \((0,\infty)^2\), why is Tonelli applicable? **Answer:** \(f\ge0\), and both iterated integrals and the product integral equal \(+\infty\).
4. **Check before swapping:** For an arbitrary signed measurable \(f\), what should you integrate first? **Answer:** \(|f|\) using Tonelli; a finite value licenses Fubini.
5. **Double series:** Suppose \(\sum_m\sum_n |a_{mn}|<\infty\). Can you reverse the sums? **Answer:** Yes, by Fubini applied to counting measure on \(\mathbb N\times\mathbb N\).
