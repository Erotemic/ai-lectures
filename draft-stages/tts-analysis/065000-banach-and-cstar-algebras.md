# Banach and C-Star Algebras: Where Functional Calculus Lives

What do we mean when we ask for a function of an operator? For a number, taking its square root is familiar. For a diagonal matrix, taking its square root means applying the square-root function to each diagonal entry. But the very same question makes sense for an operator acting on infinitely many coordinates. We want the answer not to depend on guessing eigenvectors. We want to know what kind of functions are allowed, and how to recognize the result.

An algebra is a space where we can add and multiply elements. An ordinary function algebra gives the clearest picture: add two continuous functions point by point, or multiply their values at each point. Suppose the functions live on a closed interval. We can measure their size by the largest absolute value they attain. This maximum norm makes the continuous functions into a complete space: if a sequence of functions becomes uniformly closer together, its limit remains continuous. It is both an algebra and a Banach space. We call it a Banach algebra because multiplication behaves well under its norm.

Matrices offer another Banach algebra. The objects are matrices rather than functions, but matrix multiplication still makes sense, and the operator norm measures their effect on vectors. This example introduces an important complication: matrix multiplication is not generally commutative. The order of two matrices matters. Nevertheless a single matrix and its powers commute with each other. That is why polynomials of one matrix are a natural starting point for functional calculus.

Take a two-by-two diagonal matrix with entries two and five. Its square has entries four and twenty-five. If we want to apply a continuous function, say the square root, the desired result has diagonal entries square root of two and square root of five. For this normal matrix, the construction is exactly what the continuous functional calculus formalizes. Replacing the finite list of diagonal entries with a whole spectrum is the main conceptual change.

Now introduce the adjoint, the operator analogue of conjugate transpose. An algebra equipped with an adjoint and a compatible complete norm can satisfy the C-star identity: the size of the product of an element and its adjoint is the square of the size of that element. For bounded operators on a Hilbert space, this identity follows from inner-product geometry. This extra structure is what makes the theory especially powerful.

A self-adjoint operator equals its own adjoint. A normal operator commutes with its adjoint. The small algebra generated by a normal operator, its adjoint and the identity is commutative, even if the surrounding operator algebra is not. The Gelfand representation tells us that a commutative unital C-star algebra can be treated, without loss of norm information, as continuous functions on a compact space. In the algebra generated by one normal operator, that compact space can be identified with its spectrum. In other words, working with a normal operator is, in a precise sense, working with the function that assigns each spectral point its own value.

This gives a reliable answer to our opening question. We evaluate a continuous function of a normal operator by first evaluating that function on the spectrum, then carrying it back through the representation. The identity function becomes the original operator. A constant function becomes a multiple of the identity. Products of functions correspond to products of their operators. And the operator norm is exactly the largest size of the function on the spectrum.

There are limits to this pleasant picture. For a nonnormal matrix, a continuous function defined only on its eigenvalues does not specify a useful calculus with the same norm control. For an unbounded self-adjoint operator, the spectral measure allows more functions, but their operator domains must be handled separately. A holomorphic calculus for a bounded operator works in greater generality because analytic functions can be recovered from resolvent integrals. Those are related, but different constructions. A responsible calculation begins by checking the type of operator and the regularity of the proposed function.

The practical lesson is that functional calculus is not a collection of notational tricks. It is a consequence of algebra, topology, and the geometry of the adjoint working together. The basic examples—continuous functions, matrices, and bounded operators—show why the abstract machinery is worth building.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

A complex unital Banach algebra \(A\) satisfies \(\|ab\|\le\|a\|\|b\|\). For \(\|a\|<1\), the Neumann series \((1-a)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}a^n\) converges in norm, with \(\|(1-a)^{-1}\|\le(1-\|a\|)^{-1}\).

A C*-algebra has an involution \(a\mapsto a^*\) and \(\|a^*a\|=\|a\|^2\). For compact Hausdorff \(K\), \(C(K)\) is a commutative unital C*-algebra under \(f^*(x)=\overline{f(x)}\), \(\|f\|=\sup_K|f|\). A unital commutative C*-algebra is *-isometrically isomorphic to \(C(\widehat A)\), where \(\widehat A\) is its character space.

For a bounded normal operator \(N\) on a complex Hilbert space, there is a unique unital isometric *-homomorphism \(C(\sigma(N))\to B(H)\), \(f\mapsto f(N)\), sending \(z\mapsto z\) to \(N\). Therefore \(\|f(N)\|=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(N)}|f(\lambda)|\). This does **not** extend verbatim to arbitrary nonnormal operators.

Example: if \(D=\mathrm{diag}(2,5)\), then \(\sqrt D=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt2,\sqrt5)\) and \(\|\sqrt D\|=\sqrt5\).

### Exercises with answers

1. **Neumann inversion.** If \(\|T\|\le1/3\), bound \(\|(I-T)^{-1}\|\). **Answer:** At most \(3/2\).
2. **Function calculus.** For \(D=\mathrm{diag}(2,5)\), compute \((D-2I)(D-5I)\). **Answer:** Zero, by evaluating at the two spectral values.
3. **Algebraic caution.** Is every element of \(M_2(\mathbb C)\) normal? **Answer:** No; the Jordan block \(\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}\) is not normal.
