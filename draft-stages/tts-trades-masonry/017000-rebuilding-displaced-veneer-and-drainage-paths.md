# 017000 — Rebuilding Displaced Veneer and Drainage Paths

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A section of brick veneer has begun to bow away from a house. The mortar joints look cracked, and a previous repair filled several gaps with a hard sealant. Would repointing the mortar make it safe? Not necessarily. We must understand how a veneer is supported, how it is tied back, and how water is supposed to move behind it. A displaced facing can be a connection problem even if the individual bricks remain sound.

## Veneer is not the main structural wall

Many houses have a structural wall behind a brick facing. The brick veneer carries much of its own weight down to a supporting foundation, shelf angle, or other designed support. Ties connect it to the structural backing so that wind and other lateral forces can be resisted. That division of roles matters. The veneer is not held up simply because mortar adheres to the building, and a metal tie that holds the veneer back is not automatically intended to carry all of its gravity weight.

If ties corrode, were never installed correctly, or pull out of weak backing, the veneer can move outward. If the support below settles or a steel shelf angle deteriorates, the veneer may crack and rotate. Temperature expansion, moisture movement, and restrained joints can also produce stresses. Surface cracking alone cannot identify which mechanism is active, but displacement changes the level of concern. A bowing wall should not be treated as merely cosmetic.

An owner can record cracks, bulges, offset corners, joint openings, and whether windows or door openings have distorted. That observation should be done from a safe location, without pulling or pushing on unstable masonry. Areas threatened by falling units may need to be kept clear until a competent structural and masonry assessment determines the condition.

## A cavity is part of the wall's water management

Brick and mortar can admit rainwater. A drained veneer system accepts that some moisture may pass through the outer facing. It provides a cavity or drainage space, a water-resistive barrier at the backing, flashings at transitions, and drainage outlets such as weeps. The intention is not to make every mortar joint perfectly impermeable; it is to control where water goes after it enters.

Now imagine mortar droppings filling the cavity near the bottom of a wall. Water coming down the back of the veneer can be trapped above the obstruction and accumulate against the backing. Or consider flashing that turns up at the wrong location or ends before reaching the exterior. It can direct water inward. Sealing over the visible weep openings may look tidy but disable the designed escape path. This is why a veneer repair must address drainage as well as its structural connections.

Cavity width, tie form, embedment, flashing material, and placement all depend on the wall system and applicable standards. There is no safe universal detail for adding 'some ties' through the outside face without knowing the backing material and its condition.

## Diagnose before selecting a repair

Suppose a veneer bulges in the middle of a wall, but its base appears aligned. That raises questions about missing or ineffective lateral ties. If the entire wall leans from the foundation, settlement or support movement becomes more plausible. Cracks clustered around a corroding steel lintel at a window may point to expansion of corrosion products and lost bearing. Diagonal cracks near an opening can reflect movement of the supporting structure. These are pattern-based hypotheses, not diagnoses.

The investigator may need to examine drawings, open limited areas, verify backing conditions, test tie condition with recognized techniques, and check support levels and drainage. Each step should answer a question. Is the veneer still properly supported vertically? Are lateral ties present and effective? Can moisture leave? Is water reaching the structural backing? Are movement joints functioning? The work should not begin by selecting a mortar color.

Repointing replaces deteriorated mortar in accessible joints. It can be valuable for weathering and durability, but it does not reconnect a displaced wall or restore corroded anchors behind it. Similarly, filling a moving joint with rigid mortar can transfer movement into new cracks. Material and joint compatibility matter: too-hard repair mortar can worsen damage in older brickwork.

## Rebuilding is a sequence of load, connection, and water control

A designed repair might require taking down unstable veneer in a controlled area, supporting adjacent work, correcting the foundation or shelf support, restoring the backing and water-resistive layer, installing properly selected ties and flashings, and rebuilding the masonry with suitable mortar and movement provisions. The actual sequence belongs to a qualified project design because partially dismantled masonry can be unstable, and temporary support is itself a structural system.

Careful documentation matters during opening. Photograph the previous tie condition, corrosion, cavity obstructions, water stains, and flashing terminations before they disappear behind the reconstructed wall. Hold an inspection point before closing the cavity. Later, verify that weeps are open and that the completed facing is stable and properly supported. A beautiful exposed face is only one acceptance criterion.

The central lesson is that a veneer functions as a layered assembly. Structural support keeps its weight in place. Ties keep it connected to the backing. Flashings and drainage let rainwater escape. Movement details allow materials to expand and contract without damaging each other. A repair that addresses only the visible mortar is incomplete whenever one of those hidden systems has failed.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Four systems:** vertical support (foundation/shelf); lateral tie path (veneer → tie → backing); drainage (cavity → flashing → weeps); movement accommodation (joints, lintels, thermal expansion). Diagnose each separately.

**Escalate:** outward bulge, shifted units, severe crack widening, corroded lintel, distressed shelf angle, or falling brick risk require controlled access and competent structural evaluation. Do not hand-test a bulging veneer or assume repointing restores capacity.

**Rebuild hold points:** verify concealed backing and support; document ties and embedment; verify flashing turns, end dams and weeps; keep the cavity free of mortar bridges; confirm movement joints; review after water testing as appropriate to the assembly.

**Review:** Tie placement, corrosion resistance, cavity geometry, required supports, testing and permit requirements depend on the specific wall and jurisdiction; masonry conservation materials differ for historic walls. Independent review pending.
