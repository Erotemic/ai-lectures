# Read masonry assemblies before concentrating loads

## Brick, Block, Mortar, and Drainage

Stand in front of a brick house and ask what holds up the roof. Sometimes the masonry is structural; sometimes the bricks are a veneer tied back to a wood or steel-framed building. The appearance from the street may be similar, but the forces and water-control details are different. A brick veneer is not merely a thin version of a load-bearing brick wall. It is part of a layered enclosure that should carry its own loads, remain attached under wind forces, and allow water that penetrates the brick to drain out.

Start with the materials. A brick is a manufactured unit; masonry mortar forms the joints between units. Concrete masonry units, or CMUs, are another family with hollow cells and webs that can be combined with reinforcement and grout. Mortar provides bedding, alignment and load transfer, but it is not just glue. Its properties must be compatible with the units, exposure and intended movement. Harder mortar is not always the best mortar, especially when repairing softer, older masonry.

### Compression is only part of the story

Masonry units can perform well under compression. A vertical wall can transfer gravity loads through many courses to its foundation. But wind pushes and pulls on a wall; settlement distorts its shape; temperature and moisture change its dimensions. These actions can create tension, bending and shear, which require suitable wall geometry, reinforcement, ties, anchors, support and movement details.

Consider the difference between a structural CMU wall and brick veneer. In a reinforced masonry wall, vertical reinforcing bars and grout-filled cells can participate in carrying forces, and bond beams can connect parts of the system. In an anchored veneer wall, corrosion-resistant ties transfer lateral loads to backup framing while the veneer usually carries its own weight onto a foundation ledge or engineered support. The precise limits and configuration belong to engineered design and adopted masonry standards. A wall tie whose connection has rusted away can create a serious stability issue even when the visible brick face appears intact.

### Mortar joints are designed interfaces

A horizontal bed joint supports a course. A vertical head joint closes the space between neighboring units. Joint thickness, filling, tooling, and curing influence strength and resistance to rain penetration. Voids and badly consolidated mortar can provide routes for water and air. But a neat face joint does not make a wall waterproof. Water can be absorbed into masonry pores, pass through cracks and mortar interfaces, and reach the concealed side.

That brings us to a paradox. An excellent brick veneer wall is often designed on the assumption that rain will penetrate its outer layer. Behind the brick is a drainage space and a water-control layer on the backup wall. At the base of that space and at other interruptions, flashing collects water and directs it outward through weep openings. If mortar droppings block the cavity or the weeps are sealed, an assembly that looked carefully finished may become a reservoir against the sheathing.

This is why painting over weep holes to 'stop the leak' is typically the wrong move. Weeps are outlets, not defects. The same applies to improperly sealing movement joints without preserving their designed behavior.

### Brick and water have a complicated relationship

Clay brick, concrete block, natural stone, mortar and grout each have their own pore structures and moisture behavior. Some absorb liquid water readily; others are denser. Wet masonry stores water and can release vapor when warmed. In freezing climates, saturation and freeze-thaw cycling can damage susceptible masonry or mortar. Efflorescence, the whitish salt deposits sometimes seen on surfaces, is evidence of water moving dissolved salts to a surface as it evaporates. It is a diagnostic clue, not by itself proof of the particular leak path.

Imagine a brick wall with a dark damp band above a window. The water might be entering at a failed head flashing, a horizontal ledge above, a cracked joint higher up, or an overloaded drainage cavity. Sealing the visible stain may obscure the path while letting moisture continue to accumulate. Trace where water enters, what absorbs it, where it can drain, and how the assembly is intended to dry. Masonry diagnosis uses the same logic as rain-control analysis in framed walls.

### The joint that permits movement

A long wall expands and contracts as temperature changes, while adjacent materials may move by different amounts. A masonry wall also responds to moisture changes and foundation movement. Without suitably located movement joints, the accumulating strain may express itself as cracking at a corner, an opening, or a reentrant geometry. Control and expansion joints are planned ways to accommodate movement; they are not indicators of poor craftsmanship. Their location and detailing depend on material system and structural configuration.

Repairing a crack therefore requires identifying its cause. A crack due to a shifting support is different from one caused by thermal movement; an aged mortar joint is different from fractured brick. Repointing removes deteriorated mortar and replaces it with compatible mortar, but does not automatically correct structural displacement or a failed flashing. A high-strength patch can sometimes transfer stress into weaker neighboring units rather than solve the original problem.

### Read the layers and the load path

For a learning exercise, draw three sketches: a reinforced CMU wall; an anchored brick veneer over framed backup; and an older multi-wythe brick wall. For each, label what supports gravity load, what resists lateral load, how water exits, and what joints accommodate movement. Then ask which details would be visible during inspection and which would be concealed.

The transferable lesson is that masonry serves several roles at once: structure, enclosure, finish, and weathering surface. A sound repair respects the distinctions. Strong bricks alone do not create a sound masonry wall, and perfect-looking mortar alone does not create a dry one.

## Finding Reinforcement and Interpreting Concrete Condition Tests

Before a load is concentrated in an existing assembly, its concealed contents and the limits of a condition test need to be considered.

You are asked to install a new penetration through a reinforced concrete wall. From the outside it looks like a broad, uniform slab. Inside are steel bars, perhaps post-tensioning tendons, conduits and other hidden objects. The location of a proposed hole might be harmless or might damage a critical part of the structure. A metal detector seems like the obvious solution. But detecting something metallic is not the same as understanding what it is, and finding the steel is not the same as knowing whether the concrete is sound.

Concrete inspection offers a family of non-destructive and minimally invasive methods. Some are primarily for mapping geometry. Others help assess internal discontinuities, moisture or corrosion likelihood. The skill is to match the measurement to the question and to distinguish what a method detects from what an engineer may infer from it.

### Concrete cover and reinforcement layout

Reinforcing bars resist tension and help control cracking in reinforced concrete. The concrete surrounding the steel supplies compression resistance, anchorage and some protection against environmental attack. The depth of concrete between a bar and the exposed surface is called cover. Cover matters because it influences durability, fire performance, bond behavior and the clearance for new penetrations.

A cover meter based on electromagnetic response can often help locate metal reinforcement near a surface and estimate its depth under suitable conditions. A dense mesh, two layers of bars, other embedded metal and unusual geometry complicate interpretation. The meter is answering a question about electromagnetic response, not reading a hidden engineering drawing. Apparent depth and bar size can trade off in the signal. A single scan line is therefore weaker evidence than a mapped set of intersecting scans, checked against accessible edges or records.

Ground-penetrating radar sends electromagnetic pulses into the material and analyzes reflected energy. A change in dielectric properties causes a reflection. Rebar, layer boundaries, voids and other interfaces may appear in a scan, but their visibility depends on depth, moisture, conductivity, frequency and survey geometry. Lower-frequency antennas can penetrate farther at the expense of detail; higher-frequency antennas can resolve smaller features with less penetration under some conditions. Wet, conductive concrete can attenuate the signal strongly.

The display is not a photograph inside the wall. It is a processed representation of reflections with assumptions about wave speed. Experienced interpretation is required to differentiate a bar from a conduit or other target and to estimate depth. If a hidden tendon is plausible, a casual household stud-finder-style scan is not an acceptable basis for drilling.

### Surface appearance and internal condition

A brown stain beneath a crack may suggest corrosion, but surface color alone does not establish the condition of reinforcing steel. Rust occupies more volume than the original steel and can generate tensile stresses in surrounding concrete. That process may contribute to cracking and spalling. Other causes of cracking include shrinkage, thermal movement, load effects and foundation displacement. Pattern, depth, age and movement history matter.

Sounding methods look for areas that respond differently when struck or scanned acoustically. Delaminated concrete may produce a different response from well-bonded concrete, but there are interpretation limits involving thickness, aggregate, geometry and accessibility. Ultrasonic pulse velocity and related methods help characterize wave travel and variations in material condition; they do not produce a universal concrete-strength reading without calibration and assumptions.

Half-cell potential testing adds another kind of evidence. An electrical potential is measured between reinforcing steel and a reference electrode. The measurement helps assess the likelihood of active corrosion under appropriate conditions. It does not directly measure how much steel has been lost or how much load capacity remains. Moisture, concrete resistivity, access to steel and surface treatments affect interpretation. Corrosion rate and structural consequence require additional evidence.

### A case: the suspicious balcony edge

Imagine a concrete balcony with a long crack parallel to its outer edge, scattered rust stains and one area that sounds hollow. A cover meter finds reinforcement close to the exposed surface. Radar indicates bars near the suspected crack, but signal quality varies across damp regions. These findings are coherent with reinforcement corrosion as one hypothesis; they do not prove that corrosion is the only cause. The balcony's drainage, waterproofing, load history and movement must also be examined.

A strong investigation starts with a map: dimensions, crack locations, crack widths, delamination indications, moisture sources and accessible support details. Measurements from different methods should be plotted on the same drawing. A limited exploratory opening may then validate uncertain non-destructive readings at a carefully selected location. That is an engineering decision, not a license to chip randomly at suspect steel.

For a drilling project, the same logic applies. Find existing drawings, understand what structural members might be present, use qualified scanning for the relevant hidden hazards, and confirm the proposed hole does not compromise reinforcement, post-tensioning, utilities or specified edge distances. Mark surveyed areas and confidence limits, not merely an unexplained spray-painted dot.

### Measurement does not equal diagnosis

An inspection instrument can turn an invisible object into a measurable signal. The difficult part is connecting that signal to a mechanism and a decision. A radar reflection may be a bar. A half-cell reading may indicate electrochemical conditions that favor corrosion. A hollow sound may indicate a delamination. None alone certifies structural safety.

The most reliable concrete condition assessments combine drawings, environment, symptoms, multiple measurements and targeted verification. Good reporting says exactly what was tested, where it was tested, what the instrument was capable of detecting, and what remained unknown. That is the difference between finding a signal and making an engineering judgment.

## Drilling and Anchoring in Different Masonry

That evidence frames the anchorage question: the visible fastener is only one part of the connection.

A heavy shelf is held to a wall by two visible brackets. The brackets are steel and look substantial. The screws are large. Yet the connection can fail by tearing out a fragment of brick, breaking a concrete edge, pulling from a weak mortar joint, or crushing the thin face of a hollow block. The strength of an anchor is not only a property of its metal; it emerges from the anchor, the hole, the substrate, the edge distances, and the direction of force.

Before discussing anchor types, distinguish the materials loosely called masonry. Dense cast concrete, fired clay brick, solid concrete block, hollow block, soft older brick, mortar joints, and natural stone are not equivalent anchoring media. A hollow block can have comparatively strong face shells but large voids behind them. A soft historic brick can be damaged by an expansion anchor that works well in sound concrete. Even within one wall, a drill hole may meet several different materials.

### How an anchor transmits force

Mechanical expansion anchors generate outward pressure against a hole or engage the base material mechanically. Their resistance may depend on friction, localized bearing, or an undercut geometry. Screw anchors form or follow a thread in the substrate. Adhesive anchors transfer force through a cured bonding material and the surrounding base material. Specialized hollow-wall and masonry anchors use sleeves, screens, or toggling mechanisms to engage thin shells or voids.

Each mechanism has characteristic failure modes. The anchor metal can rupture. It can pull out because the interface is inadequate. A cone or wedge of concrete can break away. A thin masonry shell can crack. An anchor near an edge can split the remaining material, even if the metal is nowhere near its strength limit. In adhesive systems, hole preparation, temperature, cure time, and compatibility with the base material are integral to performance.

Now consider load direction. A wall-mounted bracket under a shelf does not simply pull downward on each anchor. The load is offset from the wall, producing a moment. Some anchors tend to pull outward while others press the bracket inward. This prying effect can create significant tension at upper connections. A row of identical anchors does not automatically share load evenly when the bracket bends or the substrate varies.

### Drilling changes the substrate

Drilling needs to produce a hole of appropriate diameter, depth, straightness, and surface condition. A bit that wanders into a mortar joint can change the base material. A hammer drill suitable for one type of concrete may damage fragile brick or hollow block. A worn bit may produce an inconsistent hole. Anchors intended to expand against sound substrate may not hold in a hole made oversized or shattered at the edges.

Dust is more than a housekeeping issue. For certain anchors, dust left inside the hole prevents proper contact or adhesive bonding. The manufacturer's approved cleaning sequence is part of the installation. Drilling and grinding masonry can also produce respirable crystalline silica, so dust capture and appropriate exposure controls need to be part of the work method, not an afterthought.

A hole made in a wall may also penetrate a weather-control layer or a water-carrying cavity. Anchoring an exterior handrail through brick veneer is therefore not simply a drilling exercise. Is the veneer structural or only cladding? What supports the applied load? How is water kept out of the penetrated assembly? The visible masonry can be a misleading guide to the actual load path.

### Why edge distance and spacing matter

Imagine a concrete slab edge with one large expansion anchor very close to it. Tightening can generate forces that try to split off a wedge of concrete. Moving the anchor farther inward may be more useful than increasing its diameter. If several anchors are close together, their breakout regions can overlap. They may have a combined capacity less than the sum of isolated-anchor capacities. A pattern designed only to fit the bracket holes can therefore be structurally poor.

A hollow unit poses another challenge: an anchor that looks deep from the outside may engage only the front shell. It can feel tight while having little capacity against an outward load. Grouted cells, solid units, face shells, and mortar joints need to be distinguished rather than treated as interchangeable holes. Where a manufacturer's approved tables specify base-material types, embedment depths, and minimum edge distances, those assumptions determine whether its published load data apply.

Historic walls add another reason for restraint. Soft lime mortar and old bricks may be more vulnerable to localized pressure than modern sound concrete. Altering an old masonry wall can also create moisture pathways and irreversible damage. An anchor for a noncritical picture is not a precedent for a handrail, awning, overhead load, or guard connection.

### A disciplined anchoring decision

Start by identifying what the attachment must carry and how the force reaches the wall. Determine whether the base is solid or hollow, sound or deteriorated, structural or veneer. Look for cracks, free edges, concealed cavities, and likely reinforcement or utilities. Then select an anchor system whose published qualification matches that actual substrate and load case. Plan the drilling and cleaning method, and reserve room for correct embedment and spacing.

Finally, inspect what has been built. An anchor that tightened without spinning is not automatically verified. Does the bracket sit flush? Is the substrate cracked? Does the fastener have the intended engagement? Is the work documented for future inspection? For consequential connections, a qualified designer and installer may specify more than visual observation.

Masonry often appears massive, which can tempt us to assume that any hole in it provides a strong attachment. The opposite lesson is more useful: anchors concentrate force in a small portion of a brittle or heterogeneous material. Understanding exactly which portion carries the load is the heart of sound masonry fastening.

## Retaining Walls, Lateral Loads, and Drainage

A garden wall and a retaining wall may be built from similar blocks, but they do different jobs. A free-standing garden wall mostly carries its own weight and lateral environmental loads. A retaining wall also holds back soil. That soil exerts a horizontal force which tends to slide, rotate, and deform the wall. The taller the retained soil, the more consequential those effects become.

Imagine a trench where the ground on one side is two feet higher than the other. The retaining structure must prevent the higher soil from moving into the lower space. Even apparently dry soil is not a passive stack of bricks. Its grains exert pressure through friction and confinement. The pressure depends on soil type, density, moisture, wall movement, slope, and the way the backfill was compacted.

### A triangle of pressure

For a simplified vertical retaining face with level, drained, granular backfill, a common engineering model has lateral earth pressure increasing roughly with depth. Near the surface, the pressure is small; near the bottom, it is larger. Drawn along the wall, this produces a triangular pressure distribution. The total horizontal force per unit length is related to the area of that triangle, so under fixed material assumptions it grows approximately with the square of wall height.

That square relationship matters. Doubling the retained height does not merely double the lateral earth force; in the simplified case, it produces about four times the force per unit wall length. The overturning moment grows even more rapidly because the load acts over a greater height. This is a physical explanation, not a design formula suitable for all soils and geometries. Real walls also see surcharge loads, groundwater effects, frost, seismic conditions, and nonuniform soil behavior.

Consider a vehicle parking close to the top of the wall. Its wheel loads do not act directly on the masonry face, yet they add stresses within the retained soil. The resulting force distribution depends on the geometry and soil. A wall that could support an ordinary garden might not be suitable for a driveway or a steep slope. This is one reason that height alone is an incomplete description of retaining-wall risk.

### Water is a second material to retain

Now suppose rain infiltrates the backfill faster than it can drain. Water can accumulate behind the wall. Hydrostatic pressure rises with the depth of water and adds to the load on the structure, subject to the combined soil-water mechanics. Water can also weaken or wash away soil, and freezing conditions may introduce additional stresses. A wall that appears robust in dry weather can move after a wet season because the load environment has changed.

A proper drainage strategy includes more than a hole through the front face. Water must be collected, protected against clogging by migrating fine soil, and discharged to a suitable place. Freely draining backfill, appropriately specified filters or geotextiles, drainpipe geometry, surface grading, and maintained outlets are interacting components. A drain blocked at its outlet does not protect the wall merely because a perforated pipe is buried behind it.

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and the USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service both treat water pressure and drainage as central retaining-wall design concerns. Their engineering references discuss how soil and water pressures differ. This is important because replacing a cracked block without correcting water accumulation may simply restart the failure.

### A wall can fail without breaking in half

Three basic stability mechanisms are worth distinguishing. Sliding occurs when horizontal driving forces exceed the resistance available at the base and other stabilizing elements. Overturning is rotation caused by the moment of lateral loads relative to stabilizing weights and restraint. Bearing failure occurs when pressure transmitted to the foundation soil exceeds its capacity or causes unacceptable settlement. There is also global instability: a larger soil mass can move along a failure surface that reaches behind or beneath the wall. Individual concrete or masonry members can crack, bend, or shear even when the global wall position appears stable.

Reinforcement, gravity mass, geogrid, anchors, and foundation geometry address different parts of these problems. Segmental block walls may rely on geometry and mechanical interlock for low-height applications, while reinforced-soil walls use embedded reinforcement extending into properly prepared backfill. The details are not interchangeable. A decorative concrete block is not automatically a retaining-wall system.

### Observe the direction and timing of movement

Suppose an existing wall has rotated outward at the top. That suggests a different failure pattern than a wall that has settled vertically in one section, and both differ from blocks whose faces have spalled due to moisture and freeze-thaw cycling. After a rainstorm, wet streaks, ponded water above the wall, blocked outlets, and saturated soil provide useful observations. But a single crack shape cannot uniquely identify the cause.

An evaluation records wall height and length, slope above and below, nearby buildings or vehicles, soil, drainage routes, movement history, and signs of ongoing displacement. Photographs with fixed measurement references can establish whether displacement is progressing. Large or actively moving walls are not suitable for a trial repair performed by pushing blocks back into place. The retained soil may have substantial stored potential energy.

The lesson is that a retaining wall holds back a changing environment, not merely a pile of soil at one instant. Its stability is a coupled problem of forces, foundation support, water movement, material strength, and time. The most economical correct design is the one that addresses those mechanisms together rather than treating the visible face as the entire structure.

## Masonry Veneers, Ties, and Moisture Management

A brick house can look like a solid wall of masonry and actually be a wood-framed building with a thin brick exterior. That distinction changes how the wall carries weight, resists wind, and handles water. Brick veneer is not simply structural brick made thinner. It is an exterior cladding system supported and restrained by other components.

Imagine cutting a section through a typical framed brick-veneer wall. From outside inward, there is a brick wythe, a drainage cavity, a water-resistive layer, sheathing, framing, insulation, and interior finish. Some buildings use different arrangements, but this model illustrates three separate jobs: brick manages weather and appearance, structural framing carries building loads, and ties connect the veneer laterally to the backing. The cavity and flashing provide a route for water that passes the outer surface.

### Vertical weight and lateral force follow different paths

The brick veneer has significant dead weight. It usually needs a designed support at its base, such as a foundation ledge, or at designated shelf angles higher in a building. The support carries vertical weight downward. Metal ties connecting the veneer to the backing do something different: they resist out-of-plane wind forces and help keep the veneer from moving away from or toward the structure. Ties are not intended to replace the gravity support of an entire wall unless the system is specifically engineered that way.

The ties must connect to something capable of accepting their load. A beautifully placed tie fixed to deteriorated sheathing or installed without appropriate engagement cannot deliver the expected resistance. Tie material and corrosion protection matter because the cavity is intermittently wet. Wind loads reverse direction; connections may be loaded in tension as well as compression. A wall that is still standing after a storm has not necessarily demonstrated that every concealed tie remains sound.

### A brick wall is not a perfect rain barrier

Rain strikes the wall, and some water is shed at the exterior face. Other water may enter through mortar joints, cracks, or the brick itself. The design therefore needs a managed drainage path. In a cavity wall, water that reaches the backing should be directed down to flashing and out through appropriately placed openings. Flashing continuity and termination at window heads, shelf angles, wall bases, and penetrations often matter more than simply applying a water-repellent coating to the bricks.

Weep openings must communicate with the drainage cavity. Mortar droppings can obstruct a cavity or bridge water across it. A cavity that is packed solid with mortar can allow water to transfer toward the backing instead of draining cleanly. Drainage accessories, cavity dimensions, and weep spacing vary with system and design requirements. The relevant principle is to maintain a continuous path to the exterior at every interruption.

### Movement must be accommodated

Brick, concrete, steel, wood framing, and cladding materials respond differently to temperature, moisture, and loading. Fired-clay brick typically has long-term moisture expansion behavior that differs from concrete masonry shrinkage. A veneer can crack if it is restrained where movement should occur, or if supporting construction deflects beneath it. Vertical control or expansion joints, shelf-angle details, and connections are therefore not cosmetic separations. They influence how stress accumulates.

Consider a long brick wall running between two rigid corners. If thermal and moisture movement is restrained, stress may concentrate near openings or at irregular transitions. A diagonal stair-step crack in mortar may indicate movement, but its appearance alone does not identify its cause. Foundation settlement, support deflection, corroding embedded steel, insufficient movement joints, and local water damage can produce overlapping patterns.

### Openings are changes in the load path

Above a window or door, the masonry cannot hang in the air without support. A lintel, arch, or other designed element transfers its weight around the opening. The lintel's bearing, corrosion condition, connection to the surrounding masonry, and water-shedding details matter. Rusting steel can expand and damage adjacent brick or mortar. A visible horizontal crack at the lintel line may thus involve both corrosion forces and moisture accumulation.

One common repair mistake is treating every crack as a surface blemish. Repointing may improve weather resistance where joints have deteriorated, but it does not solve a failing lintel, unstable veneer, or absent support. A new bead of sealant can sometimes block the very drainage exit the wall needs. Effective repair first distinguishes an active structural or movement problem from ordinary joint weathering.

### Read the wall as a system

For a non-destructive survey, locate foundation supports and shelf angles where visible. Trace flashing and weep locations. Look for blocked drainage openings, staining, spalling, bulging, out-of-plane displacement, and cracks whose widths or directions change. Compare both sides of an opening, and record the condition after rain as well as in dry weather. Visible ties may be sparse or concealed entirely, so lack of visible evidence is not proof of sound attachment.

A veneer wall succeeds when the three paths remain deliberate: brick weight reaches its support, wind loads reach the structural backing, and water reaches the exterior. Confusing these paths leads to poor diagnosis. Learn to ask which path a detail is meant to serve, and the wall becomes easier to interpret—especially when its appearance invites you to assume it is one solid mass.

### Retaining Walls, Lateral Loads, and Drainage

**Track:** Masonry and Concrete. **Background:** compression, footing behavior, frost, and water drainage. **Purpose:** understand why retaining earth requires a different structural model from stacking a vertical wall.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Simplified earth-pressure example (not a wall design)

For an idealized drained backfill with a uniform unit weight γ and active coefficient Kₐ:

- Pressure at depth z: `p(z) = K_a * gamma * z`.
- Resultant horizontal load per unit wall length for height H: `P_a = 0.5 * K_a * gamma * H^2`.
- For this triangular distribution, the resultant acts approximately `H / 3` above the base.

For illustration, with `K_a=0.33`, `gamma=120 lb/ft³`, and `H=4 ft`, `P_a≈317 lb/ft`. If height alone becomes 8 ft, `P_a≈1267 lb/ft`; about fourfold. **Do not use these values to size a real wall.** The assumptions exclude groundwater, surcharge, compaction stress, foundation and failure modes.

##### Inspection distinctions

- **Cracked block** may be material failure or a symptom of wall displacement.
- **Outward rotation** suggests lateral load/stability evaluation, not merely repointing.
- **Wet backfill** changes stress and hydraulic conditions; inspect collection, filtration, and discharge.
- **Localized settlement** requires evaluating foundation and drainage, not solely releveling the top row.

##### Review gaps

Actual retaining walls require soil and structural design, including site geometry, surcharge, erosion, drainage reliability, frost, and adopted code. Heights that trigger engineering/permits vary; no generic number is asserted here. Evidence sources are engineering references, not property-specific calculations.

Exact tie spacing, cavity dimensions, joint details, and seismic/wind requirements are design- and jurisdiction-specific. Bulging or displaced veneer and severely distressed lintels warrant structural evaluation before superficial repair.
