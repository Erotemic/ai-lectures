# Follow concealed support and water around openings

## Masonry Wall Ties and Anchorage Inspection

A brick-faced house can look like a solid brick structure. Often it is not. The brick may be a relatively thin exterior veneer that carries its own weight while another wall behind it carries the floors and roof. Between the two are cavities, flashing, and small metal ties that connect the veneer back to its supporting wall. Those hidden connectors help keep the brick from moving outward under wind pressure and suction. Their condition matters even when the bricks themselves appear sound.

### Begin by identifying what the masonry does

A loadbearing masonry wall may support significant gravity and lateral loads. A brick veneer over wood or steel framing may instead be a cladding system, with its own foundation or shelf-angle support and ties resisting out-of-plane forces. A cavity wall can combine inner and outer wythes in still another arrangement. From the street, all three may resemble a brick wall. The first job is to establish which system exists, using plans, construction age, visible details, and inspection openings where justified.

Gravity and lateral support are different. A veneer tie can transfer an outward or inward force to the backing wall without being intended to carry the entire vertical weight of the brick. The veneer must also have a reliable path for its own dead load, often at a foundation ledge or engineered shelf angle. Corroded shelf supports and failed wall ties create different hazards. Repointing mortar can improve a weathered joint; it cannot recreate a missing structural attachment behind the wall.

Think of a large sheet loosely leaned against a wall. It may stand for years under its own weight, but a sudden push or pull can bring it down. Brick veneer is not literally a sheet, yet that analogy shows the role of positive lateral attachment. A tie system is also distributed: a few missing connectors may not be obvious visually, but concentrated loss can leave a broad area poorly supported. Conversely, a visible crack alone does not reveal whether ties are the problem.

### Water is the long-term adversary

An exterior masonry veneer sees rain, freezing, solar heating, and temperature cycles. Proper cavities and flashing provide drainage so that water penetrating the outer wythe can leave rather than accumulate against the backing wall. Weep openings are outlets, not cosmetic defects to fill. If drainage is blocked, metal ties and supports may remain wet longer, increasing corrosion risk. Poorly detailed interfaces at roofs, windows, ledgers, and ground-level transitions can supply more water than the visible brick surface suggests.

Corrosion of steel connectors can involve two effects. Loss of metal reduces cross-section and connection capacity. Expansion of rust can also push against nearby mortar and brick, producing cracking or localized movement. The location and geometry of staining or cracking may suggest a mechanism, but they cannot identify the remaining capacity of concealed ties. Mixed-metal contact, coatings, exposure, and original connector materials influence service life.

Frost damage and building movement may look superficially similar. A stepped crack near an opening could involve differential movement, an unsupported lintel, or other stress concentration. Bulging across a broader field raises a different concern: separation or bowing of the veneer or backing. A wall that has visibly bowed, shifted, or detached should be treated as a possible falling-material hazard. Stand clear of unstable areas and arrange an appropriate structural evaluation rather than probing it from a ladder.

### Follow the pattern, not one photograph

An inspection record should map elevations and identify signs in context: cracks, displaced units, bulging, rust staining, failed sealant at penetrations, obstructed weeps, deteriorating shelf angles, damp interior finishes, and movement at corners. Photograph the whole facade before taking closeups. Include a scale at cracks or joints and record date, weather, and whether the condition is changing. A single image without location can mislead future reviewers about whether the same crack grew.

The most revealing evidence may be at accessible edges: around an unfinished basement, parapet, window return, mechanical penetration, or an already-open wall. These can disclose cavity size, backing material, tie type, and whether flashing is present. Do not assume that a visible screw or corrugated strap proves a compliant connector system. Tie spacing, embedment, corrosion resistance, backing attachment, and movement accommodation are design requirements tied to the specific wall and adopted standard.

When concealed connections need assessment, a qualified investigator may select inspection openings or specialized testing. The decision should be driven by a hypothesis and a repair plan. Random drilling can cut ties, damage waterproofing, or create new leakage routes. Re-anchoring a wall without understanding its support and movement conditions can concentrate load in an already weakened zone.

### Repair the system, not its appearance

A durable intervention may require source-water control, shelf-angle work, tie replacement or supplemental anchors, cavity drainage restoration, localized masonry rebuilding, and appropriate repointing. The order matters. Replacing a cracked brick while leaving a corroding support untreated only makes the wall look better temporarily. Likewise, applying an impermeable surface coating may reduce some wetting pathways while trapping moisture that enters elsewhere. Material compatibility and drying paths still matter.

This is an example of why a masonry facade is an assembly rather than a stack of bricks. The veneer sheds rain imperfectly, drains what it admits, moves with temperature and moisture, carries its own weight, and transfers lateral forces through hidden connectors. Inspection becomes more reliable when we ask how each of those functions is currently being achieved, and what evidence would establish that it is not.

## Rebuilding Displaced Veneer and Drainage Paths

Tie and anchorage observations become a repair problem when the veneer has already moved.

A section of brick veneer has begun to bow away from a house. The mortar joints look cracked, and a previous repair filled several gaps with a hard sealant. Would repointing the mortar make it safe? Not necessarily. We must understand how a veneer is supported, how it is tied back, and how water is supposed to move behind it. A displaced facing can be a connection problem even if the individual bricks remain sound.

### Veneer is not the main structural wall

Many houses have a structural wall behind a brick facing. The brick veneer carries much of its own weight down to a supporting foundation, shelf angle, or other designed support. Ties connect it to the structural backing so that wind and other lateral forces can be resisted. That division of roles matters. The veneer is not held up simply because mortar adheres to the building, and a metal tie that holds the veneer back is not automatically intended to carry all of its gravity weight.

If ties corrode, were never installed correctly, or pull out of weak backing, the veneer can move outward. If the support below settles or a steel shelf angle deteriorates, the veneer may crack and rotate. Temperature expansion, moisture movement, and restrained joints can also produce stresses. Surface cracking alone cannot identify which mechanism is active, but displacement changes the level of concern. A bowing wall should not be treated as merely cosmetic.

An owner can record cracks, bulges, offset corners, joint openings, and whether windows or door openings have distorted. That observation should be done from a safe location, without pulling or pushing on unstable masonry. Areas threatened by falling units may need to be kept clear until a competent structural and masonry assessment determines the condition.

### A cavity is part of the wall's water management

Brick and mortar can admit rainwater. A drained veneer system accepts that some moisture may pass through the outer facing. It provides a cavity or drainage space, a water-resistive barrier at the backing, flashings at transitions, and drainage outlets such as weeps. The intention is not to make every mortar joint perfectly impermeable; it is to control where water goes after it enters.

Now imagine mortar droppings filling the cavity near the bottom of a wall. Water coming down the back of the veneer can be trapped above the obstruction and accumulate against the backing. Or consider flashing that turns up at the wrong location or ends before reaching the exterior. It can direct water inward. Sealing over the visible weep openings may look tidy but disable the designed escape path. This is why a veneer repair must address drainage as well as its structural connections.

Cavity width, tie form, embedment, flashing material, and placement all depend on the wall system and applicable standards. There is no safe universal detail for adding 'some ties' through the outside face without knowing the backing material and its condition.

### Diagnose before selecting a repair

Suppose a veneer bulges in the middle of a wall, but its base appears aligned. That raises questions about missing or ineffective lateral ties. If the entire wall leans from the foundation, settlement or support movement becomes more plausible. Cracks clustered around a corroding steel lintel at a window may point to expansion of corrosion products and lost bearing. Diagonal cracks near an opening can reflect movement of the supporting structure. These are pattern-based hypotheses, not diagnoses.

The investigator may need to examine drawings, open limited areas, verify backing conditions, test tie condition with recognized techniques, and check support levels and drainage. Each step should answer a question. Is the veneer still properly supported vertically? Are lateral ties present and effective? Can moisture leave? Is water reaching the structural backing? Are movement joints functioning? The work should not begin by selecting a mortar color.

Repointing replaces deteriorated mortar in accessible joints. It can be valuable for weathering and durability, but it does not reconnect a displaced wall or restore corroded anchors behind it. Similarly, filling a moving joint with rigid mortar can transfer movement into new cracks. Material and joint compatibility matter: too-hard repair mortar can worsen damage in older brickwork.

### Rebuilding is a sequence of load, connection, and water control

A designed repair might require taking down unstable veneer in a controlled area, supporting adjacent work, correcting the foundation or shelf support, restoring the backing and water-resistive layer, installing properly selected ties and flashings, and rebuilding the masonry with suitable mortar and movement provisions. The actual sequence belongs to a qualified project design because partially dismantled masonry can be unstable, and temporary support is itself a structural system.

Careful documentation matters during opening. Photograph the previous tie condition, corrosion, cavity obstructions, water stains, and flashing terminations before they disappear behind the reconstructed wall. Hold an inspection point before closing the cavity. Later, verify that weeps are open and that the completed facing is stable and properly supported. A beautiful exposed face is only one acceptance criterion.

The central lesson is that a veneer functions as a layered assembly. Structural support keeps its weight in place. Ties keep it connected to the backing. Flashings and drainage let rainwater escape. Movement details allow materials to expand and contract without damaging each other. A repair that addresses only the visible mortar is incomplete whenever one of those hidden systems has failed.

## Lintels, Flashing, and Openings in Masonry Veneers

A window interrupts a brick wall. The bricks above it still weigh something, and rain still enters some of the exterior joints. Those two facts explain why a masonry opening needs both structure and drainage. A lintel carries the weight over the opening. Flashing and weeps manage water that reaches the cavity. When one part fails, the visible symptom may appear somewhere else. A rust stain above a window, for example, can indicate a steel support problem, while peeling interior paint may point to water movement through the masonry assembly.

### Veneer is not a solid watertight slab

Many brick-clad buildings use brick as a veneer supported in front of a structural wall. The brick has its own weight and needs support. Ties restrain it laterally; the backup wall carries other building loads. A drained cavity behind the veneer allows water that penetrates mortar joints to move down to flashing and out through weep openings. The outer brick surface sheds most rain, but it is not assumed to stop every droplet.

A window, door, or service opening interrupts this system. The veneer load above an opening cannot simply hang in midair. Steel lintels, reinforced masonry, or engineered support details bridge the gap. Their end bearing, span, deflection, corrosion protection, and compatibility with masonry movement are design questions. A narrow steel angle that appears strong enough by eye may deflect or corrode. Deflection can crack mortar and change the flashing geometry even before the angle loses its ability to carry load.

In an older wall, the lintel may be concealed by trim or an upper brick course. If it rusts, corrosion products occupy greater volume than the original steel. Expansion can lift or crack adjacent mortar, produce horizontal cracking above the opening, or displace bricks. Repointing the crack might improve appearance but does not remove the expanding corrosion or restore a failed lintel.

### Flashing must lead water outward

Picture a drop of rain entering a mortar joint several courses above a window. It travels down the back of the brick or across a cavity tie. If it arrives at the lintel without flashing, water can run toward the window frame or into the backup wall. Properly integrated flashing intercepts that path and directs the water to the exterior. End dams and continuity at corners matter because water follows gravity and surface tension, not the nominal edge of a drawing.

Weep openings complete the exit. They may be open head joints or another specified detail. If filled with mortar droppings, sealant, or debris, the cavity can hold water. A seemingly conscientious repair that caulks every visible hole at the base of a veneer may block intended drainage. Conversely, a hole that looks like a weep is not automatically a correctly constructed drainage path. The cavity, flashing, and outlet need to be understood as a connected route.

Wall movement also matters. Brick expands and contracts with temperature and moisture; the supporting structural frame may move differently. Detailers use expansion joints, soft joints, and support arrangements to accommodate this movement. Rigidly bridging a movement joint with mortar or a new flashing bracket can redirect stress into the opening. Some cracks originate in movement rather than structural support failure. Their pattern and timing help distinguish those explanations, but no crack shape proves a unique cause.

### Read a failure from several clues

Imagine recurring dampness at the upper corner of a window after wind-driven rain. Begin with an elevation sketch. Record the window head, brick coursing, lintel ends, wall ties if visible, sealant joints, flashing terminations, and weep locations. Note wind direction, storm intensity, whether interior condensation could occur, and whether water appears only after a delay. The symptom at the window may be downstream of water entering higher in the wall.

Now imagine a stepped crack rising from the corner, with rust-colored staining along the lintel. The crack, staining, and displaced masonry together justify investigation of structural support and corrosion. But do not conclude that a steel angle is certainly bad because the wall cracked. Settlement, thermal movement, inadequate bearing, and missing control joints also deserve consideration. A useful inspection separates observations from hypotheses and identifies which concealed details must be exposed to resolve the uncertainty.

The repair sequence follows the cause. A deteriorated support may require carefully engineered temporary support while masonry is removed. A replacement lintel must carry the intended load and resist corrosion in its exposure. Flashing must be reinstated as a continuous drainage system with positive exit. The repaired masonry must have compatible mortar and movement accommodation. Merely replacing bricks without correcting water entry or support can recreate the same damage.

### A successful opening is inspectable as a system

After intervention, verify bearing, lintel alignment, corrosion protection, flashing laps and terminations, unobstructed weeps, and appropriate joint detailing before concealing them. Record photos of the hidden work. Later inspections should note whether rust staining, cracks, or interior dampness return under comparable weather. The first dry day after a repair is not a proof of long-term rain performance.

The useful mental model is two paths drawn on one detail. One is the structural load descending around an opening to adequate supports. The other is the water descending inside a drained veneer until it exits safely. A masonry opening works when both paths are continuous and when material movement does not break their connections.

## Tracing Rainwater Around Masonry Openings

The opening detail can now be tested by following the actual route of rainwater rather than assuming that the visible stain marks its entry point.

A brown stain develops beneath a brick-faced window after a windy rain. Someone points at a cracked mortar joint and recommends sealing it. Perhaps the crack matters. But brick veneer is not normally designed on the assumption that not a drop of rain will pass its outer face. It is a water-management assembly. The diagnostic task is to learn where incoming water is supposed to go, and where it is going instead.

A typical drained veneer has an outer brick layer, a cavity, a water-resistive barrier against the backing, flashings at interruptions and openings to discharge water. The wall may also have insulation, air-control layers, ties and structural backing. Each has a different role. Brick and mortar reduce exposure but can absorb water. A drainage cavity creates a path downward, while properly detailed flashing redirects that water outward. If the path ends at a window frame or dead pocket, the veneer can perform exactly as its materials permit and still leak into the building.

### Read the opening as a water course

Imagine a window with brick above it. Rain hits the wall, some is absorbed by the masonry and some may enter through joints. Water travels down the back of the veneer until it reaches a horizontal interruption. Above the window head, through-wall flashing should intercept the path and provide a route to the exterior, usually with appropriate ends and drainage outlets. A lintel may support the brick but does not, merely by existing, serve as reliable water-shedding flashing.

At the window sill, a different assembly must manage water reaching the window perimeter. The window's own drainage design, its pan or sill detail where used, the weather-resistive barrier and surrounding masonry must be compatible. If water meets a reverse lap, it may pass behind the intended water-control layer. If a flashing stops inside the wall with no outward termination, liquid can pool. If a cavity is bridged by mortar droppings, water can be directed toward vulnerable sheathing rather than drained away.

There is no single generic window detail valid for every veneer, backup wall and climate. The point is to trace gravity, capillary movement, pressure-driven rain, and drainage outlets across the specific assembly.

### Start with the weather history

A leak that occurs only during wind from one direction suggests a different exposure than a stain appearing every morning in cold weather. Record rain intensity, duration, wind direction, interior temperature and the position of the wet spot. The interior stain may be below, beside or even several feet away from the true entry. Water can follow lintels, framing, membranes or fasteners before becoming visible.

Inspect the exterior from safe access. Photograph mortar joints, parapets, roof-wall interfaces, coping, sealants, lintels, flashings, weeps and grade. Look for displaced brick, clogged openings, rust stains, cracks that move, and areas where newer repairs interrupt older drainage. A blocked weep is a clue, but an open weep does not prove that the concealed flashing is properly installed. A visible caulk bead can conceal an incomplete joint.

Then examine the indoor evidence. Does moisture appear along the top of the window, at the jamb, or below the sill? Is there evidence of condensation on the glazing or frame? Are walls wet after prolonged rain even without a particular wind direction? Moisture instruments can map the extent of wetness but require interpretation for material and depth. Metal ties, salts and different substrates can affect measurements.

### Why indiscriminate hose testing can mislead

It is tempting to turn a garden hose on the whole wall and see where water appears. A large, uncontrolled spray can wet details that would not ordinarily receive that concentration, flood a drainage cavity beyond its intended capacity or make it impossible to know which location started the leak. The test itself may damage an already vulnerable assembly. Standardized field water tests have defined equipment, pressures, procedures and acceptance criteria, and they require interpretation appropriate to the wall system. A garden hose is not a substitute for a recognized standard test.

A better initial method is a documented hypothesis. If the first suspicion is a failed sill transition, the investigation plan should distinguish water entering through the window from water arriving behind the masonry above it. If the suspicion is the head flashing, opening a suitable inspection area may reveal laps and outlets more directly than repeatedly wetting an unknown wall. When controlled water testing is warranted, it should be carried out under an appropriate professional protocol with protection against unnecessary damage.

A second trap is to treat efflorescence as proof of exactly where water entered. White salts can appear where moisture evaporates, which may be far from its starting point. They reveal water movement and soluble material, but not a unique route. Likewise, a crack in the outer wythe may admit water without being the reason the interior became wet; a correct drainage layer might handle that water safely.

### Repair the path, then verify the result

Imagine the investigation reveals that flashing above a window turns down behind the backing instead of draining outward. Sealing the mortar around the head may make the stain disappear in one light rain, but the flawed path remains. A durable correction would restore the intended flashing connection and outlet, and address any damaged backing or metal. The exact reconstruction depends on structural support, the veneer system and the condition of concealed components.

After repair, preserve photographs of what was uncovered and how the new drainage path connects. Inspect for new wetting under comparable weather, or use an appropriate controlled verification test. Water damage can take time to dry; a persistent moisture reading does not necessarily mean continued entry, and a dry surface after one day does not prove the repair is durable. Track the trend.

Brick is durable partly because it can manage wetting and drying. The craft of diagnosis is to respect that system rather than try to turn its entire exterior into a perfect waterproof film. Follow the water from impact, through any entry, down the concealed path, to its designed exit. The failure usually becomes more intelligible when the wall is understood in section rather than viewed only as a flat facade.

## Reinforced Concrete Cover, Corrosion, and Long-Term Durability

A concrete balcony can look solid until rust-colored streaks appear beneath a small crack. Later, a strip of concrete breaks loose, exposing steel. The tempting explanation is that the concrete was too weak. But the more useful question is whether the concrete could keep the embedded steel protected over years of water, salt, air and movement.

Reinforced concrete combines materials with different strengths. Concrete performs well in compression; properly detailed steel reinforcement carries tension and helps resist cracking and bending. The two work together because force can transfer through their bond and because their thermal expansion is sufficiently compatible for many structural uses. That relationship depends on adequate concrete around the steel, good material quality, proper placement and an exposure environment anticipated by the design.

### What cover is doing

Concrete cover is the thickness of concrete between the outermost reinforcing steel and the exposed surface. It protects the steel against weather and other agents, and it can provide fire resistance and adequate development of bond depending on the member and detailing. Cover is not a universal cosmetic thickness. The required dimension changes with exposure, steel size, placement and the applicable standard. A bar resting almost against the formwork may have sufficient concrete behind it but too little protective material on the exposed face.

Fresh concrete is typically highly alkaline. That chemistry supports a passive protective layer on embedded steel. Over time, carbon dioxide can react with components in the concrete and lower alkalinity in a progressing zone called carbonation. Chloride ions, which may arrive from deicing salt or marine exposure, can also destabilize steel passivity under suitable conditions. Those mechanisms differ, but both can create circumstances in which corrosion develops when moisture and oxygen are available.

Corrosion is not merely a loss of metal thickness. Rust products can occupy greater volume than the original steel, building pressure in surrounding concrete. Cracks may follow the reinforcement; the cover may delaminate and spall away. Once cover is lost, the reinforcement is more exposed, and the deterioration can accelerate. A surface patch that hides visible rust but leaves the corrosion environment active can give an appearance of repair without a durable result.

### Distinguish cracks by mechanism

A thin shrinkage crack, an overload crack, a settlement-related crack, a crack following rebar and a construction joint may look similar in one photograph. Their implications are not the same. Map crack width, location, orientation, length, displacement and any change over time. Note water exposure, rust staining, delamination sounds, nearby drains, salt exposure and evidence of recurring wetting. A structural member with significant spalling near supports deserves a different response from a nonstructural garden slab with a fine stable crack.

Consider a concrete step that chips at the leading edge after several winters. Freeze-thaw action, poor drainage, deicing-salt exposure and physical impact all deserve examination. Now consider a thin slab with rust stains aligned over a steel mesh. Corrosion-induced expansion may be part of the explanation. The repair design must start with the damage mechanism, not merely choose the same bagged patching material for both.

### A revealing measurement problem

A cover meter can estimate the position of reinforcing bars under favorable conditions. Ground-penetrating radar and other methods may help characterize cover and geometry. Neither is a magic measure of the remaining strength of an unknown structure. Hammer sounding can identify zones that sound hollow, but a change in sound may result from geometry as well as delamination. Tests for chloride content, carbonation depth and concrete condition can help establish cause when a consequential repair warrants them. Each method has sampling and interpretation limits.

Suppose a balcony slab has two visible rust stains. Testing might find very shallow cover at the edge, higher chloride concentrations near a salt-exposed surface and active delamination around some bars. The observation, together with the actual structural details, guides whether localized repair is plausible or whether more extensive intervention is required. The owner cannot infer structural capacity merely from the fraction of surface that looks intact.

### Why a repair has to be compatible

A repair must establish a sound substrate, address active corrosion and restore material continuity according to the intended service environment. Surface preparation, reinforcing-steel treatment, bond to existing concrete, curing, drainage and any coating system are connected decisions. A repair mortar may be stronger than the original concrete yet develop incompatible shrinkage or stiffness. New coatings may shift where moisture accumulates. A repair around corroding reinforcement may create electrochemical contrasts that deserve design consideration.

Long-term durability also involves keeping water away where possible. Positive drainage, joints that accommodate movement, details that do not trap deicing brine and maintenance of surface protection can matter as much as the nominal strength printed on a concrete bag. Structural repairs, especially where falling fragments could hurt people or the member carries occupancy loads, require competent investigation and design rather than an improvised patch.

### The final mental model

Think of a reinforced concrete member as a load-carrying composite and as a chemical environment around steel. The geometry and reinforcement must carry the forces. The concrete must keep those reinforcement conditions suitable across years of exposure. A durable repair restores both functions. If the appearance of concrete is all we inspect, we can miss why it deteriorated, and why it may continue to do so.

**System identification:** loadbearing masonry / cavity wall / tied veneer / anchored stone or thin veneer. Document backing structure, gravity support, tie or anchor type, cavities, flashing, drainage outlets, movement joints, and exposure.

### Tracing Rainwater Around Masonry Openings

### 019000 — Tracing Rainwater Around Masonry Openings

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Section exercise.** Draw an opening with veneer, cavity, backup wall, WRB, window, head/lintel flashing, sill drainage and weep outlets. Trace three possible routes: water behind veneer, water through the window assembly, and interior condensation. Identify where an end dam or reverse lap alters the route.

Do not conduct uncontrolled flooding on occupied or deteriorated assemblies. Repair structural corrosion, displaced masonry and hidden backing damage as separate problems.

**Mechanism chart.** Carbonation can reduce steel passivity as a reaction front advances; chloride exposure can trigger localized corrosion even without complete carbonation.
