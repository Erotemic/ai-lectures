# 014000 — Exterior Steps, Slabs, Settlement, and Movement Joints

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A concrete step has begun to pull away from the front walk. A thin crack opens each winter, partly closes in summer, and eventually one edge sits higher than the other. The obvious repair is to fill the crack. But the crack records movement; the material placed in the gap cannot decide what the ground will do next. Masonry and concrete repairs become more durable when we determine whether a joint is expressing normal movement, a loss of support, or a structural defect.

A slab works with the ground beneath it. The concrete distributes loads, and the supporting soil supplies reaction forces. If the soil settles unevenly, the slab bends. Concrete tolerates compression well but has limited tensile capacity, so the underside or top can crack under bending. Even strong concrete may crack when support changes. Making the replacement slab thicker without correcting the support problem can merely produce a heavier slab with the same uneven ground beneath it.

## Settlement is a process, not a crack pattern

Soil compresses when voids close and particles reorganize under load. Disturbed fill, insufficient compaction, washed-out material, decaying organic matter, and altered groundwater paths can all change support over time. Some soil changes occur quickly during construction; others appear over years as moisture cycles or water carries fine particles away.

A concrete walkway beside a house may move differently from a foundation because they sit on different support systems and experience different loads. A slab poured over recently disturbed backfill is especially sensitive to how that fill was placed and compacted. When a walkway tips toward the house, rainwater may be directed to the foundation, which then changes the moisture conditions that influence the soil. A movement problem can therefore amplify a drainage problem.

Vertical displacement, rotation, crack width, and whether the movement is continuing matter more than a single photograph. Mark stable reference points, take measurements at repeated intervals, and record rain, freezing weather, and recent excavation. If one side is rising seasonally, frost heave may be a candidate. If the elevation only drops over time, support loss or settlement becomes more plausible. Neither diagnosis is conclusive without examining site conditions.

## What freezing does below a step

Frost damage is not simply water expanding nine percent in every pore. In frost-susceptible soils, the migration of water toward a freezing front can create ice lenses that lift the ground. The severity depends on freezing conditions, moisture supply, soil grain sizes, and drainage. A well-drained coarse granular layer behaves differently from fine-grained, wet soil. That is one reason local frost depth, base preparation, and runoff control matter to exterior pavements.

An exterior step beside a heated building can have a different frost exposure than a detached walkway. Snow cover, plowed bare surfaces, shade, and drainage can create local variations. If a repair is intended to prevent heave, it must account for the water and ground conditions, not merely the surface appearance of the concrete.

## Why joints are intentionally built into slabs

Concrete shrinks as it dries and changes dimension with temperature. If that movement is restrained, tensile stress accumulates. Control or contraction joints create preferred locations for cracking, usually by reducing the effective section of the slab at deliberate intervals. These joints do not make concrete uncrackable; they influence where cracks develop. Construction joints identify where separate placements meet, and isolation joints allow adjacent elements to move with less force transfer. Expansion and movement joints have specific design roles and details.

A sawcut made after random cracks have formed cannot retroactively control their locations. Nor should every gap be rigidly filled. A movement joint must retain its capacity to accommodate motion; hard material bridging it can transfer loads into adjacent concrete and cause spalling. Joint sealants need compatible geometry, surface preparation, and support such as suitable backer material where specified. The sealant keeps water and debris from entering, but it does not replace the load-bearing design of the slab.

## Steps have an additional geometric burden

On a stair, the height of each riser and the depth of each tread affect how a person moves. A small unexpected difference between adjacent risers can be a trip hazard even when the stair remains structurally sound. If the first step heaves while the porch remains stationary, the series may no longer have consistent geometry. Re-leveling the surface with a patch can create a new discontinuity at the next tread.

The correct analysis includes the elevation of the landing, walkway, individual treads, drainage away from entryways, and the continuity of the stair structure. Handrails, guards, and attachment details are not accessories independent of the structure. Exterior exposure affects anchors and corrosion as well as walking surfaces.

## Diagnose before selecting a repair

Suppose a slab has one clean, relatively stable crack without vertical offset. A compatible sealing strategy may help manage water infiltration, if the joint function is understood. A slab with several displaced pieces and hollow areas beneath them points toward a support or drainage problem. An unstable stair or cracked support wall may require structural evaluation and reconstruction rather than a cosmetic fill. Patching the surface without restoring support is a poor way to test the hypothesis that settlement has stopped.

For any proposed repair, ask how water will move afterward. Will surface runoff be directed away? Is a drainage path being blocked? Will salts or repeated saturation affect the repair material? Can the slab move independently of an adjacent foundation where appropriate? Is the transition to a walkway safe throughout the seasons?

The important lesson is that concrete is only one part of an exterior step or slab. Support, water, temperature, joints, and geometry determine its service life. A durable repair fixes the controlling mechanism and then verifies that the finished walking surface is stable and correctly shaped.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Survey worksheet

Map crack geometry, vertical displacement, slab edge elevations, runoff direction, nearby downspouts, fill/backfill boundaries, evidence of washed-out fines, joint locations, seasonal change and freeze-thaw exposure. Note dates and repeat reference measurements; do not confuse crack appearance with measured movement.

### Identify the joint

| Joint / crack | Intended behavior | Typical concern |
|---|---|---|
| Control/contraction joint | Encourage crack at planned location | Debris, unsuitable rigid fill |
| Isolation joint | Decouple adjacent elements | Bridging prevents intended motion |
| Construction joint | Interface between placements | Load transfer/water detailing |
| Random active crack | Unknown or changing movement | Root cause not resolved by cosmetic patch |

**Review boundary:** Stair/landing dimensions, guards, concrete thickness, reinforcement, frost foundations, and soil remediation require site-specific engineering and adopted code. This is not a structural assessment of an existing cracked stair.
