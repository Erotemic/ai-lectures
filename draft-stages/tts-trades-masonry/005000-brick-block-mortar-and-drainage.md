# A brick wall is not necessarily a solid wall

Stand in front of a brick house and ask what holds up the roof. Sometimes the masonry is structural; sometimes the bricks are a veneer tied back to a wood or steel-framed building. The appearance from the street may be similar, but the forces and water-control details are different. A brick veneer is not merely a thin version of a load-bearing brick wall. It is part of a layered enclosure that should carry its own loads, remain attached under wind forces, and allow water that penetrates the brick to drain out.

Start with the materials. A brick is a manufactured unit; masonry mortar forms the joints between units. Concrete masonry units, or CMUs, are another family with hollow cells and webs that can be combined with reinforcement and grout. Mortar provides bedding, alignment and load transfer, but it is not just glue. Its properties must be compatible with the units, exposure and intended movement. Harder mortar is not always the best mortar, especially when repairing softer, older masonry.

## Compression is only part of the story

Masonry units can perform well under compression. A vertical wall can transfer gravity loads through many courses to its foundation. But wind pushes and pulls on a wall; settlement distorts its shape; temperature and moisture change its dimensions. These actions can create tension, bending and shear, which require suitable wall geometry, reinforcement, ties, anchors, support and movement details.

Consider the difference between a structural CMU wall and brick veneer. In a reinforced masonry wall, vertical reinforcing bars and grout-filled cells can participate in carrying forces, and bond beams can connect parts of the system. In an anchored veneer wall, corrosion-resistant ties transfer lateral loads to backup framing while the veneer usually carries its own weight onto a foundation ledge or engineered support. The precise limits and configuration belong to engineered design and adopted masonry standards. A wall tie whose connection has rusted away can create a serious stability issue even when the visible brick face appears intact.

## Mortar joints are designed interfaces

A horizontal bed joint supports a course. A vertical head joint closes the space between neighboring units. Joint thickness, filling, tooling, and curing influence strength and resistance to rain penetration. Voids and badly consolidated mortar can provide routes for water and air. But a neat face joint does not make a wall waterproof. Water can be absorbed into masonry pores, pass through cracks and mortar interfaces, and reach the concealed side.

That brings us to a paradox. An excellent brick veneer wall is often designed on the assumption that rain will penetrate its outer layer. Behind the brick is a drainage space and a water-control layer on the backup wall. At the base of that space and at other interruptions, flashing collects water and directs it outward through weep openings. If mortar droppings block the cavity or the weeps are sealed, an assembly that looked carefully finished may become a reservoir against the sheathing.

This is why painting over weep holes to 'stop the leak' is typically the wrong move. Weeps are outlets, not defects. The same applies to improperly sealing movement joints without preserving their designed behavior.

## Brick and water have a complicated relationship

Clay brick, concrete block, natural stone, mortar and grout each have their own pore structures and moisture behavior. Some absorb liquid water readily; others are denser. Wet masonry stores water and can release vapor when warmed. In freezing climates, saturation and freeze-thaw cycling can damage susceptible masonry or mortar. Efflorescence, the whitish salt deposits sometimes seen on surfaces, is evidence of water moving dissolved salts to a surface as it evaporates. It is a diagnostic clue, not by itself proof of the particular leak path.

Imagine a brick wall with a dark damp band above a window. The water might be entering at a failed head flashing, a horizontal ledge above, a cracked joint higher up, or an overloaded drainage cavity. Sealing the visible stain may obscure the path while letting moisture continue to accumulate. Trace where water enters, what absorbs it, where it can drain, and how the assembly is intended to dry. Masonry diagnosis uses the same logic as rain-control analysis in framed walls.

## The joint that permits movement

A long wall expands and contracts as temperature changes, while adjacent materials may move by different amounts. A masonry wall also responds to moisture changes and foundation movement. Without suitably located movement joints, the accumulating strain may express itself as cracking at a corner, an opening, or a reentrant geometry. Control and expansion joints are planned ways to accommodate movement; they are not indicators of poor craftsmanship. Their location and detailing depend on material system and structural configuration.

Repairing a crack therefore requires identifying its cause. A crack due to a shifting support is different from one caused by thermal movement; an aged mortar joint is different from fractured brick. Repointing removes deteriorated mortar and replaces it with compatible mortar, but does not automatically correct structural displacement or a failed flashing. A high-strength patch can sometimes transfer stress into weaker neighboring units rather than solve the original problem.

## Read the layers and the load path

For a learning exercise, draw three sketches: a reinforced CMU wall; an anchored brick veneer over framed backup; and an older multi-wythe brick wall. For each, label what supports gravity load, what resists lateral load, how water exits, and what joints accommodate movement. Then ask which details would be visible during inspection and which would be concealed.

The transferable lesson is that masonry serves several roles at once: structure, enclosure, finish, and weathering surface. A sound repair respects the distinctions. Strong bricks alone do not create a sound masonry wall, and perfect-looking mortar alone does not create a dry one.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Anatomy of a drained veneer

Record the veneer support, ties/anchors to backup, drainage cavity, WRB, through-wall flashing, end dams where applicable, and functioning weeps. Track water at window heads, shelf angles, and the base. Clean cavities and compatible corrosion-resistant accessories are design-specific requirements.

### Inspection questions

- Is this load-bearing masonry, reinforced CMU, anchored veneer, or a multi-wythe older wall?
- Are observed cracks from movement, settlement, moisture expansion, corrosion, or material deterioration?
- Does repointing use appropriate mortar for the existing masonry?
- Are wall ties, flashing and weeps accessible and functioning?

Consult applicable TMS 402/602 requirements and manufacturer/engineer details for structural and anchored veneer work; these notes explain principles but do not size reinforcement, supports or ties.
