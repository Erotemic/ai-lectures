# Place concrete on a ground-and-movement model

## Cement Is Not Concrete: Hydration, Aggregates, and the Ground Beneath

Imagine two sidewalks poured on the same day. One becomes a durable surface. The other develops cracks, a dusty top layer, and patches that flake apart after a winter. It would be easy to blame the brand of cement. But concrete performance depends on much more than a bag label: the aggregate, the water added, the proportioning of ingredients, placement, consolidation, finishing, curing, ground support, weather exposure, and even the order in which work was done.

The first vocabulary distinction is that *cement* is usually a binder, while *concrete* is a composite material made from cementitious materials, water, aggregates such as sand and stone, and possibly other ingredients. Mortar is a different material designed in part to bond masonry units together. Grout fills spaces and transfers loads in specified assemblies. A brick wall is not simply vertical concrete, and concrete is not simply a liquid that becomes rock when left alone.

### A chemical reaction, not just evaporation

Portland cement contains minerals that react with water. The broad process is called hydration. Its reaction products progressively bind aggregate particles into a hardened mass. Concrete needs appropriate moisture and temperature conditions during early development. If a surface dries too soon, the hydration process near that surface may not proceed as intended, potentially weakening the surface and affecting permeability and durability.

This explains a counterintuitive fact: adding water to fresh concrete can make it easier to work but may reduce its eventual strength and durability when the mix design is otherwise unchanged. More water leaves more capillary porosity after the material hardens, unless the mixture and hydration are considered carefully. Yet sufficient water must be available for cement hydration, and construction workability matters. The objective is not "as dry as possible" but a properly designed mixture placed and cured as intended.

Finishing also has timing. Working a surface while bleed water remains can trap or disrupt a weak near-surface layer. A beautifully smooth finish is not proof of sound underlying material. Overworking, environmental conditions, and curing may determine whether the top millimeters survive abrasion and freeze-thaw exposure.

### Aggregate is part of the engineering

Concrete contains far more aggregate by volume than the everyday word "cement" implies. Aggregate particles form a skeleton and affect workability, shrinkage, thermal behavior, and strength. Their sizes, grading, shape, cleanliness, and compatibility matter. The paste must bind them and fill voids efficiently.

Picture filling a bucket with large stones. There are spaces between them. Smaller stones can occupy some spaces; sand can occupy smaller spaces still. A well-proportioned aggregate grading can reduce the paste needed for a workable, dense composite. But the bucket analogy is incomplete because concrete must flow or be consolidated into forms and around reinforcement. Too little paste or poor compaction can leave honeycombed voids. Too much paste may increase cost or shrinkage. Proper proportioning balances competing requirements.

Air entrainment is another deliberate engineering choice for many concretes exposed to freezing and thawing. A network of microscopic air voids can relieve pressure associated with freezing water. This is different from accidentally trapping large air pockets because a mixture was inadequately consolidated. A visible bubble is not automatically evidence of freeze-thaw protection.

### Compression is only part of the story

Concrete has useful compressive strength but relatively modest tensile capacity. It may crack where flexural or direct tensile stresses develop. Steel reinforcement is frequently placed so the combined system can resist tension and control cracking, provided the reinforcement is correctly designed, positioned, developed, and protected. A slab with some mesh thrown near the bottom is not equivalent to a designed reinforced slab with adequate cover and support.

Reinforcement does not eliminate every crack. Concrete also shrinks, temperature changes cause movement, and soil support can change. Control joints are intended to influence where shrinkage-related cracks form; isolation joints accommodate selected movement between elements. Structural and movement joints have distinct purposes, so the decision to cut a line in concrete needs more than a generic spacing rule.

### The ground is an active participant

A slab rests on a support system. If the soil or granular base settles unevenly, the slab can bend or lose support. If frost-susceptible soil contains enough water and freezes under favorable conditions, ice lenses can grow and heave the ground. Drainage, base preparation, compaction, and frost-depth design can be as important as the concrete mixture itself.

Consider a walkway that rises at one edge each winter, then settles in spring. Replacing cracked sections with thicker concrete without addressing frost action or underlying water may repeat the failure. A good diagnosis asks about slope, water sources, subgrade material, compaction, drainage, and temperature history. The concrete records the problem; it does not necessarily cause it.

Foundations must carry loads into soil at pressures and deformations the ground can sustain. That requires a geotechnical picture and structural design. A small crack can be cosmetically harmless, a moisture pathway, or a sign of significant movement depending on its pattern and history. Crack width alone is not a complete risk assessment. Track whether it changes, whether adjacent surfaces move, and how water behaves before choosing a repair.

### Masonry walls carry and shed water

Brick and concrete masonry units can absorb water into their pores. Mortar joints are both connectors and potential water pathways. The assembly may be designed to manage rain through flashings, cavities, drainage openings, and weep paths rather than by assuming the exposed face never gets wet. In many walls, a drainage plane and flashing details are what keep water that penetrates the outer layer from reaching sensitive interior materials.

A tempting repair is to smear impermeable coating over every visible joint. But if water enters from above or behind, that coating may reduce outward drying without eliminating the water source. Repointing mortar also requires compatibility with the masonry units. Extremely hard or poorly matched mortar can change where stresses and moisture accumulate, especially in older masonry. Repair requires understanding the wall, not merely making all mortar joints look new.

### Anchors transfer loads through a substrate

When mounting equipment to masonry, the anchor, drilled hole, base material, embedment, edge distance, spacing, condition of the masonry, and direction of loading form one system. Expansion anchors, screw anchors, adhesive anchors, and through-bolts use different mechanisms. A strong steel bolt cannot compensate for a cracked or weak substrate. Dust in a hole may matter greatly to some adhesive systems. An anchor designed for solid concrete may not perform the same way in hollow brick or deteriorating mortar.

Even the drilling operation depends on knowing what lies behind the wall: reinforcement, utilities, waterproofing, historic fabric, and voids. A product's tested installation instructions are part of the engineering, not optional advice. Structural anchors and overhead suspended loads deserve project-specific design and verification.

### A complete explanation of failure

Return to the damaged sidewalk. We would record whether cracks follow joint spacing or settlement, whether the surface dusts or flakes, whether salt and freeze-thaw exposure are involved, and whether water pools near the slab. We would ask about the mixture, curing, temperature at placement, and ground preparation. These clues point toward different mechanisms, and several can coexist.

The central lesson is that concrete is a developing chemical composite supported by a physical environment. Masonry is an assembly of units, joints, ties, drainage details, and supports. Good work is not simply making a material hard. It is controlling how loads, water, heat, and time act on the entire construction.

## Hydration, Mix Design, and Curing

The distinction between cement and concrete becomes practical when the development of the material is followed through mixing and curing.

Pour water into a bowl of sand and let it dry. The sand may form a crust, but it does not become a durable stone. Mix water into portland cement, however, and a family of chemical reactions begins. The material gradually develops a connected solid structure. When aggregates are present, that structure binds them into concrete. It is tempting to say the wet mixture “dries into rock,” but the important process is hydration, not simply evaporation.

A concrete slab can be damaged by losing water too fast. This is initially counterintuitive. The surface looks wet; surely getting rid of water will make it hard sooner? In fact, cement hydration needs water to proceed. Curing means managing conditions after placing the concrete so that its potential properties can develop. The American Concrete Institute's explanation explicitly connects curing to the moisture and temperature needed for hydration and to the reduction of damaging volume changes.

### What is reacting?

Portland cement is manufactured from mineral raw materials that are heated and processed into clinker and then ground, often with additional constituents. When cement meets water, several reactions begin. The hydration products include calcium silicate hydrate, often shortened to C-S-H, which contributes strongly to binding and strength, along with other products such as calcium hydroxide. The chemistry is more involved than a single reaction that turns all water into solid material. Some water becomes chemically bound; some occupies pores; some can escape.

Sand and crushed stone are usually much of the mixture by volume. These aggregates do not serve merely as cheap filler. Their size distribution, shape, cleanliness, stiffness and interaction with paste affect workability, shrinkage, stiffness and many aspects of durability. Cement paste alone behaves differently from concrete containing a well-designed aggregate skeleton.

### Why adding water is not a free improvement

Imagine two batches with the same cement and aggregates. The first is stiff. Someone adds extra water until it flows easily. The concrete may become more convenient to place, but it now has a higher ratio of water to cementitious material. Much of the water beyond what the reactions consume will eventually leave behind capillary pores. Under comparable mix and curing conditions, excessive water tends to increase permeability and reduce strength and durability.

This does not mean “as little water as possible” is a universal recipe. The material must still be properly mixed, placed, consolidated and cured. Admixtures can change workability without simply increasing free water. A low water-to-cementitious-material ratio may also demand more careful curing, because the paste can consume available internal moisture and limit continued hydration. Mix design is a system of tradeoffs, not a single magic ratio.

### The first hours are not the final strength

Concrete changes after it is mixed. Early reactions and particle interactions affect setting, the transition from workable material to something that resists deformation. Strength continues to develop after set. A slab that can support a foot impression-free is not necessarily ready for a heavy point load. A standard reference age for comparing many concretes is twenty-eight days, but that number is a reporting convention, not an instant at which all reactions stop. Strength development depends on composition and the history of moisture and temperature.

Temperature complicates this story. Warm conditions often accelerate early hydration, so a mixture can set faster and develop early strength sooner. But very high curing temperatures can alter the developing microstructure and reduce later strength under some conditions. Cold conditions slow hydration. If concrete freezes before achieving sufficient resistance, its structure may be damaged. This is why both hot- and cold-weather concreting have specialized procedures and why a generic “wait two days” rule is unreliable.

### Curing is a controlled environment

There are multiple ways to provide curing conditions. Wet coverings, water supply, membranes, blankets or controlled enclosures may be used depending on the mixture, geometry, finish, weather and intended performance. The common goal is to control moisture loss and temperature during the period when the material is developing properties. Curing compounds, when used, create a membrane that restricts evaporation; they are not a substitute for meeting every surface finish or subsequent adhesion requirement.

Thin exposed slabs are particularly sensitive to wind, low humidity and heat because they have substantial surface area compared with their volume. Evaporation can outpace bleeding water rising to the surface. Then the surface may shrink while the underlying concrete is still plastic, producing plastic-shrinkage cracks. Other cracks arise later from drying shrinkage, thermal movement, restraint, settlement or load. Calling every crack a “curing crack” is not diagnosis.

### Reinforcement and joints cannot fix everything

Steel reinforcement can help resist tension and control crack opening in designed concrete members. It does not make cement paste stop shrinking. Joints are often arranged to manage where movement occurs, but they do not eliminate the underlying dimensional change. A driveway, a foundation wall and a structural beam have different load, exposure and reinforcement requirements. Knowing the chemistry does not authorize a one-size-fits-all reinforcement plan.

Consider a small pad that will support equipment. Its long-term performance depends on soil support and drainage, geometry, load, reinforcement if needed, concrete quality, placement, consolidation, finishing, joints and curing. Perfect cement chemistry on poor subgrade will not prevent settlement. Similarly, good subgrade cannot compensate for a porous mixture damaged by an early freeze. Each stage matters.

### Observation beats appearance alone

A smooth gray surface is not proof of good concrete. Some defects remain internal. Where a project warrants it, technicians evaluate delivery records, placement conditions, curing history, cores or specified strength tests. Cracks are assessed by pattern, timing, location, movement and consequence. A hairline surface crack and an actively widening structural crack are not the same problem.

There is an instructive analogy to cooking. Ingredients matter, but the process and temperature history determine the final structure. Yet concrete has an added twist: water is part of the chemical reaction, and letting the mixture lose water too early can prevent the material from reaching its potential. When we say “curing,” we mean allowing cement chemistry and microstructure to develop under appropriate conditions. The job does not end when the surface looks dry.

## Compression, Tension, Reinforcement, and Joints

Walk across an ordinary concrete sidewalk and look for the straight grooves dividing it into panels. Then look for the finer, less orderly cracks that sometimes cross the panels. The grooves are usually intentional. The cracks may be expected to some degree, but their pattern can still reveal something about how the concrete was placed, supported, cured, and restrained.

Concrete is often called strong, but that adjective is incomplete. It generally resists compression much better than tension. And its strength changes as the cement hydrates and the material matures. These two facts explain the need for reinforcement, the importance of joints, and why a slab can crack without immediately failing as a structure.

### Push on it; pull on it

Compression tends to squeeze a specimen shorter. Tension tends to pull it longer. Concrete's hydrated cement paste binds aggregates into a rigid composite that can carry substantial compressive stress. Tiny flaws and the interfaces between paste and aggregate make its direct tensile behavior comparatively weak. Once a crack forms, ordinary unreinforced concrete has limited ability to carry tensile force directly across that open crack.

That does not mean every part of a concrete element experiences compression. Think of a simply supported horizontal beam carrying a downward load. It bends. In the familiar ideal case, the upper fibers tend to shorten in compression while the lower fibers stretch in tension. Reversing the loading or support arrangement can change where tensile stress occurs. Designing with concrete therefore requires knowing the force path, not merely selecting a compressive-strength label from a bag.

### Steel changes what the composite can do

Reinforced concrete places steel bars or other suitable reinforcement where the structure needs tensile resistance and crack control. The concrete surrounds the steel, transfers force to it through bond, and protects it when the cover, exposure conditions, and material quality are adequate. Reinforcement layout matters: steel in the wrong place may do little for the actual tension zone.

Picture a loaded slab or beam. Concrete and steel do not work as two independent parts, one stacked on the other. With proper bond and anchorage they share strain over relevant regions and transfer stress across their interface. A bar's development length, bend detail, cover, splice, and location can be as important as how much steel is present. A photograph showing 'there is rebar' is not a proof that a structural design is sound.

Masonry structures introduce related but not identical behavior. Mortar joints, unit shape, reinforcement, grouting, and confinement all affect load paths and cracking. A brick wall and a reinforced cast-in-place beam cannot be analyzed as interchangeable concrete objects merely because both are hard and mineral-based.

### Concrete also moves when nobody loads it

Fresh and maturing concrete changes volume. As moisture leaves, drying shrinkage can occur. Temperature changes also cause expansion and contraction. These strains are not automatically dangerous when a piece is free to move. The trouble begins when movement is restrained by the ground, a neighboring wall, a rigid connection, a change in thickness, or even uneven drying across a slab.

If a material wants to contract but is prevented, tensile stress develops. If that stress exceeds the local capacity, a crack forms. The location of the crack depends on geometry, restraints, weak planes, reinforcement, and the history of the concrete. This is why more compressive strength alone does not guarantee crack-free flatwork. Mixture proportions, curing, placement temperature, reinforcement, and joint details all matter.

Reinforcement can distribute cracking into more numerous and narrower cracks rather than eliminating cracking. That is a central distinction. 'Rebar prevents cracks' is an incorrect expectation. It can help control crack width and, when engineered for the structural role, provide tensile capacity after concrete has cracked.

### Different joints do different jobs

A contraction joint creates a planned weakened location so that shrinkage-related cracking is more likely to occur along that line. In a slab, this can be made by a properly timed and detailed saw cut or other suitable method. It does not make shrinkage disappear; it changes where the movement can localize.

An isolation joint separates elements that need to move somewhat independently, such as a slab next to a fixed column or wall in appropriate designs. A construction joint marks an interface between separate placements. It may need special reinforcement, preparation, or load-transfer details depending on the structure. Confusing these categories is a common route to misunderstanding: a decorative groove is not automatically an adequate movement joint, and a cold joint is not automatically an intentionally engineered construction joint.

Imagine pouring a rectangular patio slab beside a foundation. Without thinking about restraints, one might bond everything rigidly together and expect the entire surface to remain seamless forever. In reality, the slab and foundation may experience different shrinkage, temperature changes, and settlement. A designer must decide which interfaces should transfer load and which should accommodate movement. Those goals can be in tension with each other.

### Read a crack as evidence

A hairline crack confined to surface paste after curing is a different observation from a wide crack showing differential vertical movement. A crack continuing through a wall, changing over time, or accompanied by settlement carries different implications. Its width alone is not an all-purpose diagnosis. Location, timing, direction, water exposure, support conditions, and change over time are relevant.

Surface repairs that cover a crack without understanding the movement can fail again. An epoxy that bonds a static crack may be inappropriate for a joint designed to move. A flexible joint sealant has different purposes from a structural repair. Likewise, patching the surface of a slab does not correct an unstable base underneath it.

Return to the sidewalk grooves. The builder is not admitting failure by including them. They are part of a strategy for material movement. Concrete is not a stone that remains perfectly fixed after it hardens. It is a composite that gains strength, exchanges moisture, changes temperature, carries stresses, and responds to restraint. Durable masonry work begins by understanding all of those behaviors together.

## Footings, Compaction, and Frost

Material strength does not remove the need for a foundation and ground that can support the intended arrangement.

A concrete footing can be thick, carefully reinforced, and well cured, yet the structure above it still moves. Why? Because the footing does not float in isolation. It passes load into soil. Whether the building settles evenly, settles unevenly, or heaves upward in winter depends on the ground's properties, moisture, temperature, and load history as well as the strength of the concrete.

Think of the footing as a device for spreading force. A concentrated post load becomes a pressure distributed over an area of soil. For the simplest mental model, average bearing pressure is load divided by area. Doubling the bearing area can halve the average pressure for the same centered load. But soil is not a uniform spring. It contains mineral grains, water, air, voids, roots, layers, and sometimes previous fill or buried material. Its ability to carry load without unacceptable settlement depends on those conditions.

### Soil is an assembly of grains and voids

Sand and gravel are granular materials with frictional contact between particles. Clay and silt behave differently because particle size, surface chemistry, and water strongly affect their response. Loosely placed fill may change shape when loaded because particles rearrange into a denser configuration. If one side of a foundation bears on well-established natural ground and the other on poorly compacted fill, unequal movement may occur even if each footing is identical.

Compaction deliberately reduces air-filled void space by mechanical effort at appropriate moisture content. Soil that is too dry may resist rearrangement; soil that is too wet may lose effective support or become difficult to densify. Different soils respond differently to vibratory rollers, tampers, and other equipment. Professional earthwork uses defined materials, lift thicknesses, moisture control, and field density or other acceptance tests. Pressing soil with a shovel until it 'feels hard' is not a substitute for a specified, verified bearing preparation.

### Frost can push from below

In freezing regions, frost heave is not simply the small volume change of a puddle freezing in place. In susceptible fine-grained soils, water can migrate toward the freezing front and form ice lenses. As these lenses accumulate, they can lift soil and structures. The process requires freezing temperatures, a frost-susceptible soil, and an available source of water. Remove or control any one of those ingredients and the risk changes, although real ground conditions rarely fit a simple on-off model.

Imagine a walkway that rises along one edge every winter and settles in spring. One location may be wetter or contain finer soil than another. When the thaw comes, the same wet ground may temporarily lose stiffness, allowing settlement or rutting. Repeated cycles can tilt steps, crack slabs, and shift attached structures. This is why drainage belongs in the same discussion as foundation design.

Footings in cold climates are commonly designed to resist frost effects through sufficient embedment or an approved frost-protection strategy such as an engineered insulation design. The appropriate depth is not one universal number: climate, site, building use, soil, and local adopted requirements matter. A shallow slab or deck pier can sometimes be designed successfully, but only by respecting the frost, water, and load conditions instead of ignoring them.

### Water pressure also acts sideways

Basement walls interact with soil that exerts lateral pressure. Water standing in poorly drained backfill can add hydrostatic pressure. Foundation drainage systems, surface grading, waterproofing or damp-proofing layers, and appropriate backfill can change those loads and the routes by which water reaches the wall. A wall that leaks does not necessarily need more interior paint; it may need an examination of outside water sources and drainage pathways.

The same lesson applies to retaining walls. A wall that holds back several feet of soil is not just a stack of heavy blocks. It must resist sliding, overturning, and pressure from retained soil and water. Drainage is often integral to the intended design. A seemingly minor change in grade, surcharge from vehicles, or blocked outlet can change the loading significantly.

### Movement tells a story, but not always the whole story

A crack can arise from concrete shrinkage, thermal effects, differential settlement, frost heave, loading, or multiple processes together. Its location, direction, width, displacement, moisture history, and change over time are clues. A horizontal crack in a basement wall has different implications from a hairline crack at a shrinkage joint in a slab. A single photograph at one date seldom resolves the cause.

A useful inspection record includes the crack's endpoints, measured offset or width, date, weather context, nearby grading and drains, and whether windows or doors have begun to bind. There is no safe universal crack-width cutoff that determines whether a building is structurally sound. The consequences depend on the assembly and loading.

Concrete strength and reinforcement matter, but they are only part of the foundation system. The larger system includes soil, water, temperature, and load transfer. If you want to understand what the concrete is doing, begin by asking what the ground beneath and beside it is doing.

## Exterior Steps, Slabs, Settlement, and Movement Joints

A concrete step has begun to pull away from the front walk. A thin crack opens each winter, partly closes in summer, and eventually one edge sits higher than the other. The obvious repair is to fill the crack. But the crack records movement; the material placed in the gap cannot decide what the ground will do next. Masonry and concrete repairs become more durable when we determine whether a joint is expressing normal movement, a loss of support, or a structural defect.

A slab works with the ground beneath it. The concrete distributes loads, and the supporting soil supplies reaction forces. If the soil settles unevenly, the slab bends. Concrete tolerates compression well but has limited tensile capacity, so the underside or top can crack under bending. Even strong concrete may crack when support changes. Making the replacement slab thicker without correcting the support problem can merely produce a heavier slab with the same uneven ground beneath it.

### Settlement is a process, not a crack pattern

Soil compresses when voids close and particles reorganize under load. Disturbed fill, insufficient compaction, washed-out material, decaying organic matter, and altered groundwater paths can all change support over time. Some soil changes occur quickly during construction; others appear over years as moisture cycles or water carries fine particles away.

A concrete walkway beside a house may move differently from a foundation because they sit on different support systems and experience different loads. A slab poured over recently disturbed backfill is especially sensitive to how that fill was placed and compacted. When a walkway tips toward the house, rainwater may be directed to the foundation, which then changes the moisture conditions that influence the soil. A movement problem can therefore amplify a drainage problem.

Vertical displacement, rotation, crack width, and whether the movement is continuing matter more than a single photograph. Mark stable reference points, take measurements at repeated intervals, and record rain, freezing weather, and recent excavation. If one side is rising seasonally, frost heave may be a candidate. If the elevation only drops over time, support loss or settlement becomes more plausible. Neither diagnosis is conclusive without examining site conditions.

### What freezing does below a step

Frost damage is not simply water expanding nine percent in every pore. In frost-susceptible soils, the migration of water toward a freezing front can create ice lenses that lift the ground. The severity depends on freezing conditions, moisture supply, soil grain sizes, and drainage. A well-drained coarse granular layer behaves differently from fine-grained, wet soil. That is one reason local frost depth, base preparation, and runoff control matter to exterior pavements.

An exterior step beside a heated building can have a different frost exposure than a detached walkway. Snow cover, plowed bare surfaces, shade, and drainage can create local variations. If a repair is intended to prevent heave, it must account for the water and ground conditions, not merely the surface appearance of the concrete.

### Why joints are intentionally built into slabs

Concrete shrinks as it dries and changes dimension with temperature. If that movement is restrained, tensile stress accumulates. Control or contraction joints create preferred locations for cracking, usually by reducing the effective section of the slab at deliberate intervals. These joints do not make concrete uncrackable; they influence where cracks develop. Construction joints identify where separate placements meet, and isolation joints allow adjacent elements to move with less force transfer. Expansion and movement joints have specific design roles and details.

A sawcut made after random cracks have formed cannot retroactively control their locations. Nor should every gap be rigidly filled. A movement joint must retain its capacity to accommodate motion; hard material bridging it can transfer loads into adjacent concrete and cause spalling. Joint sealants need compatible geometry, surface preparation, and support such as suitable backer material where specified. The sealant keeps water and debris from entering, but it does not replace the load-bearing design of the slab.

### Steps have an additional geometric burden

On a stair, the height of each riser and the depth of each tread affect how a person moves. A small unexpected difference between adjacent risers can be a trip hazard even when the stair remains structurally sound. If the first step heaves while the porch remains stationary, the series may no longer have consistent geometry. Re-leveling the surface with a patch can create a new discontinuity at the next tread.

The correct analysis includes the elevation of the landing, walkway, individual treads, drainage away from entryways, and the continuity of the stair structure. Handrails, guards, and attachment details are not accessories independent of the structure. Exterior exposure affects anchors and corrosion as well as walking surfaces.

### Diagnose before selecting a repair

Suppose a slab has one clean, relatively stable crack without vertical offset. A compatible sealing strategy may help manage water infiltration, if the joint function is understood. A slab with several displaced pieces and hollow areas beneath them points toward a support or drainage problem. An unstable stair or cracked support wall may require structural evaluation and reconstruction rather than a cosmetic fill. Patching the surface without restoring support is a poor way to test the hypothesis that settlement has stopped.

For any proposed repair, ask how water will move afterward. Will surface runoff be directed away? Is a drainage path being blocked? Will salts or repeated saturation affect the repair material? Can the slab move independently of an adjacent foundation where appropriate? Is the transition to a walkway safe throughout the seasons?

The important lesson is that concrete is only one part of an exterior step or slab. Support, water, temperature, joints, and geometry determine its service life. A durable repair fixes the controlling mechanism and then verifies that the finished walking surface is stable and correctly shaped.

### Cement Is Not Concrete: Hydration, Aggregates, and the Ground Beneath

**Track:** Masonry and Concrete. **Background:** Ordinary arithmetic. **Purpose:** establish the material science and load/moisture behavior behind concrete and masonry work.

#### Technical companion

**Concepts:** Cement hydration develops a hardened binder; curing maintains suitable moisture and temperature; lower water-to-cementitious-material ratio may increase strength under appropriately designed placement and curing conditions; effective air entrainment for freeze-thaw exposure differs from entrapped voids.

**Evidence map:** American Concrete Institute Committee 308 defines curing as the management of moisture and temperature so cementitious reactions develop desired properties. ACI reference information addresses the need for curing and its dependence on mix and conditions. Product-specific masonry-anchor tables and applicable engineering codes, not introductory rules of thumb, govern real load-bearing connections.

**Desk exercise:** For a cracked exterior stair landing, diagram four distinct causal paths: plastic-shrinkage or drying-shrinkage cracking, unsupported substrate, freeze-thaw deterioration, and water penetration. Name an observation that helps discriminate each.

### Hydration, Mix Design, and Curing

**Track:** Masonry and Concrete. **Background:** Cement, aggregate, concrete and foundations. **Purpose:** explain why mixture proportions and post-placement conditions control concrete's development.

#### Technical companion: quantities, mechanisms and scope

**Definitions:** `w/cm` is the mass ratio of mixing water to total cementitious materials; it is not the percentage of water in the fresh mix. `Curing` manages moisture and temperature after placement. `Setting` describes loss of workability/transition to rigidity, not attainment of design strength.

**Mechanism audit:** Cement hydration forms products including C-S-H; capillary porosity and workability depend on mixture and consolidation; curing reduces premature moisture loss. ACI's *What Is Curing?* and *How Does Temperature Influence Curing?* are explicit authoritative committee responses. The different mechanisms of plastic, drying and thermal cracking should not be conflated.

**Planning questions:** What is the specified strength and exposure class? What is the mixture and design `w/cm`? What are ambient and subgrade temperatures, wind and humidity? How will the surface retain moisture? Who controls placement, finishing, joints, testing and curing? Answers depend on project documents and adopted standards.

**Exercise:** Compare two hypothetical identical batches in windy, dry weather. One is protected to retain moisture; the other is left exposed. Predict where and when each might differ, separating early surface cracking from long-term strength development.

### Compression, Tension, Reinforcement, and Joints

**Track:** Masonry and Concrete. **Background:** Cement hydration and curing. **Purpose:** explain why a strong slab can crack and how structural reinforcement differs from crack control.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Mechanics and nomenclature

- Stress: `σ=F/A` for uniform axial loading. This formula alone does not analyze flexure, shear, anchorage, or stability.
- Strain: `ε=ΔL/L`. Restrained free shrinkage or thermal strain can generate stress.
- **Contraction joint:** planned crack-localization or movement feature in appropriate concrete systems.
- **Isolation joint:** separation to permit relative movement between elements.
- **Construction joint:** interface between separate placements; may be structural depending on design.

ACI 224R discusses crack-control strategies; ACI 302.1R covers floor slab construction, joint arrangements, and the expectation that some random cracks can occur despite good practices.

##### Field design status

Do not infer rebar size, embedment, saw-cut timing/depth, joint spacing, load capacity, or acceptable crack width from this general lesson. These depend on structural engineering, material properties, member dimensions, climate, site conditions, adopted regulations, and referenced ACI documents. Professional structural review remains required for consequential repairs or design changes.

### Footings, Compaction, and Frost

**Track:** Masonry and Concrete. **Background:** Concrete curing, stress, reinforcement, and movement joints. **Purpose:** explain how the ground, not just the concrete, controls the behavior of foundations and slabs.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Simplified pressure model

For a centered vertical load \(P\) on a rectangular footing of width \(B\) and length \(L\), \(q_{average}=P/(BL)\). This is an *average contact pressure*, not a determination of allowable bearing capacity or settlement. Eccentricity, load combinations, uplift, frost and lateral forces require more sophisticated analysis. Avoid applying this model to retaining-wall stability or designing a foundation.

##### Site-history exercise

Draft a section showing footing, compacted or natural bearing soil, fill layers, final grade, surface drainage, groundwater indication, frost zone, and any perimeter drain. Annotate which soil properties are observed and which are unknown. Compare settlement (movement down) with frost heave (movement up) and distinguish each from material shrinkage.

##### Review boundary

Subgrade acceptance, compaction testing, required bearing depth, drainage and retaining-wall design are site- and jurisdiction-specific. FHWA and USDA technical sources explain mechanisms; they do not certify a private foundation design. Structural changes or recurrent movement call for a qualified assessment.

### Exterior Steps, Slabs, Settlement, and Movement Joints

### 014000 — Exterior Steps, Slabs, Settlement, and Movement Joints

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Survey worksheet

Map crack geometry, vertical displacement, slab edge elevations, runoff direction, nearby downspouts, fill/backfill boundaries, evidence of washed-out fines, joint locations, seasonal change and freeze-thaw exposure. Note dates and repeat reference measurements; do not confuse crack appearance with measured movement.

##### Identify the joint

| Joint / crack | Intended behavior | Typical concern |
|---|---|---|
| Control/contraction joint | Encourage crack at planned location | Debris, unsuitable rigid fill |
| Isolation joint | Decouple adjacent elements | Bridging prevents intended motion |
| Construction joint | Interface between placements | Load transfer/water detailing |
| Random active crack | Unknown or changing movement | Root cause not resolved by cosmetic patch |

**Review boundary:** Stair/landing dimensions, guards, concrete thickness, reinforcement, frost foundations, and soil remediation require site-specific engineering and adopted code. This is not a structural assessment of an existing cracked stair.
