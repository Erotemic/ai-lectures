# 019000 — Tracing Rainwater Around Masonry Openings

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A brown stain develops beneath a brick-faced window after a windy rain. Someone points at a cracked mortar joint and recommends sealing it. Perhaps the crack matters. But brick veneer is not normally designed on the assumption that not a drop of rain will pass its outer face. It is a water-management assembly. The diagnostic task is to learn where incoming water is supposed to go, and where it is going instead.

A typical drained veneer has an outer brick layer, a cavity, a water-resistive barrier against the backing, flashings at interruptions and openings to discharge water. The wall may also have insulation, air-control layers, ties and structural backing. Each has a different role. Brick and mortar reduce exposure but can absorb water. A drainage cavity creates a path downward, while properly detailed flashing redirects that water outward. If the path ends at a window frame or dead pocket, the veneer can perform exactly as its materials permit and still leak into the building.

## Read the opening as a water course

Imagine a window with brick above it. Rain hits the wall, some is absorbed by the masonry and some may enter through joints. Water travels down the back of the veneer until it reaches a horizontal interruption. Above the window head, through-wall flashing should intercept the path and provide a route to the exterior, usually with appropriate ends and drainage outlets. A lintel may support the brick but does not, merely by existing, serve as reliable water-shedding flashing.

At the window sill, a different assembly must manage water reaching the window perimeter. The window's own drainage design, its pan or sill detail where used, the weather-resistive barrier and surrounding masonry must be compatible. If water meets a reverse lap, it may pass behind the intended water-control layer. If a flashing stops inside the wall with no outward termination, liquid can pool. If a cavity is bridged by mortar droppings, water can be directed toward vulnerable sheathing rather than drained away.

There is no single generic window detail valid for every veneer, backup wall and climate. The point is to trace gravity, capillary movement, pressure-driven rain, and drainage outlets across the specific assembly.

## Start with the weather history

A leak that occurs only during wind from one direction suggests a different exposure than a stain appearing every morning in cold weather. Record rain intensity, duration, wind direction, interior temperature and the position of the wet spot. The interior stain may be below, beside or even several feet away from the true entry. Water can follow lintels, framing, membranes or fasteners before becoming visible.

Inspect the exterior from safe access. Photograph mortar joints, parapets, roof-wall interfaces, coping, sealants, lintels, flashings, weeps and grade. Look for displaced brick, clogged openings, rust stains, cracks that move, and areas where newer repairs interrupt older drainage. A blocked weep is a clue, but an open weep does not prove that the concealed flashing is properly installed. A visible caulk bead can conceal an incomplete joint.

Then examine the indoor evidence. Does moisture appear along the top of the window, at the jamb, or below the sill? Is there evidence of condensation on the glazing or frame? Are walls wet after prolonged rain even without a particular wind direction? Moisture instruments can map the extent of wetness but require interpretation for material and depth. Metal ties, salts and different substrates can affect measurements.

## Why indiscriminate hose testing can mislead

It is tempting to turn a garden hose on the whole wall and see where water appears. A large, uncontrolled spray can wet details that would not ordinarily receive that concentration, flood a drainage cavity beyond its intended capacity or make it impossible to know which location started the leak. The test itself may damage an already vulnerable assembly. Standardized field water tests have defined equipment, pressures, procedures and acceptance criteria, and they require interpretation appropriate to the wall system. A garden hose is not a substitute for a recognized standard test.

A better initial method is a documented hypothesis. If the first suspicion is a failed sill transition, the investigation plan should distinguish water entering through the window from water arriving behind the masonry above it. If the suspicion is the head flashing, opening a suitable inspection area may reveal laps and outlets more directly than repeatedly wetting an unknown wall. When controlled water testing is warranted, it should be carried out under an appropriate professional protocol with protection against unnecessary damage.

A second trap is to treat efflorescence as proof of exactly where water entered. White salts can appear where moisture evaporates, which may be far from its starting point. They reveal water movement and soluble material, but not a unique route. Likewise, a crack in the outer wythe may admit water without being the reason the interior became wet; a correct drainage layer might handle that water safely.

## Repair the path, then verify the result

Imagine the investigation reveals that flashing above a window turns down behind the backing instead of draining outward. Sealing the mortar around the head may make the stain disappear in one light rain, but the flawed path remains. A durable correction would restore the intended flashing connection and outlet, and address any damaged backing or metal. The exact reconstruction depends on structural support, the veneer system and the condition of concealed components.

After repair, preserve photographs of what was uncovered and how the new drainage path connects. Inspect for new wetting under comparable weather, or use an appropriate controlled verification test. Water damage can take time to dry; a persistent moisture reading does not necessarily mean continued entry, and a dry surface after one day does not prove the repair is durable. Track the trend.

Brick is durable partly because it can manage wetting and drying. The craft of diagnosis is to respect that system rather than try to turn its entire exterior into a perfect waterproof film. Follow the water from impact, through any entry, down the concealed path, to its designed exit. The failure usually becomes more intelligible when the wall is understood in section rather than viewed only as a flat facade.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Section exercise.** Draw an opening with veneer, cavity, backup wall, WRB, window, head/lintel flashing, sill drainage and weep outlets. Trace three possible routes: water behind veneer, water through the window assembly, and interior condensation. Identify where an end dam or reverse lap alters the route.

**Evidence form.** Rain intensity and timing, wind direction, wetting delay, moisture-map locations, interior/exterior photographs, sealant and weep status, prior repairs, visible settlement. Distinguish entry point, travel path, and point of visible damage.

**Testing distinction.** ASTM E1105 and other procedures require specified setup/pressure/spray and qualified interpretation; their results are not interchangeable with a hose check. Do not conduct uncontrolled flooding on occupied or deteriorated assemblies. Repair structural corrosion, displaced masonry and hidden backing damage as separate problems.

**References.** Brick Industry Association, *Technical Notes 7, Water Penetration Resistance – Design*, *Technical Notes 7B, Construction and Workmanship*, and *Builder Notes: Brick Veneer Construction*.
