# 018000 — Lintels, Flashing, and Openings in Masonry Veneers

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A window interrupts a brick wall. The bricks above it still weigh something, and rain still enters some of the exterior joints. Those two facts explain why a masonry opening needs both structure and drainage. A lintel carries the weight over the opening. Flashing and weeps manage water that reaches the cavity. When one part fails, the visible symptom may appear somewhere else. A rust stain above a window, for example, can indicate a steel support problem, while peeling interior paint may point to water movement through the masonry assembly.

## Veneer is not a solid watertight slab

Many brick-clad buildings use brick as a veneer supported in front of a structural wall. The brick has its own weight and needs support. Ties restrain it laterally; the backup wall carries other building loads. A drained cavity behind the veneer allows water that penetrates mortar joints to move down to flashing and out through weep openings. The outer brick surface sheds most rain, but it is not assumed to stop every droplet.

A window, door, or service opening interrupts this system. The veneer load above an opening cannot simply hang in midair. Steel lintels, reinforced masonry, or engineered support details bridge the gap. Their end bearing, span, deflection, corrosion protection, and compatibility with masonry movement are design questions. A narrow steel angle that appears strong enough by eye may deflect or corrode. Deflection can crack mortar and change the flashing geometry even before the angle loses its ability to carry load.

In an older wall, the lintel may be concealed by trim or an upper brick course. If it rusts, corrosion products occupy greater volume than the original steel. Expansion can lift or crack adjacent mortar, produce horizontal cracking above the opening, or displace bricks. Repointing the crack might improve appearance but does not remove the expanding corrosion or restore a failed lintel.

## Flashing must lead water outward

Picture a drop of rain entering a mortar joint several courses above a window. It travels down the back of the brick or across a cavity tie. If it arrives at the lintel without flashing, water can run toward the window frame or into the backup wall. Properly integrated flashing intercepts that path and directs the water to the exterior. End dams and continuity at corners matter because water follows gravity and surface tension, not the nominal edge of a drawing.

Weep openings complete the exit. They may be open head joints or another specified detail. If filled with mortar droppings, sealant, or debris, the cavity can hold water. A seemingly conscientious repair that caulks every visible hole at the base of a veneer may block intended drainage. Conversely, a hole that looks like a weep is not automatically a correctly constructed drainage path. The cavity, flashing, and outlet need to be understood as a connected route.

Wall movement also matters. Brick expands and contracts with temperature and moisture; the supporting structural frame may move differently. Detailers use expansion joints, soft joints, and support arrangements to accommodate this movement. Rigidly bridging a movement joint with mortar or a new flashing bracket can redirect stress into the opening. Some cracks originate in movement rather than structural support failure. Their pattern and timing help distinguish those explanations, but no crack shape proves a unique cause.

## Read a failure from several clues

Imagine recurring dampness at the upper corner of a window after wind-driven rain. Begin with an elevation sketch. Record the window head, brick coursing, lintel ends, wall ties if visible, sealant joints, flashing terminations, and weep locations. Note wind direction, storm intensity, whether interior condensation could occur, and whether water appears only after a delay. The symptom at the window may be downstream of water entering higher in the wall.

Now imagine a stepped crack rising from the corner, with rust-colored staining along the lintel. The crack, staining, and displaced masonry together justify investigation of structural support and corrosion. But do not conclude that a steel angle is certainly bad because the wall cracked. Settlement, thermal movement, inadequate bearing, and missing control joints also deserve consideration. A useful inspection separates observations from hypotheses and identifies which concealed details must be exposed to resolve the uncertainty.

The repair sequence follows the cause. A deteriorated support may require carefully engineered temporary support while masonry is removed. A replacement lintel must carry the intended load and resist corrosion in its exposure. Flashing must be reinstated as a continuous drainage system with positive exit. The repaired masonry must have compatible mortar and movement accommodation. Merely replacing bricks without correcting water entry or support can recreate the same damage.

## A successful opening is inspectable as a system

After intervention, verify bearing, lintel alignment, corrosion protection, flashing laps and terminations, unobstructed weeps, and appropriate joint detailing before concealing them. Record photos of the hidden work. Later inspections should note whether rust staining, cracks, or interior dampness return under comparable weather. The first dry day after a repair is not a proof of long-term rain performance.

The useful mental model is two paths drawn on one detail. One is the structural load descending around an opening to adequate supports. The other is the water descending inside a drained veneer until it exits safely. A masonry opening works when both paths are continuous and when material movement does not break their connections.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Section drawing.** Show brick veneer, cavity, backup wall, wall ties, lintel bearing, head flashing with end dams, weeps, and movement joint. Add two independent arrows: gravity load and bulk-water route.

**Inspection evidence.** Map cracking, joint displacement, rust staining, wetting timing, out-of-plane movement, and weep obstructions. Infrared or moisture readings alone cannot certify concealed flashing or structural soundness.

**Repair gates.** Replacement of a supporting lintel or removal of supported masonry requires structural evaluation and designed shoring. Corrosion resistance, bearing, flashing, and joint dimensions are not universal. Preserve intentional drainage and movement joints.

**Sources.** Brick Industry Association Technical Notes on veneer, ties, and detailing; National Park Service masonry conservation and problem-diagnosis guidance. Independent engineering review pending.
