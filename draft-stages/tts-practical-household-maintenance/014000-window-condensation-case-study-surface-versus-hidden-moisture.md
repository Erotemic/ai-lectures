# 014000 — Window Condensation Case Study: Surface versus Hidden Moisture

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Every cold morning, water beads along the bottom of a bedroom window. The owner wipes it away, replaces the curtains, and decides the window is defective. But a pane of glass can collect water even when it is installed correctly. Warm indoor air contains water vapor. When that air touches a surface cold enough, the water vapor can condense into liquid. To diagnose the problem, we must distinguish the surface we can see from the moisture paths hidden within the wall.

## Follow temperature and vapor, not the stain

Indoor air at a given temperature and humidity has a dew-point temperature: the temperature at which that air, if cooled without changing its moisture content, would become saturated. If the interior glass edge falls below the dew point, condensation is plausible. The glass edge and frame may be colder than the center of the pane because of spacers, materials, geometry, and air movement near the window. Heavy curtains can reduce warm room-air circulation at the glass and create a colder local pocket.

Suppose a room is at seventy degrees Fahrenheit and fifty percent relative humidity. Its dew point is roughly fifty degrees. If a window frame stays at forty-five degrees, the frame is below that dew point and can collect water. Lower the indoor humidity or increase the local surface temperature, and the condensation tendency changes. That is why the same window can be wet one week and dry the next without any leak in its flashing.

The key is that visible interior condensation does not prove rainwater entered from outdoors. Conversely, a dry pane does not prove the wall cavity behind it is dry.

## Identify the actual location of the water

Is moisture on the room-facing surface of the glass? Is it trapped between panes of an insulated glazing unit? Is it on the paint or trim just below the frame? Is water appearing after rain even when outdoor temperatures are mild? These are different clues.

Water between sealed glass panes suggests a failure of the glazing unit's seal, with loss of its intended dry cavity. Interior surface water suggests local surface temperature and indoor humidity should be examined. Wet trim after wind-driven rain suggests bulk-water entry, perhaps at flashing or another opening detail, but room-side condensation can also wet that trim over time. Evidence of one mechanism should not cause us to ignore the others.

Record exactly where the wetness begins. A photograph taken before wiping helps. Note indoor temperature and relative humidity, outdoor conditions, curtain position, and whether cooking, showering, or drying clothes recently added moisture. A small hygrometer can help identify whether the room's humidity is consistently high. A surface thermometer can supplement the observations, although emissivity and reflective surfaces limit some infrared tools.

## Hidden moisture follows different paths

A wall beside a window can become wet from rain driven through a failed flashing detail, from interior air leaking into a cold cavity, or from vapor diffusion and local drying limitations. Air movement can carry far more moisture into a cavity than diffusion through an intact surface in many real situations. The location of the wetting and the available drying directions determine whether materials remain damp long enough to decay or support microbial growth.

A homeowner may seal the visible interior trim gap with caulk. That may reduce an air leak at the room side, which can be useful in the right assembly. But if rain is entering behind the cladding, interior caulk will not restore the exterior drainage plane. It may hide the symptom temporarily while the cavity remains wet. Likewise, covering stained drywall with paint does not change the water path.

The window is part of a layered assembly. Cladding sheds much rain, flashing directs water outward around vulnerable interfaces, the water-resistive barrier provides a drainage plane, and the air barrier limits pressure-driven air movement. These systems may share materials, but their functions should be analyzed separately.

## A disciplined diagnostic sequence

Start with the simplest observations. Is condensation widespread on other cold surfaces or only at one window? Does it occur only during freezing weather? Is the room over-humidified? Does running a bathroom exhaust or improving appropriate ventilation change the pattern? Are window coverings trapping a cold pocket? If several windows behave similarly on cold mornings, indoor humidity and glass surface temperature deserve attention before a conclusion about flashing.

Next examine the rain relationship. Does water appear after a wind-driven storm on a mild day? Is the staining localized to one side, sill, or nearby wall? Are there signs of failed exterior joints, poorly sloped surfaces, or missing drainage details? Correlating the timing of wetness with weather can distinguish a rain path from a dew-point problem more effectively than a single inspection.

Noninvasive moisture measurements may help identify a suspicious region, but meters respond differently to materials, salts, metal, temperature, and depth. A comparative reading is not an automatic moisture percentage for every wall assembly. Thermal imaging can reveal temperature differences, yet cannot identify hidden water reliably on its own. If the evidence points to a concealed recurring leak, qualified investigative opening may be needed to see the water path and extent of damage.

## A repair must preserve the rest of the wall

If the source is surface condensation, improvements may include reducing excessive indoor moisture, restoring suitable ventilation, avoiding trapped stagnant air at the glass, and improving window thermal performance where justified. If the source is rain intrusion, the repair must restore the exterior water-management sequence so water drains outward. If air leakage into a cavity is involved, continuity of the air barrier and assembly drying conditions matter.

Avoid solving one symptom by creating another. Sealing an enclosure more tightly may change ventilation needs. Adding an impermeable interior layer to a wall that relies on inward drying can alter moisture performance. Replacing a window without addressing failed sill flashing may leave the same rainwater path in place.

## Verify over the conditions that caused the problem

After an intervention, look for the same triggering conditions. Did interior window condensation stop on a cold morning with comparable room humidity? Does the repaired opening remain dry through wind-driven rain? Does trim material dry rather than remain persistently damp? Keep a short log over the relevant season. A repair that appears successful during one mild afternoon has not yet been tested against the original failure.

Window condensation is an excellent lesson in household diagnosis because the same visible water can have multiple sources. The durable method is to locate the water, reconstruct when it appears, distinguish surface physics from hidden pathways, and choose a repair that matches the mechanism rather than the stain.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Example: surface condensation

For interior air near `70°F` and `50% RH`, the dew point is approximately `50°F`; an interior frame at `45°F` is plausibly below the dew point. Actual local humidity and surface temperature matter. A rough calculator is illustrative, not evidence of concealed wetting.

### Diagnostic comparison

| Observation | Primary candidates | Discriminating information |
|---|---|---|
| Glass room-side beads on cold mornings | Indoor vapor + cold glass | Indoor RH, frame temperature, curtain air circulation |
| Fog enclosed between glass panes | Failed insulating-glass seal | Verify fog is within sealed space |
| Trim wet after warm wind-driven rain | Bulk-water entry | Flashing, rain timing, wetted path |
| Wall cavity persistently damp | Rain, air leakage, drying restriction | Assembly inspection and measured moisture trends |

**Review boundary:** Moisture meters and IR images are indirect observations. Restoration must follow actual enclosure water, air, and vapor control details; suspected concealed decay/mold and structural damage warrant appropriate assessment.
