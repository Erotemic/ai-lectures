# Trace water intrusion from symptom to source

## How Leaks Progress Through Materials

A brown mark appears on the ceiling below an upstairs bathroom. The homeowner assumes the bathtub drain is leaking. That is one plausible cause. But water might be entering beside the shower enclosure, traveling along a pipe, or arriving from rain through the exterior wall. The visible stain tells you where some water ended up, not necessarily where it entered.

This is the first principle of moisture diagnosis: separate the source, the pathway, the storage, and the symptom. The source supplies water. The pathway carries it. Materials may store it for hours, days, or longer. The stain, smell, swelling, or dripping is the evidence we observe. When those stages are confused, repairs frequently target the wrong location.

### Water can travel before it becomes visible

Liquid water often follows gravity, but its path through a building is not a straight vertical line. It can spread along a beam, the underside of decking, a pipe, or an impervious sheet. Capillary forces can draw water through tiny openings or porous materials, sometimes across or upward against gravity over short distances. Certain materials absorb and retain moisture without appearing obviously wet at first.

Think of a roof leak near a vent pipe. Rain enters near the penetration, runs along roof sheathing, drips onto framing, and travels until it reaches a gap in ceiling drywall. The ceiling stain appears far from the roof opening. Cutting drywall directly beneath the stain may reveal wet material but not the original defect.

A leak can also be intermittent. Wind-driven rain may cross a detail that remains dry in calm weather. A supply fitting might drip only when a fixture valve is opened or when pressure changes. A drain connection leaks only while wastewater passes. Condensation forms when a surface is cold enough relative to surrounding air and moisture. These mechanisms create different time signatures.

### Wetting and drying compete

A building material's moisture condition changes according to how much water arrives, how much moves elsewhere, and how much evaporates or drains away. If wetting repeats faster than drying, moisture accumulates. A minor drip that dries between events may leave little trace at first; a repeated or continuous source can eventually produce swelling, corrosion, deterioration, or biological growth.

Drying depends on temperature, airflow, vapor pressure, material permeability, and the available paths. A layer of impermeable flooring or coating can slow drying on one face. A hidden cavity can remain damp after the exposed surface looks normal. Conversely, a stain left from an old event can remain visible after the assembly has dried. Moisture meters and observations can help distinguish current dampness from historical marking, but no single meter reading establishes the source.

### Ask when the symptom happens

Build a timeline. Does the ceiling spot enlarge after someone showers, after a toilet flush, after rain with wind from one direction, or after a spell of humid weather? Does it appear immediately or with a delay? Is there a water meter indicating unexpected use when all fixtures are off? Are nearby supply pipes, waste lines, and exterior penetrations in plausible positions?

These are not formal proofs. A rain-associated symptom can still involve condensation in some circumstances, and water from a plumbing leak may accumulate before appearing. But correlations guide a test plan. One carefully chosen observation that separates hypotheses is more useful than ten guesses.

Suppose the stain appears after heavy rain but never after bathroom use during dry weather. That weakens the hypothesis of a continuously leaking supply line and strengthens a weather-related path. Suppose it appears only while the tub drains. That points more strongly toward the drain or surrounding water-management details. The crucial habit is to change one condition at a time where doing so is safe and practical, record results, and avoid dismantling large areas based on an untested assumption.

### Material damage is a separate assessment

Even after identifying and stopping the source, materials may need drying, cleaning, replacement, or inspection for loss of strength. Wet insulation may fail to perform as intended, and porous finishes may hold moisture. Metal fasteners can corrode. Wood can swell or deteriorate if repeatedly damp. A surface finish may conceal wet backing.

For clean-water damage, prompt drying matters. U.S. Environmental Protection Agency guidance commonly emphasizes drying damp materials within roughly twenty-four to forty-eight hours to reduce mold growth risk. That is a prevention guideline, not a promise that every material dried within that window is safe or that every item wet longer must be discarded. Extent of wetting, contamination category, material type, and hidden conditions matter. Sewage or other contaminated water calls for different handling from a small clean-water spill.

A moisture meter can be useful comparatively: measure a suspected wet area and a similar known-dry area. But settings, material density, conductive salts, and hidden metal can influence readings. A pinless meter may show a misleading high reading above a screw or pipe. The reading is evidence of possible moisture, not a direct map of every liquid-water path behind a wall.

### Repair the mechanism, then verify

Imagine patching the ceiling and repainting as soon as the stain stops growing. The finish looks better, but the source may still be active. A sound repair sequence is to locate and stop the water entry, determine what materials are wet, dry or remove damaged materials as appropriate, restore the assembly, and monitor the result under conditions similar to those that triggered the problem.

Verification is vital. A roof repair made on a clear day has not yet experienced the wind-driven rain that originally produced the symptom. A drain repair needs a suitable test under the use pattern associated with the leak. Documenting the problem with dates and photographs can reveal whether the rate of damage is changing and help explain the history to a professional.

The visible stain is a message sent by a hidden system. It is important evidence, but it speaks only for the point where water finally revealed itself. The practical investigator traces backward through the path, accounts for storage and drying, and treats repairs as hypotheses to test rather than cosmetic victories.

## Water Intrusion Case Study: From Symptom to Repair

The possible routes for water now give us competing explanations to test against an actual stain.

A brown stain appears on the ceiling of a downstairs room, several feet from an exterior wall. There is a bathroom upstairs, a window above the nearby porch roof, and a radiator pipe running through part of the ceiling cavity. The stain grows after a storm, but someone also remembers seeing it damp on a dry day. It would be easy to call a roofer, or a plumber, or a painter. The real question is which evidence would distinguish the possible sources.

Water damage has at least four elements: a source, a route, a material that absorbs or transmits water, and the visible symptom. Water may move along joists, pipes, wiring, membrane seams, or the underside of a roof before dripping somewhere else. Gravity matters, but capillary action and air-pressure differences can also move moisture. The location of the stain is an endpoint of the path, not necessarily the beginning.

### Build a timeline before opening the wall

Imagine making a simple record. On Monday the stain is dry. Tuesday evening brings wind-driven rain; Wednesday morning its perimeter is darker. On Thursday the upstairs bathtub is used heavily, but no obvious change follows. On Friday a humid day produces a small patch of condensation on a cold pipe nearby. Each observation changes our confidence in competing explanations, though none by itself proves a cause.

The important discipline is to record observations without prematurely assigning causes. 'Ceiling wet after rain' is an observation. 'Window flashing failed' is a hypothesis. 'The shower leak caused the staining' is another hypothesis. Keeping those categories separate prevents an early guess from quietly becoming the conclusion in every later conversation with contractors.

### Draw the water pathways

Start with a plan and a section through the room. Mark the roof slope, gutters, window head and sill, exterior cladding, interior ceiling, pipes, fixtures, and framing direction. Water from a failed window flashing detail might travel downward within the wall, turn along a horizontal framing member, then emerge under the ceiling. A plumbing leak could follow a pipe before dripping. Condensation may accumulate on a cold uninsulated surface when humid air reaches it.

Rain entry, plumbing leaks, and condensation have different triggers. Rain entry correlates with precipitation, wind direction, and wetting of specific exterior faces. Plumbing faults correlate with fixture use, supply pressure, or drainage events. Condensation depends on surface temperature, air moisture content, and air movement. These correlations are useful evidence but can overlap. A roof may retain water after rain, and a small plumbing leak may be intermittent.

### Measure carefully, but understand the instruments

A moisture meter can help map relative differences in material response. It does not automatically identify the chemical source or entry point of water. Pin meters and noninvasive meters respond differently to material composition, density, metal, coatings, and geometry. Readings compared across different materials are not always directly comparable. Establish a reference in a similar dry material and document the measurement conditions.

Infrared imaging can reveal surface temperature differences. A cool patch may be caused by evaporation, a thermal bridge, missing insulation, or a cold pipe. It is not a photograph of water. A useful investigation combines thermal observations with physical moisture evidence, event history, and the construction diagram. No single gadget can remove the need for reasoning.

### Separate containment, repair, and restoration

Suppose the investigation establishes that wind-driven rain enters above the window through a failed flashing detail. The immediate priorities are limiting additional water entry and safely managing wet materials. The durable repair corrects the exterior drainage and air/water-control details so water is directed outward. Only after the source is controlled and affected materials are appropriately dried and assessed should interior finishes be restored.

Painting over the stain first reverses that order. It can conceal the symptom while moisture continues to damage framing or support mold growth. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency emphasizes that moisture control is the key to mold control and advises prompt drying of damp materials, generally within twenty-four to forty-eight hours after a leak or spill when feasible. Extensive damage, contaminated water, or electrical and structural concerns may require specialists.

### Preserve evidence of the repair, not just the cost

A useful repair record includes dates, weather, photographs, moisture observations, the hypotheses considered, what was opened, what was found, the completed corrective detail, and how performance was subsequently verified. A receipt alone cannot demonstrate that the water route was repaired. Photos before closure can help a future owner understand what is behind the finish.

Verification should match the original mechanism. For a rain leak, observations across relevant subsequent storms may be more informative than a dry-day visual inspection. A controlled water test may help when designed and performed appropriately, but poorly applied water can create new damage or obscure the true path. For a plumbing repair, appropriate pressure or use testing is more relevant. For condensation, temperature and humidity measurements during representative conditions matter.

### A second hypothesis remains possible

Imagine the flashing repair stops the major storm leak, but a faint damp patch reappears each winter. That does not necessarily disprove the flashing repair. The building may have had two independent moisture mechanisms: exterior leakage and condensation at a cold piping or framing surface. Diagnosing a second mechanism is different from reflexively replacing the first repair.

This is the heart of maintenance reasoning. A home is a network of overlapping systems, and one symptom can be produced by more than one source. Good diagnosis maps the mechanisms, collects evidence that discriminates between them, intervenes at the cause, and verifies the changed behavior. A correctly repaired stain is the last visible result of a successful investigation, not the first task on the work order.

## Damp Basement Case Study: Drainage versus Humidity

A basement smells musty in late August. One corner of the wall feels cool and damp. The homeowner considers a dehumidifier, a waterproof coating, and an exterior excavation, each with a very different cost. Which one addresses the cause? There is not enough information yet. Dampness is an observation; water arriving through a foundation, water condensing from indoor air, and moisture evaporating from a slab can produce overlapping symptoms. The first repair is to build a better model of how water is moving.

### Three paths into the same room

Start with bulk water. Rainfall, roof runoff, grading, clogged drainage, and high groundwater can drive liquid through below-grade cracks, joints, or porous materials. This often has a relationship to rain or snowmelt, but water can migrate slowly through soil so the visible response may lag the weather. A wet line at the wall-floor joint after heavy rainfall is evidence worth pursuing, not automatic proof of a particular foundation failure.

Next consider air carrying water vapor. Warm humid air can enter a cool basement through open windows, cracks, gaps, and ventilation pathways. As the air cools, its relative humidity rises even if no additional water vapor is introduced. If a wall or pipe surface is below the air's dew-point temperature, condensation can form. A basement can therefore become damp on a sunny humid day with no exterior leakage at all.

Finally, consider vapor and moisture moving through or out of materials. A concrete slab in contact with damp soil can exchange moisture with its surroundings even if no puddle appears. New materials may contain construction moisture. A damp wall can retain moisture long after an entry event ends. It may take time to dry, and the path of drying changes with coatings, floor finishes, room temperature, and ventilation.

### The dew point provides a testable hypothesis

Relative humidity by itself is not the quantity that determines condensation. It describes the fraction of saturation vapor pressure at the current air temperature. Dew point is the temperature to which that air would need to cool, at approximately unchanged vapor content, to reach saturation. If room air has a dew point above the temperature of an exposed pipe, that pipe can collect condensation. A wall can behave similarly, with local conditions and surface properties adding complexity.

Imagine indoor air at seventy-five degrees Fahrenheit with sixty percent relative humidity. Its dew point is approximately sixty degrees Fahrenheit. A basement surface around fifty-five degrees can collect water from that air. Run a dehumidifier and reduce the moisture content, and condensation may disappear even though the wall temperature stays much the same. That would be evidence for an air-moisture mechanism. It would not prove that there is no groundwater problem elsewhere.

Temperature and humidity instruments need context. A single inexpensive humidity display placed directly against a wet wall may not represent the bulk room. A surface thermometer or thermal image shows temperature, not water content. A moisture meter can help compare material conditions, but readings depend on material, salts, depth, and meter type. Use repeated observations in consistent locations rather than treating one meter reading as a laboratory diagnosis.

### Make the competing explanations predict different things

A good hypothesis can be tested because it makes a prediction. If roof runoff is the main driver, moisture should correlate with rainfall or snowmelt and perhaps be most evident near drainage defects. If indoor condensation is the main driver, water may appear on the coldest surfaces during high indoor dew points, even without precipitation. If plumbing is leaking, the wet area may change with fixture use. If groundwater pressure is persistent, the wall may remain damp through extended dry weather.

Record rain dates and intensity as best you can, indoor temperature and humidity, visible moisture location, and whether mechanical equipment or windows were in use. Photos with the same reference point help identify progression. Look outdoors for disconnected downspouts, soil sloping toward the house, roof valleys dumping water in one spot, or discharge water recycling to the foundation. These are ordinary water-path clues, not proofs of any one hidden defect.

### What each proposed repair actually changes

A dehumidifier removes water vapor from indoor air and may lower the dew point enough to stop condensation. It does not stop bulk liquid water entering through a crack. A surface coating may change evaporation or transport through a wall, but it does not make exterior hydrostatic pressure disappear. A drainage intervention alters where liquid water travels; it may be appropriate for exterior water intrusion but irrelevant to humidity produced by air exchange. A sump pump can remove water reaching a collection system, not identify why water reached it.

Consider opening basement windows on a humid summer day. If outdoor air has a higher dew point than basement air, ventilation can increase moisture load even when the outdoor air feels fresher. In a cool climate at a dry time of year, ventilation might help remove moisture. The decision depends on the water content of the incoming air and on the thermal conditions, not merely whether a window is open.

An impermeable floor covering placed over a moisture-emitting slab can trap moisture at the interface, damaging adhesive or flooring. Conversely, a suitable vapor-control layer may be an intended part of a tested floor system. The difference lies in compatible materials, substrate conditions, and the actual moisture source. A repair needs a defined functional objective and a mechanism for reaching it.

### Follow the case to a decision

Suppose our basement wall is dry throughout spring rainstorms but shows droplets in late summer when its surface is cool. The droplets appear first behind storage boxes where air movement is poor. A dehumidifier lowers room dew point and the droplets stop. That combination strongly supports condensation as a major mechanism. It does not establish why the wall remains unusually cold or whether stored moisture is present in concealed material. Continue observing rather than announcing all water problems solved.

Now change the evidence. Suppose water appears at the wall-floor joint within hours of hard rain, with staining that follows a foundation crack, while the room dew point stays below the measured wall temperature. Bulk entry becomes more plausible. Exterior water management and, when appropriate, foundation drainage evaluation move up the priority list. A dehumidifier may help drying afterward, but it cannot be the principal water-control intervention.

Mold growth and damaged materials require attention to moisture source, contaminated material, occupant exposure, and appropriate remediation practices. Painting over a persistent damp surface does not remove the moisture mechanism. The central habit is to distinguish the location where water becomes visible from the path by which it arrived. A damp basement is a small fluid-transport investigation, not a contest between repair products.

## Roof Leak Case Study: Evidence Across Weather Events

A brown mark appears in a bedroom ceiling after a storm. The mark is near the center of the room. A roofer replaces a few shingles directly above it, but the stain returns during the next windy rain. The first repair followed the visible symptom, not the path water traveled through the building.

Water can enter a roof at one location and appear somewhere else. It may run down an underlayment, along a rafter, across the top of insulation, or over a mechanical duct before reaching the plasterboard. Wind can drive rain uphill beneath laps or around flashings. Snow can melt and refreeze at roof edges. Indoor moisture can also condense on cold roof components, producing wetting that resembles a leak. The point of this case study is to use weather, location, and assembly geometry to distinguish those possibilities.

### Build a timeline before choosing a repair

Start with dates. When was the stain first noticed? Did the roof experience heavy rainfall, sustained wind from a particular direction, snow accumulation, freezing, or thawing? Did the stain grow during precipitation or only afterward? Did it appear when a bathroom fan was running, during cold dry weather, or when a plumbing fixture was used? Record the observations as they happen. Memory becomes less reliable after several storms.

Take repeatable photographs of the ceiling with a ruler or fixed reference, and mark the outer edge of the visible wet area without damaging the finish. Note whether the material is soft or sagging and whether electrical fixtures are nearby. Significant sagging or active water near electricity is a more urgent condition than old, stable discoloration. Protect occupants and the area first; do not allow a ceiling to fail while pursuing a perfect diagnosis.

Then draw a section of the roof and upper wall. Include pitch, valleys, penetrations, chimneys, vents, dormers, windows, gutters, and the route water would take if a flashing joint failed. The stain's horizontal position is a final arrival point. Trace likely paths back toward the upper roof, including framing members that can transport water laterally.

### Compare competing hypotheses

Suppose the stain appears only after strong winds from the west. A flashing defect on a west-facing wall-to-roof intersection becomes more plausible than ordinary vapor condensation. If the stain grows after an extended cold spell without rain but with indoor humidity and ice accumulation, condensation or ice-dam-related infiltration deserves attention. If it appears during showers regardless of weather, investigate nearby plumbing or ventilation ducts. None of these correlations proves a mechanism; each changes which locations deserve inspection first.

A moisture meter can map the relative extent of wet materials, but it cannot reveal where water entered. An infrared camera may highlight a cool wet area, yet thermal bridges and air leakage can look similar. A photograph of missing shingles is valuable evidence, but a roof can leak through failed pipe boots or chimney flashings even when the fields of shingles are intact.

Roof coverings shed water. The roof assembly must also manage water at discontinuities, where rain changes direction or the surface is interrupted. A valley concentrates runoff. An uphill side of a chimney needs a compatible water diversion and flashing design. A wall-to-roof joint must coordinate step flashing, counterflashing, cladding, and the weather-resistive barrier. Applying a bead of sealant to a visible crack may be a temporary measure, but it is rarely a substitute for understanding the drainage path.

### Inspect the safe, accessible places first

From a safe location, observe displaced coverings, damaged flashing, clogged gutters, ponding, vegetation, and debris. If an attic is safely accessible, inspect for wet sheathing, staining on framing, saturated insulation, and water tracks. An inspector should not step on unsupported ceiling finishes or disturb unknown electrical conditions. Roof access in wet, icy, steep, or fragile conditions is a separate fall-risk decision and may require a qualified contractor.

The interior investigation should also ask whether the roof's air boundary is continuous. Warm indoor air leaking into a cold attic can deposit condensation, particularly around penetrations and poorly vented or blocked cavities. The cure for that mechanism differs from resealing a chimney. A ceiling stain does not know which trade is responsible for it.

### A controlled test has boundaries

Sometimes a contractor uses a staged water test to recreate suspected infiltration. This should not mean spraying the entire roof at once until water appears somewhere. Begin with defined areas and durations; observe one region at a time and avoid creating damage or flooding that would not otherwise occur. Such testing requires judgment about roof construction and water capacity and should be performed with appropriate competence. A failed recreation does not prove the roof is sound: the storm may have required specific wind pressure, temperature, or water volume.

After a repair, record exactly which component was changed and why. Photograph concealed flashing layers before they are covered. Check that water has a continuous outward path, not merely that one joint is coated. Allow affected materials to dry appropriately and evaluate whether damaged insulation or interior finishes need replacement. If staining returns, compare it with the original weather conditions rather than immediately repeating the same repair.

### Close the diagnostic loop

The final case report should tell a compact story. The symptom appeared at one ceiling location. Weather records suggested a narrow set of causes. The inspection located a plausible water entry path. The repair restored continuity of a particular water-control layer. Subsequent storms offered evidence about whether the original symptom was eliminated. If the repaired area has not yet faced comparable weather, the conclusion remains provisional.

This is the same method used in many maintenance problems: separate the observation from its location, reconstruct the path, test the leading hypotheses, and verify under conditions that could have caused failure. A stain is where water became visible. A successful repair finds where water stopped being controlled.

## Intermittent Leak Case Study: Rain, Plumbing, and Condensation

Intermittent wetting is a useful final test of this method because the evidence may appear under more than one kind of event.

A dark stain appears on a bedroom ceiling. Two days later it is dry. A month afterward the stain grows. The room above contains a bathroom, and the roof slopes toward the same corner. The owner calls it a roof leak, while a plumber suspects the shower. The stain itself cannot decide between them. Intermittent water damage is an exercise in timing, paths, and controlled observations.

### Water follows geometry, not room labels

A wet spot appears where water becomes visible, not necessarily where it entered. Liquid can run along rafters, pipe exteriors, wiring, joists, or the back of plaster before finding an opening. A ceiling stain several feet from a roof penetration may still come from that penetration. A shower leak can emerge only when someone stands in a particular place or when a drain carries enough flow. Condensation can occur when humid indoor air contacts a cold surface inside an assembly. Three different sources may produce nearly identical stains.

The first action is to protect people and property. Water near electrical fixtures, sagging ceiling material, or visible structural damage can create hazards. Limit access to affected areas and stop active water sources where safely possible. Electrical equipment exposed to water should not be opened or energized casually. Document the original condition before painting over it or cutting a hole, provided doing so is safe.

Then ask when the stain changes. Rain, shower use, toilet flushing, dishwasher operation, cold weather, and indoor humidity create different time patterns. Keep a simple log of weather, plumbing use, indoor conditions, and observations. A photograph with date, reference ruler, and location is far more useful than 'it seemed bigger.' The stain's boundary can be traced gently in pencil on an appropriate surface, but do not damage material or expose yourself to suspected mold or hazardous coatings.

### Use timing as a natural experiment

Suppose the ceiling becomes damp eight hours after a wind-driven storm, even when no one has used the upstairs bathroom. That raises the roof or exterior-wall hypothesis. But stored water may take hours to travel through insulation and framing, so lack of immediate dripping does not rule out rain. Now suppose a dry spell continues for a week, and the spot becomes wet shortly after a shower. That strengthens the plumbing hypothesis, though the correlation could still involve a different water source.

Condensation has another signature. It may intensify on cold nights or during periods of high indoor humidity, especially near thermal bridges or air-leakage paths. A stain under an attic hatch might be associated with warm humid air leaking into a cold roof space. The investigator should measure indoor relative humidity and temperatures, and examine ventilation and air-boundary details rather than assume there is a broken pipe.

These patterns are hypotheses, not verdicts. A roof leak and condensation can coexist, or a shower leak can worsen preexisting wet materials. The goal is to design tests that change one relevant variable at a time.

### Test without manufacturing a flood

An investigator might first inspect the attic after rain, looking for wet sheathing, stains on framing, defective flashing, and moisture paths. Safe access is a prerequisite; stepping off framing or onto a weak ceiling is dangerous. A moisture meter can help compare locations and follow drying, but it does not tell you what caused the moisture. Readings depend on material, probe type, salts, and hidden metal. Thermal cameras similarly show apparent surface temperature patterns, which may be consistent with moisture but not proof of it.

Plumbing tests can be separated by use. Does a drain leak only while water is flowing? Does a supply line leak even when fixtures are off? Does splashing a shower wall produce leakage that a simple drain test does not? Tests involving hidden piping, waterproofing, or water spray should be designed to avoid worsening damage. If a source is suspected but not visible, controlled access and professional testing are often more economical than saturating an assembly with large amounts of water.

One particularly instructive case involves a stain near a bathroom fan. The roof and plumbing appear intact, but the exhaust duct terminates in a cold attic and has an unsealed joint. Moist air condenses inside or outside the duct and drains toward the ceiling. The failed function is neither the roof membrane nor the shower drain. The test is to trace the fan duct's entire route and its connection to the outdoors, including insulation and air sealing where required.

### Repair the cause and the wet materials

A successful repair eliminates the water or moisture path, not just the visible mark. Replacing stained drywall before correcting flashing, a leaking shower connection, or condensation can produce a fresh stain within weeks. Determine what materials must dry, which can be cleaned, and which have lost strength or are contaminated. Persistent moisture supports mold growth; containment, appropriate protection, and professional remediation may be needed depending on conditions and materials.

After the source correction, continue the same observation log through representative weather and use conditions. A roof repair cannot be verified on a sunny day alone if the original leak required wind-driven rain. A shower repair should be checked under its relevant wetting and drainage conditions. If inaccessible areas remain damp, apparent surface dryness may be misleading. Use repeat measurements and, where appropriate, controlled opening to confirm drying.

Record both what was found and what remains unknown. 'Failed flashing at roof penetration repaired, no moisture detected at ceiling during two subsequent storms; hidden cavity not directly inspected' is better than 'leak fixed forever.' A maintenance archive can preserve photographs, repair dates, moisture readings, invoices, and material details for later diagnosis.

The important skill is resisting the urge to name the first plausible source. Rain, plumbing, and condensation follow different physical mechanisms, but they intersect in the same building cavities. Map the possible routes, compare timing, perform safe discriminating tests, and verify drying. The leak is understood when the evidence explains both where the water came from and how it reached the stain.

### Roof Leak Case Study: Evidence Across Weather Events

### 013000 — Roof Leak Case Study: Evidence Across Weather Events

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Event correlation log

Record event date, rain duration/intensity, wind direction, temperature/freezing, snow/ice, indoor humidity, HVAC/fan states, observed ceiling changes, attic observations, and any plumbing use. Take images from fixed locations. Mark **observed**, **inferred**, and **not inspected** facts separately.

##### Diagnostic tree

- Only wind-driven rain: investigate oriented roof/wall junctions, penetrations, flashing and water-control continuity.
- Thaw after snow accumulation: investigate ice damming, drainage obstruction, and attic heat/air leakage.
- Cold weather without precipitation: investigate air leakage and surface condensation.
- Repeated fixture use independent of weather: investigate plumbing and mechanical condensate pathways.

**Editorial boundary:** This is a causal reasoning case, not a prescription to perform unsafe roof access or indiscriminate water testing. Hidden structural damage, active electrical wetting, and sagging finishes require prompt qualified evaluation.

### Intermittent Leak Case Study: Rain, Plumbing, and Condensation

### 017000 — Intermittent Leak Case Study: Rain, Plumbing, and Condensation

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Evidence table:** date/time; rain/wind direction; fixture use; HVAC/exhaust state; indoor RH and temperature; surface moisture readings with material/meter type; photo and scale; test performed; result; uncertainty.

**Candidate pathways:** roof flashing → sheathing → framing → ceiling; shower waterproofing → wall cavity → joist; supply piping → ceiling even without fixture use; drain leak → only during flow; warm moist air → cold duct/sheathing → condensation and drip.

**Tests:** controlled observations, visible attic inspection from safe platforms, localized plumbing checks after isolation, calibrated moisture comparisons, and targeted professional opening. No indiscriminate hose tests or dangerous attic access. Repair the source before closing wet cavities.

**Limits:** Mold, unsafe electrical conditions, structural softness, asbestos/lead-containing legacy finishes, or heavy sagging require suitable qualified assessment. Absence of a new stain over a dry week is not proof of success.
