# The pressure behind an apparently simple faucet

Open a faucet in a house served by a private well. Water arrives at useful pressure even though the well may be deep underground and the pump is not running at that instant. The apparent simplicity conceals three different things: a source of groundwater, a machine that moves it, and a store of pressurized water. Understanding the three separately makes the familiar complaint 'my well pressure is bad' much easier to investigate.

A well intersects water-bearing rock or soil. Its casing and sanitary seal help keep surface contamination out of the water supply. A submersible or other suitable pump lifts water and creates the pressure needed to overcome elevation and pipe resistance. The well has a replenishment rate; the pump has a delivery curve; the household has a time-varying demand. Those rates do not have to match at every second, but they cannot remain out of balance indefinitely.

## Three limits that feel alike at the tap

First, there may be too little water entering the well over time. The pump can lower the water level faster than the formation replenishes it. Second, the pump may not deliver adequate flow against the total required head. Third, the house may have restrictions or controls that waste pressure after the pump has done its work: clogged filters, scale, partly closed valves, undersized pipes, or a failing regulator. All three can produce a weak shower, yet the repairs would be different.

Pressure describes force per area. Flow describes volume per time. Raising pressure at an upstream gauge does not guarantee that water will flow freely through a narrow or blocked branch. A pressure measurement taken while everything is closed is a static reading; the useful test for many complaints is how pressure behaves while water is actually flowing. Observe the gauge before demand, during a sustained demand, and when the pump starts and stops. A trace tells more than a single number.

## What the pressure tank does

Many traditional well systems use a pressure switch and tank. The switch starts the pump when pressure reaches a lower setting, then stops it at an upper setting. Inside a modern diaphragm or bladder pressure tank, compressed air on one side of a flexible barrier stores energy while water occupies the other. As the water is used, the air expands and maintains pressure between pump cycles. The tank is not an extra well and does not increase the long-run yield of the aquifer. It is a short-term buffer that reduces frequent starts and smooths the experience at the fixtures.

The tank's advertised physical volume is not the same as the usable volume delivered between pump cut-out and cut-in. That usable quantity is called drawdown. It depends on the air charge and pressure range. Imagine a nominally large tank that holds surprisingly little usable water because the pressure conditions are wrong. The pump may cycle rapidly despite the tank looking intact from outside. Conversely, replacing the pressure switch without checking the tank can leave a waterlogged system short-cycling.

Short-cycling matters because repeated starts can stress a motor and the associated electrical equipment. But do not assume every frequent cycle means a failed tank. A leaking fixture or buried service line can create continuous demand. A faulty check valve may permit water to flow back. A failed pressure switch can behave erratically. The better question is: what makes the indicated pressure decrease, and at what rate, when all intended water uses are stopped?

## The system curve is a moving target

The pump must overcome both elevation and friction. Lifting water a given vertical distance imposes a static head requirement even with no flow. Friction loss grows as flow increases, and the demand at the house changes when multiple fixtures run. A pump's available head normally declines at higher flow. The actual operating point occurs where the pump's ability and the system's demand match. This is why a bigger pump is not automatically a good answer. Excessive pumping can expose a limited-yield well, worsen cycling, or exceed what downstream equipment can manage.

Variable-speed controls can alter the operating pattern by adjusting pump speed to demand. They still cannot create water the well cannot supply. A pressure tank often remains part of the system, though its size and role may differ. The practical choice depends on measured flow requirements, water level, electrical and pump characteristics, and expected service life.

## Diagnose with a timeline

If a shower loses flow only when a washing machine starts, compare supply and branch limitations. If every faucet briefly works, then runs weak after minutes, consider whether well recovery or sustained pumping is the issue. If a pump starts every few seconds with no fixtures deliberately running, investigate hidden use, check valves, tank function, and controls. Record observations without opening energized equipment or dismantling a pressurized vessel.

A private well is also a drinking-water source, not merely a hydraulic machine. Changes in water clarity, taste, or test results require separate water-quality reasoning. A pump diagnosis is not a substitute for appropriate water testing or maintenance of the well's sanitary protection. Once we divide the system into source, machine, pressure storage, distribution, and water quality, a vague pressure complaint becomes a set of hypotheses we can actually test.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Pump/tank sketch

Draw well and static water level, pump, check valve, pressure gauge, pressure switch, air-charged diaphragm tank, treatment/filter, and fixtures. Show the cut-in/cut-out cycle and label **drawdown** as water delivered between pressure thresholds, not tank shell capacity.

### Worked physics

Idealized pressure gain for lifting water a height `h` is `ρgh`: for `h=10 m`, about `98 kPa`, equivalent to approximately `14.2 psi`, **before friction and required fixture pressure**. That does not specify a pump model or required installation pressure.

### Observation table

| Symptom | Distinguishing measurement |
|---|---|
| Low flow everywhere under demand | Gauge behavior, flow rate, filter pressure drop, pump operation |
| Rapid pressure cycling | Cut-in/out readings, timing, unexplained water use, tank evaluation |
| Declining flow during long demand | Pump performance, well recovery and drawdown, treatment blockage |
| Change in water quality | Qualified laboratory test and inspection of well protection |

Qualified well professionals should assess pressurized tanks, pump controls, electrical work, and sanitary well integrity. Manufacturer pressure settings and local drinking-water provisions govern the actual system.
