# 123000 — George Dantzig: Choices, constraints, and the mathematics of planning

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Family and mathematical education, depression-era statistics, wartime Air Forces logistics, Project SCOOP, invention of the simplex algorithm, RAND, Berkeley and Stanford, operations research, and the distinction between practical efficiency and theoretical complexity. **Background:** linear equations and elementary inequalities are explained.

During the Second World War, governments faced problems for which mathematics was not a distant intellectual luxury. How many crews could be trained? Which supplies should be sent first? How should scarce aircraft, fuel and labor be assigned when every useful allocation ruled out another? These are questions about choices under constraints. George Dantzig helped turn that kind of planning into a systematic mathematical discipline. His most influential contribution, the simplex algorithm for linear programming, would eventually become a standard instrument in industry, transportation, manufacturing and computing. It was not created in a vacuum by a man who thought about inequalities all day. Its origins lie in family education, government statistics, war, and the early promise of electronic calculation.

George Bernard Dantzig was born in Portland, Oregon, in 1914. His father, Tobias Dantzig, had traveled from Russia to France and studied mathematics in Paris before emigrating to the United States. His mother, Anja Ourisson, studied languages and became a linguist. The family's first years in America were financially difficult. Tobias took physical labor before finding opportunities to pursue his scholarly career. George's childhood therefore combined considerable intellectual support with firsthand experience of uncertain employment and money. Mathematics was present in the household, but it was not automatically an easy school subject for him.

Dantzig later recalled doing poorly in mathematics at first and then becoming captivated by geometry. His father supplied problem after problem, and a schoolteacher encouraged him. The family connection was not a shortcut to high grades; it was a way of learning the habit of sustained analytical work. Geometry rewards a person who changes a picture, draws an auxiliary line, or recognizes that a difficult-looking arrangement is governed by one simple relation. The mature simplex algorithm uses a different mathematical language, but a similar habit is visible: rather than enumerate every possible decision, reorganize the problem so useful structure becomes apparent.

Tobias Dantzig wrote *Number: The Language of Science*, a book addressed to a broader readership. George helped prepare some figures while he was still a teenager. This small collaboration matters because it puts a future mathematical scientist in contact with mathematical communication as well as problem solving. A diagram must persuade somebody else, not merely satisfy the person who drew it. Later in life George would make sophisticated optimization understandable to engineers and administrators who needed to use it.

He studied mathematics and physics at the University of Maryland and graduated in 1936. The same year he married Anne Shmuner, a partnership that continued through the different institutional settings of his career. At the University of Michigan he earned a master's degree in 1937, but he did not proceed immediately into an uninterrupted university professorship. Employment in applied statistics offered a route toward practical mathematical problems during a difficult economic period. He worked for the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics, where the handling of large amounts of information was not an abstract exercise.

The next important change brought him to the University of California, Berkeley, to study statistics with Jerzy Neyman. Neyman had been part of the European transformation of statistical inference and now helped establish Berkeley as a major center of the field. Dantzig's training connected probability and statistical reasoning with questions of systematic decision. His later research would not be a branch of statistical testing, but statistical education gave him another way of seeing the difference between a model, data and a method of computation.

One story from Berkeley became famous. Dantzig arrived late to a class, saw two problems on the board, and assumed they were homework. He solved them, although they had been presented as open problems in statistics. He later recalled the episode, and the resulting work became connected with his doctoral research. The story is often retold as if a student's ignorance of an impossible task magically made it possible. That moral is too simple. He was a highly trained mathematician working under an expert supervisor, and the problems were within the reach of his methods once he took them seriously. The anecdote is valuable as a piece of Dantzig's retrospective account, not as proof that every difficult problem is only a matter of confidence.

Then war changed the scale of American planning. Dantzig served in the statistical control operations of the United States Army Air Forces. The tasks involved personnel and material allocation on a scale that ordinary local judgment could not easily coordinate. In a war, planning errors were not simply financial inefficiencies; they could waste critical resources. A unit might need more pilots, but pilots required training time and aircraft. Aircraft required parts, factory capacity, crews and fuel. The quantities were linked. A choice that looked optimal for one department could damage the entire system.

What makes such a problem mathematical? First identify the decisions. Suppose a factory makes two kinds of equipment, A and B. Every unit of A uses two hours of machine time and one unit of material. Every unit of B uses one hour of machine time and two units of material. The factory has one hundred hours and eighty material units. If we call the production quantities x and y, the constraints say that twice x plus y cannot exceed one hundred, and x plus twice y cannot exceed eighty. We also require nonnegative quantities. The feasible choices form a region rather than one preordained answer.

Now suppose A yields four units of benefit and B yields five. The objective is to maximize four times x plus five times y, subject to those constraints. You could list all integer production plans if the numbers were small. But wartime supply problems could involve hundreds or thousands of connected variables. Blind enumeration grows too expensive. The hope is to use the shape of the allowed region and the fact that all the relations are linear. Dantzig's algorithm exploited exactly that structure.

After the war, Dantzig returned to doctoral work and joined an Air Force planning project known as SCOOP, for Scientific Computation of Optimal Programs. The word program meant a plan or schedule, not a computer program in the modern everyday sense. He developed the simplex method in 1947. Linear programming was already emerging from more than one research tradition, including mathematical economics and earlier work by Leonid Kantorovich in the Soviet Union. Dantzig's contribution was a general computational method and a powerful way of formulating practical planning problems. The algorithm became a central reason such problems could be solved systematically.

Here is the geometric intuition. For a linear objective optimized over a suitable bounded polygon, a maximum can be found at a corner, unless all points along some edge are equally good. You do not need to try every point in the interior. In our factory example, consider the corner where both resources are exactly exhausted. Twice x plus y equals one hundred; x plus twice y equals eighty. Solving gives x equals forty, y equals twenty. The objective is four times forty plus five times twenty, which is two hundred sixty. Compare other corners to decide whether that is the best plan. It is, for this example. The mathematical freedom was transformed into a small set of promising candidates.

The simplex method is not literally a person wandering around a two-dimensional drawing. In hundreds of dimensions a corner is described by a set of active constraints and variables, and the calculation updates that representation. Each improvement moves from one basic feasible solution to another, guided by the objective. One must handle degeneracy, unbounded regions, and numerical precision. Yet the picture of moving along edges of a polyhedron captures the central insight: use structure to avoid a naive search through all possibilities.

There is a useful subtlety in that geometric picture. If the feasible region has a flat edge on which the objective is constant, several production plans can be equally good. If two constraints meet in a degenerate corner, an algorithm may change its bookkeeping without improving the numerical objective. If the region is unbounded and the objective can increase forever along some allowable direction, there is no finite optimum at all. A computational method must distinguish these cases. The two-product drawing gives the intuition, but implementing a reliable method means specifying pivot rules, keeping track of bases, and handling ties and rounding errors.

There is another subtlety: the real quantities are not always continuous. A factory cannot manufacture half an aircraft. A shipping decision may need to be either made or not made; a worker's shift may occupy an indivisible block. Linear programming allows fractional values unless extra constraints are imposed. In some problems the resulting fractional solution is informative; in others it is physically impossible. Integer programming and combinatorial optimization confront this further difficulty, and their history includes contributions by many researchers after Dantzig. His simplex method transformed a particular class of models, but no single algorithm instantly solved every planning problem with discrete choices.

Dantzig was not alone in exploring these questions. John von Neumann understood related convexity and duality ideas, and there was lively interaction among mathematical economists, military planners and computing researchers. Duality gives a second interpretation of a linear program. The original problem chooses production quantities; its dual can attach values to limited resources. If an hour of machine time is especially scarce, how much benefit could be gained by having one more hour? That is a question about shadow prices, an economic reading of a mathematically derived inequality. In a good model, the dual can explain why the recommended plan is optimal, not merely produce a list of outputs.

Such explanations helped build the new field called operations research. A mathematical model might be elegant but useless if nobody could state the real constraints, or if the numbers were unreliable. The task required conversation with practitioners. Some constraints were physical: only so much fuel exists. Others were institutional: a shipment cannot be rerouted without approvals. Models rarely captured all of them. An algorithm's success did not excuse the analyst from asking which assumptions were valid.

One celebrated collaborative problem at RAND involved the traveling salesman. Given a set of cities and a distance between each pair, find a round trip visiting every city once with the smallest total distance. This is not a continuous linear program in the simplest sense: the choices are discrete, and the number of possible tours grows enormously. In 1954 Dantzig, Ray Fulkerson and Selmer Johnson published a solution to a forty-nine-city instance, using mathematical inequalities and computational reasoning to certify an optimal tour. It was joint work, and the later field of cutting-plane methods cannot be assigned entirely to Dantzig. The episode illustrates how ideas from linear programming could become part of more complicated discrete optimization.

At RAND he also confronted the practical fact that a numerical method and a machine implementation are different achievements. The earliest computers had very limited memory by modern standards, and input, output, scaling and roundoff all mattered. Writing a large model on paper did not mean that a machine could store its entire coefficient matrix conveniently. Good algorithms needed organizations of the data that permitted progress without repeatedly processing everything. That challenge encouraged research on sparse representations and problem decomposition, developments that would continue long after the first simplex implementations.

Dantzig spent time at the RAND Corporation, where problems of planning, economics, mathematics and computing were being pursued in the setting of the Cold War. He later held academic posts at Berkeley and Stanford. His shift from government operational planning into universities helped make mathematical programming a teachable field. Students could learn the theory, implement algorithms, and take them to companies or laboratories. Operations research eventually became part of business schools, engineering departments, computer science and applied mathematics. It had no single institutional home because its questions crossed disciplinary boundaries.

The rise of electronic computers changed the stakes. A method that is feasible only for a few variables on paper might become useful for thousands on a machine; conversely, a badly designed algorithm can exhaust computing resources despite electronic speed. Dantzig's simplex method proved extraordinarily effective on many industrial problems. Yet later theoretical analysis showed that specially constructed families of linear programs can make the simplex method require exponentially many steps. The distinction is important. Good observed performance over many practical instances does not prove a favorable worst-case complexity bound.

In the 1970s research on polynomial-time algorithms for linear programming changed the theoretical conversation, and later interior-point methods offered new computational possibilities. Dantzig did not need to be declared wrong for those developments to matter. Optimization is a living field in which algorithms coexist and improve, and where the best method depends on problem structure, numerical properties, and implementation. The simplex method remains influential not because no alternative exists but because its conceptual and practical achievements survived the arrival of those alternatives.

Return to the factory example and ask what happens if the material supply rises from eighty units to eighty-one. With the optimal plan unchanged in structure, the extra unit of material has a marginal value of two units of the objective. A similar extra hour of machine time has marginal value one. These numbers come from the dual solution, and they are informative precisely because they attach economic meaning to constraints. But the conclusions apply only over the range where the same optimal basis remains appropriate. If the factory gains an enormous stockpile of material, the machine-time limit may become the only constraint that matters, and the old shadow price ceases to describe the marginal change.

This kind of sensitivity analysis can be as valuable to a planner as the chosen optimum. A manager might not be able to implement the recommended plan exactly, but might be able to purchase a little more capacity. The dual interpretation says which shortage is currently most restrictive in the model. It also makes it possible to ask whether data errors would alter a decision. An optimum calculated from falsely precise prices and capacities can be less useful than a robust family of near-optimal plans. The history of operations research includes learning to put such questions alongside the algorithm itself.

Dantzig's life connected several phases of twentieth-century applied mathematics: the pre-computer habit of analytical geometry, wartime statistical control, Cold War systems planning, and later academic training in optimization. He was not simply a theoretician whose equations were eventually adopted by industry. He worked in the settings where models were made, used, and sometimes found wanting. That experience helps explain why the simplex method's practical numerical behavior remained a subject of sustained interest to him long after its initial formulation.

One should also be careful with the language of maximizing efficiency. An objective function is not a moral definition of the public good. In a factory the objective might be profit; in a hospital it might be average waiting time; in a military operation it might be readiness. A linear program optimizes the function supplied to it under the stated constraints. It cannot determine, by algebra alone, whether the objective was wise, who bears its costs, or whether a missing constraint protects something essential. Dantzig's work gave planners an extraordinary power to formalize decisions, but responsibility for the model's purpose remained with people.

With Philip Wolfe, he developed the Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition principle for large structured linear programs. The idea is to exploit a problem whose variables or constraints fall into related blocks. Instead of presenting every possible decision to one giant calculation at once, a master problem can coordinate smaller subproblems, adding useful columns when needed. This is a serious conceptual advance: the mathematics of large systems can use their organization, not merely their size. It also reveals why an algorithm invented for general linear programs continued to generate new methods for logistics, scheduling and network design.

In 1960 Dantzig became a professor of operations research at Berkeley and helped develop its academic program. He moved to Stanford in 1966, where his work included research on large-scale systems and the connections between energy, economics and policy. He published *Linear Programming and Extensions* in 1963, a text that helped shape the education of a generation of researchers. Later interests included optimization under uncertainty, quadratic programming and computational systems. At Stanford he worked in a setting that put mathematics in continual conversation with engineering and computer science. These later decades matter: his life was not over intellectually when the 1947 algorithm had been named.

Dantzig wrote, taught and continued to work on optimization throughout his career. He received recognition from the scientific and engineering communities, including honors associated with the development of operations research. He died in 2005. His intellectual story extends beyond the invention of a single algorithm: it concerns the growth of a discipline in which a real-world planning question is translated into equations, examined critically, and solved with the aid of computation.

Go back to the factory with two products. Once a person chooses the resource limits and the values assigned to the products, the mathematics can say what follows. With better algorithms and better machines, the same kind of reasoning can coordinate enormously complicated systems. But it begins with a human act of description. That is the bridge George Dantzig helped build: from wartime shortages and bureaucratic plans to a rigorous geometry of decisions, where the difficult work is not only finding an optimum but knowing what, precisely, has been optimized.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and attribution

- **1914:** Born in Portland, Oregon, to Tobias Dantzig and Anja Ourisson.
- **1936:** University of Maryland degree; married Anne Shmuner.
- **1937:** Master's degree at Michigan; federal labor-statistics work followed.
- **Late 1930s / early 1940s:** Study under Jerzy Neyman at Berkeley; the famous 'unassigned homework' anecdote comes from later recollections.
- **World War II:** Statistical control and combat analysis in the Army Air Forces.
- **1947:** Simplex method developed in the context of Air Force planning and Project SCOOP.
- **Postwar decades:** RAND Corporation; later Berkeley and Stanford academic appointments; growth of operations research and mathematical programming.
- **2005:** Died in California.

### Mathematical companion

- **Example primal:** maximize \(4x+5y\) subject to \(2x+y\le100\), \(x+2y\le80\), and \(x,y\ge0\). The optimum is \((x,y)=(40,20)\), with objective \(260\).
- **Vertex check:** \((50,0)\) yields \(200\), \((0,40)\) yields \(200\), \((40,20)\) yields \(260\).
- **Dual formulation:** minimize \(100u+80v\) subject to \(2u+v\ge4\), \(u+2v\ge5\), \(u,v\ge0\). At optimum \(u=1\), \(v=2\) gives \(260\). Dual variables are shadow prices only within the model.
- **Historical attribution:** Dantzig invented the simplex algorithm; earlier optimization traditions include Kantorovich and others. The distinction between theoretical worst-case exponential behavior and successful practical computation should remain explicit.
