# Gene H. Golub: Why does a good algorithm need to respect subspace geometry?

Consider a modest fitting problem. You have measured two quantities on several occasions and want to estimate how each influences the result. The first column of your data table records one quantity. The second records another. Unfortunately, the two columns are nearly copies of each other. The measurement process has given you two numbers where there is almost only one independent direction.

In exact mathematics, the instruction is straightforward. Choose the coefficients that make the predicted results as close as possible to the observations. In actual computation, this straightforward instruction becomes dangerous. A formula can magnify the tiny difference between two columns until that difference is submerged in rounding. A computer can faithfully execute every operation it was told to perform and still return a misleading answer.

Gene Howard Golub spent his career investigating problems of this kind. How should we represent the geometry of a matrix so that computation does not discard the very information we need? What makes two formulas that are identical on paper behave differently when carried out with finite precision? What should an algorithm tell us when the problem itself is too sensitive for a confident answer?

His work helped make the singular value decomposition practical. It influenced the solution of least-squares problems, the comparison of subspaces, and the treatment of vast systems arising from differential equations. His influence also came through the people he trained, the books he wrote, the research meetings he organized, and the numerical-analysis community he worked to connect. To understand why this mattered, we should begin in a city household far from the research laboratories where electronic computation was just starting to take shape.

## Chicago, work, and a path into mathematics

Golub was born in Chicago on February twenty-ninth, nineteen thirty-two. His parents had immigrated to the United States during the nineteen twenties: Bernice from Latvia and Nathan from Ukraine. His father delivered bread for a bakery. His mother sewed caps. Gene and his older brother grew up in a household where money was limited, and Gene began working while he was still a boy. He later recalled jobs at a pharmacy and elsewhere, and described going to performances as an usher when admission tickets were beyond what he could afford.

These details come partly from an extended oral-history interview recorded in two thousand five. The interview lets us hear how Golub understood his own early life, rather than simply reconstructing it from degrees and publication lists. He remembered long journeys through Chicago neighborhoods, the pressure of antisemitism during his school years, and a home in which Yiddish remained familiar even as the children learned English. Mathematics appealed to him, but he did not remember himself as an exceptionally successful student. He spoke more about the opportunities and constraints around his education than about any childhood revelation of genius.

He attended Wright Junior College, spent a year at the University of Chicago, and transferred to the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. In nineteen fifty-three he completed a bachelor's degree in mathematics. A master's degree in mathematical statistics followed in nineteen fifty-four. At that point his research interests still lay close to statistics. He worked on factor analysis, a method for representing observed correlations by a smaller number of underlying variables. Even at this early stage, the practical issue was numerical: how can a computer extract stable structure from measured numbers?

The computer available at Illinois was the ILLIAC One, one of the early electronic machines developed for scientific calculation. A course in programming led Golub into the computing laboratory. There he encountered the practical cost of mathematical instructions. An algorithm had to fit the machine's memory, execute in a reasonable time, and survive the limited precision of its arithmetic. Code was part of the research, not simply the last step after a theorem.

One of his instructors, Franz Hohn, introduced him to matrix theory. In the laboratory he worked under John Purcell Nash, who taught digital computer programming at Illinois and should not be confused with the game theorist John Forbes Nash. Such details help distinguish the actual network around early computing from the familiar names that later dominate retellings. Golub's mathematical education was taking place among people who could discuss a matrix theorem in the morning and inspect how it behaved on the machine in the afternoon.

For his doctorate, Golub worked with Abraham Taub. The thesis, completed in nineteen fifty-nine, concerned Chebyshev matrix polynomials in iterative methods for solving linear equations. It compared ways of obtaining an answer by successive approximations rather than by eliminating all the unknowns at once. This was a major shift from his earlier statistical work, and it supplied a theme that would return decades later: a matrix algorithm is useful only if its convergence and its numerical costs can be understood.

## A first lesson in iteration

Imagine trying to solve a large linear system. You might begin with a rough guess for the unknown vector, compute how badly the guess satisfies the equations, and use that discrepancy to improve the guess. The discrepancy is called a residual. If the desired equation is A times x equals b, the residual is b minus A times the current x.

Suppose, for a simple illustration, a method changes the error along one direction by multiplying it by one half at each step. After one step, the error is half as large. After two, one quarter. After three, one eighth. But another direction might shrink by nine tenths per step, making overall convergence much slower. The matrix controls the different error directions, and an iteration has to contend with all of them.

Chebyshev methods choose coefficients across several steps to reduce the worst amplification over a known interval of spectral values. The idea is not to repeat the same correction blindly. It is to choose a polynomial that is small over the eigenvalue range in which errors can survive. Chebyshev polynomials are effective because, after suitable scaling, they solve a minimax problem: among polynomials with a prescribed normalization, they control the largest oscillation on an interval.

Here is a simple reason polynomials enter. If one correction changes the error by multiplying it by some matrix M, then two repetitions multiply it by M squared. More general weighted combinations of previous corrections produce a polynomial in M. In the eigenvector direction associated with an eigenvalue lambda, this matrix polynomial acts like the ordinary number obtained by evaluating the polynomial at lambda. The difficult matrix question has become a family of scalar questions, one for each eigenvalue. We can design a polynomial to keep all of those scalars small, provided we know enough about where the eigenvalues lie.

There are qualifications. A method designed for a real spectral interval may not behave the same way for a nonnormal matrix, whose eigenvectors need not form a well-conditioned basis. Floating-point errors also intervene. But the central problem is clear: an efficient algorithm must exploit information about the mathematical structure of its input.

During the final stages of this research, Richard Varga was investigating overlapping questions. Rather than turning competing work into a priority dispute, Varga and Golub compared their results and went on to publish together. Their two-part nineteen sixty-one paper analyzed Chebyshev semi-iteration, successive over-relaxation, and second-order Richardson methods. Golub later recalled Varga's generosity in handling their overlap. That collaboration was an early illustration of a habit that became characteristic of Golub's career: an encounter with another researcher could change the development of an entire program of work.

## Cambridge, Kahan, and a new question

After his doctorate, Golub received a National Science Foundation fellowship and went to Cambridge in England. He worked near Maurice Wilkes's EDSAC Two project, an important setting in the development of electronic computing. He shared an office with William, often called Velvel, Kahan. The two men cared intensely about numerical accuracy, but they approached it with different temperaments and strengths. Kahan would become a leading authority on floating-point arithmetic. Golub was developing algorithms for numerical matrix problems.

Golub returned to the United States, worked for a time at Lawrence Radiation Laboratory and Space Technology Laboratories, and in nineteen sixty-two arrived at Stanford as a visiting assistant professor. George Forsythe was helping establish computer science as an academic discipline there. Golub would later spend the academic year nineteen sixty-five to sixty-six at the Courant Institute in New York, then return to Stanford as an associate professor in its newly formed computer science department.

Meanwhile, a hard problem had emerged. A matrix, especially a rectangular one, can be understood through the lengths by which it stretches certain perpendicular directions. These lengths are its singular values. They have a long mathematical history predating Golub. The challenge for the nineteen sixties was to compute them on real machines without destroying the information contained in the smallest lengths.

Take a matrix with two input coordinates and two output coordinates. In one direction it stretches by ten. In the perpendicular direction it stretches by one ten-thousandth. If you care about the nearly invisible direction, a seemingly small absolute error in the computation may be enormous relative to the answer you seek. Computing eigenvalues of the matrix formed by transposing the original matrix and multiplying the two together looks attractive. That construction is symmetric and has eigenvalues equal to the squares of the singular values. But it also squares their relative scale. A ratio of ten thousand between stretching factors becomes a ratio of one hundred million between eigenvalues.

The exact mathematical identity has become a warning about implementation. Forming the squared matrix can lose small information during arithmetic before the eigensolver even starts. The issue had practical consequences in least squares, data analysis, and the recovery of numerical rank. Golub and Kahan set out to compute singular values in a way that retained the original geometry more faithfully.

## What singular values actually measure

Before their algorithm, work through a matrix we can visualize. Let the input have two coordinates. Define a transformation that takes the first input coordinate, triples it in one output direction and quadruples it in another, while ignoring the second input coordinate altogether. In a two-by-two matrix, its first column is three, four, and its second column is zero, zero.

Feed in the vector one, zero. The output is three, four, whose length is five. Feed in zero, one. The output vanishes. The singular values are therefore five and zero. The first right singular direction is the first coordinate axis. Its matching left singular direction is the unit vector pointing three-fifths along the first output axis and four-fifths along the second. The second right singular direction is annihilated.

This example tells us more than the raw matrix entries. The transformation remembers one combination of input coordinates and forgets another. If we observe the output six, eight, the first input coordinate must be two. The second remains undetermined. There are infinitely many exact solutions, all of the form two in the first coordinate and anything in the second. The minimum-length solution is two, zero. The pseudoinverse selects it.

In general, a singular value decomposition factors a matrix into three parts. First, an orthogonal change of input coordinates chooses right singular directions. Then a diagonal rectangular matrix stretches those directions by nonnegative singular values. Finally, another orthogonal change of coordinates places the stretched directions into the output space. The columns associated with a zero singular value reveal the kernel. The nonzero columns reveal the range. If a singular value is very small but nonzero, the map preserves that direction in principle, yet recovering it from noisy observations can be extremely sensitive.

This is why singular values belong to geometry and to numerical analysis at the same time. They describe the exact action of a linear map on spheres. They also measure which inverse calculations amplify error. In our simple example, there is no need to invert the lost direction. In a nearby problem, where the lost direction is stretched by one millionth rather than zero, an inverse calculation might multiply measurement noise in that direction by a million.

One can define the decomposition without an algorithm. To compute it reliably on a finite machine is another achievement altogether. That was the point of the Golub–Kahan work.

## Why a bidiagonal matrix is the right intermediate object

Golub and Kahan's nineteen sixty-five paper, *Calculating the Singular Values and Pseudo-Inverse of a Matrix*, introduced a practical route to the decomposition. Its first major step was to reduce a general rectangular matrix to bidiagonal form using orthogonal transformations. Bidiagonal means that all entries vanish except those on the main diagonal and one neighboring diagonal. A large, complicated array is transformed into a narrow chain of interacting coordinates.

Why is that a good intermediate representation? Orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean lengths. If we rotate or reflect the input coordinates, and independently rotate or reflect the output coordinates, the map has the same stretching factors. Its singular values are unchanged. The operations can also be implemented with favorable error properties. We gain a simpler matrix without deliberately forming the square of the original one.

One especially useful transformation is a Householder reflection. Picture a mirror in a high-dimensional space. The reflection changes the direction of a vector without changing its length. We can choose the mirror so that a given column points entirely along a coordinate axis. Every component below its leading entry becomes zero. Next, a reflection acting from the other side can remove entries to the right of the desired row position. By alternating left and right reflections, we remove almost everything while retaining a diagonal and one adjacent diagonal.

Here is a two-dimensional illustration. Start with the vector three, four. Its length is five. A suitable orthogonal transformation sends it to five, zero. We have not changed the length. We have concentrated the same information in a simpler coordinate description. In a larger matrix, successive transformations perform a version of this operation on selected remaining rows and columns. The accumulating rotations or reflections become the singular-vector factors when the reduction is complete.

The bidiagonal matrix is still not generally diagonal. Its neighboring entries couple adjacent coordinates. But the second stage is now a structured numerical problem, rather than a fresh arbitrary-matrix problem. Golub and Kahan addressed that second stage as well, and subsequent development improved it. Later work with Peter Businger treated complex matrices. The nineteen seventy paper with Christian Reinsch gave a practical algorithm for singular value decomposition and least-squares solutions, including a refined bidiagonal stage related to the QR algorithm.

It is worth distinguishing the contributions precisely. Golub did not invent the abstract singular value decomposition. Nor is it historically accurate to attach one complete production algorithm solely to the first paper. The nineteen sixty-five work with Kahan, and the later developments with Businger and Reinsch, were steps in making an existing mathematical decomposition computationally effective. The historical achievement lies in the numerical algorithm, its analysis, and the ensuing practical reach of the decomposition.

We can now return to the nearly duplicate columns from the opening problem and see why the computational route makes a difference.

## A small least-squares problem that defeats a tempting formula

Consider a matrix whose first column is one, zero, and whose second column is one, epsilon. Epsilon is a small positive number. The two columns point almost along the same line. If epsilon is nonzero, the matrix is invertible, but the inverse becomes more sensitive as epsilon approaches zero.

Suppose the measured output is one, epsilon. What input could have produced it? Taking zero units of the first column and one unit of the second gives exactly the output. Thus the correct solution is zero, one. If the output were instead one, zero, the exact solution would be one, zero. The two outputs are close when epsilon is small, yet their corresponding input coefficients differ by one in each coordinate. That is the essential conditioning problem: small changes in the data can cause large changes in the answer.

A standard derivation of least squares leads to the normal equations: transpose the input matrix, multiply by the matrix again, and solve the resulting symmetric system. For our example, the symmetric matrix has first row one, one, and second row one, one plus epsilon squared. Its determinant is epsilon squared. All of the information distinguishing the columns now sits in an addition of epsilon squared to one.

Take epsilon equal to one hundred-millionth. Its square is one part in ten quadrillion, too small to survive addition to one at the working precision. In common double-precision arithmetic, adding this square to one rounds back to one. The computed normal-equation matrix may look exactly singular even though the original matrix is nonsingular. The method has lost the critical information before solving the final equations.

The singular values tell a clearer story. The larger singular value is close to the square root of two. The smaller is close to epsilon divided by the square root of two. The condition number, roughly the ratio of these values, grows like two divided by epsilon. That is already bad. But forming the normal equations makes the eigenvalue ratio grow like four divided by epsilon squared. The numerical procedure has compounded an intrinsically sensitive problem by squaring its condition number.

An orthogonal factorization avoids explicitly constructing this squared matrix. Householder QR exposes the small independent component directly in a triangular factor. An SVD goes further, producing the directions and magnitudes that explain the sensitivity. Neither method can recover information that the measurements never contained. And it would be wrong to promise that a backward-stable algorithm always returns accurate coefficients in an ill-conditioned problem. What it can do is avoid injecting a needlessly larger computational error and provide information about which directions are poorly determined.

That distinction shaped Golub's work on least squares. A small residual means the fitted output is close to the observations. It does not automatically mean that each coefficient is close to a uniquely meaningful true value. If two columns nearly agree, many pairs of coefficients can describe nearly the same fitted observations. The SVD makes this ambiguity visible through a small singular value and its associated right singular vector.

Think again about the map that carries input two, zero into output six, eight. A pseudoinverse can select the minimum-norm solution even when there is no unique inverse. If a small singular value rather than a true zero is present, an analyst may have to decide whether recovering that direction is justified by the measurement accuracy. Truncated singular value methods and regularization make that choice explicit. They are applications of the decomposition, not automatic consequences of computing it.

Golub's statistical beginnings remained relevant. Factor analysis and regression had first brought him face to face with matrices drawn from imperfect observations. Decades later, he was still asking which geometric features of those matrices could be extracted responsibly.

## When it is the model itself that is in error

Ordinary least squares assumes the data matrix is fixed and errors are concentrated in the observed output. In many experiments this is a questionable model. Both sides of an equation may have been measured. Imagine points that should lie close to a straight line. We record both horizontal and vertical coordinates with errors. If we treat the horizontal coordinates as exact, we ask a different question from the one the physical experiment supports.

A simple illustration is a collection of points near a diagonal line through the origin. Their measured coordinate pairs are perhaps one, one-point-one, then two, one-point-nine, then three, three-point-one. Ordinary vertical least squares chooses a slope by minimizing squared vertical displacements. But if both coordinates were measured with comparable uncertainty, a perpendicular displacement may be the more meaningful error. Finding a line whose normal direction makes these perpendicular errors as small as possible is a singular-vector problem.

To see why, place the measured points as rows of a matrix with two columns. A line through the origin has a perpendicular unit direction n. Each point's signed perpendicular displacement from the line is its dot product with n. Collecting all those displacements gives the matrix times n. Their total squared size is the squared length of that result. Thus the best normal direction is the unit vector minimizing the length of the matrix times n. The SVD tells us this is a right singular vector for the smallest singular value. Once the normal is known, the line itself is perpendicular to it.

This calculation gives a geometric entry into total least squares. In the general regression setting, the observations and the predictor variables are collected together in an augmented matrix. One searches for a low-rank correction that makes the data consistent with an exact linear relationship. Under the standard nondegeneracy conditions, the smallest right singular vector determines the coefficients. Care is needed with scaling, with intercepts, with the uniqueness of the fit, and with measurement errors of unequal variance. A perpendicular fit is appropriate only when its error model matches the experiment.

The history here is more intricate than a slogan about inventing a technique. Errors in variables and related fitting methods had been studied long before Golub and Charles Van Loan. Golub later described how Christian Reinsch's work exposed him to related calculations and how the terminology and numerical analysis developed with Van Loan. Their nineteen eighty paper, *An Analysis of the Total Least Squares Problem*, gave a systematic SVD treatment of the problem, including its sensitivity. The method's usefulness lies partly in explaining when a fitted line is well determined and when small variations can change it drastically.

If the two singular values of a centered two-column data matrix are widely separated, there is a preferred direction along which the points lie. If they are almost equal, the point cloud is nearly round, and many lines fit it about equally well. The mathematics does not conceal that ambiguity. The gap between singular values indicates whether the fitted direction is stable.

Once again, the geometric object is not a particular arrangement of coefficients. It is a subspace or direction that the data support, together with information about its sensitivity. This is one reason the SVD became useful far outside the original problems of matrix factorization.

## Measuring an angle between two fitted subspaces

Consider two teams that independently estimate a two-dimensional signal space inside a much larger measurement space. They return different columns as bases. How should we compare the spaces? Comparing column entries directly is meaningless: each team may have rotated, rescaled, or reordered its basis without changing the space at all.

Åke Björck and Golub answered this computational question in their nineteen seventy-three paper on angles between linear subspaces. Their central reduction is easy to state once we understand orthonormal bases. First obtain matrices whose columns are orthonormal and span the two spaces. Next form the matrix of inner products between these two sets of columns. Finally compute its singular values. Those numbers are the cosines of the principal angles between the spaces.

Work through the simplest case. One line lies along the first coordinate axis. A second line points thirty degrees away from it in the plane. Let both lines be represented by unit direction vectors. Their inner product is the cosine of thirty degrees, about point eight-six-six. The overlap matrix contains just that one number. Its only singular value is point eight-six-six, so the principal angle is thirty degrees.

A second example reveals why a list of angles is needed. Let one plane be spanned by the first and second coordinate axes in three-dimensional space. Let the other plane be spanned by the first coordinate axis and by a direction making sixty degrees with the second axis, tilting toward the third. The two planes share one exact line, so their first principal angle is zero. Their remaining directions meet at sixty degrees, so the second angle is sixty degrees. After choosing aligned orthonormal bases, the overlap matrix is diagonal, with entries one and one half. Its singular values expose exactly those two angles.

The geometry is invariant under any rotation of either plane's chosen basis. If we replace a basis by another orthonormal basis for the same plane, we multiply the overlap matrix by orthogonal factors, which do not change its singular values. That is the mathematical reason the algorithm measures spaces rather than their arbitrary coordinates.

This does not eliminate numerical subtleties. If the original spanning columns are almost dependent, constructing their orthonormal bases may itself be sensitive. And for two almost coincident lines, the cosine is so close to one that recovering the angle by taking an inverse cosine may lose accuracy. Complementary computations using perpendicular components can supply the sine of a small angle more accurately. Björck and Golub studied these issues as well as the numerical merits of Householder transformations and modified Gram–Schmidt.

The comparison of subspaces is a direct application of Golub's broader program: find a representation in which the invariant of interest can be computed, and avoid formulas that amplify the wrong errors. The same viewpoint applies to canonical correlations in statistics, to signal subspaces in data analysis, and to numerical approximations of eigenspaces. Exact principal angles are geometric objects; their reliable computation is a separate achievement.

## When a matrix becomes too large to factor

The SVD is powerful, but a direct factorization is not always what a computational problem needs. In a weather model, an engineering simulation, or a discretized partial differential equation, the number of unknowns may be enormous. Often the matrix is sparse: most of its entries are zero. One may be able to compute the product of the matrix and a vector cheaply without ever storing the full matrix as a dense array.

This brings us back to Golub's doctoral interest in iteration. The most important numerical methods for huge linear systems often work with a sequence of carefully chosen search directions. The conjugate gradient method is a classical example for symmetric positive-definite matrices. Starting from an approximate solution, it uses residual information and produces directions that are orthogonal with respect to the quadratic form defined by the matrix. In exact arithmetic, it reaches the solution in at most as many steps as there are unknowns. In practice, finite precision and the distribution of eigenvalues affect its convergence.

A geometric illustration helps. Suppose a quadratic function forms a bowl above a two-dimensional plane. Its level curves are ellipses. If the ellipses are nearly circular, the steepest direction toward the center is informative. If they are extremely elongated, a naive steepest-descent method may zigzag across the narrow valley while making slow progress down its length. Conjugate gradients uses directions adapted to the quadratic geometry. It tries to avoid repeatedly undoing the progress made in a previous direction.

Preconditioning changes the coordinates so that the problem is easier for the iterative method. Imagine stretching the elongated ellipse into something closer to a circle. The original solution remains recoverable, but the transformed system may allow much faster progress. The difficult part is finding a transformation that improves the spectrum without costing more than the saved iteration is worth.

In a nineteen seventy-six collaboration, Paul Concus, Golub, and Dianne O'Leary developed a generalized conjugate-gradient treatment for numerical elliptic partial differential equations. The method became widely associated with preconditioned conjugate gradients. Their paper drew on the earlier method of Hestenes and Stiefel while addressing the actual computational obstacles encountered when applying it to discretized physical systems. The collaboration illustrates how Golub connected structural mathematics, algorithm design, and the demands of scientific applications.

Other problems called for direct methods that exploited special structure. One example came from the Poisson equation, which describes equilibrium temperature or electric potential under suitable boundary conditions. On a rectangular grid, the discrete operator often separates into contributions from each coordinate direction. With carefully chosen transforms, one can diagonalize one part of the operator and reduce a large multidimensional calculation to many smaller ones. This gives a fast Poisson solver.

Why should separation help? Imagine a square grid whose temperature at each interior point depends on four neighbors. Treating every grid point as an unrelated unknown loses the regular structure of the grid. But along one coordinate direction there are familiar oscillating modes, much like standing waves on a string. If a transform decomposes the solution into these modes, each mode satisfies a simpler equation in the other direction. Instead of attacking one huge coupled system, we solve a collection of smaller structured problems.

Golub worked with Bill Buzbee and Clair Nielson on fast direct methods in this area. These methods were useful because scientific models were beginning to exceed the memory and time available for ordinary elimination. Golub's career therefore had two complementary sides: preserving geometry when a matrix is small enough to factor, and exploiting structure when a matrix is too large for a direct dense computation.

Both sides depend on identifying what a calculation can preserve. Orthogonality is valuable in a factorization because it protects lengths. Conjugacy is valuable in an iterative method because it organizes corrections relative to a quadratic form. Separation is valuable in a grid problem because it exposes independent modes. None of these is a magic formula applied to all inputs. Each is a response to a precise mathematical structure.

## A Stanford department and a laboratory of conversations

Golub's return to Stanford in nineteen sixty-six placed him in a department still defining what computer science research would become. George Forsythe had insisted that numerical computation deserved academic stature. Golub helped build a setting in which numerical analysis could flourish alongside theoretical computer science, programming systems, and scientific applications. He became a full professor in nineteen seventy, chaired the department in the early nineteen eighties, and later held the Fletcher Jones professorship.

His students and collaborators have described an environment in which research happened through regular conversation. Visitors arrived from other institutions and countries. Young researchers could meet established figures. Problems appeared on blackboards, over meals, and during meetings. An idea might begin as a question about a statistical model and turn into a paper about matrix factorizations. The connections among subjects were strengthened by the personal connections among researchers.

Dianne O'Leary, who later wrote a National Academy biographical memoir of Golub, worked with him on iterative methods and numerical analysis. Other students and colleagues pursued optimization, matrix computations, numerical software, and scientific modeling. Golub advised about thirty doctoral students. That figure is more than a measure of academic productivity. It describes a multiplying network of people whose research was shaped by his insistence on useful and mathematically credible numerical algorithms.

He also cared about making the field accessible to people who were not already inside its most influential institutions. His hospitality at Stanford became well known. Colleagues recalled visitors staying at his home, students being introduced at conferences, and researchers receiving invitations that helped them enter an international conversation. The personality behind the technical articles had consequences for how the field developed.

In the nineteen eighties Golub served as president of the Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics. He founded or helped establish influential journals devoted to scientific computing and matrix analysis. And when electronic communication began to reshape research communities, he recognized the value of a regular digest of numerical-analysis announcements. The NA-Digest circulated research news, meetings, jobs, and mathematical questions to people who otherwise might not have heard about them. The system was simple, but it changed who could participate in a discussion.

There is a parallel between those efforts and his algorithmic work. A numerical method becomes more valuable when it can be used reliably by people far from its inventors. A research community becomes more productive when ideas move across institutional boundaries. In both settings, a local insight becomes a shared capability.

## A book that made matrix computation a coherent subject

In nineteen eighty-three Golub and Charles Van Loan published *Matrix Computations*. The book grew in part from lectures Golub had given at Johns Hopkins and from their collaborative work on numerical linear algebra. Van Loan played an extensive role in developing and organizing the material. Golub later spoke appreciatively of that collaboration, crediting Van Loan's work in turning lectures and research problems into a sustained exposition.

The book did something different from a collection of famous algorithms. It made the subject intelligible as a connected field. Orthogonal transformations, least squares, eigenvalue computations, singular values, iterative methods, and conditioning could be understood as related ways of meeting the same demands: exploit structure, control numerical error, and spend computational effort where it changes the answer.

Consider what we now know about the original two-column fitting problem. The normal equations are short, but they square the condition number. QR factorization is longer to describe but preserves more numerical information. The SVD can be more computationally expensive, yet it exposes both the fitted solution and the poorly determined directions. A method that is best for one task may be inefficient for another. The right comparison must consider not just arithmetic counts but the answer we actually need, the conditioning of the input, and how the algorithm behaves under rounding.

That is a coherent intellectual discipline. It resists the temptation to choose an algorithm by memorizing a formula. It asks the analyst to identify the invariant being computed, the mathematical assumptions that make it meaningful, and the failure modes of the numerical representation.

The practical influence of *Matrix Computations* came from making such questions teachable across generations. Graduate students could use it to learn methods, researchers could use it as a reference, and people in neighboring fields could understand why one implementation was safer than another. Later editions expanded its coverage. The first edition appeared during Golub's lifetime; a fourth edition was published after his death.

## The person at the center of the matrices

Golub received wide professional recognition, including election to both the National Academy of Engineering and the National Academy of Sciences. But colleagues' recollections repeatedly return to his interest in people. He remembered former students, kept in touch with collaborators, and enjoyed introducing researchers to one another. An extraordinary number of people came to associate a visit to Stanford with a visit to Golub's intellectual circle.

There was also a serious side to the joy he took in mathematics. Numerical results can be wrong. A program that runs without errors can return an answer whose apparent precision is entirely unjustified. Golub's papers show a sustained willingness to confront that problem directly, whether it appears in the small singular values of a rectangular matrix, the refinement of a least-squares solution, or the convergence of an iterative solver.

In two thousand five he sat for a long oral-history interview with Thomas Haigh. By then he had been active in numerical analysis for about half a century. The interview moves among childhood memories, research collaborations, early computers, departmental politics, his books, and the people who helped his work develop. It presents scientific accomplishment as a sequence of encounters and decisions. A question asked by a colleague changes a theorem. A visiting researcher supplies the missing part of an algorithm. A promising calculation becomes useful when someone writes it down clearly enough for others to implement.

Golub died in November two thousand seven, aged seventy-five. He had been born on leap day, a date that made his birthday an easy detail for colleagues to remember. His legacy includes numerical methods still recognizable in modern mathematical software, but it is wider than any one factorization.

The lesson of our opening least-squares example can now be stated precisely. A numerical algorithm acts on a representation of a mathematical object. When the object is a subspace, the representation may be a badly conditioned set of columns. When the object is a linear transformation, the relevant geometry may be its orthogonal directions and stretching factors. When the object is a huge physical system, the useful structure may be sparsity, conjugacy, or separation into modes. A good algorithm takes that structure seriously. It should avoid destroying information that is present, reveal sensitivity that cannot be avoided, and help the user distinguish an accurate calculation from a well-determined answer.

Gene Golub's work made these principles concrete. Singular values became reliably computable quantities rather than merely abstract geometric facts. Least squares gained numerical methods that exposed rank and conditioning. Principal angles became comparisons of subspaces that did not depend on arbitrary basis choices. Large systems gained methods that used their structure rather than fighting it. And the people who learned to ask those questions carried them into new branches of science and engineering.

### 1. SVD and the minimum-norm pseudoinverse

For any real matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), an SVD is \(A=U\Sigma V^T\) with orthogonal \(U,V\) (or thin orthonormal-column factors) and nonnegative diagonal entries \(\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_r>0\). The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse is \(A^+=V\Sigma^+U^T\), where \(\Sigma^+\) inverts the positive singular values and leaves zero entries zero.

The spoken rank-one example is
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&0\end{pmatrix},\quad b=\begin{pmatrix}6\\8\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma(A)=(5,0),\quad A^+b=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
All exact solutions are \((2,t)^T\); the pseudoinverse chooses \(t=0\). For nonzero small singular values, \(\|A^+\|_2=1/\sigma_{\min}^+(A)\), which controls noise amplification on the range. The SVD predates Golub; Golub–Kahan's numerical achievement concerned its stable computation.

### 2. Golub–Kahan bidiagonal reduction

The 1965 paper computes orthogonal \(Q_L,Q_R\) with \(Q_L^TAQ_R=B\), where \(B\) is bidiagonal. Successive Householder reflectors operate alternately on columns and rows. Singular values are preserved since
\[
B^TB=Q_R^TA^TAQ_R.
\]
The equation is an identity for analysis, **not** an instruction to form \(A^TA\) numerically. The 1970 Golub–Reinsch paper develops the practical iterative bidiagonal SVD stage. Peter Businger's 1969 contribution addresses complex SVD algorithms. See Golub–Kahan (1965), §§2–4, and Golub–Reinsch (1970).

### 3. Why normal equations square conditioning

For \(\varepsilon>0\), set
\[
A_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},\quad
A_\varepsilon^TA_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1+\varepsilon^2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The determinant of the normal-equations matrix is \(\varepsilon^2\). The squared singular values are the eigenvalues of \(A_\varepsilon^TA_\varepsilon\):
\[
\lambda_{\pm}=\tfrac12\bigl(2+\varepsilon^2\pm\sqrt{4+\varepsilon^4}\bigr),\quad
\sigma_\max\sim\sqrt2,\quad \sigma_\min\sim\varepsilon/\sqrt2.
\]
Thus \(\kappa_2(A_\varepsilon)\sim2/\varepsilon\), while \(\kappa_2(A_\varepsilon^TA_\varepsilon)=\kappa_2(A_\varepsilon)^2\sim4/\varepsilon^2\). For \(\varepsilon=10^{-8}\), the double-precision rounded value of \(1+\varepsilon^2\) is \(1\). With \(b=(1,\varepsilon)^T\), the exact solution is \((0,1)^T\); with \(b=(1,0)^T\), it is \((1,0)^T\). This distinction separates inherent ill-conditioning from the additional harm of normal-equation formation.

### 4. Principal angles with Björck (1973)

For subspaces \(F,G\) represented by orthonormal-column matrices \(Q_F,Q_G\), the cosines of the \(q=\min(\dim F,\dim G)\) principal angles are the singular values of \(M=Q_F^TQ_G\), in nonincreasing order. Replacing either frame by a different orthonormal basis multiplies \(M\) by orthogonal factors, leaving singular values unchanged. Example:
\[
F=\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2),\quad G=\operatorname{span}\Bigl(e_1,\tfrac12e_2+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}e_3\Bigr),\quad
M=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/2).
\]
The angles are \(0,\pi/3\). The 1973 paper develops methods for cosines and sines and tests numerical orthogonalization approaches. Small \(\theta\) should not be extracted solely from \(\arccos(1-\delta)\) when \(\delta\) can be erased by rounding.

### 5. Orthogonal regression and total least squares

Given an \(m\times2\) matrix \(C\) whose rows are measured points \((x_i,y_i)\) and a line through the origin with unit normal \(n\), the sum of squared **orthogonal** distances is \(\|Cn\|_2^2\). The minimizer is a right singular vector for \(\sigma_\min(C)\). For general \(Ax\approx b\), form the augmented data matrix \([A\ b]\); a singular vector of its smallest singular value gives a consistent rank-reducing correction. Writing that vector as \((z,t)\), the regression coefficients are \(x=-z/t\) when \(t\ne0\) and the standard TLS rank and uniqueness conditions hold. If \(t=0\), this formula does not define a finite solution. Scaling of variables determines the physical error model. See Golub–Van Loan (1980), pp. 883–893.

### 6. Chebyshev semi-iteration and the error polynomial

For a linear stationary iteration with error propagation \(e_{k+1}=Me_k\), the error after \(k\) steps is \(M^ke_0\). A semi-iterative combination gives \(e_k=p_k(M)e_0\) for a suitable polynomial. If \(M\) is symmetric, \(\|p_k(M)\|_2=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(M)}|p_k(\lambda)|\). The interval-minimax properties of scaled Chebyshev polynomials yield acceleration when spectral bounds are known. Non-normal matrices require additional analysis; scalar spectral bounds alone do not uniformly control \(\|p_k(M)\|\). Golub–Varga (1961) develops comparisons among several schemes.

### 7. Conjugate gradients, structure, and conditioning

For SPD \(A\), conjugate gradients minimizes the \(A\)-norm of the error over Krylov affine subspaces in exact arithmetic. The conventional convergence bound involves \(\bigl((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)\bigr)^k\), with a factor of two. Preconditioning changes the relevant spectrum by applying an SPD approximation \(M\) to \(A\); in exact arithmetic the effective symmetric matrix is \(M^{-1/2}AM^{-1/2}\). The 1976 Concus–Golub–O'Leary treatment studies its application to discretized elliptic PDEs, building on Hestenes–Stiefel (1952). Fast Poisson solvers exploit separable grid operators, for example \(I\otimes T+T\otimes I\), diagonalizable in an eigenbasis for \(T\).

### 8. Historical chronology and attribution

- **1932:** Born in Chicago on February 29; parents Bernice and Nathan Golub had immigrated from Latvia and Ukraine.
- **1953–1959:** University of Illinois bachelor's (1953), master's in mathematical statistics (1954), and doctorate under Abraham Taub (1959); ILLIAC I programming and Chebyshev iterative methods.
- **1959–1960:** NSF fellow at Cambridge, EDSAC II environment, shared office with William Kahan.
- **1961:** With Richard Varga, two-part paper on Chebyshev semi-iterative and Richardson methods.
- **1962:** Visiting assistant professor at Stanford; **1965–1966:** visiting appointment at Courant; **1966:** associate professor at Stanford in the new computer-science department.
- **1965:** With W. Kahan, *Calculating the Singular Values and Pseudo-Inverse of a Matrix*, *J. SIAM Ser. B Numer. Anal.* 2(2), 205–224; submitted in 1964.
- **1969:** With P. Businger, complex-matrix SVD algorithm, *Communications of the ACM* 12, 564–565.
- **1970:** With C. Reinsch, *Singular Value Decomposition and Least Squares Solutions*, *Numerische Mathematik* 14, 403–420.
- **1973:** With Åke Björck, *Numerical Methods for Computing Angles Between Linear Subspaces*, *Mathematics of Computation* 27, 579–594.
- **1976:** With Paul Concus and Dianne P. O'Leary, generalized/preconditioned conjugate gradients for elliptic PDEs.
- **1980:** With Charles Van Loan, *An Analysis of the Total Least Squares Problem*, *SIAM J. Numer. Anal.* 17(6), 883–893.
- **1981–1984:** Stanford CS department chair (some shorter summaries give tenure to 1985; memoir and detailed institutional history give 1984).
- **1983:** *Matrix Computations*, with Charles Van Loan, Johns Hopkins University Press; later editions 1989, 1996, and posthumously 2013.
- **1985–1987:** President of SIAM; influential founding editor and communicator in the numerical-analysis community.
- **1990/1993:** Elected to the National Academy of Engineering and National Academy of Sciences respectively.
- **2005:** Oral history interview by Thomas Haigh, October 22–23.
- **2007:** Died November 16 in California.

Golub's original memoir and oral-history recollections describe firsthand memories, while dates and publication metadata are independently supported by journals, institutional records, and the National Academy memoir. The abstract SVD, principal-angle geometry, classical CG, and errors-in-variables modeling predate Golub's computational contributions.
