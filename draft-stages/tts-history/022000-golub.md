# Gene H. Golub: Why does a good algorithm need to respect subspace geometry?

An eigenvalue theorem can tell us what the right answer means without telling us how to compute it. A numerical algorithm must address an additional problem: the data in memory are rounded, and the computation itself introduces more rounding.

Gene Golub was a central figure in numerical linear algebra. His work on matrix factorizations and the numerical solution of linear-algebra problems influenced the algorithms used throughout scientific computing. With Åke Björck he published a 1973 analysis of methods for computing angles between linear subspaces.

Suppose two subspaces are represented by tall matrices. We can take their column spans, but raw columns may be far from orthonormal. The first step is therefore a QR factorization: rewrite each matrix as an orthonormal frame times a small upper-triangular factor. The frames preserve the subspaces while giving a clean coordinate system for measuring angles.

Now compute the singular values of the overlap between the two frames. Each singular value is a cosine of a principal angle. A value close to one means nearly aligned directions. A value close to zero means directions that are nearly perpendicular.

The original paper compared Householder transformations and modified Gram–Schmidt in this setting. Both can construct bases, but their numerical behavior differs. A fast method is not automatically an accurate method for every geometric quantity, particularly when the input columns are nearly dependent.

This matters because a perturbation estimate may be tested against computed angles. If the numerical method for measuring the angles is unstable, a graph can appear to contradict the theorem even though the underlying problem has not. The computation of the quantity being bounded deserves scrutiny along with the bound itself.

Golub's historical role in this story is to turn spectral geometry into a discipline of reliable computation. The numerical representation should preserve the mathematical invariant that matters. A subspace is unchanged by replacing one spanning basis with another, and a good algorithm should aim to produce the same angles from either description, within justified numerical error.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

Let \(A=Q_AR_A\) and \(B=Q_BR_B\) be thin QR factorizations of
full-column-rank matrices. Then
\(\cos\theta_i=s_i(Q_A^*Q_B)\).
Principal angles depend on the column spaces, not on the scaling of the
original columns. Björck–Golub (1973) discusses QR implementation choices.
