# 065000 - Karl Weierstrass: The schoolteacher who rebuilt analysis

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Prussian schooling, a father's career expectations, Gudermann, years of secondary teaching, Berlin academic life, students including Kovalevskaya, and the reconstruction of mathematical rigor. **Background:** an informal idea of functions and limits is enough.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

For more than a decade, the man who would become one of Europe's most influential teachers of advanced mathematics taught adolescents in provincial Prussian schools. He supervised lessons, examined pupils, and carried out the routine duties of a schoolmaster. In his spare hours he worked on problems so specialized that few nearby colleagues could judge them. Karl Weierstrass did not take the conventional path from promising university student to young professor. His reputation arrived in middle age, and it was created partly by a paper that had to travel from an ordinary schoolteacher's desk to the small international community capable of understanding it.

Weierstrass was born in Ostenfelde in Westphalia in October eighteen fifteen, in the year of Waterloo and the reorganization of Europe after Napoleon. His father, Wilhelm, worked in the Prussian administration, and the family moved as his posts changed. His mother, Theodora, died when Karl was a child. He was the eldest of four children. A state official's family valued education, but valued it also as the route to a reliable occupation. A career in the expanding Prussian bureaucracy promised stability that mathematical research, especially for someone without independent wealth, could not guarantee.

At school in Paderborn, Weierstrass worked hard and performed very well. He also helped with bookkeeping, an early indication that intellectual talent did not exempt him from family responsibilities. His father wanted him to study finance and public administration. In eighteen thirty-four Karl entered the University of Bonn with that expectation hanging over him. Mathematics attracted him far more than the prescribed courses, and his university years became troubled. Later accounts describe his retreat into student social life, fencing, and drinking, and his failure to take a qualifying examination. Those facts should not be turned into a diagnosis from a distance. He was caught between a profession his family considered sensible and a vocation for which he had no clear institutional route.

There was a second chance, though not the one he might have imagined. He went to Muenster to prepare for teaching examinations, and there he encountered Christoph Gudermann, who lectured on elliptic functions. Gudermann was part of the generation that had absorbed the new work of Abel and Jacobi. For Weierstrass, the encounter was decisive. He found a subject where algebraic calculations, infinite series, and complex variables came together in a difficult and promising way. Gudermann recognized the quality of his mathematical work. The young man still faced the ordinary requirements of teacher certification, but he was beginning to produce ideas beyond what an examination asked of him.

After a probationary year at a school in Muenster, he was assigned to Deutsch-Krone in West Prussia in eighteen forty-two. Later he taught in Braunsberg, now Braniewo in Poland. These names matter. The lands he moved through were Prussian provinces with their own languages, religions, and local cultures; many of their place names changed through later political upheavals. To say he was simply a 'German university mathematician' erases the long period when he worked far from Berlin and did not belong to a university at all. Teaching posts provided a salary, but the work left him little protected research time.

A secondary-school teacher was expected to teach more than an advanced mathematics seminar. Weierstrass's assignments included ordinary school subjects, and a Prussian gymnasium had examinations, timetables, discipline, reports, and supervision. His private mathematical calculations were carried on against that routine. It is possible to look back and call those years a period of obscurity, but such a label mistakes fame for activity. The school system supplied him a livelihood and, intermittently, a place to publish; it did not provide the intellectual community of a university mathematics department. The gap between ability and professional status could remain visible for years without producing a clean public crisis.


The image of Weierstrass isolated in a classroom is only partly right. He read, developed lectures, wrote mathematical papers, and kept in touch with the publication world. School programs sometimes included scholarly reports, and he used that opportunity to put specialized results into print. Such an outlet did not automatically reach the mathematicians who would know why an argument mattered. In eighteen fifty-four he published a paper on Abelian functions in *Crelle's Journal*. The journal associated with Abel's career now became the means by which a different mathematician escaped professional obscurity.

The response was rapid. The University of Koenigsberg granted him an honorary doctorate. Leading mathematicians took notice, and negotiations began for a university position. In eighteen fifty-six he received a post at the Berlin Industrial Institute and an appointment at the University of Berlin. By eighteen sixty-four he held a full professorship there. The chronology is striking: a researcher whose arguments had been developing since the early eighteen forties was suddenly treated as a major intellectual force. Recognition was not proof that the previous years had been wasted. It was proof that scholarly work can mature outside the institutions best equipped to reward it, while remaining vulnerable to the absence of an audience.

Berlin gave Weierstrass students. A large audience mattered to the future of his mathematics because he built much of his mature reputation through teaching. His lectures on analytic functions, elliptic functions, calculus, and the foundations of analysis attracted mathematicians from many countries. Students took notes, discussed proofs, and carried his methods elsewhere. The person later remembered for exact mathematical definitions was also a lecturer with a distinctive way of developing an entire subject over a sequence of courses. Some results became broadly known through these lecture traditions before appearing in a definitive published form under his own name.

To understand why, consider what a nineteenth-century mathematician might mean by a function being continuous. In simple examples, it means the graph can be drawn without lifting a pencil, or that nearby inputs give nearby outputs. Those descriptions are persuasive but can mislead. A statement that is true for all functions cannot be established merely by pointing to examples a reader can sketch. There were complex functions, functions defined by infinite series, and limiting constructions whose behavior contradicted geometric intuition. Weierstrass worked toward methods in which the precise size of an allowed error and the precise choice of a neighborhood could carry the argument.

The familiar epsilon-delta form expresses the idea in a way a listener can understand. Suppose we want the value of a function near a point to differ from its value at that point by less than any chosen positive error. Continuity means that, after the error has been chosen, we can find a sufficiently small range of input differences that guarantees that output bound. The order of choices is the heart of the statement. We do not pick one range in advance and hope it works for every desired accuracy. We are allowed to respond to each demanded accuracy with a corresponding input tolerance. There were predecessors to this method, including Bolzano and Cauchy; the history is not a single moment when Weierstrass invented rigor. His lectures gave such formulations a systematic and influential role.

Complex analysis was a particular center of his work. A power series is an infinite sum built from successive powers of a variable, like a polynomial that may extend indefinitely. Inside its region of convergence it can have tightly controlled behavior. Weierstrass treated analytic functions through such local expansions and the ways those local descriptions could be continued from one region to another. One can picture a complicated landscape being mapped by overlapping charts. Each chart describes a small neighborhood; compatibility on overlaps allows one to extend knowledge without pretending that a single formula must describe the entire world at once. The mathematics is more exact than the image, but the image shows why local power series became a foundation for global function theory.

In eighteen sixty-one his health failed badly enough to interrupt his lectures for an extended period. Later he sometimes lectured seated while a student wrote on the board. Teaching could be physically demanding. Students' notebooks and recollections became part of the evidence through which later generations learned his methods.

The household in Berlin also mattered. Weierstrass lived with his sisters Clara and Elise, and their father later joined them. The sisters helped sustain domestic life while Karl's public responsibilities grew. This is not a minor note about a professor's housekeeping. A person who spends a working day teaching and analyzing proofs is supported by labor that does not appear on the title page of a mathematical memoir. His sisters' presence and their friendships with visitors made the household a site of intellectual hospitality as well as family life. The philosopher's image of solitary thought tells us very little about the practical organization of a scholar's days.

One of the most discussed results associated with him concerns a continuous function that is differentiable nowhere. For the simple curves shown to beginners, continuity and smoothness seem to go together. But continuity only controls small changes in function values; it does not require a well-defined slope at each point. Weierstrass exhibited an infinite sum of oscillations whose total variation could be controlled enough to ensure continuity, while the local oscillations prevented a stable tangent slope anywhere. Other mathematicians, including Bolzano, had explored related phenomena earlier. The importance of Weierstrass's example is that it became a clear and widely influential counterweight to the assumption that a continuous curve is necessarily smooth almost everywhere.

What makes such an example historically significant is not a taste for pathological objects. It changes how one proves ordinary statements. If continuity alone cannot ensure a derivative even at a single point, then any argument using derivatives must say what additional conditions are needed. Mathematics gains power by discovering where its intuitions fail. That was the intellectual atmosphere in which Weierstrass's school became so influential: analysis had grown large enough to require explicit conditions for operations that had seemed safe on familiar functions.

There is another, more personal reason his name remains central. In eighteen seventy, Sofia Kovalevskaya sought advanced mathematical study in Berlin. University regulations excluded women from attending courses as ordinary students. Weierstrass examined her work and agreed to teach her privately. This was not a small favor in the circumstances. A student could not acquire academic recognition in Berlin through the normal route, and private instruction demanded time and serious attention. Their sustained mathematical relationship helped make possible the papers for which she later received a doctorate at Goettingen, under arrangements that did not require ordinary residence or classroom admission there.

According to biographical research on their correspondence, they met regularly for private lessons over several years. Her companion Julia Lermontova, who pursued chemistry, was also welcomed into the household circle. The relationship became personal and enduring: mentor and student exchanged letters, and Weierstrass followed Kovalevskaya's development as a professional mathematician. It is worth thinking about the asymmetry. He could invite an exceptionally talented young woman into his home and devote serious time to her work; he could not simply give her the ordinary university rights that a male student would receive. The work of teaching across that boundary exposed the boundary itself.


It would be inaccurate to make Weierstrass the sole origin of Kovalevskaya's achievements. She was a powerful mathematician in her own right, and her work drew on multiple discussions and intellectual resources. But his recognition of her talent, his willingness to teach her despite the formal barrier, and his later correspondence were important. The contrast is worth holding in mind: a university that could employ one of the era's leading mathematicians refused his outstanding female student the ordinary status of student. Individual mentorship could counter a barrier for one person; it did not erase the institution that created the barrier.

Weierstrass's pupils included figures who later reshaped mathematics in different directions: Georg Cantor, Hermann Schwarz, Goesta Mittag-Leffler, and many others attended or absorbed his lectures. Mittag-Leffler's own editorial networks later helped transmit new work internationally. The Berlin school was not a group of people repeating a master's definitions. Some students carried those methods into areas where they questioned assumptions, built new research programs, and sometimes disagreed with colleagues. To describe the school's influence as a family tree of theorem names misses the classrooms, journals, lectures, and letters that sustained it.

Weierstrass also had significant disagreements with other mathematical approaches. In algebraic and geometric subjects, different researchers placed different emphasis on direct calculation, symbolic generality, abstract methods, and geometric understanding. He favored a particular program in analysis and complex function theory, but he did not work in an intellectual vacuum. Dedekind, Riemann, Kronecker, and others were building alternative ways of thinking about the foundations of mathematics. Riemann's global geometric ideas and Weierstrass's local analytic methods could appear to pull in different directions. Later mathematics drew strength from both, and the two names now appear in many results that require their methods to cooperate.

The disagreements sometimes became personal. Weierstrass and Kronecker differed strongly about the legitimacy of various infinite and abstract mathematical constructions. Letters to Kovalevskaya show Weierstrass frustrated by attacks on his lectures and methods. He considered leaving Berlin late in his career, then remained. The episode complicates the image of the serene founder of rigor. Exact definitions do not abolish disputes about what should count as a legitimate object or a satisfying proof. Mathematicians can agree on a computation and still disagree sharply about the philosophical and pedagogical direction of the field.


In later life Weierstrass received honors and became a central figure in the Berlin mathematical establishment. For a time he served as university rector. Health problems continued, and a circle of students and colleagues supported both his teaching and the preservation of his work. He died in Berlin in February eighteen ninety-seven. The professional story covers an extraordinary arc, from a son diverted into public-finance studies, to a schoolteacher publishing outside the expected channels, to a professor whose lectures helped define an international mathematical culture.

An expression like 'the father of modern analysis' is too simple for the history. Cauchy had already worked toward greater precision. Riemann approached functions through a different, extraordinarily fertile geometry. Dedekind asked what the real numbers themselves are. Numerous teachers made the notation and ideas usable. But Weierstrass made a distinctive contribution: he showed what could be achieved by beginning with carefully defined local operations and demanding that every passage to a limit explain its conditions. His life also reveals how methods spread. A theorem can survive in a journal. A way of proving can survive because generations of students learned to ask a better question whenever a proof appeared to leap across a gap.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1815-1841:** Born in Westphalia; Prussian official's household and frequent moves; Bonn administrative studies left without examinations; Gudermann's influence and teacher preparation at Muenster.
- **1842-1856:** Teaching at Deutsch-Krone and Braunsberg, mathematical research in relative isolation; 1854 publication in *Crelle's Journal* and honorary doctorate; Berlin appointments in 1856.
- **1860s-1880s:** Systematic university courses on analysis and functions; illness interrupted lecturing from late 1861; private teaching of Kovalevskaya beginning 1870; wide student network and international reputation.
- **1897:** Death in Berlin.

The epsilon-delta method had precursors; avoid exclusive invention claims. The nowhere-differentiable function has predecessors and later variants; the lecture addresses the reception of Weierstrass's influential example, not a priority myth. Kovalevskaya's intellectual independence must remain clear. Prussian place names are given in historical form where appropriate; many places are now in Poland.

### Mathematical display companion
- Continuity at \(a\): \(\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\,(|x-a|<\delta\Rightarrow |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon)\), assuming \(x\) lies in the domain.
- Power-series viewpoint: \(f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n\) in a disk of convergence, with analytic continuation through overlapping neighborhoods when possible.
- A Weierstrass-type example: \(f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a^n\cos(b^n\pi x)\) for suitable \(0<a<1\), sufficiently large odd integer \(b\), and additional inequalities ensuring nondifferentiability. Parameter conditions are essential; not every such series has the claimed property.
