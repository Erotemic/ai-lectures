# 035000 — Isaac Newton: How can the same mathematics explain the sky and the falling apple?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** broader mathematical history. **Background:** only school algebra and geometry are assumed; calculus and mechanics are introduced locally.

Isaac Newton is often presented as a monument rather than a person. The monument is familiar: gravity, calculus, optics, the Principia, the apple. The person is harder to see. He was a solitary and intensely ambitious scholar, a careful experimentalist, a skilled geometer, a master of algebraic calculation, a public servant who later directed the Royal Mint, and a man drawn into bitter disputes over credit, religion, and scientific authority. If we flatten him into a list of discoveries, we miss the real historical question. How did one mathematician build a language powerful enough to connect falling bodies, planetary orbits, tides, light, and curves drawn by equations? The lecture's guiding question is this: how can the same mathematics explain the sky and the falling apple?

Newton was born in 1642, the year Galileo died, in Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire. He entered Trinity College, Cambridge, in the early 1660s. There he encountered the changing mathematical world of seventeenth-century Europe: Euclid and classical geometry, Descartes's analytic geometry, Wallis's algebraic methods, and the astronomical revolutions of Copernicus, Kepler, and Galileo. Then plague closed the university in 1665 and 1666, and Newton returned home. Those years later acquired almost mythical status because they contained decisive work on fluxions, generalized binomial expansions, optics, and gravitation. Myth exaggerates, but the basic truth remains: Newton used that interruption to think at unusual depth.

Let us begin with a simple problem of motion. Suppose a body moves in a straight line with constant speed. Its position after time t is the initial position plus speed times time. That is easy. Now suppose the speed itself changes. Then we need a way to describe a changing quantity and its rate of change at the same time. Newton's language for this used fluent quantities, which flow in time, and fluxions, which measure their instantaneous rates of change. If x is a changing position, then its fluxion is what we would now call dx over dt. He did not write modern derivative notation, but the underlying concept is there: motion is described by a changing magnitude and the rate at which it changes.

A familiar example is uniformly accelerated motion. If the acceleration is a constant a and the initial velocity is v naught, then the velocity after time t is v naught plus a t, and the position after time t is x naught plus v naught t plus one half a t squared. Today this is an elementary exercise in calculus. Historically it mattered because it taught mathematicians to express physical regularity using a differential relationship. The equation x double dot equals a tells us what kind of curve position must follow. This way of thinking becomes decisive when the acceleration is no longer constant but depends on where the body is.

Now move from the earth to the heavens. Kepler had already discovered three laws of planetary motion from Tycho Brahe's data. Planets move in ellipses with the sun at a focus. Equal areas are swept out in equal times. And the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbit's semimajor axis. Those laws were empirical regularities. They described the motion but did not yet explain why it had that form. Newton's step was to ask what force law would generate exactly such motions.

Here one piece of geometry does enormous work. If a force always points toward a fixed center, then the radius vector sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The reason is that a central force changes the velocity only in the radial direction. It does not create a sideways twist of angular momentum. In a small time interval, the body moves nearly along a straight segment; the triangles formed by successive radius vectors have equal area when the impulse is central. This is the geometric heart of Kepler's second law. Newton turned that observation into a general principle: central forces imply areal velocity conservation.

The next step is more difficult. Suppose the force toward the center has size proportional to one over r squared, where r is the distance from the center. What orbit follows? Newton showed that the resulting paths include conic sections: ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas. In the bounded case the orbit is an ellipse. There are several ways to derive this today. One efficient method begins with polar coordinates and angular momentum conservation. If h equals r squared theta dot, then h is constant. For a central inverse-square force the radial equation becomes a second-order equation for u equals one over r as a function of theta. The equation takes the form u double prime plus u equals a constant. Its solutions are of the form u of theta equals c times one plus e cosine of theta minus theta naught. Inverting gives r equals p over one plus e cosine of theta minus theta naught, the standard polar equation of a conic.

The derivation looks compact in modern notation, but its meaning is profound. A single differential law, the inverse-square law, generates the family of observed planetary paths. Even more striking, the same law predicts terrestrial phenomena. If the moon is continually falling toward the earth while moving sideways fast enough to miss it, then celestial motion and ordinary falling are not different kinds of events. They are one phenomenon described at different scales. This unification is one of the greatest intellectual achievements in the history of science.

It is worth pausing over what Newton did not assume. He did not begin by declaring a grand metaphysical system. He built an argument from measurable regularities, geometric lemmas, and quantitative comparison. Take the moon. If an object dropped near the earth falls a certain distance in one second, and if gravity weakens like one over r squared, then at the moon's much greater distance the fall in one second should be much smaller. Newton compared this predicted deflection with the moon's actual departure from inertial straight-line motion and found rough agreement. That is an example of mathematical explanation in a strong sense: the same formula accounts for both laboratory and astronomical behavior.

The book in which this was set out, Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica of 1687, is one of the most influential scientific works ever written. Its style is famously difficult. Newton often used geometrical arguments where we might expect calculus, partly because classical geometric form carried intellectual authority and partly because he wanted the results to stand independent of contentious infinitesimal notation. The Principia is not a textbook. It is a tightly structured demonstration that moves from definitions and axioms to consequences about motion, fluids, tides, precession, and the shape of the earth.

One should not imagine Newton working in isolation from all others. Edmond Halley was essential in drawing the Principia out of him and financing publication. Robert Hooke had independently emphasized inverse-square attraction and later fought with Newton over priority. Christiaan Huygens contributed fundamental results on circular motion and centrifugal force. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed his own differential calculus with a notation that eventually proved far more convenient than Newton's. Science moved through rivalry as well as insight, and Newton was exceptionally sensitive to questions of recognition.

The calculus dispute with Leibniz remains notorious. Newton's method of fluxions was developed earlier in private manuscripts, while Leibniz published first and created the notation d x and integral signs that became standard. British defenders of Newton later turned the matter into a national campaign, and Newton himself, while president of the Royal Society, played an improper role in inquiries that purported to be neutral. Historically, the right conclusion is not that one of them simply stole from the other. Both made foundational contributions. Newton's methods were deeply connected to motion and series expansions. Leibniz provided symbolic tools and a public analytical method that shaped later mathematics.

A short example shows why such symbolic tools mattered. Consider the curve y equals x to the third power. A small change h in x changes y by x plus h cubed minus x cubed. Expanding gives three x squared h plus three x h squared plus h cubed. Dividing by h and letting h shrink leaves three x squared. That derivative measures the instantaneous slope. Newton handled such calculations by fluxions and vanishing quantities; Leibniz by differentials. Either way, local linearization becomes the key idea. Curves can be studied through infinitely small change. In mechanics that means trajectories can be studied through instantaneous velocity and acceleration.

Newton's mathematics was not confined to mechanics. In optics he carried out a series of careful experiments with prisms. White light, he showed, is not pure and then stained by glass. It is composed of rays of different refrangibility, which a prism separates into colors. A second prism can recombine them into white light. This overturned older theories in which color arose from modifications imposed on originally homogeneous light. Here the mathematical theme is different but parallel: a complex phenomenon becomes intelligible when decomposed into simpler components with stable quantitative behavior.

Another mathematical theme central to Newton is infinite series. He generalized the binomial theorem so that expressions like one plus x to the power alpha could be expanded even when alpha is not a positive integer. For example, one plus x to the one half power equals one plus one half x minus one eighth x squared plus one sixteenth x cubed and so on, at least for suitable x. Such expansions made it possible to approximate complicated functions with polynomials, integrate term by term, and solve problems that resisted closed elementary expression. Series methods became one of the great engines of eighteenth-century analysis.

Newton also developed what is now called Newton's method for solving equations numerically. Suppose we want a square root of two, so we seek a zero of f of x equals x squared minus two. Start with a guess, say one point five. The tangent line at that point meets the x-axis at a better guess, given by x new equals x old minus f of x old divided by f prime of x old. For one point five this yields one point four one six six and then one point four one four two, rapidly converging to the true value. The method is not merely practical. It exemplifies a larger Newtonian pattern: use local linear information to tame a nonlinear problem.

Because Newton became a universal emblem of genius, later generations often forgot the texture of his life. He spent years on alchemy and theology. He served in Parliament briefly but silently. As Warden and then Master of the Mint, he pursued counterfeiters with surprising administrative energy. He navigated London scientific politics skillfully and sometimes ruthlessly. The image of the absent-minded dreamer is incomplete. Newton could be secretive, combative, and institutionally effective.

There were also limitations to the Newtonian framework. His law of gravitation gives extraordinarily accurate predictions, yet it does not explain gravity's mechanism in a modern field-theoretic sense. Newton himself famously refused to feign hypotheses about causes he could not derive from phenomena. The laws of motion and universal gravitation formed an enormously successful predictive system, but later centuries would revise parts of the picture through relativity and quantum theory. Newton's achievement was not to finish physics forever. It was to show what exact mathematical science could be.

Let us return to the core mathematical idea. Why do the sky and the falling apple submit to one theory? Because the theory does not describe objects by their material names. It describes them by quantities: position, velocity, mass, force, rate of change, and geometric relation. Once a phenomenon can be encoded in those terms, the same formal structure can apply to many domains. This is a profound shift in the philosophy of science. Mathematics is not just a bookkeeping device for data already understood. It is the form in which physical understanding itself can be expressed.

The legacy of this shift reaches well beyond astronomy. Differential equations became the standard language for heat flow, fluid motion, electricity, elasticity, population growth, and later quantum mechanics. The notion that a law of change can be written locally and then unfolded into a global trajectory is one of Newton's deepest gifts to science. Even when later mathematics abandons his notation or corrects his physics, it continues to inhabit the world he helped define.

It is also important to remember the human cost of genius myths. Newton's story has sometimes been told as if science advances through isolated extraordinary men appearing above history. That distorts the record. Newton learned from predecessors, relied on correspondents, argued with rivals, and wrote within institutions. At the same time, the record should not be flattened in the other direction. There are moments when an individual really does reorganize a field. Newton's synthesis of geometry, analysis, and physical law is one of those moments.

A final worked image may help. Picture a planet at one point on its orbit. During a tiny interval it wants to continue straight ahead by inertia. At the same time, gravity pulls it a little toward the sun. The actual motion is the combination of those two tendencies. Repeat that over and over and the orbit emerges as a continuous curve. In modern numerical language, one might approximate the path by tiny time steps. In conceptual language, one sees Newton's whole method at work: analyze change locally, preserve geometry globally, and search for the law that makes the observed pattern inevitable.

That is why Newton remains central to the history of mathematics. He was not only a great calculator or a brilliant geometer. He helped establish a template for exact science. State the quantities. Find the rate laws. Use geometry or analysis to deduce motion. Test the deductions against the world. When those steps succeed, earthly events and celestial events become chapters of one mathematical story. The falling apple and the moving moon do not merely resemble one another. They are governed by the same structure of law.

Newton's laws of motion deserve their own attention because they changed the grammar of explanation. The first law says that a body persists in rest or uniform straight motion unless compelled to change by impressed force. This gives inertia a precise conceptual role. The second law relates change of motion to applied force. In modern form we write force equals mass times acceleration, though Newton's own wording in terms of change of momentum is slightly broader and handles variable mass more cleanly. The third law says that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Together these laws make interaction mathematically tractable. One no longer explains motion by assigning each kind of object a natural place or tendency. One studies quantitative interactions.

Consider circular motion as a warm-up. If a body moves with speed v around a circle of radius r, the acceleration points inward with magnitude v squared over r. Huygens derived this before Newton, but in the Newtonian framework it becomes a bridge between kinematics and force. If the moon circles the earth, then the inward acceleration required by its path must be supplied by gravity. This provides a concrete comparison between orbital motion and falling bodies. The success of that comparison was one of the strongest empirical arguments for universal gravitation.

The Principia also introduced rules of reasoning in natural philosophy. These were not formal axioms of logic but methodological commitments. Similar effects should, as far as possible, be assigned the same causes. Qualities found in all tested bodies should be treated as universal. Propositions inferred from phenomena should be accepted until other phenomena require correction. Such rules helped justify the leap from terrestrial experiments to celestial mechanics. They are part of why Newton's work became more than a technical tour de force. It offered a model of disciplined inference.

Another striking theme in the Principia is perturbation. Real planets do not move as isolated two-body systems. They tug on one another. The moon is affected by both earth and sun. Jupiter slightly disturbs Saturn. Comets follow elongated paths that challenge simple circular schemes. Newton did not solve every such many-body problem in a modern sense, but he showed how approximate reasoning could be systematic. Start from an idealized principal motion, then estimate the effect of additional small forces. This way of thinking later became essential across applied mathematics.

His work on the earth's shape is a good example. If the earth rotates, then centrifugal tendency should make it bulge at the equator and flatten at the poles. Newton used a balance between gravitational attraction and rotational effects to argue for an oblate spheroid rather than a perfect sphere. This was not merely a curiosity. It linked terrestrial geometry, dynamics, and astronomy. Later expeditions and measurements turned such predictions into tests of competing physical theories.

Tides provided another arena in which Newtonian gravitation displayed explanatory reach. Tides are not caused simply by the moon pulling water upward at one place. They arise because the moon's pull varies across the earth, producing differential forces. The water on the side nearest the moon and the side farthest from it is displaced relative to the solid body of the earth, giving two tidal bulges. Solar attraction modifies the pattern, yielding spring and neap tides depending on geometric alignment. Again the point is not merely that Newton had an answer. The point is that a subtle and irregular earthly phenomenon could be brought within the same mathematical framework as the motions of planets.

Newton's optical work likewise extended beyond the famous prism experiment. In Opticks he formulated queries rather than theorems, encouraging future investigation into the nature of light, color, and matter. He also designed the reflecting telescope now called the Newtonian telescope. By replacing a large refracting lens with a mirror, he avoided chromatic aberration produced by ordinary lenses. Here practical instrument design and theoretical reasoning supported each other. Better theory suggested a better device, and a better device in turn supported better observation.

There is a revealing contrast between Newton's mechanics and his optics. In mechanics the dominant mathematical image is differential law, local change, and conserved quantity. In optics the image is decomposition, analysis of components, and experimental control. Yet both domains share a common intellectual virtue: the refusal to be satisfied with rough qualitative talk where exact quantitative structure can be found. That shared virtue may be a deeper unity in Newton's work than any single doctrine.

Newton's later life at the Mint can seem remote from mathematics, but it illustrates another aspect of exactness. English coinage at the time suffered from clipping, counterfeiting, and inconsistent silver content. The Great Recoinage required both logistical organization and numerical control. Newton supervised assays, standards, and enforcement with extraordinary seriousness. His mathematical habits were not confined to ivory-tower theory; they appeared in administration where precise quantities had public consequence.

The disputes of his life also matter historically because they shaped transmission of mathematics. British loyalty to Newton's notation and prestige slowed the adoption of the Leibnizian calculus on the Continent's analytic terms. Meanwhile, the Bernoullis, Euler, and others pushed differential methods forward in ways that outpaced the British school. Newton's authority was immense, but authority can both guide and freeze a tradition. The history of mathematics is not only about who discovers ideas first; it is also about which forms of expression make further work easier.

One can see this in the contrast between geometric proof and symbolic calculation. Newton often returned analytic results to a Euclidean style of presentation. That gave the Principia enduring rigor and grandeur, but it also made parts of the work difficult to extend. Later mathematicians translated many Newtonian results into the notation of differential equations and power series, after which whole new families of problems became approachable. Translation across styles is itself a historical act of mathematical creation.

There is also a moral in Newton's treatment of universal gravitation. Action at a distance troubled many contemporaries. How could one body influence another across empty space? Newton answered that he was establishing the law, not pretending to specify an underlying mechanical cause. This was a disciplined limitation. It demonstrated that science can progress substantially even when mechanism remains uncertain. One need not solve every philosophical problem before building a quantitatively reliable theory.

For students today, Newton's work is sometimes too familiar to feel revolutionary. We meet derivatives in school, solve second-order differential equations, and treat gravity as a standard example. Historical distance helps restore the surprise. Before Newton, it was not obvious that geometry, algebra, and physical experiment could be fused into a single explanatory system of this scope. After Newton, the dream of exact mathematical physics became one of the central ambitions of science.

And yet his legacy is not merely one of expansion. It also taught later mathematicians where care is needed. Infinite series can diverge. Infinitesimal reasoning can become ambiguous without rigorous foundations. Gravitational n-body systems can behave with complexity that resists exact solution. Newton opened many doors that later analysts had to secure more firmly. That is the normal fate of foundational work. Its success reveals the next layer of difficulty.

There is a final pedagogical reason Newton matters so much. His work teaches the power of idealization. No actual planet moves in a perfect isolated ellipse, and no laboratory prism is conceptually innocent. Yet by simplifying intelligently, Newton found structures sturdy enough to survive later correction. Idealization is not falsification when it is used knowingly to reveal the dominant terms in a phenomenon. This lesson remains central in every mathematically serious science.

Newton also changed the status of prediction. Medieval astronomy could often predict positions numerically, and early modern natural philosophy could offer qualitative causes. Newtonian science joined the two more tightly. From laws and initial conditions one could, at least in principle, derive future behavior. That promise generated both confidence and hubris in later centuries. Even when later physics discovered limits, instability, and probabilistic structure, the Newtonian image of lawful prediction remained a standard against which new theories defined themselves.

To study Newton historically, then, is not simply to honor a genius. It is to watch a new conception of science take shape: mathematically exact, experimentally responsive, and ambitious enough to treat the cosmos as one connected system. That conception is now so familiar that it can become invisible. Newton helps make it visible again.
