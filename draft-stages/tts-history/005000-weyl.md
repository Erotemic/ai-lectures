# 005000 — Hermann Weyl: When do spectra survive a changing world?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** Spectral analysis, geometry, and symmetry. **Background:** elementary eigenvalues and inner products; the lecture introduces its more advanced objects as needed.

Consider a very simple experiment. Take a symmetric two-by-two matrix. Its first coordinate direction has eigenvalue one. Its second direction has eigenvalue one plus a tiny positive number. Now exchange the two diagonal entries. The two matrices differ by only that tiny number in operator norm. Their ordered eigenvalue lists are exactly the same. Yet the eigenvector carrying the smaller eigenvalue has moved from one coordinate axis to the other, through a right angle. How can the spectral numbers be perfectly stable while the corresponding directions are completely different? What should we measure if we want to know whether a physical mode, or a computed eigenspace, has remained trustworthy?

This question gives us an entrance into the work of Hermann Weyl. He lived from 1885 to 1955 and worked in an era when mathematics was changing its picture of what an operator, a spectrum, and even a geometric space could be. His name is attached to eigenvalue inequalities, to the large-scale distribution of frequencies, to essential spectrum, and to the mathematics of symmetry. Those are distinct theorems, arising from distinct problems. But they share a persistent question: which features of a mathematical object are intrinsic enough to survive a change in coordinates, an approximation, or a physical transformation?

To follow this story, it helps to keep three pictures in mind. The first is a finite matrix, where a spectrum is a list of eigenvalues. The second is an infinite system, where there may be infinitely many frequencies or even spectral values without ordinary eigenvectors. The third is a collection of symmetries, where changing the description of a system need not change what can be observed. Weyl worked in all three settings. Moving among them lets us understand his breadth without treating his career as a catalogue of unrelated named results.

Weyl was born in Elmshorn, in northern Germany, in 1885. He studied mathematics and physics at Munich and Göttingen and completed his doctorate in Göttingen under David Hilbert in 1908. Göttingen was then a center of mathematical analysis and physics, with Hilbert's work on integral equations and spectral problems particularly influential. Instead of asking only how to solve one differential equation, mathematicians were asking how whole families of such equations fit into a common theory. A vibrating string, a heat equation, and an integral equation might all be studied through linear operators, the functions on which those operators act, and their spectral data.

Hilbert was more than a signature on a doctoral certificate. In an autobiographical recollection, Weyl described taking Hilbert's great report on algebraic number theory home for a summer and working through it before he had learned many of the standard prerequisites. He remembered that study as one of the happiest stretches of his life. The anecdote reveals intellectual appetite without requiring us to guess at his inner life. His memories make clear how completely that difficult book occupied his summer. From that education came a taste for moving across mathematical boundaries rather than remaining within one narrowly defined problem.

Weyl's early research belonged to that environment. His doctoral work investigated singular integral equations, and his subsequent work engaged differential equations with singularities. These were demanding problems because the comfortable rules of finite matrices do not transfer automatically to infinite-dimensional operators. One may have a densely defined operator rather than a matrix acting on every vector. An inverse may fail to exist even where there is no ordinary eigenvector. A small change in a boundary condition can change the spectral data. The insistence on specifying spaces, domains, and limiting processes was part of the intellectual atmosphere in which Weyl developed.

In 1913, he moved to the Federal Institute of Technology in Zürich. He had also written a book, The Idea of the Riemann Surface, published that year, bringing together complex analysis, topology, and geometric ways of thinking about functions. The point of a Riemann surface is that a function appearing multiple-valued in the ordinary complex plane may become single-valued when we understand the right underlying space. A square root, for example, seems to have two possible values at a nonzero complex number. On a suitable two-sheeted surface, the apparent ambiguity is represented by different points of the space. This is a useful preview of Weyl's style: a problem that looks like a failure of formulas may be a failure to choose the right geometric object.

His move to Zürich also changed his household. In 1913 he married Helene Joseph, known as Hella, a philosopher and translator who had studied with Edmund Husserl. Their two sons were Joachim and Michael. Hella was an active intellectual presence rather than a decorative figure in a famous mathematician's biography. Weyl's attraction to philosophical problems about space, continuity, and the meaning of mathematical existence belongs in this setting. He was forming a family and an intellectual home while attempting to connect geometric analysis with the rapidly changing foundations of physics.

But return to our two-by-two matrix. Let the first matrix have diagonal entries one and one plus epsilon. Let the second have those entries exchanged. Epsilon is positive and may be as small as we like. The difference between the matrices is diagonal, with entries epsilon and minus epsilon, so its operator norm is epsilon. That norm measures the largest possible stretching of a unit vector by the difference. For this pair the norm really is epsilon, because the coordinate axes attain it. The matrices are close in a precise and strong sense.

Their spectra, meaning their sets of eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, agree. In increasing order both lists are one, then one plus epsilon. The smallest-eigenvalue direction of the first matrix is the first coordinate axis. For the second matrix it is the second axis. Thus the direction has changed by ninety degrees. This example does not violate an eigenvalue perturbation theorem. It shows why an eigenvalue theorem and an eigenvector theorem answer different questions. To control an individual direction, the distance separating its eigenvalue from other eigenvalues must enter somewhere. Here that separation is epsilon, exactly the scale of the perturbation.

Weyl's name is attached to a family of inequalities about the eigenvalues of sums of Hermitian matrices, meaning complex matrices equal to their conjugate transpose. For real matrices, symmetric is the corresponding condition. A particularly useful consequence says that adding a Hermitian perturbation of operator norm at most epsilon moves each ordered eigenvalue by at most epsilon. The bound sounds nearly obvious once stated. Understanding its proof, though, reveals why it needs no information about eigenvectors and no assumption that the two matrices commute.

Start with the Rayleigh quotient. Give a symmetric matrix a unit vector, multiply the vector by the matrix, and take its inner product with the original vector. For a diagonal matrix this is a weighted average of the diagonal eigenvalues, with weights given by the squares of the vector's coordinates. If the matrix is changed by a perturbation E, the same Rayleigh quotient changes by the inner product of the vector with E applied to it. The absolute value of that extra quantity is at most the operator norm of E. This is a statement about every unit vector, not just eigenvectors of one matrix.

Now use the min–max characterization of an ordered eigenvalue. In one version, we choose a subspace of a prescribed dimension, look at the largest Rayleigh quotient inside it, and then seek the subspace that makes that largest value as small as possible. It is a game of selecting subspaces and measuring the most expensive direction within each selection. Because every Rayleigh quotient changes by at most epsilon, the largest quotient in any fixed subspace changes by at most epsilon. Taking the best choice among subspaces cannot amplify that uniform error. The resulting ordered eigenvalue therefore changes by at most epsilon as well.

Notice what has not appeared in this reasoning. We did not choose an eigenbasis that varies continuously. We did not match individual eigenvectors. We did not assume that A and E could be diagonalized in the same basis. The min–max construction compares numbers assigned to all subspaces, so it is independent of a preferred basis. The essential mathematical operation is the passage from a pointwise, uniform bound on Rayleigh quotients to a bound on their variational extrema. The subspace dimension controls which eigenvalue is selected.

There is a stronger form of the story, historically associated with Weyl's 1912 work on eigenvalues of sums. Order the eigenvalues from largest to smallest. If the first matrix has its i-th largest eigenvalue at one height and the second matrix has its j-th largest at another height, a suitable eigenvalue of their sum is bounded above by the sum of those two heights. The precise index is i plus j minus one, provided it does not exceed the dimension. Why such an index? Because two large subspaces must intersect in a space whose dimension can be calculated. On that intersection, both Rayleigh quotients satisfy the relevant upper bounds, so their sum does too.

Let's hear that dimension argument without carrying a string of subscripts. In a three-dimensional room, two distinct planes through the origin must meet in at least a line. A vector on that common line belongs to both planes, so any estimate valid on the whole first plane and any estimate valid on the whole second plane are simultaneously valid on the line. Higher-dimensional spaces obey the same counting rule. The codimensions of two subspaces add, which puts a lower bound on the dimension of their intersection. This simple geometry is the engine behind many spectral inequalities: we choose spaces that control two quadratic forms and find a direction where both controls hold.

For a numerical check, let one two-by-two matrix have diagonal entries three and zero, and another have entries zero and two. Their sum has eigenvalues three and two. In descending order, the second eigenvalue of the sum is two. One Weyl inequality bounds it by the top eigenvalue of the first matrix, three, plus the bottom eigenvalue of the second, zero. Another bounds it by zero plus two. The second estimate is exact. The point is not the arithmetic difficulty. The point is that different choices of subspace intersection lead to different valid bounds; we keep the best information without assuming shared eigenvectors in general.

The inequality is now familiar enough to use carefully. It tells us that a perturbation small in operator norm cannot move any ordered eigenvalue very far. It does not tell us that a particular eigenvector has moved only a small angle. Our original example had identical ordered eigenvalues and perpendicular eigendirections. If a cluster contains several nearly equal eigenvalues, asking each eigenvector to preserve its identity may be the wrong question. The whole invariant subspace associated with the cluster may be the stable object, especially when the cluster is separated from the rest of the spectrum by a gap.

To make that distinction visible, consider a three-dimensional matrix with eigenvalues zero, a small epsilon, and five. The first two coordinate axes span a low-energy plane. We may exchange the first two diagonal entries, just as before. Individual eigenvectors for zero and epsilon exchange, but the plane spanned by them stays exactly the same. Its orthogonal projection is unchanged: keep the first two coordinates and erase the third. A statement about this two-dimensional spectral subspace can therefore remain perfectly stable even though a statement about each internal basis direction fails. Spectral clusters can have a geometric identity that their individual eigenvectors do not.

Next, perturb across the gap instead of within the cluster. Take a two-by-two matrix with eigenvalues zero and five. Add a symmetric off-diagonal coupling of size one half. Picture the matrix as two previously independent directions that now communicate weakly. The new eigenvalues are one half of the quantity five plus or minus the square root of twenty-six. The lower one is about negative five hundredths, and the upper one about five and five hundredths. The eigenvalues have barely shifted. The associated eigenvectors have also tilted, this time by a small, controlled angle rather than a right angle. The angle is half the arctangent of one divided by five, approximately five point seven degrees.

Why is the tilt small? The gap of five competes against a coupling of one half. To mix the two modes strongly, the off-diagonal perturbation would need to overcome their spectral separation. The exact two-by-two formula says that the tangent of twice the rotation angle equals twice the coupling divided by the original gap. With a small coupling, the angle is roughly the coupling divided by the gap. That ratio is the mental model behind a great deal of subspace perturbation theory. It belongs to the later development of estimates for angles and projections, including the work of Davis and Kahan. Weyl's eigenvalue inequalities provide essential spectral control, but an angle theorem needs an additional geometric argument.

This is an important division of responsibility. Weyl's ordered-eigenvalue bound says where spectral values can move. To follow an eigenspace, we also identify which values belong to a cluster and measure the gap isolating it from the rest. A projection onto the cluster then gives a coordinate-free way to compare the old and new spaces. Principal angles describe how one subspace sits relative to another. A residual may tell us how much an approximate vector fails to satisfy an eigenvalue equation. The gap determines how that failure converts into angular error. None of these distinctions is optional when eigenvalues nearly coincide.

Weyl's spectral interests went beyond finite matrices. Suppose a musical instrument or a bounded vibrating region has many natural modes. As we examine higher and higher frequencies, the number of modes below a frequency threshold increases. Rather than asking for every eigenvalue separately, we can ask for the overall growth of the counting function. Weyl proved a foundational asymptotic result of this kind in 1911, now called Weyl's law. It concerns the high-energy spectrum of the Laplace operator under suitable boundary conditions. In the leading term, the size of the region determines the abundance of high-frequency modes.

Begin with the simplest example, a string of length L fixed at both ends. Its allowable standing waves have one, two, three, and so on half-wavelengths fitted into the string. The corresponding eigenvalues of the positive operator obtained by negating the second derivative are the squares of the integer multiples of pi divided by L. If we choose a large eigenvalue threshold, how many modes lie below it? Solve the eigenvalue inequality for the integer mode number. The answer is the greatest integer not exceeding L times the square root of the threshold divided by pi. The counting function therefore grows like that expression, with an error of less than one.

That calculation is exact in one dimension, and it teaches the idea behind the general law. For a sufficiently regular bounded region in higher-dimensional Euclidean space, count the eigenvalues of the positive Dirichlet Laplacian below a large threshold. To leading order, the count is proportional to the volume of the region times the threshold raised to half the spatial dimension. The coefficient includes the volume of the unit ball and a power of two pi. The shape of the boundary affects more refined terms and corrections, but the leading abundance of high-frequency modes is governed by volume. This does not mean that two regions of equal volume have identical spectra.

One way to hear Weyl's law is as a kind of geometric bookkeeping. A sufficiently high-frequency wave can oscillate rapidly in many directions. The allowed modes fill an expanding region of frequency space. Counting them resembles counting lattice points inside a growing ball. The original partial differential equation is still present, and boundary conditions matter. But the leading term is controlled by how much physical space the modes occupy and how large the corresponding region of frequency space has become. This bridges analysis, geometry, and what is now called spectral asymptotics.

A two-dimensional drum makes the story more vivid. Its vibration modes have nodal curves, regions of positive and negative displacement, and increasingly intricate oscillation patterns. No finite list of modes captures the sound completely. Yet at high frequency the number of modes below a threshold has a leading term proportional to the drumhead's area. Two drumheads can share that area and still have different patterns and different low-frequency notes. The counting law does not reconstruct the drum's shape. It predicts how densely its spectrum accumulates at high energy. Once again, a coarse spectral invariant may be stable or predictable even when detailed eigenfunctions are not.

Now change the infinite-dimensional example. Instead of counting high frequencies, consider an operator on square-summable sequences. Let it multiply the first coordinate by one, the second by one half, the third by one third, and so on. Every standard coordinate unit vector is an eigenvector, with its own positive eigenvalue. These eigenvalues accumulate at zero. Zero belongs to the spectrum because the operator cannot have a bounded inverse: applying a hypothetical inverse to the n-th coordinate vector would require multiplying its size by n. Yet zero is not an eigenvalue, because multiplying a sequence coordinate by coordinate with nonzero factors cannot produce the zero sequence unless the original sequence was zero.

This is where infinite-dimensional spectral theory breaks our finite-matrix habits. In finite dimensions, every spectral value of a matrix is an eigenvalue. In infinite dimensions, a spectral value can arise because an inverse fails to be bounded or everywhere defined even when no nonzero eigenvector is killed by the operator minus that value. To test zero in our diagonal example, use the n-th coordinate vector as an approximate eigenvector. Its norm is one. Its image under the operator has norm one over n, which tends to zero. We can make the equation for eigenvalue zero almost true with normalized vectors, though never exactly true for a nonzero vector.

Those unit vectors also move farther and farther out along the coordinate axes. Against any fixed square-summable test vector, their inner products tend to zero, because the test vector's individual coordinates tend to zero. This is called weak convergence to zero. The unit vectors do not converge to zero in norm; their norms remain one. That contrast is important. Approximate eigenvectors with norm one demonstrate spectral proximity. If they also converge weakly to zero, they can characterize the essential spectrum of a self-adjoint operator. They describe spectral behavior that cannot be captured by a finite collection of isolated eigenvectors.

In our diagonal example, every positive eigenvalue one over n is isolated and has multiplicity one. The accumulation point zero is different. It belongs to the essential spectrum. We can change the first coordinate multiplier from one to seven, or alter a finite number of coordinates, without removing the accumulation of eigenvalues at zero. Such a change is a finite-rank perturbation and therefore compact. Weyl's theorem about the essential spectrum says, in the self-adjoint setting, that compact self-adjoint perturbations preserve the essential spectrum. This result has a different purpose from the finite-matrix eigenvalue inequality. It tells us which parts of an infinite spectrum survive an entire class of perturbations.

The word essential is doing real work here. If an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity moves under a compact perturbation, the essential spectrum may remain unchanged. It is the accumulated or non-discrete spectral structure that persists. A compact perturbation can be large in operator norm, so the theorem is not simply another version of the small-epsilon eigenvalue bound. The relevant hypothesis is compactness, an approximation property that allows the perturbation to be uniformly approximated by finite-rank maps. In infinite dimensions, the kind of perturbation matters as much as its numerical size.

We should distinguish two criteria that are easy to confuse in speech. For a self-adjoint operator, a real number lies in its spectrum if there are unit vectors in the operator domain on which the operator minus that number becomes arbitrarily small. For the essential spectrum, one adds the requirement that such unit vectors can escape weakly toward zero. In the diagonal example, the standard unit vectors serve both purposes at zero. At an isolated eigenvalue one over three, an exact eigenvector exists, but a weakly vanishing sequence with arbitrarily small residual at that same value does not. The gap around that isolated value prevents it.

The distinction is useful in differential operators. A Schrödinger operator on an unbounded region may have a continuous range of scattering energies and some isolated bound-state energies. A localized change in a potential may move or create discrete eigenvalues without altering the broad background of essential spectral behavior under appropriate compactness conditions. The exact hypotheses depend on the operator and its domain; one cannot replace them with a statement about arbitrary changes of a potential. Weyl's principle provides a way to separate persistent spectral structure from finite or localized corrections.

Another branch of Weyl's work seems far from eigenspaces but reflects a similar interest in limiting distribution. Imagine putting a point on a circle and repeatedly rotating it through a fixed angle. If that angle is a rational fraction of a full turn, the orbit repeats after finitely many steps. If the angle is irrational as a fraction of a turn, the points never form a finite periodic orbit. But absence of periodicity alone is weaker than saying that the orbit spends the right proportion of time in every arc. Weyl proved a fundamental equidistribution theorem: the fractional parts of successive integer multiples of an irrational number become uniformly distributed between zero and one.

A simple example makes the difference visible. Add a rotation of roughly one third of a full turn over and over. If it were exactly one third, we would visit only three points. Shift the angle to an irrational value near one third, and the orbit no longer closes. Over many steps it fills the circle statistically. Uniform distribution means that an arc covering one quarter of the circumference eventually receives about one quarter of the points, regardless of where the arc starts. It is a frequency statement about visits, not a claim that the points become evenly spaced at every finite stage.

Weyl's proof method is connected to complex exponentials. Represent a point on the unit circle by a complex number of length one. For each nonzero integer frequency, average the corresponding rotating complex phases. When the basic rotation angle is irrational, the terms form a geometric sum with a denominator that does not vanish. The sum stays bounded as the number of terms grows, so dividing by the number of terms drives its average to zero. A criterion using these exponential averages then implies uniform distribution. The mathematics transforms a question about intervals on a circle into a question about cancellation of rotating complex vectors.

There is an appealing contrast here. Weyl's law for Laplace eigenvalues counts frequencies in a large spectral region. Weyl's equidistribution theorem describes how points generated by an irrational rotation spread around a circle. They are separate results with separate proofs, despite sharing their author's name. Both convert a potentially intricate collection into a limit law. One counts high-energy modes; the other counts visits to arcs. Each is stronger than an informal observation that something seems dense, and each requires its own limiting argument.

Weyl did not confine his thinking to analysis. In his work on Riemann surfaces he treated the underlying geometry as part of what makes complex functions intelligible. In his writing on the continuum and foundations of mathematics, including a 1918 book, he examined what can be built from mathematical definitions and what assumptions are being used when one speaks of infinite totalities. He engaged with philosophy, including the influence of phenomenology and intuitionism. These interests are part of the historical record, but they should not be mistaken for an extra hypothesis in an eigenvalue theorem. The min–max inequality works whether or not its reader accepts Weyl's philosophical views.

The philosophical dimension was therefore personal as well as academic. Weyl's interest in phenomenology arose through a living philosophical community, not through a footnote added to his mathematical papers. He could be attracted to a rigorous formal construction and at the same time wonder whether the construction captured how space and time are experienced. That does not make his technical theorems depend on a private worldview. It helps explain why the same writer could publish results about eigenvalues and long reflections on the continuum without treating the subjects as alien to one another.

His career then crossed deeply into mathematical physics. In Zürich, Weyl became interested in the geometry of general relativity. His 1918 book Space, Time, Matter developed relativity in a sophisticated geometric language. He also proposed an extension of the geometry of spacetime in which local standards of length could vary from point to point. This was an early gauge idea, but its physical interpretation was different from the phase symmetry now central to quantum field theory. Einstein objected that the proposal would conflict with the stable characteristic frequencies of atoms. Weyl's original physical proposal was not accepted in that form.

Einstein's criticism of his proposed theory of gravitation was a serious test of this ambition. The two scientists shared a conviction that geometry could reveal structure in physics, but they did not agree about what counts as an acceptable physical consequence. A local change of scale might be mathematically elegant; if it makes the measured properties of atoms depend on their past, the physical model faces a problem. Weyl's capacity to enter such a dispute and continue developing the mathematical ideas is part of his working character. An unsuccessful physical hypothesis can still leave concepts that other theories use differently.

The difference between those two versions of gauge thinking matters. A change of the local unit of length is one kind of freedom in description. A change of the local phase of a complex quantum wavefunction is another. They are mathematically related through the idea of a local transformation, but they are not interchangeable physical hypotheses. Weyl revisited gauge invariance in the quantum setting around 1929, when the changing quantity was complex phase. A useful historical narrative includes both the initial failure and the later transformation of the idea. Calling the 1918 theory simply modern electromagnetism would erase the conceptual change.

We can explain the quantum phase idea with one calculation. A complex wavefunction has a magnitude and a phase. If we multiply the wavefunction by a complex number of magnitude one, its absolute square does not change. That is a global phase change. Now allow the phase multiplier to vary with position. Ordinary differentiation notices the changing phase: when we differentiate the product, an extra term appears involving the derivative of the local phase. A derivative-based physical equation will generally change unless something else transforms as well. To maintain the same form of the equation, introduce a connection, or gauge potential, and use a derivative corrected by that potential.

Picture the corrected derivative as a rule for comparing phases at neighboring locations. If every local phase convention changes, the potential changes too, by an appropriate gradient term, so the corrected derivative transforms in the same way as the wavefunction. Observable predictions can then be independent of the chosen local phase convention. The companion gives the signs and formulas. In narration, the lesson is that comparing something at different positions requires a rule for how the local frames are related. This is another case in which an apparently troublesome extra term tells us that a geometric structure has been omitted.

Symmetry also gives a new reason to care about invariant subspaces. Consider a simple operation that exchanges two coordinates. Applying it twice returns the original vector. Its two special directions are easy to find: the diagonal direction, where the coordinates are equal, and the anti-diagonal direction, where they are opposites. The exchange operation leaves the first direction unchanged and reverses the second. It is therefore natural to split the space into these two one-dimensional invariant subspaces. If another operator commutes with the exchange symmetry, it preserves the same splitting. A matrix calculation that initially mixes two coordinates becomes two simpler calculations.

For continuous rotations, the principle is similar but the representation theory is richer. A representation assigns linear operators to symmetry transformations in a way that preserves composition. Rotate first by one angle, then another, and the associated operators multiply to represent the combined rotation. In quantum mechanics, states live in a complex Hilbert space and many physical symmetries act by unitary operators. Operators commuting with those symmetries acquire constrained block structure; degeneracies in their spectra can reflect symmetry. Representation theory tells us which invariant pieces are possible and how they combine.

During the 1920s Weyl developed major work on representations and invariants of classical groups, drawing together earlier work by mathematicians including Issai Schur and Élie Cartan. His 1928 book Group Theory and Quantum Mechanics put symmetry at the center of the emerging quantum theory. This did not mean that Weyl alone invented group representations or the mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics. It meant that he helped connect those tools to the structure of physical states and observables. The connection is still fundamental: a selection rule in atomic physics can arise because a proposed transition is incompatible with the relevant symmetry.

The language of invariant subspaces now connects the physics back to our first matrix. When two eigenvalues are equal, their common eigenspace has many possible orthonormal bases. A rotation inside it need not change the operator at all. A symmetry may explain why the degeneracy occurs. Under perturbation, an arbitrarily chosen basis for the degenerate space may move dramatically, but the whole space can remain the right object to follow. The geometry of projections and principal angles is built for that task. Weyl's spectral inequalities do not by themselves prove a modern eigenspace-angle theorem, yet his work on spectra and symmetry supplies important pieces of the language those later theorems use.

In 1930 Weyl accepted the position once held by Hilbert at Göttingen. Professionally, it was a return to the institution where his mathematical life had begun; personally, it was entangled with a growing political danger. His wife Hella was Jewish, and the Nazi regime's policies also threatened their sons. The possibility of an American appointment offered safety and a loss of the intellectual home that had formed him. The series of decisions that followed was full of hesitation, regret, and renewed attempts to protect his family.

His negotiations with the Institute for Advanced Study involved repeated acceptances and withdrawals. At one point Weyl sent a message saying his doubts had been overcome, then changed his mind again. He worried about leaving the German language in which so much of his intellectual work took place, and about whether he would belong at the new institution. By the time the danger was undeniable, an earlier opportunity had been lost. Abraham Flexner and Oswald Veblen eventually helped arrange another opening, and Weyl, Hella, and their children reached the United States in 1933. To call this simply a change of job would erase both the reluctance and the risk.

Princeton provided an extraordinary mathematical environment, but exile did not cancel the losses of Göttingen. The rise of National Socialism scattered colleagues and broke the university community that had fostered modern analysis and algebra. Weyl's relationship with Emmy Noether makes that rupture concrete. They had worked within the same Göttingen world; after she was driven out, she found refuge at Bryn Mawr. When she died unexpectedly in 1935, Weyl spoke at her funeral. His remembrance honored both her mathematical creativity and the openness with which she had continued to work amid hostility. This was a mathematician mourning a friend and an intellectual world, not merely recording a professional accomplishment.

At the Institute for Advanced Study Weyl continued teaching, writing, and advising younger scholars. His books on groups, invariant theory, and symmetry gave his work a second life among readers who had never attended a seminar in Göttingen or Zürich. Letters in the Institute's archive show that family contact continued alongside mathematical collaboration. Hella died in 1948, and in 1950 Weyl married the sculptor Ellen Lohnstein Bär. He retired from the Institute in 1951 and divided his later years between Princeton and Switzerland. He died in Zürich in 1955. His life had crossed the collapse of the academic world in which he was trained, and his mathematics had helped build another one.

By then, the label Weyl had become attached to so many concepts that the names themselves could be confusing. Weyl's eigenvalue inequalities concern sums and perturbations of Hermitian matrices. Weyl's law concerns the asymptotic count of high-energy eigenvalues of differential operators. Weyl's theorem on essential spectrum concerns what persists under compact perturbations in a self-adjoint setting. Weyl's equidistribution theorem concerns the statistical distribution of number sequences modulo one. Weyl groups, Weyl tensors, and Weyl operators belong to still other parts of mathematics and physics. One name does not identify one universal spectral theorem.

The lasting connection among these achievements is methodological. In a matrix problem, we can vary the operator and still compare ordered spectral values because a variational characterization survives the change. In a vibrating region, we can replace exact high-frequency formulas by an asymptotic count that captures a geometric leading term. In an infinite-dimensional operator, we can separate discrete eigenvalues from essential spectral behavior that resists compact changes. In a symmetric physical theory, we can distinguish an observable structure from a freedom to change its description. These are different ways of asking what a mathematical statement is really about.

For one final calculation, return to the two matrices with nearly equal eigenvalues and swapped coordinate axes. There is no paradox: the ordered eigenvalues are stable because they are the same numbers. The selected eigenvector is unstable because the gap isolating its eigenvalue has shrunk to the scale of the change. If instead we follow the whole two-dimensional cluster, its projection has not moved. If we separate a mode from the rest by a much larger gap, a small coupling produces a small tilt. The right stability claim depends on the right object and the right hypotheses. Hermann Weyl's career teaches that lesson on a much larger scale. Across analysis, geometry, number theory, and physics, he repeatedly sought mathematical descriptions that reveal which features persist and which depend on our choice of coordinates or representation.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### The two-by-two stability example

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). With eigenvalues ordered increasingly,

\[
A_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1+\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}1+\varepsilon&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\|A_\varepsilon-B_\varepsilon\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\varepsilon.
\]

The ordered spectra coincide, but the one-dimensional eigenspaces for
\(\lambda_{\min}\) are orthogonal. The complete two-dimensional cluster is the
same subspace for both operators.

### Weyl's inequalities and the min–max mechanism

For finite Hermitian \(A\) and \(E\), with eigenvalues ordered increasingly,

\[
|\lambda_j(A+E)-\lambda_j(A)|\leq\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}.
\]

Indeed \(|\langle Ex,x\rangle|\leq\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\) for unit \(x\), so
Courant–Fischer extrema change by at most the same bound.

With eigenvalues ordered *decreasingly*, one form of Weyl's sum inequalities is

\[
\lambda_{i+j-1}(A+B)\leq\lambda_i(A)+\lambda_j(B),
\qquad i+j-1\leq n.
\]

Choose subspaces of dimensions \(n-i+1\) and \(n-j+1\) on which the respective
Rayleigh quotients do not exceed those eigenvalues. Their intersection has
dimension at least \(n-i-j+2\). Apply the min–max principle to the sum on
that intersection.

### A gap controls eigendirections, not merely eigenvalues

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(0,g)\) with \(g>0\) and
\(E=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&t\\t&0\end{psmallmatrix}\),

\[
\lambda_\pm(A+E)=\frac{g\pm\sqrt{g^2+4t^2}}2,
\qquad \tan(2\varphi)=\frac{2|t|}{g},\quad 0\leq\varphi<\pi/4.
\]

At \(g=5,t=\tfrac12\), the eigenvalues are
\((5\pm\sqrt{26})/2\), and \(\varphi=\tfrac12\arctan(1/5)\approx0.09870\)
radians, or \(5.65^\circ\). For small \(|t|/g\), eigenvalue shifts are
second-order \(O(t^2/g)\), while angular motion is first-order \(O(|t|/g)\).
For a three-dimensional cluster example, exchanging the first two eigenvalues
of \(\operatorname{diag}(0,\varepsilon,5)\) changes the internal eigenvectors
but leaves the cluster projection \(\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)\) fixed.

### Weyl's law from a vibrating string

With Dirichlet boundary conditions on \((0,L)\),
\(-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\) has eigenfunctions \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) and
\(\lambda_n=(n\pi/L)^2\). Thus

\[
N(\Lambda)=\left\lfloor\frac{L\sqrt{\Lambda}}\pi\right\rfloor
\sim\frac{L\sqrt{\Lambda}}\pi.
\]

On sufficiently regular bounded \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d\), the leading
Dirichlet-Laplacian asymptotic is

\[
N(\Lambda)\sim\frac{\omega_d\operatorname{vol}(\Omega)}{(2\pi)^d}
\Lambda^{d/2},\qquad\Lambda\longrightarrow\infty,
\]

where \(\omega_d\) is the unit-ball volume. This says nothing by itself
about identical low-energy eigenvalues or the uniqueness of a domain's shape.

### Essential spectrum and the weakly escaping sequence

On \(\ell^2\), set \(T e_n=n^{-1}e_n\). The spectrum is
\(\{1/n:n\geq1\}\cup\{0\}\). Zero is spectral but not an eigenvalue.
The standard unit vectors satisfy

\[
\|e_n\|=1,\quad \|Te_n\|=1/n\longrightarrow0,\quad e_n\rightharpoonup0.
\]

For any \(x\in\ell^2\), \(\langle e_n,x\rangle=\overline{x_n}\to0\).
Hence zero belongs to the essential spectrum. Every positive \(1/n\) is an
isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity. Weyl's compact-perturbation
theorem for self-adjoint operators preserves essential spectrum. The
normalization, residual, domain and weak-null requirements are distinct;
in particular a weak-null condition is **not** needed in the ordinary
self-adjoint approximate-point spectrum criterion.

### Equidistribution and cancellation

For irrational \(\alpha\) and every nonzero integer \(k\), the average

\[
\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N e^{2\pi i k n\alpha}
=\frac{e^{2\pi i k\alpha}}N
\frac{1-e^{2\pi i k\alpha N}}{1-e^{2\pi i k\alpha}}
\longrightarrow0.
\]

Since the denominator is nonzero and the numerator is bounded, Weyl's
criterion yields equidistribution of the fractional parts of \(n\alpha\).
This number-theoretic result is different from Weyl's spectral-counting law.

### Gauge change in the quantum phase convention

For \(D_A=\partial-iA\), take
\(\psi'=e^{i\chi}\psi\) and \(A'=A+\partial\chi\). Then

\[
D_{A'}\psi'=e^{i\chi}D_A\psi.
\]

This is an illustrative modern phase-gauge formula, **not** a literal
restatement of Weyl's original 1918 length-scale gauge hypothesis. The
quantum phase reinterpretation is associated with his 1929 work.

### Historical and attribution notes

Weyl earned his doctorate under David Hilbert in Göttingen (1908), published
*Die Idee der Riemannschen Fläche* in 1913, worked at ETH Zürich from 1913,
returned as Hilbert's successor at Göttingen in 1930, and emigrated to the
Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton in 1933. His *Raum–Zeit–Materie*
appeared in 1918; *Gruppentheorie und Quantenmechanik* appeared in 1928.
His contributions to essential-spectrum theory, matrix eigenvalue inequalities,
eigenvalue counting, and equidistribution address separate problems with
separate mathematical assumptions. The matrix min–max discussion also depends
on the work of Courant, Fischer, and others; geometric principal-angle bounds
were developed further by later authors including Davis and Kahan.
