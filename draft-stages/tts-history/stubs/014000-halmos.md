# Paul Halmos: What is the geometry hidden inside a pair of projections?

Two projections are easier to picture than two arbitrary operators, but together they can still hide rich geometry. They can share directions, be perpendicular on other directions, and meet at intermediate angles on the rest of the space. A useful theorem separates those cases.

Paul Halmos was a mathematician and influential expositor of operator theory. His 1969 paper Two Subspaces gave a compact, systematic description of how two closed subspaces sit relative to each other. The decomposition is important because it makes proofs about projections look like calculations in a few recurring blocks.

Begin with the obvious pieces. There may be vectors belonging to both subspaces. There may be vectors in the first subspace and perpendicular to the second, or vice versa. There may also be vectors perpendicular to both. Those components can be handled separately; their projections act as zeros or identities.

The remaining part is the generic position, where neither subspace has one of those exceptional intersections. In finite dimensions we can understand it through principal-angle planes. In each plane, the first projection keeps one axis. The second projection keeps a line tilted by some angle. Its matrix has entries built from the squared cosine, squared sine, and their product.

This block representation is powerful because many identities become scalar trigonometry. The norm of the difference between the two rank-one projections is the sine of the angle. A canonical direct rotation has its own two-by-two rotation matrix. The full-space operator can be studied by combining the contributions of these mutually orthogonal blocks.

The general Hilbert-space picture may require operators playing the roles of cosine and sine rather than a finite list of scalar angles. But the organizing principle stays the same: separate exceptional intersections from a generic angular part.

Halmos's result is a structural theorem. It tells us what geometry a pair of subspaces can have. Davis–Kahan bounds then ask how spectral hypotheses restrict that geometry under perturbation.

The historical contribution is a choice of normal form that reveals the moving pieces. Instead of handling two projections by raw algebra alone, we can inspect their common directions and principal-angle blocks and make the underlying problem visible.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For \(K=\operatorname{span}(e_1)\) and
\(L=\operatorname{span}(\cos\theta\,e_1+\sin\theta\,e_2)\),
\(P_K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) and
\(P_L=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}c^2&cs\\cs&s^2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
Their difference has eigenvalues \(\pm\sin\theta\), so
\(\|P_K-P_L\|=\sin\theta\).
