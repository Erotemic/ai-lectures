# Tengyao Wang: Why do rectangular data require a singular-subspace theorem?

Many data sets do not produce symmetric square matrices. A table of observations by variables, a matrix of pairwise interactions, or a rectangular signal model has different input and output spaces. Its important directions are singular vectors, rather than eigenvectors of a single symmetric matrix.

Tengyao Wang coauthored the 2015 Biometrika paper A Useful Variant of the Davis–Kahan Theorem for Statisticians with Yi Yu and Richard Samworth. Besides its population-gap formulation for symmetric eigenspaces, the paper presents an extension relevant to asymmetric or non-square matrices and the corresponding singular subspaces.

Picture a tall matrix that maps two input coordinates into three measured output coordinates. Its leading right singular vector lies in the two-dimensional input space. Its leading left singular vector lies in the three-dimensional output space. These vectors are linked through the singular value, but they do not inhabit the same space and should not be conflated.

Perturb the matrix by a small error. There are now two geometric questions. How far has the leading right singular subspace moved among input directions? How far has the leading left singular subspace moved among output directions? In an application one may care about one, the other, or both.

One mathematical trick is to place the rectangular matrix and its transpose into the off-diagonal blocks of a larger symmetric matrix. The resulting eigenvalues encode positive and negative singular values. That helps connect singular-subspace perturbation to self-adjoint techniques. It does not erase the need to state the correct gap assumptions or to distinguish the two sides of the singular decomposition.

A gap between retained and discarded singular values is the important resource. Within a tightly clustered group, individual singular vectors may rotate freely while their combined span remains meaningful. This is the same subspace-versus-basis distinction found in symmetric eigenspace analysis.

The results in the 2015 article belong jointly to Yu, Wang and Samworth. This recording emphasizes the rectangular extension as a distinct mathematical idea in that collaboration: the practical data matrix may not be symmetric, but the language of singular values and principal angles still makes its subspace errors measurable.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For \(M\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), let
\(\mathcal H(M)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&M\\M^*&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
The eigenvalues include signed singular values of \(M\). The corresponding
left and right singular subspaces lie in \(\mathbb R^m\) and \(\mathbb R^n\),
respectively. Refer to the exact rectangular theorem in Yu–Wang–Samworth
(2015) for the separation and norm hypotheses.
