# Walther Ritz: How do infinitely many possible motions become a finite calculation?

A vibrating plate can bend in infinitely many ways. A computation cannot search through every possible shape. It needs a small family of shapes that is rich enough to approximate the motion we want.

Walther Ritz was a Swiss mathematician and physicist whose short career produced methods with a lasting computational life. His work in the years around 1908 and 1909 developed practical variational approximations, including applications to vibrations. The Rayleigh–Ritz method bears both his name and Lord Rayleigh's, but historians have discussed the precise relationship between their methods and earlier variational work.

Here is the idea in a small setting. Suppose a displacement function is approximated as three chosen trial shapes multiplied by three unknown coefficients and added together. The energy of the displacement becomes a quadratic expression in those three coefficients. The condition that its quotient should be stationary produces a three-by-three eigenvalue problem. A difficult infinite-dimensional question has been compressed into a tractable finite matrix.

There is a price for that compression. The answer is exact for the chosen trial space, but not necessarily for the full physical system. The computed eigenvalue is a Ritz value. The corresponding combination of trial functions is a Ritz vector or Ritz function. If you substitute it into the original operator equation, the leftover error is the residual.

A crucial geometric property makes the method effective: with the standard self-adjoint Ritz construction, that residual is orthogonal to the trial space. This is Galerkin orthogonality. It says the trial approximation has already eliminated the part of the error that the chosen trial space is able to represent. The remaining residual points outward, toward directions not yet included.

That picture leads directly to eigenvector and subspace error analysis. We need to know how much of this outward residual enters spectral directions that should not be present. A separation between wanted and unwanted spectral values helps convert residual size into an angular error estimate.

Ritz's place in the story is constructive. He supplied a procedure for asking a hard variational problem a finite set of questions. The later perturbation theorems explain how much trust to place in the answers.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

Let \(E\) be a column-orthonormal trial frame and \(A=A^*\). Define
\(H=E^*AE\) and \(R=AE-EH\). Then \(E^*R=0\).
A Ritz pair \(Hy=\lambda y\) yields \(x=Ey\) and
\(Ax-\lambda x=Ry\). The residual is in the orthogonal complement of the
trial subspace, but its size alone does not select the target eigenspace.
