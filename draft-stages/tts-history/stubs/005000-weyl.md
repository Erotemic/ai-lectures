# Hermann Weyl: Why can eigenvalues remain stable while their eigenvectors rotate?

Start with a two-by-two symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are one and one plus a very small number. Rotate its eigenvectors through a quarter turn. The matrix entries change only on the scale of that small separation, but the individual eigendirections have changed dramatically. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors answer different stability questions.

Hermann Weyl worked across mathematics and mathematical physics in the early twentieth century. Among his contributions are spectral inequalities that relate the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices to perturbations and matrix structure. His 1912 work belongs to the history of such inequalities. The name Weyl also appears elsewhere in analysis and geometry, so it helps to specify which result is meant.

For a Hermitian matrix, order the eigenvalues from smallest to largest. If you add another Hermitian matrix of operator norm at most epsilon, each ordered eigenvalue moves by at most epsilon. This can be understood through the min–max principle: every unit vector's Rayleigh quotient changes by at most epsilon, so the extremal values obtained by taking minima and maxima over subspaces change by at most epsilon too.

But this bound controls a list of numbers, not a choice of basis vectors. If two eigenvalues are almost equal, the perturbation may rearrange the preferred directions inside their two-dimensional cluster. Trying to label one eigenvector as the same physical direction before and after perturbation can become unstable.

A more meaningful object is often the whole subspace corresponding to a cluster. A gap separating that cluster from the rest of the spectrum can remain substantial even when the eigenvalues inside it are close. The cluster subspace can then be much more stable than its individual basis vectors.

This distinction shaped the later Davis–Kahan questions. A theorem about eigenvalue displacement is not yet a theorem about spectral projection movement. The latter asks how much a subspace tilts and requires the relevant spectral separation.

Weyl's role in this history is the rigorous comparison of spectra under changing operators. The modern lesson is a division of labor: eigenvalue inequalities control where spectral numbers can go; principal-angle inequalities control the subspaces carrying them.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For Hermitian \(A,E\) with ordered eigenvalues, Weyl's operator-norm estimate is
\(|\lambda_j(A+E)-\lambda_j(A)|\le\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\).
Let \(A_\delta=\operatorname{diag}(1,1+\delta)\) and
\(B_\delta=\operatorname{diag}(1+\delta,1)\). Then
\(\|A_\delta-B_\delta\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\delta\), while their lower
eigenvectors are orthogonal.
