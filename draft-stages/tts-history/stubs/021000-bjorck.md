# Åke Björck: How can principal angles be computed reliably from matrices?

A subspace is often given by a rectangular matrix whose columns span it. To compare two such subspaces, it seems natural to compute inner products between their columns. But if those columns are poorly scaled or nearly dependent, their raw overlap matrix can be misleading.

Åke Björck worked in numerical linear algebra and least-squares computation. His 1973 paper with Gene Golub, Numerical Methods for Computing Angles Between Linear Subspaces, gave practical algorithms for finding principal angles and principal vectors from matrix descriptions of spaces. The work makes a crucial distinction between the mathematical subspace and the numerically imperfect coordinates used to represent it.

Imagine two lines given by column vectors. Doubling the length of one column does not change the line, yet it doubles their raw inner product. We must normalize before interpreting an inner product as the cosine of an angle. For higher-dimensional subspaces, this means constructing orthonormal bases, often through a QR factorization.

Once both frames are orthonormal, multiply the transpose of one by the other. The resulting small overlap matrix records their relative alignment. Its singular values are the cosines of the principal angles. For example, if two two-dimensional subspaces share one direction and differ by a sixty-degree rotation in another, the overlap singular values are one and one half. The angles are zero and sixty degrees.

This algebra is exact on paper. On a computer, rounding error complicates it. A nearly rank-deficient spanning matrix can make an orthonormal basis hard to compute accurately. Different orthogonalization algorithms have different stability and cost characteristics. Björck and Golub's paper compared practical choices and analyzed the conditioning of the angular computation.

The calculation serves two roles in spectral perturbation. First, it provides a way to measure the actual movement of a computed subspace against a reference. Second, it clarifies which input data matter: the subspaces, not arbitrary scalings of the columns chosen to represent them.

Björck's contribution helps connect the elegant geometry of principal angles to the numerical responsibility of computing them. A formula that is invariant under a change of basis should be implemented in a way that respects that invariance as closely as floating-point arithmetic allows.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For full-rank \(A=Q_AR_A\) and \(B=Q_BR_B\), with orthonormal
columns in \(Q_A,Q_B\), principal-angle cosines are singular values of
\(Q_A^*Q_B\). Singular values of \(A^*B\) generally do not have this
meaning without normalization. Reference: Björck and Golub (1973),
*Math. Comp.* 27, 579–594.
