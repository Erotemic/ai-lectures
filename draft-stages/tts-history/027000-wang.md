# Tengyao Wang: Why do rectangular data require a singular-subspace theorem?

Consider a data table with one thousand observations and fifty measured variables. It is a rectangle, with one dimension labeling individual observations and the other labeling different kinds of measurements. A scientist may want to replace those fifty variables with two or three combinations that capture a stable signal. The singular value decomposition provides a natural way to do this, and most numerical software can compute it.

Now imagine collecting the same data again. Some observations have changed, some measurements have noise, and some entries are missing. The newly computed singular vectors are different. Does that mean the underlying structure has changed? Or has a stable subspace merely been rotated by estimation error? One must answer this question separately for the left and right singular subspaces, because they live in different coordinate spaces.

The issue lies at the meeting point of numerical linear algebra and statistical inference. Classical perturbation theory tells us how matrix errors affect spectral subspaces. Statistics asks how large those errors can be when they arise from random or incomplete observations. Computer science adds a further question: even if an accurate estimator exists in principle, can a reasonable algorithm calculate it within the resources we have?

Those three questions run through the research of Tengyao Wang. During his doctoral work at Cambridge, he coauthored the 2015 Biometrika paper A Useful Variant of the Davis–Kahan Theorem for Statisticians with Yi Yu and Richard Samworth. Its rectangular-matrix extension gives a convenient population-singular-gap formulation. His later work has examined sparse principal components, the computational difficulty of optimal estimation, change-point detection, missing data, and the movement from theoretical methods into biomedical and other scientific collaborations.

The trajectory makes Wang a useful figure through whom to understand a change in statistical mathematics. For much of the twentieth century, computing a matrix decomposition reliably was itself a major challenge. Modern high-dimensional statistics takes those algorithms as building blocks, and asks what they estimate, when they succeed, and whether their computational cost is compatible with the best available statistical guarantees.

## Training across mathematics, computation, and statistics

Wang's published academic record describes early research on generalized Schur functions and rational interpolation with Joseph Weiss and Vladimir Bolotnikov, papers dating from 2011 and 2012. Those topics concern analytic functions and prescribed interpolation data. They precede his main statistical publications and reveal mathematical training that extended beyond any one statistical algorithm.

A classical interpolation problem asks for a function that takes specified values at specified points, perhaps subject to a constraint on its magnitude inside the unit disk. Such problems can be translated into positivity conditions on structured matrices. Even without tracing every technical detail of Wang's early results, their relationship to subsequent spectral work is evident: linear algebra appears as a tool for understanding what families of functions are possible.

At Cambridge he wrote a 2013 Part Three essay on applications of empirical process theory and later completed a 2016 doctorate entitled Spectral Methods and Computational Trade-offs in High-Dimensional Statistical Inference. Richard Samworth was his doctoral supervisor. The thesis title neatly identifies two interests that would persist through his later career. Spectral methods extract low-dimensional directions from a high-dimensional object. Computational trade-offs arise when the mathematically ideal solution is too expensive to find.

An early collaboration with Sébastien Bubeck and Nitin Viswanathan concerned multiple identifications in multi-armed bandits, published in 2013. In a bandit problem one repeatedly chooses among options whose rewards are unknown, trading off the value of exploration against the benefit of choosing an option that already appears favorable. Identifying several good options differs from maximizing reward at every step. It asks how much information can be gathered with a limited number of experiments.

This is related to matrix estimation through a common statistical dilemma: observations are costly, but the target cannot be identified from no observations at all. A method can be computationally fast and still need too much data, or statistically efficient and require a search over an enormous collection of possibilities. High-dimensional inference forces us to consider both costs.

Wang later held a research fellowship at the Cantab Capital Institute for the Mathematics of Information in Cambridge, then a lectureship at University College London. He moved to the London School of Economics, where his current academic profile lists him as professor of statistics and a program director for the MSc in Statistics, Financial Statistics. These moves also trace the development of a research agenda spanning methodological statistics and practical applications.

## Why a rectangular matrix has two geometries

Take a very simple rectangular matrix. It has three rows and two columns. Its first column is the vector whose entries are three, zero, zero. Its second column is the vector whose entries are zero, one, zero. Think of the matrix as a transformation from two input coordinates to three output coordinates. It stretches the first input axis by three, stretches the second by one, and places the resulting vectors along the first two output axes.

The largest singular value is three. The corresponding right singular vector is the first input axis, a direction in two-dimensional space. Its left singular vector is the first output axis, a direction in three-dimensional space. The second singular value is one, with its own pair of left and right vectors. Each pair is linked by the transformation: the matrix sends the right vector to the singular value times the left vector.

Now alter the matrix. Replace its first column by three times the vector whose first two coordinates are the cosine and sine of a small angle. Leave the second column unchanged. The dominant output direction turns, but because the two columns are no longer orthogonal, the right singular vector may also move. It can be a different rotation in the two-dimensional input space. One must distinguish the output question from the input question.

Here is an even cleaner illustration. Let the original matrix have first column three times the first output coordinate and second column the second output coordinate. Multiply it on the left by a rotation acting on the first and third output coordinates. The right singular vectors do not change at all: the matrix times its transpose has rotated, but the transpose-times-matrix Gram matrix is identical. The left singular vectors rotate with the output space. Conversely, multiplying the matrix on the right by an input-space rotation changes the right singular vectors while the left singular subspace can remain the same.

This is why a rectangular perturbation result should state exactly which side it controls. A row of observations and a column of features are different kinds of objects. An application that cares about feature loadings typically needs right singular vectors. An application reconstructing a column space of signals may care about the left singular vectors. One can sometimes bound both with the same constants, but it is still two assertions about different spaces.

A common computational trick turns the rectangular problem into a symmetric one. Place the matrix in the upper-right block of a larger square matrix, its transpose in the lower-left block, and zeros in the other two blocks. The resulting matrix is symmetric. Its nonzero eigenvalues are positive and negative copies of the singular values of the original rectangle. Each singular pair gives an eigenvector formed from its left and right members. This allows symmetric eigenspace tools to be brought into a problem that started with two coordinate spaces.

But there are zero eigenvalues too. They account for directions in the input or output space that the rectangular transformation does not use. Near zero, singular-value multiplicities and rank changes become important. A symmetric embedding explains much of the geometry; it does not remove the need to state exactly which singular block is identifiable.

## From Wedin to a population-only separation

In 1972 Per-Åke Wedin developed influential singular-subspace perturbation bounds. They extended the geometry of eigenspace rotation to rectangular matrices. The residual and gap conditions in such theorems can involve spectral quantities from both the original and perturbed matrices, depending on the formulation.

The 2015 paper by Yu, Wang and Samworth returned to this issue in a statistical setting. Their first main result concerned symmetric matrices: a bound for the angle between selected sample and population eigenspaces using a gap between population eigenvalues alone. Their rectangular extension performed an analogous service for singular subspaces. It controlled the selected left and right singular subspaces using quantities associated with the population matrix and a norm of the error.

There is a simple conceptual route from the symmetric theorem. For a rectangular matrix A, the matrix A transpose times A is symmetric. Its eigenvectors are the right singular vectors of A, and its nonzero eigenvalues are the squares of A's singular values. If A changes to A hat, compare the two Gram matrices. Apply a symmetric subspace perturbation bound to them.

Why do squares appear in the denominator? Because the eigenvalues of the Gram matrices are squared singular values. If the retained singular values are three and the discarded ones are one, their squared gap is nine minus one, or eight. In the associated Gram matrix, that is the spectral separation that controls a subspace angle. The squared gap is a natural consequence of the reduction, rather than an arbitrary adjustment.

The numerator also changes. We have to compare A hat transpose times A hat with A transpose times A. Expanding the difference produces terms involving the matrix error multiplied by the original and perturbed matrices. Consequently, the bound involves both the error norm and the size of the original matrix. The full expression has to be retained in applications, but its reasoning can be understood in three steps: reduce to symmetric Gram matrices, control the difference of those Gram matrices, and divide by a population squared-singular-value gap.

The same argument applied to A times A transpose controls the left singular subspace. The two bounds are structurally parallel. This is a mathematically clean explanation of why the extension works. It also shows its limitation: if the singular-value gap closes, or if the perturbation overwhelms it, the bound may cease to be informative.

The 2015 article is a joint result. Its authors distinguished their work from Wedin's foundational 1972 theory, which they were refining for statistical use. The first author, Yi Yu, had a research background in high-dimensional inference. Wang's subsequent doctoral work emphasized statistical and computational trade-offs. Samworth provided mentorship and collaboration within Cambridge's statistical research community. Credit for the common theorem belongs to all three.

## Work through the rectangular perturbation

Start with a matrix having singular values four and two. Suppose its right singular directions are simply the two coordinate axes. Its squared singular values are sixteen and four, so the squared gap is twelve. If we perturb the matrix by a small amount, the right Gram matrix changes. The principal-angle theorem gives a quantitative upper bound for how far the leading right direction can move.

Now compare this with a matrix whose singular values are four and three point nine. The squared gap is sixteen minus fifteen point two one, or point seven nine. Even if the perturbation has the same norm, there is much less spectral separation to resist rotation. The effect on the leading singular vector can be much larger.

It is useful to keep the geometric interpretation in front of the algebra. A singular vector is a direction where the matrix stretches by a particular factor. If the largest and second-largest stretch factors are almost equal, there is little preference between those directions. Noise can swap or rotate them. If one direction stretches much more than its competitors, the preferred direction is harder to dislodge.

Now add a third singular value equal to the second one. The second and third right singular vectors are not uniquely determined separately. If the application requires their combined two-dimensional span and that span is separated from the remaining singular directions, a block theorem can still give meaningful control. The meaningful object is the span, not an arbitrary basis chosen by a numerical routine.

One must also handle zero singular values carefully. In a rectangular matrix, zero can appear with different multiplicities when we look at A transpose times A and A times A transpose. A vector in the kernel of A is a right-side zero direction; a vector orthogonal to the image is a left-side zero direction. The two spaces can have different dimensions. For positive singular values, matched left and right vectors are linked cleanly. At zero, that pairing requires more care.

These qualifications are especially important in modern data analysis. Rank selection, missing data and factor estimation often bring estimated singular values near zero. A perturbation theorem cannot decide how many genuine factors there are without assumptions separating signal from noise. It tells us what follows once the target and its spectral separation have been specified.

## An estimator may exist but be computationally inaccessible

The rectangular theorem was only one part of Wang's doctoral research. Another influential line concerned sparse principal component analysis. Ordinary PCA finds a direction maximizing variance. Sparse PCA adds a requirement: the direction should involve only a small number of variables. Perhaps a genetic signal appears in a handful of genes among thousands. Perhaps a financial factor depends on a small, interpretable collection of assets.

Suppose the leading population direction uses only three of one hundred variables. A purely statistical ideal might search all sets of three variables, calculate the best direction within each set, and choose the result with the highest sample variance. This exhaustive search can achieve an excellent fit in small cases. But as the number of variables grows, the number of possible supports becomes enormous. There are more than one hundred and sixty thousand choices of three variables from one hundred, and the count grows sharply for larger supports.

A practical algorithm needs a shortcut. One possibility is a convex relaxation: replace the difficult sparse optimization by a related optimization problem whose feasible region is easier for a computer to search. Another is to use random projections or coordinate screening. These methods may be fast, but they do not necessarily attain the same statistical error as the best estimator that ignores computational cost.

In a 2016 Annals of Statistics paper, Wang, Quentin Berthet and Richard Samworth investigated this trade-off. Under a widely used computational-hardness assumption associated with planted clique problems, they established a regime where no randomized polynomial-time procedure could attain a certain minimax-optimal estimation rate over their specified model classes. They also analyzed a semidefinite-relaxation-based estimator.

It is vital to state the qualification. The result is conditional on a complexity assumption and on precise statistical classes and performance measures. It does not prove that every sparse PCA problem is intractable. Nor does it say a fast method can never be accurate. It shows that there are plausible model regimes in which the statistical optimum and efficient computation may be separated.

This is a different kind of impossibility from a small spectral gap. A small gap can make a target statistically hard to identify even for an unlimited computer. A computational lower bound asks whether a target that is statistically estimable can be reached within a realistic computational budget. Wang's work brought those two forms of difficulty into a common research narrative.


## The best direction may not be the best explanation

A singular vector answers a precise optimization problem. For a unit right vector, its image under the matrix has some length. The first right singular vector maximizes that length; successive singular vectors solve related constrained problems. In a data matrix, the optimization often becomes a way to summarize variation. Yet an analyst may want something different: a direction that predicts an outcome, identifies a change, or can be interpreted in a small number of variables.

These targets can disagree. Imagine two measured variables. The first has substantial random variation that is unrelated to the phenomenon of interest. The second has smaller random variation but carries a persistent shift when the process changes. PCA can prioritize the first variable because it accounts for more total variance. A change-point statistic should prioritize the second because it contains the signal separating the regimes.

A concrete model makes the disagreement clear. Before a change, both measurements have mean zero. The first has variance one hundred and the second has variance one. After the change, only the second mean increases by two. The population covariance of the stationary noise is diagonal, with values one hundred and one. Its first principal component points along the first measurement. Projecting onto that component removes the mean shift entirely. Projecting onto the second coordinate preserves the change of two.

This is not a failure of PCA. It has solved the maximum-variance problem correctly. The mistake would be to treat maximum variance as synonymous with maximum information about the change. A method such as sparse projection built from cumulative-sum contrasts uses a different objective tailored to the scientific signal. The geometry remains spectral, but the matrix being decomposed has been designed to represent the task.

The same issue appears in missing-data problems. The leading eigenvector of a naively filled-in table may faithfully describe the filled-in table, but the table may no longer reflect the underlying observations. Numerical accuracy of the decomposition and statistical accuracy of the input matrix are different properties. They need different diagnostics, and theorems connect them only under explicit assumptions.

For a listener learning statistical numerical linear algebra, this is a useful habit: before interpreting a singular vector, ask which matrix produced it, which objective the vector optimizes, and which population quantity that matrix is meant to estimate. Only then ask whether the computed direction is stable under perturbation.

## Sparse projections for sudden changes

Imagine watching a thousand sensors that report once per second. One piece of equipment starts malfunctioning, and only five sensors show a shift in their average readings. Looking at any single sensor gives a weak signal. Averaging all the sensors dilutes the change by mixing the five affected channels with the nine hundred and ninety-five unaffected ones.

There is a better direction in which to project the data. If we knew which five channels changed and by how much, we could form a weighted combination that amplifies their shared shift. The problem is that the change direction is unknown. We need to estimate it from the same noisy data used to locate the change.

This idea underlies Wang and Samworth's 2018 paper on high-dimensional change-point estimation via sparse projection. Their method, called inspect, uses a cumulative-sum transformation to collect evidence about a change over possible split times. It then solves a convex optimization problem designed to identify a sparse projection direction. The leading left singular vector of the resulting object is used to project the original multivariate time series. Finally, a univariate change-point procedure searches the projected sequence.

The architecture is elegant because it separates tasks. First find a promising direction in measurement space. Then detect a change in a one-dimensional sequence, where the problem is better understood. This is another place where singular-vector geometry connects to inference. If the estimated projection direction is close to the true signal direction, projecting onto it preserves much of the change magnitude. If the direction is nearly perpendicular, the change can disappear into noise.

A simple calculation captures the idea. Suppose the change vector has entries two in the first two coordinates and zero elsewhere. Its length is the square root of eight, a little under two point eight three. Project onto the unit vector with equal positive weights on those two coordinates. The projected mean shift is the full length of the change vector. Project onto a unit vector supported only on a third coordinate and the shift becomes zero. The raw observations contain the same change, but the projection determines whether the change is visible in the one-dimensional series.

Wang and Samworth analyzed both estimation of the projection and localization of changes. Their method comes with theoretical guarantees under assumptions about sparsity, signal strength and noise, and with an R implementation. Wang received the Royal Statistical Society Research Prize in 2019, as recorded in his LSE profile. The contribution illustrates how the pursuit of a stable singular direction can lead directly to a working statistical algorithm.

## When the data matrix has holes

A second complication in modern data tables is missingness. Some laboratory measurements fail. Some patients skip questions. An instrument records some channels intermittently. If every entry were observed, PCA would work with the full matrix. If entries are missing, filling them with zero alters the data and can produce spurious directions.

There is a common statistical correction when missingness probabilities are known. Reweight observed entries, or observed pairs, by their probabilities of being observed. In a homogeneous model where every entry is recorded with the same probability, this can be effective. But realistic datasets often have heterogeneous missingness. A frequently measured variable and a rarely measured variable may be distorted in different ways. A single global adjustment need not solve the problem.

Wang's 2022 work with Ziwei Zhu and Richard Samworth studied high-dimensional PCA with heterogeneous missingness. They demonstrated limitations of an observed-proportion-weighted estimator, including its failure to obtain exact recovery in certain noiseless settings. They proposed primePCA, an iterative procedure that alternates between estimating a low-dimensional subspace and using that subspace to impute the missing entries.

To understand the iteration, picture a data table whose rows lie approximately in a two-dimensional plane in a much larger feature space. Start with a rough estimate of the plane. For a row with several missing coordinates, project the observed information onto that approximate plane and use the implied coordinates as provisional replacements. Now that the table is more complete, recompute the leading singular subspace. If the new plane is better, the next round of imputations improves too.

The procedure is self-reinforcing when it starts sufficiently near the true structure and when the pattern of observed entries supplies enough information. It can fail when a crucial feature is almost never observed or when the low-rank structure is aligned with a missing coordinate in an unfortunate way. Such situations motivate assumptions about incoherence and about how the missingness mechanism interacts with the signal.

The authors established geometric convergence of the error in a suitable noiseless regime, with conditions involving signal strength and properties of the observation mechanism. Their result emphasizes average features of missingness rather than relying only on the worst-observed coordinate. The paper also supplied software. Here spectral perturbation geometry appears inside an iterative algorithm: each step must update the subspace accurately enough for the next step to be useful.

## A statistician writing about a statistical ancestor

In 2023 Wang wrote a short personal commentary for Harvard Data Science Review about the influence of Sir David Cox. It is especially informative because it is a rare public source in which Wang explicitly describes his intellectual priorities. Cox's work combined mathematically refined methods with problems arising in medicine and other sciences. Wang recalls being impressed by the Cox proportional-hazards model as a student and by the way Cox linked a clean mathematical construction to a practical inferential question.

Wang also explains a later research problem inspired by that perspective. In a high-dimensional model there may be a relatively sparse difference between two regression coefficient vectors that we wish to test, while the nuisance coefficients themselves are dense. Assuming sparsity of the nuisance effects could make the analysis mathematically convenient but scientifically implausible. In a 2022 paper with Feng Gao, Wang investigated two-sample testing in that setting using a method called complementary sketching.

Here is the conceptual problem. Two hospitals fit models relating many predictors to the same outcome. We want to test whether the predictor effects differ between the hospitals. The effects shared by both hospitals may be complicated and spread across many predictors. If our method demands that the shared effects be sparse, it can fail even when the difference we care about is simple. A successful procedure must somehow neutralize the complicated common structure without falsely assuming it is absent.

Complementary sketching uses linear transformations to remove nuisance variation and expose the difference of interest. The technical results quantify statistical limits under specified conditions. The significance for this portrait is the research attitude Wang articulated himself: identify the parameter that is scientifically relevant, and design an inferential method that does not conceal the difficult nuisance structure behind a convenient but unjustified hypothesis.

His publication list also contains work with clinicians, molecular biologists and materials scientists: studies of medical prognosis and anatomy, a COVID-19 diagnostic platform, characterization of CRISPR activation, and machine learning for battery materials. These are joint projects with specialists in the relevant fields. They show the same tension between mathematical structure and imperfect measurements, now in settings where decisions and scientific interpretations depend on the details of the data.


## Another limit: assumptions are part of the algorithm

Consider sparse PCA once more. Suppose a researcher knows that the true factor uses a handful of features. This sounds like valuable extra information, but it is only valuable if the chosen coordinates and the strength of their signal can actually be learned from the data. At very low signal strength, many different sparse supports may fit nearly equally well. An exhaustive algorithm cannot resolve that statistical ambiguity; the observations simply contain too little information.

Now raise the signal strength. With enough data, a hypothetical exhaustive estimator may accurately identify the factor. Yet there may still be so many candidate supports that enumerating them is impractical. A fast relaxation might lose something in estimation accuracy. We have moved from an information limit to a computational one. Understanding which limit binds is essential before spending money on more data or more computing power.

At a third stage, suppose the data are sufficient and a fast method succeeds under a familiar model, but observations are missing more often for certain variables. The guarantee may no longer apply. Repairing that failure may require a new estimator such as primePCA or additional information about the observation process. Here the obstacle is neither a basic spectral calculation nor pure computational cost; it is the relation between the model's assumptions and how the data were collected.

This sequence of cases explains why Wang's publications repeatedly join algorithms with lower bounds. An upper bound shows what a particular procedure can achieve under a specified model. A lower bound shows that no procedure in a specified class can beat a certain risk level. A computational lower bound adds a restriction on the algorithms being considered, usually under a complexity assumption. Without identifying which kind of claim is being made, one can easily mistake a property of a method for a fundamental property of a problem.

## The wider problem of changes and constraints

Wang continued to develop change-point methods through collaborations on online detection, multiscale methods, and high-dimensional streams with missing observations. In an online setting a procedure observes data as they arrive and must decide whether to signal a change. Raising the detection threshold reduces false alarms but may delay detection. Lowering it responds more quickly but can produce too many alarms. If the signal appears in only a few coordinates, high dimension complicates both sides of that trade-off.

The shift from a one-off batch calculation to an online procedure is substantial. Batch analysis can revisit the entire dataset. Online analysis has to maintain a state or summary and make decisions from partial history. Statistical theorems must therefore specify a false-alarm criterion and a detection-delay criterion. A guarantee about the accuracy of a final estimated change time does not automatically imply that a real-time alarm is reliable.

Wang's more recent published projects include robust high-dimensional mean-change testing with heavy-tailed noise, methods for regression changes in the presence of dense nuisance parameters, and statistical procedures under missingness. His work with Yi Yu and others on robust mean change-point testing shows that the two research programs continued to intersect after their 2015 spectral paper. The basic idea is familiar from the rectangular-matrix setting: identify the structure that is stable enough to estimate, then quantify how observational imperfections affect it.

In 2023 the Royal Statistical Society awarded Wang the Guy Medal in Bronze, specifically citing work in high-dimensional statistics, change-point estimation, dimension reduction and missing data. He also received the Society's Research Prize in 2019. Those awards reflect several branches of a research program, not simply the importance of a single theorem.

His LSE research profile describes an interest in statistical limitations imposed by computational constraints, as well as practical tasks in nonparametric inference, sparse detection and scientific applications. These themes connect mathematical analysis to the actual constraints of computational statistics, from finite samples and missing observations to the cost of finding a useful estimator.

## What an accurate singular vector can and cannot promise

Return to the original rectangular data table. We estimate a leading singular subspace. A perturbation theorem says that if the estimation error is sufficiently controlled relative to a spectral gap, the estimated space will be close to the population space. That is an important guarantee, but it is only one step in a complete statistical analysis.

The target itself may be poorly identified because its singular values are nearly tied. A sparse version of the problem may be statistically solvable but computationally difficult. Missing entries may produce biased estimates unless their observation mechanism is modeled. A signal that changes over time may invalidate a stationary population matrix. The software may solve its numerical problem to high accuracy while that numerical problem is a poor representation of the scientific question.

Each difficulty demands a different remedy. Larger spectral separation helps control rotation. Additional observations may reduce statistical error. Structural assumptions such as sparsity or low rank can make estimation possible in high dimension, while also adding potential modeling risk. Convex relaxations can make a problem computationally manageable, sometimes at a statistical price. Iterative imputation can use low-rank structure to repair incomplete tables, provided the missingness pattern contains enough information. Sequential analysis replaces a single retrospective estimate with a rule balancing speed and false alarms.

Wang's research links these remedies rather than presenting one as universal. The 2015 theorem, jointly with Yu and Samworth, made classical subspace geometry fit the statistical population-versus-sample distinction. His later work asked what happens when the desired subspace is sparse, when computing an optimal estimator is hard, when entries are missing, or when the signal changes while we observe it.

The central insight is that a mathematical direction is useful only after we understand what identifies it, how the observations reveal it, and whether we can compute it. A rectangular matrix contains two geometries, one on each side. Statistical inference must also keep sight of two limits: the information provided by the data, and the computational work required to extract it. Wang's contributions show how those limits can be studied together.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Left and right singular directions

For \(A\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}\), the SVD is \(A=U\Sigma V^T\). Singular triples satisfy \(Av_j=\sigma_j u_j\), \(A^T u_j=\sigma_j v_j\) for each positive singular value. The right vectors inhabit \(\mathbb R^q\), the left vectors \(\mathbb R^p\). For positive singular values, \(A^TA v_j=\sigma_j^2v_j\), \(AA^T u_j=\sigma_j^2u_j\). Zero eigenspaces have dimensions depending on \(p,q,\mathrm{rank}(A)\).

### 2. Symmetric embedding

\[
H(A)=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\ A^T&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{(p+q)\times(p+q)}.
\]
If \(Av=\sigma u\) and \(A^T u=\sigma v\), then \(H(A)(u,v)^T=\sigma(u,v)^T\) and \(H(A)(u,-v)^T=-\sigma(u,-v)^T\). When \(\sigma>0\), vectors are normalized by \(1/\sqrt2\) if \(u,v\) are unit. The remaining spectrum contains zeros of multiplicity \(p+q-2\mathrm{rank}(A)\).

### 3. Original rectangular population-gap theorem

For \(A,\widehat A\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}\), let \(E=\widehat A-A\); let singular values be ordered decreasingly and first consider a **nonterminal target block** \(1\le r\le s<\mathrm{rank}(A)\), \(d=s-r+1\). Under the positive **squared singular gap**
\[
\gamma_2=\min\{\sigma_{r-1}^2-\sigma_r^2,\ \sigma_s^2-\sigma_{s+1}^2\}>0,
\]
with the upper absent neighbor omitted and with the next squared singular value taken from the corresponding Gram spectrum, Theorem 3 of Yu–Wang–Samworth (2015) implies for the corresponding right singular spaces
\[
\|\sin\Theta(\widehat V,V)\|_F \le
\frac{2(2\sigma_1(A)+\|E\|_{\rm op})\min\{\sqrt d\|E\|_{\rm op},\|E\|_F\}}{\gamma_2}.
\]
The aligned basis bound has coefficient \(2^{3/2}\) in place of 2; analogous bounds hold for left singular subspaces. The displayed bound is the paper's Gram-matrix formulation in the regular case with a separated target block. At a terminal block, the zero eigenspace and the smaller of the row and column dimensions require explicit treatment when identifying the complementary Gram spectrum.

### 4. Gram perturbation mechanism

\[
\widehat A^T\widehat A-A^TA
=E^T\widehat A+A^TE,
\qquad
\|\widehat A^T\widehat A-A^TA\|_{\rm op}
\le(2\|A\|_{\rm op}+\|E\|_{\rm op})\|E\|_{\rm op}.
\]
A corresponding Frobenius bound is \((2\|A\|_{\rm op}+\|E\|_{\rm op})\|E\|_F\). Apply the 2015 symmetric theorem to the Gram matrices with eigenvalues \(\sigma_j^2\). The transpose version controls left singular spaces.

### 5. Simple rectangular tests

For
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad R_\phi=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\phi&0&-\sin\phi\\0&1&0\\\sin\phi&0&\cos\phi\end{bmatrix},
\]
we have \((R_\phi A)^T(R_\phi A)=A^TA=\mathrm{diag}(9,1)\): right singular vectors are fixed. But left singular vectors rotate by \(R_\phi\). Replacing \(A\) with \(A Q_\psi\), for a \(2\times2\) orthogonal rotation \(Q_\psi\), preserves \(AA^T\), while the right singular directions rotate by \(Q_\psi^T\). This demonstrates that the two angle questions are separate.

A leading squared singular gap is \(4^2-2^2=12\) for singular values \((4,2)\); it is \(4^2-3.9^2=0.79\) for \((4,3.9)\). Such gaps enter the Gram-based variant, whereas Wedin-type residual formulations can be expressed using singular-value rather than squared-singular-value separations.

### 6. Sparse projection and CUSUM

Given a high-dimensional time series \(X_t\in\mathbb R^p\) whose mean shifts by \(\Delta\in\mathbb R^p\) at a change point, a unit projection \(v\) produces a scalar mean jump \(v^T\Delta\). The maximal absolute jump over unit \(v\) is \(\|\Delta\|_2\), attained by \(v=\pm\Delta/\|\Delta\|_2\), from Cauchy–Schwarz. If \(\Delta=(2,2,0,\ldots,0)^T\), the direction \((1,1,0,\ldots,0)^T/\sqrt2\) yields a jump \(2\sqrt2\), while \(e_3\) yields zero. Wang–Samworth (2018) estimate a useful sparse direction using a convex program on the CUSUM-transformed data and a singular vector, then perform univariate localization. This calculation is motivational; it is not a derivation of the paper's full convergence guarantees.

### 7. Sparse PCA combinatorial burden

For \(p\) coordinates and support size \(k\), exhaustive support search has \(\binom pk\) candidates. \(\binom{100}3=161700\); \(\binom{1000}{10}\) is astronomically large. Wang–Berthet–Samworth (2016) establish a conditional statistical–computational gap over specified model classes relying on a planted-clique hardness assumption. Neither the conditional lower bound nor the practical value of convex relaxations is a universal statement about every sparse PCA instance.

### 8. Iterative imputation geometry

Let \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\) be approximately low rank and let \(\Omega\) index observed entries. A conceptual alternating scheme uses a current rank-\(r\) estimate \(X^{(t)}\) to form
\[
Y^{(t)}=\mathcal P_\Omega(X)+\mathcal P_{\Omega^c}(X^{(t)}),\qquad
X^{(t+1)}=\operatorname{rank}_r(Y^{(t)}),
\]
where \(\operatorname{rank}_r\) denotes the truncated SVD. This simplified schematic explains projection and imputation; it is **not** the exact algorithmic definition of primePCA. Zhu–Wang–Samworth (2022) prove geometric error contraction under suitable assumptions in a noiseless regime, with nuanced treatment of heterogeneous missingness and incoherence.
