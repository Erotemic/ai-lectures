# 049000 — Johannes Kepler: Why would a planet abandon the perfect circle?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** mathematical astronomy, empirical error, ellipses, and a life amid confessional conflict. **Background:** circles, ratios, area, and the meaning of a speed.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine that a planet is supposed to follow a perfect circle. You use that idea, along with many carefully adjusted smaller circles, to predict where the planet will appear in the night sky. For a while the predictions look good. But for Mars, after painstaking calculation, a small disagreement remains. The difference is eight minutes of arc: eight sixtieths of a degree, only a fraction of the apparent width of a little finger at arm's length. Would you dismiss it as measurement noise, or would you reconsider the shape of the heavens?

Johannes Kepler chose not to dismiss the discrepancy. He believed the observations of Tycho Brahe were good enough to make those eight minutes significant. What followed was not the instant discovery of an ellipse. It was years of competing models, computation, geometrical ingenuity and a willingness to discard cherished pictures. Kepler's eventual laws of planetary motion changed astronomy, although they did not by themselves provide Newton's later explanation of gravity.

Kepler was born in fifteen seventy-one in Weil der Stadt, in the territory of the Holy Roman Empire. His family circumstances were often unstable. His father, Heinrich, served as a soldier; his mother, Katharina, came from a family whose social and economic position offered limited security. Kepler endured childhood illnesses and impaired eyesight, yet he became an exceptionally persistent calculator and a reader of mathematics. His story is not about a young observer who had the world's best telescope. Much of his decisive work used numerical observations made by others and the geometrical questions they raised.

He trained within a Lutheran educational world and studied at Tübingen, where the mathematician Michael Mästlin introduced him to Copernican astronomy. Religious belief was not an optional decorative layer of Kepler's thinking. He sought mathematical order because he believed the universe possessed a rational structure, and he hoped that astronomy could reveal something of it. His first major book proposed an elaborate arrangement of planetary distances using nested regular solids. That particular model did not survive. It matters because it shows Kepler beginning with an ambitious mathematical picture that the observations would force him to revise.

His working life was repeatedly redirected by religious politics. He taught mathematics in Graz, then lost security there amid pressures on Protestants in Catholic-ruled territories. Eventually he joined the household of the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe near Prague. Tycho had amassed some of the most precise pre-telescopic planetary observations in Europe. The relationship between the two men was difficult: Tycho guarded access to observational material, while Kepler wanted the freedom to test large theoretical ideas. Yet their skills were complementary. Tycho's decades of measurements and Kepler's capacity to build mathematical models ultimately mattered together.

Tycho died in sixteen hundred one. Kepler became imperial mathematician to Rudolf the Second and took up the challenge of Mars. He inherited a task that seemed narrow: reconcile observed positions with a geometrical description of a planet's path. But before asking what curve Mars traces, we must distinguish two questions. Where is the planet? And how quickly does it move along that path? A circle can answer the first question only if we supply a rule for the timing. Likewise, knowing the shape of an ellipse does not alone tell us where a planet will be next Tuesday.

To see why timing matters, consider walking around a circular track. Two people follow exactly the same geometric curve. One walks steadily; the other stops at the far side and then hurries to catch up. If you know the track but not their speed patterns, you cannot predict their positions after a minute. The ancient and early modern astronomical systems therefore carried complicated mechanisms for both geometry and time. Kepler's achievement included a new relationship between them.

In his search for Mars's path, he tried geometrical models derived from circular motion and from ideas about the Sun influencing planets. He found approaches that could closely resemble the data, yet refused to treat the remaining disagreement as irrelevant. His later writings make the celebrated eight-minute discrepancy part of an argument that led him beyond an established astronomical framework. The number was small, but its implication was large: the model could be more elegant than the world it described and still be wrong.

Kepler's first two planetary laws were presented in his New Astronomy, published in sixteen hundred nine. The first says that a planet travels on an ellipse, with the Sun at one of the ellipse's two foci. A focus is not the center. Picture an ellipse as a stretched circle. It has a long axis and a short axis. Along its long direction lie two special interior points, the foci. For a circular orbit the foci coincide at the center; for a noncircular ellipse they separate. The Sun occupies one focus, so the planet's distance from the Sun changes as it travels.

We can make that concrete with numbers. Let the distance from the center of an ellipse to one end of its long axis be ten units. Let the distance from the center to a focus be two units. Then, along that axis, the nearer end lies eight units from the focus, and the farther end lies twelve units away. That is a ratio of three to two between the far and near distances. The example is exaggerated compared with Earth's actual orbit, which is nearly circular. Its point is that an ellipse with a focus at the Sun naturally gives both a nearest point and a farthest point.

Another way to look at the ellipse is through eccentricity, a number measuring how far its foci lie from the center relative to the long-axis radius. An eccentricity of zero describes a circle. Values approaching one describe increasingly elongated ellipses. If our semimajor axis is ten units and eccentricity is one fifth, the focus lies two units from the center, giving the eight-unit and twelve-unit distances we just calculated. This is not a new kind of planetary force; it is a compact description of the shape. A planet can therefore follow a closed path while changing its distance from the Sun, something a Sun-centered circle cannot express.

It is useful to be precise about the difference between the center and the focus because diagrams can conceal it. A circle has no preferred long axis, and its center is naturally the reference point. A very gently stretched ellipse may still look almost circular. If the Sun sits at a focus displaced only slightly from the center, casual inspection of a picture will not settle the matter. An accurate model must be tested against where a planet is measured to appear, at many times and from different positions of Earth. That is exactly why Tycho's observations, rather than Kepler's initial taste in geometry, drove the revision.

The second law concerns time. Draw an imaginary line from the Sun to the moving planet. During a short interval, that line sweeps out a narrow region. Kepler's law says equal time intervals sweep out equal areas. When the planet is close to the Sun, the line is shorter, so it has to swing through a larger angle in the same time to enclose the same area. When the planet is farther away, the longer line can sweep the same area with a smaller angular change. Thus the planet moves faster in its orbit near the Sun and more slowly far away.

This is easier to remember with a physical analogy, though Kepler did not possess our later mechanics. Suppose a person carries a long broom from one point while walking around it, always tracing a narrow triangular patch of ground in one second. If the broom is short, the person must turn it faster to cover the same patch. If it is longer, a smaller turn covers the patch. The area rule constrains the combination of distance and angular speed, not simply the length of the path traveled in a fixed interval.

Today we explain the area law using conservation of angular momentum under a central force. In modern notation the quantity distance squared multiplied by the rate at which the direction changes is constant for a particle acted on by a force aimed at a fixed center. But that is a later explanation. Kepler reasoned within his own physical and philosophical picture of forces and solar influence. It would be anachronistic to say he applied Newtonian gravitation, which was developed decades after Kepler died.

The third law came a decade later, in The Harmony of the World, published in sixteen nineteen. It relates a planet's orbital period, the time it takes to make one complete journey, to the size of its ellipse. The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the ellipse's semimajor axis. Semimajor axis means half the length of the long axis. The law allows us to compare two planets without solving every detail of their positions on every day.

Take Earth as a reference. Use one year as its period and one astronomical unit as its semimajor axis. Mars has a semimajor axis about one point five two astronomical units. Cube one point five two, and you get about three point five. Take the square root, and you get about one point eight eight. So the third law predicts a Martian year of roughly one point eight eight Earth years. That agrees with observation. The calculation does not require us to memorize the exact value of the gravitational constant, because the comparison is between bodies orbiting the same central mass in a good approximation.

Kepler's search for harmony was neither merely numerical nor identical with modern scientific method. He connected the geometry of the planets to musical intervals, proportional relationships and a theological interpretation of nature. Many of those connections did not become accepted physics. Still, the third law emerged from the search, and it became one of the clues Newton used when formulating gravitational theory. We can acknowledge the productive role of a framework without treating everything in that framework as correct.

Meanwhile Kepler had a life that could not be separated from war, family and institutional insecurity. He married Barbara Müller in fifteen ninety-seven; several of their children died young. Barbara died in sixteen eleven, and Kepler moved to Linz. He married Susanna Reuttinger in sixteen thirteen, and their household again grew. His positions demanded calendar making, horoscopes, official tables and correspondence as well as research. These were part of how a working mathematician secured an income in a period when astronomy was not a salaried research profession in the modern sense.

One of the gravest crises concerned his mother, Katharina, who was accused of witchcraft in a Württemberg legal proceeding. Her case unfolded over several years, not in a single heroic afternoon. Kepler undertook substantial legal and intellectual work on her behalf, drawing on the assistance of lawyers and university scholars. She was eventually released and not convicted. The surviving records show the frightening material consequences of religious accusation, social suspicion and judicial power. The case also reminds us that early modern scientific thinkers inhabited communities with laws and fears very different from ours. Kepler's work on planetary theory did not protect his family from those pressures.

He also struggled to publish astronomical tables based on Tycho's observations. Logarithms, recently introduced by John Napier, promised to reduce enormous computational labor by turning multiplication into addition. Kepler recognized their usefulness and worked to understand and produce his own tables. In sixteen twenty-seven he published the Rudolphine Tables. These were not a glamorous alternative to his planetary laws; they represented the painstaking business of making predictions that other astronomers could check. Their usefulness helped secure the new astronomy beyond theoretical argument.

The Thirty Years' War disrupted the regions where Kepler lived and worked. Financial promises were often unpaid. He moved in search of patronage and practical stability and died in sixteen thirty at Regensburg, while pursuing outstanding payments. A life described only through the three laws would omit the material conditions that determined what he could calculate, print and preserve.

There is another lesson in Kepler's success. Astronomical observations are not pure truths delivered without interpretation. They are made by people with instruments, procedures and uncertainties. Tycho's data were accurate enough to challenge Kepler's models because of sustained observational practice. Kepler's courage was not simply trusting a number. It was testing whether the discrepancy persisted across a serious model and whether alternative mathematical descriptions could account for it without arbitrary repairs.

Return once more to the little eight-minute disagreement. If Kepler had been satisfied with an approximate circular model, he might have produced predictions adequate for some purposes and moved on. Instead the difference became a reason to rethink what could count as a planetary path. That is why his laws belong in the history of mathematics as well as astronomy. An ellipse is a geometric object; an area rule is a relation between geometry and time; the period law relates measurements across distinct orbits. All three are simple to state. Their discovery required decades of work, the labor of multiple observers, and a life lived amid pressures that a formula does not reveal.

## Mathematical and historical companion (not spoken)

### Geometry of an elliptic orbit

For an ellipse with semimajor axis \(a\), eccentricity \(0\le e<1\), and the Sun at a focus:

\[
r_{\mathrm{peri}}=a(1-e),\qquad r_{\mathrm{aphe}}=a(1+e).
\]

For an idealized case \(a=10, e=0.2\), the distances are 8 and 12 units. The **orbital position in time** requires the area law as well as the ellipse geometry.

### Equal areas and angular momentum (modern explanation)

\[
\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac12 r^2\dot\theta=\mathrm{constant},
\]

for motion under a central force. Kepler arrived at the law centuries before this compact Newtonian interpretation.

### Period scaling

For two planets about the same dominant central mass, in the Keplerian approximation,

\[
\frac{T_1^2}{T_2^2}=\frac{a_1^3}{a_2^3}.
\]

With Earth at \(a=1\) AU and \(T=1\) year, Mars at \(a\approx1.524\) AU gives \(T\approx\sqrt{1.524^3}\approx1.881\) years.
