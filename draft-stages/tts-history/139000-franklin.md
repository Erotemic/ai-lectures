# 139000 — Rosalind Franklin: A life in structures, not a single photograph

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Background:** No prior molecular biology required; diffraction, DNA, and virus structure are explained without advanced crystallography.

Suppose that you know Rosalind Franklin from one photograph. It is not even a photograph of her face. It is an X-ray diffraction pattern, a black image crossed by pale, carefully spaced marks, known as Photograph 51. It helped reveal the structure of DNA and became the central exhibit in a disputed history of scientific credit. But consider the investigator who made it. She did not wander into a laboratory and take one lucky picture. She understood the physical problem, built and improved the apparatus, worked with a doctoral student, controlled water content in fragile samples, and used the patterns to reason about structures smaller than visible light could resolve. Before DNA she had become an expert on coal and carbon. After DNA she led important research on viruses. A life reduced to one photograph leaves out most of the science and much of the person.

Rosalind Elsie Franklin was born in London on 25 July 1920 to Ellis and Muriel Franklin. Her family belonged to a well-connected British Jewish community with strong expectations of education, public service, and responsibility to others. The family was not poor, and her opportunities should not be described as if they were. Yet there were very real institutional barriers for women who wanted to pursue science. Franklin attended St Paul's Girls' School, an academically demanding school that encouraged pupils to think beyond a conventional domestic future. She liked sport and travel and grew attached to mountains and hiking, pleasures that would accompany her demanding professional life.

Her family was also involved in social and refugee relief work. The decades of her youth were marked by increasing persecution of Jews across continental Europe and then war. The personal letters that survive place a young woman making choices about education and work in a larger family and political history. It would be careless to read later professional disagreements as a complete explanation of her character, but equally careless to imagine that her identity and the rules governing women's careers never affected the opportunities before her. The institutions into which she entered were more open to her talent than many earlier universities had been, yet not fully open on equal terms.

Franklin's friendships and recreation offer something else than sentimental color. Scientific work depends on a person's willingness to remain with uncertain problems for a long time. Her long walks and climbs, her pleasure in Paris, and her attachment to family show that she did not live as an impersonal calculating machine. In the archives there is a person who could enjoy a demanding holiday and then return to demanding laboratory work. We need not invent what she privately felt about each injustice to see a whole life in the correspondence.

The Second World War transformed the surroundings in which she trained. In 1938 she entered Newnham College, Cambridge, to study natural sciences, concentrating on chemistry. Women could study and pass Cambridge examinations, but the university did not yet award them degrees on the same terms as men. The difference was not merely ceremonial. It was a public reminder that accomplishment and formal membership were governed by different rules. She completed her studies in 1941 and entered research work, receiving a Cambridge doctorate in 1945. The young chemist did not begin with the chemistry of heredity. She began by investigating why different carbon materials behaved differently.

In 1942 she joined the British Coal Utilisation Research Association. Britain at war depended on fuel, and carbon materials had uses from industrial processes to filtering substances in respirators. Coal looks like one kind of black rock until you measure how gases move through it. Some carbon structures contain pores and passageways that admit small molecules but not larger ones. Franklin investigated the relationship between coal, heat, porosity, and the movement of gases. She used helium and other measurements to investigate the spaces within materials. The work helped explain how different coals and chars behaved, and it gave her an enduring scientific lesson: a structure that cannot be seen directly may be reconstructed from how matter passes through it.

Think of a box with several kinds of openings. You cannot look inside, but you can test how quickly different-sized beads enter. If small beads enter and large ones do not, you learn something about the openings. A carbon pore is not literally a neat tunnel with a door, and real gas sorption demands much more careful physical chemistry. Yet the reasoning is related. The behavior of a probe tells you about internal geometry. This is one reason the coal years should not be dismissed as practice before her 'real' discovery. They were first-rate research on complex materials and part of the training that later made her formidable in diffraction analysis.

Carbon also provided a striking example of classification through structure. When some carbonaceous materials are heated sufficiently, their disordered arrangements can reorganize toward graphite, with stacked layers. Other carbons resist that transformation and remain non-graphitizing, with a more constrained network. Franklin helped distinguish these behaviors experimentally. The categories were not arbitrary labels attached to the raw material's source. They came from observing how atomic organization changed under treatment. Her later work on DNA repeated the intellectual move: a sample's response to a controlled change in humidity revealed something about how its molecular structure could vary.

The wartime application to filtering gases was real, but that does not mean every result had to be justified by immediate military utility. She was doing physical chemistry with broad implications for porous materials. Technical competence made other directions possible. A scientific career can move between domains because a method that detects an otherwise invisible structure is useful in more than one field.

After the war Franklin found a scientific home in Paris. From 1947 to 1950, extending into the transition back to Britain in 1951, she worked in a French government chemistry laboratory with Jacques Mering. She learned X-ray diffraction techniques and studied carbons that could or could not become graphite when heated. She developed a professional circle in which she was a respected specialist rather than an apprentice waiting for biology to arrive. She was fluent in French and enjoyed Parisian friendships and culture. Family letters and colleagues' accounts make it impossible to sustain the flattened portrait of a person who had no life except resentment and solitary work. She liked company, food, travel, jokes, and demanding outdoor walks. She could also be exacting, sharp, and impatient with careless argument. None of those traits explains her experimental achievements by itself.

X-ray diffraction is a way to learn about repetition without seeing individual atoms. An X-ray beam strikes matter. The wave is scattered by electrons in atoms, and waves scattered from different positions can reinforce or cancel one another. A repeating three-dimensional arrangement produces structured bright and dark regions on a detector. The image is not a snapshot of the molecule's outline. It is a coded record of spatial relationships. Extracting a molecular structure requires preparation, mathematical analysis, chemical constraints, and sometimes a model tested against the pattern.

Why does repetition make a pattern? Imagine many people clapping steadily at evenly spaced seats in a hall. At some listening positions, the sounds reach you in step and reinforce each other. Elsewhere they interfere. X-rays are waves, not claps, but the principle that spatial separation affects reinforcement and cancellation carries over. In a crystalline solid the pattern may contain many sharp spots. In fibers made of roughly parallel long molecules, the geometry is more complicated and particular symmetries can produce characteristic streaks and crosses. The experimenter must infer structure from the pattern rather than mistake the image for a photograph of the structure itself.

Even a mathematically sophisticated investigator cannot always work backward uniquely from an intensity pattern. The detector records how strongly waves reinforce at different angles but not all the phase information needed to place scattering centers directly. In crystallography this is known as the phase problem. Different arrangements can sometimes reproduce related aspects of an intensity pattern, and extra physical or chemical constraints are needed. Franklin studied Patterson methods, which use the measured intensities to infer information about distances among scattering centers. That was one attraction of the highly ordered A form: its richer spot pattern might support a more detailed calculation. Model builders might instead propose a structure and check whether it agreed with known spacings and chemical geometry. Both strategies were legitimate, and the solution ultimately drew on complementary strengths.

This is why the familiar opposition between a plodding data collector and imaginative model makers is misleading. Selecting the right sample state, building stable humidity control, deciding which mathematical reconstruction is trustworthy, and rejecting models that violate chemical constraints all demand imagination. Conversely, a good model is not a substitute for empirical evidence. The discovery depended on bringing the two into contact.

In 1951 Franklin accepted a position at King's College London in a biophysics research program directed by John Randall. The institutional arrangements were badly managed. Franklin understood that she was being assigned independent X-ray work on DNA fibers alongside the graduate student Raymond Gosling. Maurice Wilkins had already been working with DNA material and did not receive a sufficiently clear, shared account of the new division of responsibilities. The resulting conflict cannot be explained purely by incompatible personalities; it was partly an organizational failure in a competitive institution. Franklin's experiences as a woman scientist influenced the environment, but a biography that tells only of insult and exclusion can still erase her intellectual agency.

The DNA itself was difficult material. It could be drawn into fibers containing many oriented molecules, but its diffraction pattern depended strongly on water content. Franklin and Gosling learned to control humidity with unusual care and established that their samples could take different structural forms. They called them A and B. Drier samples often produced a more complicated, more ordered pattern suitable for detailed mathematical analysis; more hydrated samples produced the B-form pattern that is now so familiar. The distinction was not decorative. A good scientist cannot infer one unique structure from a pattern without knowing whether a change in conditions has altered the material being measured.

By May 1952 their photographic series contained the famous Photograph 51. Its cross-like marks were powerful evidence of helical structure. More recent study of Franklin's notebooks has made another image crucial to the story: Photograph 49. In a 2026 analysis, historian Alistair Sponsel and crystallographer Brian Sutton argue that Franklin had already extracted important helical dimensions from 49, and made 51 deliberately as a better-centered and publishable image. That is a scholarly reconstruction from the surviving record, not something we should claim to have learned by peering into her private thoughts. It directly challenges the old claim that she obtained the clearer photograph without appreciating what it meant.

Let us read the evidence in ordinary language. The B-form pattern indicates an axial repeat of about thirty-four angstroms. An angstrom is one ten-billionth of a meter, so thirty-four angstroms is 3.4 nanometers. Another regular distance is about 3.4 angstroms between stacked bases. Divide thirty-four by 3.4 and you get ten. That is the approximate number of stacked base-pair levels in one turn of ordinary B-form DNA. This is not enough by itself to reveal which bases pair, how many chains there are, or exactly how the phosphate backbone is arranged. It is a powerful geometric constraint that rules out many imagined models.

Franklin's work also indicated that the phosphate groups belonged on the exterior of the structure, where water could interact with them. These details were not raw visual gifts handed out by nature. They came from refined specimens, diffraction physics, measurement, and a chemist's judgment about where material belonged. She continued the more difficult analysis of the A form because it promised additional structural information. Treating that choice as proof she 'failed to see a helix' is a misunderstanding of an investigator who was attempting to determine both forms rigorously.

At Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick were building physical models rather than conducting their own DNA fiber diffraction program. Their work drew on ideas from chemistry, molecular bonding, and multiple sources of experimental evidence. Erwin Chargaff's findings about base proportions and the chemical geometry of base pairing helped constrain possible structures. Wilkins and his colleagues at King's supplied important diffraction evidence. Franklin and Gosling's observations mattered enormously. In early 1953 Wilkins showed Watson Photograph 51 without Franklin's knowledge. Other experimental findings reached Cambridge through professional channels, including a report that Crick examined. The conditions of communication and credit were poor, but the story is more complex than a single theft of a photograph or a scientist who held all the facts but refused to interpret them.

The crucial model had two complementary strands. If the paired bases fit in a consistent way, one strand's sequence could specify the other. This made the double helix more than an object of elegant geometry. It suggested a way genetic information might be copied. Watson and Crick published their model in *Nature* on 25 April 1953. In that same issue Franklin and Gosling published their X-ray evidence, as did Wilkins and colleagues. A reader encountering the papers in print might easily suppose that experimentalists had simply confirmed a finished theory. The actual sequence of communication reveals that experiments were part of constructing the model in the first place.

The credit question is legitimate. Wilkins showed a photograph to a rival group without Franklin's awareness; Watson's later memoir portrayed her in a way historians have found deeply misleading. The recognition attached to the famous model did not reflect the full experimental and intellectual collaboration. Yet a second distortion can arise when the correction says that Franklin did everything and the Cambridge researchers merely copied an answer. Historians Matthew Cobb and Nathaniel Comfort, working from archival material, have argued for restoring Franklin as an equal participant in the collective discovery rather than a person incapable of interpretation. We can acknowledge unfairness without replacing one caricature with another.

By the time the DNA papers appeared, Franklin was moving to Birkbeck College in London to work with J. D. Bernal. There she entered a more congenial scientific environment and organized a research group investigating viruses. The tobacco mosaic virus was a rod-shaped infectious particle that offered a challenging problem in structural analysis. Its protein coat and RNA could be studied through diffraction and other methods, and Franklin's group advanced knowledge of how its components were arranged. She collaborated with younger researchers including Aaron Klug and was involved in work extending toward other viruses, including the poliovirus. This was neither a consolation prize nor a minor appendix to DNA. Structural virology was developing into a field, and Franklin was helping create it.

Tobacco mosaic virus offered a particularly useful test. It is not an amorphous blob: many protein subunits are arranged in a regular helical architecture around genetic material. Diffraction from oriented virus preparations could constrain how those subunits repeated and where the nucleic acid lay relative to them. Franklin's team developed evidence that the RNA belonged inside the protein coat, not on its outside. A structural hypothesis here had consequences for how a virus particle could be assembled from repeated components. The work demanded team science: sample preparation, diffraction, mathematical interpretation, and the knowledge of virologists and chemists had to meet.

It is also worth remembering how quickly biology was changing. In the early 1950s, establishing the structure of one nucleic acid or one virus did not automatically explain how proteins were encoded, how a virus multiplied, or how a cell operated. These became linked problems of molecular biology through decades of additional research. Franklin's contributions belong to that broad construction of methods and questions. DNA became the famous image, but the later virus investigations illustrate a rapidly developing experimental program of her own.

It is important to place her later work beside her earlier carbon studies. In all three areas she asked how a complex object is organized when the internal arrangement cannot be watched directly. With coal, probes and diffraction exposed the properties of pores and carbon layers. With DNA, humidity control and fiber patterns constrained a molecular helix. With viruses, carefully prepared specimens and diffraction data illuminated ordered assemblies of protein and nucleic acid. The subjects changed, but the craft and the habit of extracting geometry from indirect evidence stayed with her.

Her research life was cut short. Diagnosed with ovarian cancer in 1956, she continued working through treatment and periods of illness. She died in London on 20 March 1958, aged thirty-seven. Accounts that treat the illness as a simple, proven consequence of her X-ray experiments exceed the evidence. There was radiation exposure in crystallographic work at the time, but assigning an individual's cancer to one specific occupational cause is not justified by the surviving biographical record. Her colleagues carried forward the virus program and remembered a skilled, determined research leader.

In 1962 Watson, Crick, and Wilkins received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning nucleic-acid structure and information transfer. Franklin had died four years earlier. Nobel awards cannot be used as a straightforward retrospective test of who supplied the indispensable ideas. Nor can one prove that a living Franklin would have received one of the three available places. The historical question is not a speculative prize ballot. It is what the documents show she discovered, reasoned, communicated, and enabled others to do.

If you return to Photograph 51 now, try to see neither a miracle nor a stolen trophy. See a carefully prepared diffraction experiment made by Franklin and Gosling; a scientific career that made the experiment possible; colleagues whose knowledge crossed institutional lines; and an inquiry shaped by both cooperation and failures of recognition. Then look beyond it, toward charcoal pores and the repeating architecture of a plant virus. The most fitting tribute to Rosalind Franklin is to restore her to the full scale of the work she actually performed.

## Non-spoken companion: diffraction and the archival debate

- **A diffraction pattern is not a direct image of a molecule.** For oriented DNA fibers, features of the pattern constrain helical periodicity and spacing. B-form approximations: axial repeat 34 Å; vertical base stacking 3.4 Å; about ten base-pair levels per turn. The complete double-stranded model needs additional chemical and geometric constraints.
- **Photograph 49 and Photograph 51:** Sponsel and Sutton's open-access historical reconstruction, published 5 October 2026, argues that Franklin performed important measurements on 49 and intentionally created 51 as a clearer record of the same sample. State this as a scholarly archival finding; it modifies simplified older accounts.
- **Sharing and credit:** Wilkins showed Watson Photograph 51 without Franklin's awareness; professional and supervisory arrangements at King's were confused. The 1953 *Nature* papers included Franklin and Gosling's research, but presentation order does not establish the causal order in which the model was developed. Avoid both 'she did not understand her data' and 'the entire double helix was simply stolen'.
- **Scientific career:** Coal, graphite, DNA, tobacco mosaic virus, and further virus studies were connected by methods of structure inference. Franklin led research at Birkbeck from 1953 onward.
- **Health and prizes:** Do not assert a documented causal link between occupational X-rays and her cancer. Nobel speculation is counterfactual; the award occurred in 1962 after her death.
