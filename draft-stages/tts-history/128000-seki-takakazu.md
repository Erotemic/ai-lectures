# 128000 — Seki Takakazu: Mathematics in Edo, without a European mirror

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Edo-period institutions, Chinese and Japanese mathematical texts, counting rods and algebraic elimination, the 1674 challenge book, teacher–student collaboration, determinants, sums of powers, posthumous publication, and the problem of biographical evidence. **Background:** Elementary equations and sums are explained.

In 1712, in Edo, the city now called Tokyo, a mathematical book appeared after the death of the man whose work it presented. Among its calculations were formulas for sums of consecutive powers of integers, using numbers that modern mathematicians call Bernoulli numbers. The year matters. In Basel, Switzerland, the work of Jakob Bernoulli on similar numbers was published in 1713, also after his death. The two mathematical worlds were far apart. There is no evidence that one man learned these calculations from the other. Both had been led, by problems about algebra and sums, toward mathematical structures that now carry one man's name.

The Japanese mathematician was Seki Takakazu. His name is often included in a list of people who supposedly discovered European mathematics before Europeans did. That way of celebrating him is misleading. It makes European achievement the standard against which a Japanese life must be measured. Seki was not waiting to become Newton or Leibniz. He worked within a different mathematical language, inherited different texts, belonged to different institutions, and answered different intellectual challenges. His story is richer when we learn what he was actually trying to do.

We need to begin with a gap in the record. Seki's year of birth is not securely established. Some conventional biographies give 1642, occasionally with a specific birthplace, but the National Diet Library of Japan treats his birth year as unknown, and specialist historical work places plausible estimates in the early 1640s. For a biographical narrative that uncertainty is not a minor footnote. It means we should not build an elaborate childhood scene around one precise birthday. We know a great deal more about certain books and his later official duties than about the private daily life of the boy who became the most famous name in early Japanese mathematics.

The world in which he grew up was governed by the Tokugawa shogunate. After decades of war in the previous century, Japan's ruling order sought stability through a hierarchy of political relationships, domains, hereditary positions and regulation of movement. Edo was a center of government and urban culture. Merchants, officials, craftspeople and teachers needed arithmetic for their work, and mathematics also became a field of recreation, competition and reputation. It would be wrong to imagine that everyone in Edo had access to the same instruction. Opportunities depended on social standing, contacts, books, leisure and the ability to find teachers. But mathematics was not confined to a university system resembling the European one. There was no direct equivalent of a seventeenth-century Paris academy organizing Seki's career.

He came from a samurai family and was adopted into the Seki household. Adoption had practical and social functions in the warrior class: it could continue a household and its hereditary position. Seki's professional life followed the obligations of that position. He served the Tokugawa household associated with the Kōfu domain, working in administration and accounting. The surviving descriptions emphasize financial responsibilities rather than a full-time salary for mathematical research. Later his master's political rise brought Seki into the orbit of the shogun's court at Edo. A man remembered today for algebra was, through much of his career, also a working official managing the material arrangements of government.

There is a danger in romanticizing that double life. It does not follow that Seki secretly did calculations while his superiors were not looking, or that administrative arithmetic automatically produced his most abstract results. We have insufficient evidence for such scenes. What we can say is that the institutions supporting his mathematics were not identical to those supporting Newton or Huygens. He developed his methods through access to books, work on difficult problems, contact with other mathematical enthusiasts, and collaboration with disciples. Those conditions shaped the form in which his ideas survived.

One especially important inheritance came from China. Mathematical texts traveled to Japan through multiple historical routes, carrying computational techniques, notation, numerical problems and methods of arranging coefficients. A book could have several generations of life: written in one region, copied in another, studied by a teacher, corrected by a student, and reworked for a new set of problems. Japan's seventeenth-century restrictions on overseas contact did not erase earlier centuries of exchange, nor did they prevent all contact with Chinese and Dutch traders. But they did shape which books were available. Seki's mathematical reading was strongly connected to Chinese works circulating within Japan.

Among the books he studied were writings associated with Yang Hui and Zhu Shijie. Yang Hui had worked in thirteenth-century China on numerical methods and arrangements of coefficients. Zhu Shijie's mathematical books, published at the end of the thirteenth and beginning of the fourteenth centuries, used algebraic techniques for complex problems involving unknown quantities. Seki copied a Yang Hui text in 1661, according to a specialist account, correcting some errors as he did so. This was more than passive preservation. Copying and checking mathematics is itself a way of finding out what the author meant, where a step fails, and how a method might be extended.

The devices known as counting rods helped organize calculation. Imagine a board on which numbers are represented by place-value arrangements of rods, with distinct signs or conventions for positive and negative quantities. Instead of writing an algebraic expression in a modern row of letters and symbols, one could lay out coefficients and manipulate their relationships. Chinese mathematical traditions had developed powerful techniques using such representations. Those who know modern polynomial arithmetic may recognize the underlying operations, but the surface appearance and methods of explanation were different. A historian should not translate every rod configuration into modern algebra and then pretend the old notation had no significance of its own.

One important tool was a method for representing a polynomial equation in one unknown, called tengenjutsu in the Japanese tradition, inherited from the Chinese tian yuan technique. To appreciate the problem, begin with the statement that a certain number, multiplied by itself, then increased by three times the number, equals ten. In modern notation we would write x squared plus three x equals ten. We can move every term to one side, giving x squared plus three x minus ten equals zero. But the result is not tied to the letter x. The real mathematical content is an organized set of coefficients that can be manipulated to find possible values of the unknown.

There is a familiar solution: x can be two, because four plus six is ten. The other root is negative five, because twenty-five minus fifteen is ten. A representation that handles both positive and negative quantities and can carry out systematic polynomial operations is already substantial. It allows one to treat the equation as an object rather than starting over with a separate trick for every problem. Seki's work on representing algebraic expressions and eliminating unknowns extended that possibility within the existing tradition.

The next problem is harder. Imagine two quantities, x and y, constrained by two relations. Their sum is seven, and their product is twelve. If you know one of them, the other is seven minus that number. Substitute that relationship into the product: x times seven minus x equals twelve. Rearranged, the equation says x squared minus seven x plus twelve equals zero. The possibilities are three and four. A problem with two unknowns has been turned into a polynomial equation with one unknown by elimination. This small calculation captures the conceptual need behind much more elaborate systems: find a controlled way to remove unknown quantities without losing the relationships that matter.

Elimination is a method, not a single trick. For larger algebraic systems, a person needs repeatable transformations and notation capable of recording their intermediate results. Seki's development of what the National Diet Library calls boshoho, a more flexible way to represent algebraic expressions, was important for just this reason. The method made multiple unknown quantities and their relations easier to express. It helped extend inherited techniques beyond situations in which a single unknown could be handled naturally. A modern reader may hear echoes of resultants, symbolic algebra and matrix methods. Those echoes are illuminating as comparisons, but they are not evidence that Seki already possessed all the modern concepts.

Japanese mathematics also had a culture of challenge problems. In the early 1670s Kazuyuki Sawaguchi published a book ending with difficult questions. The questions were not private homework graded by an instructor; they were invitations for others to demonstrate methods and skill. Seki's *Hatsubi sanpō*, published in 1674, supplied solutions to fifteen such problems. This was the one mathematical book that Seki himself published during his lifetime, according to the National Diet Library's account. Its title is often translated in terms of revealing hidden mathematical meaning or principles. The text brought him a public reputation, yet it also created a dispute over what exactly had been shown.

Seki supplied answers and significant explanation but withheld detailed working procedures for some of the more difficult solutions. We know this not merely from later admiration but from the book's own preface and the response it produced. His reasons included a desire not to obscure the results with an enormous account of procedures, while his followers encouraged him to make the work available. The missing methods became controversial. Other mathematicians questioned whether the published answers were fully justified. That matters because a numerical answer and a reproducible argument are not the same product. A challenge-solver may know a result is correct; a reader still needs to know how to reach it.

This dispute brought one of Seki's most important students into the story. Takebe Katahiro, together with his brother and other collaborators, became essential to preserving, explaining and developing the mathematical school associated with Seki. Takebe published a commentary in 1685 that supplied detailed operations for the challenge book. In that sense the students did not merely hand down a master's completed ideas. They made the ideas public in a usable form and, in the process, contributed mathematical work of their own. The boundaries of attribution are sometimes difficult to draw because later editions and school texts were produced through collective labor.

Seki also wrote about subjects now recognized as aspects of determinants. A determinant is a number associated with a square array of coefficients. For two equations in two unknowns, the determinant tells us something about whether the equations can have a unique solution. Consider the equations two x plus y equals five, and x plus two y equals four. Their coefficient array has two and one in the first row, one and two in the second. Multiply the first diagonal entries, two times two, and subtract the cross product, one times one. The result is three. Because it is not zero, the two equations have a unique solution. That solution is x equals two and y equals one.

Now change the second equation so it is exactly twice the first: four x plus two y equals ten. The determinant of the new coefficient array is zero. The equations no longer identify one single point. They describe the same line, and infinitely many pairs satisfy both. If the right-hand side of the second equation were eleven instead, the same dependent coefficients would produce inconsistent conditions. A zero determinant does not by itself tell us whether we have infinitely many solutions or none; it says the coefficient array is not invertible. This distinction is a useful modern way to understand why determinant-like calculations matter.

Historical descriptions often say that Seki discovered determinants in 1683 before Leibniz's independent work. That can communicate priority, but it can also compress the story too aggressively. Techniques for solving linear equations, eliminating variables and combining coefficients existed in different traditions long before the word determinant acquired its modern meaning. Seki developed expressions and operations that belong to the prehistory of determinant theory. The precise relationship between those methods and later general determinants requires attention to surviving manuscripts, notation and the goals of each calculation. Claiming that he invented the entire modern theory of matrices would turn an impressive historical achievement into an inaccurate slogan.

For Seki, algebra did not end at solving small systems. He explored polynomial equations, numerical approximations, and geometrical calculations, including the areas of regular polygons. Why would areas require algebra? Suppose you know the circumference of a regular polygon but not the radius of its circumscribed circle, or you want a polygon with so many sides that a direct construction becomes tedious. One may derive relations involving chords, angles and lengths, then solve a polynomial or approximate an unknown. This connects symbolic manipulation to the very old ambition of computing the circle. A mathematician who can represent and transform equations can handle geometrical problems that would be unwieldy as isolated diagrams.

One can approach the circle through inscribed regular polygons. Start with a square inside a circle. Its perimeter is too short to equal the circumference, because straight chords cut inside the arcs. Double the number of sides; the perimeter gets closer. Continue doubling, and a systematic sequence of approximations emerges. In another direction, circumscribed polygons yield perimeters that exceed the circumference. A sufficiently careful calculation can squeeze the circumference between two numerical bounds. This is a method with antecedents in several mathematical cultures; Seki's circle-related work belongs to a long history of approximation, not to a claim of inventing pi.

The connection between algebra and numerical approximation becomes clearer when a difficult polynomial has no convenient factorization. Suppose you want a positive root of x squared equals two. One can begin with one, which is too small, and two, which is too large. Testing one point four gives a square of one point nine six; testing one point five gives two point two five. The true answer is between them. Methods that systematically refine such approximations become powerful for equations of higher degree, when the shape of the curve is difficult to draw and no simple explicit root is available. Seki and other Japanese mathematicians developed algorithms in this setting. They should be compared carefully with European root-finding techniques rather than automatically identified with a later named algorithm.

Perhaps the most striking connection across continents is the theory of sums of powers. If you add the first n positive integers, you can arrange the terms in pairs: first plus last, second plus second-to-last, and so on. Each pair sums to n plus one. The total is n times n plus one divided by two. For the first ten positive integers, that is ten times eleven divided by two, or fifty-five. We have converted a growing list of additions into a formula that works for every positive integer n.

Now sum the squares instead. One squared plus two squared plus three squared, continuing to n squared, equals n times n plus one times twice n plus one, divided by six. For n equal to three, this gives three times four times seven divided by six, or fourteen, matching one plus four plus nine. The formula looks less obvious than the one for ordinary sums. Yet it has a regular form, a polynomial in n of degree three. Summing cubes gives the square of the sum of the first n integers. These relationships are not isolated coincidences. There is a pattern connecting the sum of kth powers to a polynomial in n of degree k plus one.

Bernoulli numbers provide an organized way of describing that pattern. They appear as coefficients in general formulas for the sums, though conventions for the sign of the first Bernoulli number differ. The mathematical content cannot be reduced to a name. Seki's calculation concerned the relationships among powers, binomial coefficients and sums. His posthumously published tables contain the numerical ingredients that we now recognize as Bernoulli numbers. His work was one of the genuinely independent historical paths to these coefficients. It was not a copy of Jakob Bernoulli's manuscript, and there is no good reason to treat the Japanese calculation as merely a belated imitation of a European discovery.

There is an attractive but mistaken version of this story in which two men, one in Basel and one in Edo, race invisibly toward a single global finish line. The calendar reveals that Seki's results were printed one year before Jakob Bernoulli's; therefore, the story concludes, the mathematical object should be renamed and the contest settled. But Bernoulli's *Ars Conjectandi* and Seki's *Katsuyō sanpō* were both posthumous publications. Neither printer's date reveals the full moment at which its author achieved the underlying result. They arose from different intellectual surroundings, and priority in one calculation does not settle broader questions of method or influence. It is more revealing to understand why similar mathematical structures arose in distinct mathematical traditions.

A simple identity helps explain how a mathematician might discover the sums. Take consecutive cubes: the cube of k plus one minus the cube of k. Expand the first term, and almost everything involves powers of k that we may already know how to sum. When you add this difference from k equal to one through n, the intermediate cubes cancel, leaving the cube of n plus one minus one. The same argument can be repeated at higher degrees. It creates a recurrence: knowledge of lower sums helps you find the next one. One does not need a shared language or international correspondence to be led from polynomial expansion to the coefficients that later acquired Bernoulli's name.

Seki's own route is not fully recoverable from the published tables. The historian Tomoko Kitagawa emphasizes how difficult it can be to tell exactly how he calculated the coefficients, because methods were sometimes omitted or transformed in later editions. We can reconstruct plausible mathematics and study surviving pages, but reconstruction is not an eyewitness account. That is a recurring feature of historical mathematics. A modern derivation that reproduces an old answer proves the answer is mathematically coherent. It does not prove the old author used the modern derivation.

The issue of transmission complicates biography too. Seki's works circulated in manuscripts and in texts prepared by disciples. His school developed an identity after his death, when followers increasingly invoked his name as a source of authority. Scholars such as Takebe had their own mathematical results, including sophisticated work on approximations. If a modern lecture credits every achievement of the later school to Seki personally, it erases the people who preserved and extended the mathematics. Conversely, refusing to attribute anything to Seki because his manuscripts passed through other hands would ignore a meaningful body of evidence about his own work.

Japanese mathematical education also developed through private teaching groups and, in some settings, mathematical problems displayed on wooden tablets, known as sangaku, at shrines and temples. The survival of such tablets and the accounts of competing schools help historians reconstruct a mathematical public that was neither identical to a university classroom nor wholly private. Mathematical activity crossed occupational divisions: people from varied social positions could study, teach and display problems, subject to the constraints of their time. The exact origin and spread of every practice is not something that can be assigned to one celebrated mathematician. Seki participated in a broader culture that continued to change through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

What of his political life? The record associates him with the Kōfu branch of the Tokugawa house. His master, Tokugawa Ienobu, was designated to succeed the reigning shogun and would become the sixth shogun after Seki's death. Seki's official status rose accordingly, and the mathematics of accounting would have been useful in a government that organized money, goods and retainers. But his late-life transition did not make him the hidden author of shogunate policy. A careful life distinguishes the official appointments we can document from political decisions for which we have no evidence of his role.

Nor can we fill the personal record by copying stories common to many biographies of prodigies. Some retellings say that a household servant recognized his mathematical gifts when he was nine years old. Such anecdotes appear in secondary biographical accounts, but they do not have the same evidentiary status as a dated printed book or an official administrative record. The reliable basis of this portrait is his public activity, the writings that survive, the work of his students, and the material history of manuscripts. Where his private motives, daily schedule or childhood emotions are unknown, the honest sentence is that they are unknown.

This care is especially important with the word isolated. Tokugawa restrictions limited contact with Europe; they did not place Japanese mathematicians outside history. Chinese texts were available and actively studied, and the mathematical culture of Japan had its own lines of transmission. On the other hand, there is no evidence of a direct route by which Seki's Bernoulli-number calculations reached the Bernoulli family in Basel. Saying both things at once avoids a false choice between total isolation and a world in which every parallel discovery must have passed along an invisible trade route.

Seki died in Edo in 1708. His work did not end there, because disciples helped edit and publish it. The 1712 *Katsuyō sanpō* brought forward results that a European mathematician could have recognized as closely related to developments in European algebra. Later Japanese writers called him a mathematical sage, and schools identified themselves with his lineage. His name became a symbol for the technical sophistication of wasan, the Japanese mathematical tradition of the Edo period. But symbols of a tradition can become simpler than the people they commemorate. Our task is to retain the historical complexity even while admiring the accomplishment.

It is tempting to end with a medal-counting conclusion: Seki discovered this many things before Europe, therefore he belongs beside Newton. We can do better. He inherited Chinese algebra, modified its tools, responded to mathematical challenges, argued about methods, collaborated with students, and left behind work that was edited into a legacy after his death. A reader in Edo could find a question difficult without knowing that a reader in Basel was facing a related problem. The mathematical truth could be the same while the paths to it were not.

The lesson for the history of mathematics is not only that important results were obtained in Japan. It is that mathematical problems, not continents or national canons, should guide our comparisons. A method for expressing several unknowns may be transformative in one notation even if it resembles a technique in another. A table of sums may emerge through a tradition of algebraic elimination without any direct encounter with European calculus. And the work of understanding a mathematician may require us to say more about his books, friends and institutions than we can say about his childhood. Seki Takakazu deserves a biography that accepts that bargain. His mathematics is substantial enough without filling the silence in the record with inventions.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and geographic cautions

- **c. 1640–1645, Japan:** Plausible estimated birth interval; exact date and birthplace remain disputed. Do not make the commonly repeated 1642 or Fujioka claim categorical.
- **1661, Japan:** Specialist account describes Seki transcribing and correcting a Yang Hui mathematics book; a book-transmission fact, not a precise record of Seki's residence on that date.
- **From 1665, Kōfu domain and Edo:** Service in the Tokugawa administrative household, with accounting duties; events move between locations, and precise residences are not continuously attested.
- **1674, Edo-period Japan:** Publication of *Hatsubi sanpō*, the one mathematics book he published himself.
- **1680s, Japan:** Manuscripts and collaborative development of algebraic techniques; Takebe's 1685 commentary helps make methods explicit.
- **1703–1708, Edo:** Advancement associated with Ienobu's elevation, late official career and death in 1708.
- **1712, Edo:** Posthumous *Katsuyō sanpō* published by disciples; sums-of-powers tables contain what we now call Bernoulli numbers.
- **1713, Basel:** Separate posthumous appearance of Jakob Bernoulli's *Ars Conjectandi*. Basel is comparison geography, **not a place Seki visited**.

### Mathematical companion

- **Elimination:** \(x+y=7\), \(xy=12\) implies \(x(7-x)=12\), then \((x-3)(x-4)=0\). This is a modern pedagogical demonstration of the problem, not a literal transcription of Seki's notation.
- **Two-by-two determinant:** \(\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc\). Zero determinant means the coefficient matrix is singular; it does not alone distinguish inconsistent from non-unique systems.
- **Sums of powers:** \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} k=n(n+1)/2\), \(\sum k^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6\), \(\sum k^3=[n(n+1)/2]^2\).
- **Bernoulli conventions:** The sequence appearing in period sources often uses \(B_1=+1/2\), whereas many modern generating-function conventions use \(B_1=-1/2\). Describe the convention when comparing old tables.
- **Telescoping:** \((k+1)^{m+1}-k^{m+1}\), expanded and summed, relates \(\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^m\) to sums of lower powers. This is a modern explanation of why the coefficients arise; it is not evidence of Seki's exact unpublished algorithm.

### Documentary limits

- The National Diet Library and specialist scholarship treat the birth year as unknown; database-ready dates must carry that uncertainty.
- Seki did not simply practice a modern European calculus with Japanese symbols. Similar formulas need not mean identical concepts or proof traditions.
- The name “Bernoulli numbers” reflects the history of reception and European nomenclature, not a claim that the mathematical pattern could only have been derived in Basel.
- Seki's surviving results include work mediated by Araki and the Takebe brothers. Do not convert school achievements into exclusive individual priority.
- Edo, Kōfu and China are different geographic roles here: a work center, an administrative center, and a textual source civilization, respectively. Basel is comparison geography.
