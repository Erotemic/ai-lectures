# 048000 — Nikola Tesla: How can mathematics make a magnetic field rotate?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** polyphase electricity, electrical engineering, invention, and historical myth. **Background:** angles, circles, basic multiplication, and the idea of electric current.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine a compass placed at the center of two coils of wire. The coils are set at right angles. If current flows through the first, it produces a magnetic field pointing in one direction. Current through the second produces a field pointing in a perpendicular direction. Now imagine changing both currents together. The first rises while the second falls; later the first reverses while the second takes over. At the right timing, the compass does not simply swing back and forth. It turns continuously around.

That small thought experiment leads into one of the most consequential engineering ideas of the nineteenth century: a rotating magnetic field produced without mechanically rotating the coils. It was central to the induction motor and to the polyphase electrical systems developed by Nikola Tesla and other inventors. The mathematics is not decorative. It describes how electrical oscillations, carefully offset in time, can act like a rotating machine.

Tesla was not primarily a research mathematician, and it would distort his life to claim otherwise. He was an inventor and electrical engineer whose ideas depended on geometry, phase, resonance and physical construction. His career also attracts extravagant myths. We can learn more by placing his actual inventions and failed ventures in their historical setting than by treating him either as a flawless prophet or as a mere celebrity.

He was born in eighteen fifty-six in Smiljan, then part of the Austrian Empire, in a Serbian family. His father was an Orthodox priest. Biographical accounts describe his mother, Đuka, as ingenious with domestic tools and mechanisms. The picture of a mechanically inventive household is grounded in Tesla's reminiscences and later biographical writing, although the precise details of every childhood story are harder to verify. His education took him through Karlovac and the polytechnic at Graz. He did not finish a conventional engineering degree there. Accounts of illness, interrupted study and family expectations differ in emphasis; they should not be compressed into the familiar tale of an unrecognized prodigy effortlessly defeating his teachers.

In Budapest, during work associated with the telephone exchange, Tesla thought seriously about motors that might operate without the sparking contacts of direct-current machines. He later described a formative insight about a rotating field during a walk. That particular scene comes largely through his own retrospective telling. What we can establish securely is that he developed functioning polyphase motor designs and patented crucial parts of a system in the eighteen eighties. The idea took shape in a much wider international environment: Galileo Ferraris independently investigated rotating fields in Italy, and engineers such as Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky helped develop commercially practical three-phase systems. Engineering history rarely fits into a single inventor's name.

Why is a rotating field useful? Consider the weakness of a field that merely alternates in one fixed direction. It grows, shrinks, reverses, and grows again. A motor rotor may respond to such variation, but arranging reliable self-starting and useful torque is not straightforward. By contrast, if the field's direction turns continuously, a conducting rotor can interact with the changing magnetic environment and tend to follow it. This is the essential physical intuition behind a polyphase induction motor.

We can derive the rotating field with elementary mathematics. Put one coil along an east-west axis and a second along a north-south axis. Let the first contribute an eastward magnetic strength that varies like the cosine of an angle. Let the second contribute a northward strength that varies like the sine of the same angle. The angle itself advances uniformly with time. At the start, cosine is one and sine is zero: the field points east. A quarter turn later, cosine is zero and sine is one: the field points north. A half turn later it points west. Three quarters through the cycle it points south. Then it returns to east.

Here is the clever part. At every moment the sum of the squares of the two components equals one. That is the familiar identity, cosine squared plus sine squared equals one. So in this idealized two-axis construction, the field's direction changes while its magnitude stays constant. We have built a rotating vector from two oscillating quantities. The hardware realizes the geometry: two or more appropriately placed winding groups carry currents with appropriate phase offsets.

There is a physical subtlety. A real induction motor does not work simply because the rotor sees a direction arrow and tries to follow it. A changing magnetic field relative to the rotor induces currents in conducting rotor elements. Those currents interact with the field and generate torque. If the rotor rotated at exactly the same speed as the field in the simple steady-state induction model, there would be no relative variation and therefore no induced rotor current to maintain the load torque. Engineers call the small difference in rotational speed slip. The rotor normally turns a little more slowly than the synchronous speed of the field. That difference is not an engineering embarrassment; it is part of the mechanism.

The word phase describes where one repeating motion is within its cycle. Two people walking around a circular path can have the same speed but remain a quarter turn apart. If the first stands at the east side when the second stands at the north side, their positions trace the same circle with a fixed offset. Electrical phase works similarly, though it describes the progression of voltage or current rather than the position of a person. The offset is what permits separate windings to create a field that moves around instead of simply swelling and shrinking.

A modern three-phase power system uses three sinusoidal quantities spaced evenly through a cycle, each separated by one third of a full turn, or one hundred twenty degrees. The windings are also arranged in space. When the geometry and currents are properly paired, their combined fundamental magnetic field rotates. The mathematical pattern is much like the two-phase example, but the engineering arrangement is different. That distinction prevents a common mistake: thinking that an induction motor requires exactly the two orthogonal coils of our first thought experiment. It requires an appropriate rotating field, and several winding arrangements can provide one.

Frequency matters as well. If a supply completes sixty electrical cycles each second, a two-pole rotating field completes sixty mechanical revolutions each second, which is thirty-six hundred revolutions per minute. With four poles, the synchronous field rotates at half that rate, eighteen hundred revolutions per minute. The physical induction rotor under ordinary motor load turns somewhat more slowly, because it needs slip. The arithmetic links an audible hum, an electrical cycle, and a mechanical shaft speed, even if the listener has no motor available to examine.

Tesla's early United States patents included a coordinated vision of generator, transmission lines, currents with controlled phase relationships, and motor. He emigrated to the United States in eighteen eighty-four, briefly worked for Thomas Edison's organization, and later established efforts of his own. Their relationship is often retold as a melodrama of two men with opposite personalities. There were real commercial and technical conflicts in the wider contest over electrical systems, but the victor was not decided by which inventor made the best insult. It depended on patents, capital, engineering labor, generating stations, appliances, distribution networks and the customers who needed power.

George Westinghouse purchased rights associated with Tesla's patents in eighteen eighty-eight and worked to commercialize alternating-current equipment. This arrangement gave Tesla a route from laboratory models to industrial infrastructure. It also placed him within a team and a company whose own engineers solved difficult practical problems. The history of electrification includes transformers, insulation, frequency choices, circuit protection, and dozens of related inventions that were not Tesla's alone.

The choice between alternating current and direct current is worth examining without partisan mythology. Direct current flows with a steady polarity; alternating current reverses direction periodically. Either can move useful electrical power. The central historical advantage of alternating-current systems was that transformers made it practical to raise and lower voltage efficiently in the networks of that time. If a transmission line delivers a fixed amount of power, a higher voltage generally allows a smaller current. Resistance heats a wire at a rate proportional to the square of the current. Halve the current and the resistive heating, for the same resistance, becomes one quarter. Long-distance distribution can therefore waste much less energy at high voltage. We must add that real alternating-current systems involve power factor and reactive effects; our simple multiplication of voltage and current is an introduction, not a complete grid model.

The eighteen ninety-three World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago gave alternating-current lighting a prominent public stage. Commercial power transmission from Niagara Falls to Buffalo followed in the eighteen nineties. Neither was the work of one inventor alone. Tesla's motor patents and his public demonstrations were important; so were Westinghouse's manufacturing resources and the work of many engineers, including those who selected and improved the equipment that actually went into service. A successful device differs from a convincing demonstration, and a working city-scale system differs from either.

Tesla enjoyed dramatic scientific exhibitions. He built and demonstrated high-frequency transformers now associated with his name, and explored phenomena involving strong alternating fields, resonance and luminous discharges. These demonstrations were visually arresting because invisible electrical relations became visible in light and sparks. But spectacle could obscure the distinction between an effect observed in a room and a technology proved economical on a continental scale.

Resonance is a useful example. A child on a swing gains amplitude if pushes arrive at the appropriate rhythm. An electrical circuit with inductance and capacitance can also exchange energy between magnetic and electric forms at a natural frequency. Repeated input at that frequency can produce large oscillations, limited by damping and other losses. Tesla's experiments exploited related principles. But no resonant system creates energy from nothing. Every actual apparatus has finite efficiency, and its behavior must be measured rather than guaranteed by an appealing analogy.

In Colorado Springs in eighteen ninety-nine, Tesla pursued large-scale high-voltage experiments. Some observations were significant; many claims and interpretations later attached to the laboratory exceed what its documented results establish. He became increasingly interested in wireless signaling and in delivering electric power over great distances without conventional conductors. His plan at Wardenclyffe, on Long Island, combined ambitious scientific and commercial ideas. Financier J. P. Morgan invested in it, but the project did not achieve a functioning worldwide wireless-power system. Funding, competing wireless technologies, shifting goals and the enormous physical demands of power transmission mattered. A failure to realize a grand design is not evidence that a hidden, nearly finished device was suppressed.

The later years reveal a life under increasing strain. Tesla's public standing survived, but financial security and sustained institutional backing did not. His preferences and routines became subjects of fascination in newspaper accounts. Popular retellings sometimes use those descriptions to assign modern medical diagnoses or to convert his social isolation into a tidy moral lesson. The evidence is not strong enough for that. We can say that his work had become difficult to finance, that he remained attached to projects and claims that did not always win technical acceptance, and that he died in New York in nineteen forty-three.

Tesla is often portrayed as if he invented every major component of the modern electrical world, from radio to the internet. That account obscures what he actually accomplished. He contributed important, patentable motor and power-system designs; he explored high-frequency electrical effects; he demonstrated technologies that captured the imagination. Other inventors independently found related principles, and later engineers made the installations reliable and widespread. Historical credit becomes more precise, not less generous, when it is distributed to the people who genuinely performed the work.

The rotating-vector example also gives us a disciplined way to understand invention. Before building the machine, we can write down two periodic motions and ask what their sum does. Then we can ask whether a real coil actually produces the desired field; whether the rotor responds; how much energy becomes heat; whether the machine can start under load; and whether the complete system is practical. Each question changes the scale at which an idea must succeed. Tesla's career traveled across those scales, sometimes triumphantly, sometimes without a workable solution.

We began with two coils and a compass. The mathematical identity behind their combined field is simple enough for school algebra. Its electrical realization helped make factories, trains, pumps and power networks possible. Tesla's story shows how much can follow from a geometric idea when someone has the patience to build it, and also how much more than the idea itself is required before an invention becomes a dependable part of the world.

## Mathematical and historical companion (not spoken)

### Two-phase rotating field (idealized)

For orthogonal coils with equal field amplitude and 90-degree temporal phase displacement:

\[
\mathbf B(t)=B_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf x}+B_0\sin(\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf y},\qquad
\lVert\mathbf B(t)\rVert=B_0.
\]

This idealization describes a rotating vector, not the detailed spatial field of an industrial stator. Real three-phase and two-phase windings achieve analogous rotating fundamental field components with appropriately distributed coils.

### Induction-motor slip

\[
s=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{sync}}-\omega_r}{\omega_{\mathrm{sync}}}.
\]

For a passive induction rotor supplying nonzero steady load torque, positive slip is generally required. Actual torque depends on rotor resistance, leakage reactance, field distribution and operating conditions.

### Resistive transmission losses

\[
P_{\mathrm{loss}}=I^2R.
\]

A simplified fixed active-power comparison uses \(P\approx VI\). In alternating-current networks the exact three-phase formulas include power factor and line/phase conventions. Raising voltage can reduce current and resistive loss, but does not eliminate other losses or grid constraints.
