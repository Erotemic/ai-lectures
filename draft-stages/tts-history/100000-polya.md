# 100000 - George Pólya: Discovering how to discover

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Budapest and the family of Jakab Pólya, an indirect path to mathematics, Hurwitz and Zürich, refusal of military service, Stella Weber, work with Szegő, random walks and enumeration, migration to Stanford, and the educational revolution of *How to Solve It*. **Background:** simple random walks and counting by symmetry are introduced in speech.

A student asks a teacher how to solve a problem. The teacher knows the answer, but instead of giving it, asks another question. Can you restate the problem? Have you seen a similar one? Can you solve a smaller case? This kind of conversation is now familiar in mathematics classrooms, and George Pólya helped give it a language. His book How to Solve It changed the way generations of teachers thought about helping students discover an argument for themselves. Yet the author was not simply a specialist in teaching. He was a research mathematician whose work ranged across probability, analysis, combinatorics and mathematical physics. To understand his advice, we need to understand the kind of mathematical life that produced it.

Pólya was born in Budapest in December eighteen eighty-seven, under the Austro-Hungarian monarchy. His father, Jakab, was trained as a lawyer and worked in insurance while trying to establish himself academically in economics and statistics. The family surname had earlier been Pollák. His father's decision to use the more Hungarian-sounding Pólya belonged to a period when national identity and professional opportunity were closely connected. His parents came from Jewish families but had converted to Catholicism before George's birth. These details are not a set of labels that explain his later mathematics. They show the overlapping worlds of language, religion and ambition in which a child grew up in late nineteenth-century Budapest.

Jakab died when George was a boy. His mother, Anna, and the older children helped maintain the household. The family wanted education for its children, though money and professional standing could not be taken for granted. George attended school and later university, but mathematics was not his first settled ambition. He studied subjects including law, languages, literature and philosophy. Only gradually did he turn toward mathematics. He later remembered being dissatisfied with the way finished proofs were presented, as if discoveries had sprung into existence fully polished. His real question was not only whether an argument was valid but how anyone had thought of it.

That question was practical. A student can copy the steps of a proof and still be unable to solve a new problem. Pólya wanted to know what skilled mathematicians do when the route is not visible. Do they vary an example? Draw a picture? Work backwards? Guess a pattern and test it? These tactics are not infallible algorithms. They are ways to make an unfamiliar problem more approachable. His own biography illustrates the point. He reached a mathematical career by a route less direct than that of a child trained from the beginning for a single subject.

There was a personal difficulty beneath the changing university enrollments. Pólya did not know early on what he wanted to be. He was drawn to philosophy and languages as well as to science, and he remembered coming to mathematics later than the students who seemed to know their destination from childhood. The story is worth telling because his mature teaching philosophy grew from that feeling of difficulty. An examination may convince us that a proof is correct without teaching us how anyone thought to begin it. Pólya regarded the act of discovery as a legitimate subject of reflection. He was not asking for a mechanical recipe that would make invention automatic; he wanted to study the questions that help a thinker get unstuck.

The Budapest mathematical world connected students across generations. Problems could move from a classroom to a journal or a private conversation, and a surprising solution could make a student's reputation long before a first formal appointment. Pólya's friendships with other Hungarian mathematicians, especially Szegő, grew inside that environment. His later teaching books can feel informal, but they came from someone who knew how demanding mathematical research really was. He was not simplifying because he did not know deeper mathematics. He was trying to make some of the habits of researchers visible to students who had never watched a theorem being invented.

He studied and traveled in the mathematical culture of central Europe, visiting Göttingen and learning from leading researchers. A particularly important influence was Adolf Hurwitz, a mathematician whose work and teaching joined careful analysis with a taste for elegant arguments. An incident during Pólya's student years led him to leave Göttingen after a conflict on a train. Retelling it as a comic mishap can distort the power of academic authorities at the time; for a young scholar, a university disciplinary decision could change the available future. Hurwitz helped secure him a position at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Zürich in nineteen fourteen. Switzerland offered him colleagues and relative stability just as Europe entered war.

The war was not a distant event. Pólya held pacifist views and refused demands to return to Hungary for military service. He later acquired Swiss citizenship, but the refusal complicated his ability to visit his homeland for many years. The choices brought professional and personal costs. He married Stella Vera Weber in nineteen eighteen, and the couple built their life in Switzerland. Her family had connections to academic science. Zürich became the setting for a remarkable body of mathematical work, and it was also a home in which the pressures of European politics could not simply be ignored.

Pólya collaborated with Gábor Szegő, whom he had known from Budapest. Szegő had proved a conjecture of Pólya's early in his career, and they later assembled a two-volume collection of problems and theorems in analysis. It appeared in the nineteen twenties. They organized many problems around methods rather than by topic alone. This is more radical than it may sound. A conventional textbook might put all the questions about integrals in one chapter and all the questions about series in another. A methods-oriented book asks whether the same trick, substitution, inequality or symmetry can solve problems that look unrelated. Learning a method makes it portable.

His research illustrates why this mattered. Consider a simple random walk on a grid. Place a walker at the origin, and on every step choose a neighboring grid point with equal probability. In one dimension the walker steps left or right. In two dimensions there are four directions; in three there are six. Does the walker eventually return to the starting point? In nineteen twenty-one Pólya proved a striking distinction. For a simple symmetric walk on the ordinary integer lattice, return is certain with probability one in dimensions one and two, but not in dimension three or higher.

It is easy to misunderstand that statement. It does not mean that every two-dimensional path returns after a fixed number of steps. Many paths wander far away, and some conceivable infinite paths never return; the assertion is about probability. Nor does it mean that three-dimensional walks never return. They often do. It means that the probability of eventual return is less than one. Adding a single spatial dimension changes the long-run behavior of a random process. This result has connections to potential theory and the diffusion of particles, yet it can be asked in the language of a child choosing directions at intersections.

A different probabilistic idea developed by Pólya and Florian Eggenberger shows how a simple model can reveal the effects of reinforcement. Imagine a jar containing two colors of balls. Draw one at random, return it, and add another ball of the color just drawn. At first the colors may be evenly represented. But an early run of one color makes that color more likely to appear in later drawings, which in turn increases its share. The model is not the same as drawing independent balls from a fixed jar. It was developed in nineteen twenty-three by Pólya and Florian Eggenberger as a model of dependent or contagious events, in a field where related models had already been considered. The important insight is that a small change in a sampling rule can produce a radically different long-run pattern.

This model has been used as a way to think about contagion, feedback and path dependence, though its assumptions are very special. A classroom can explore it with almost no advanced prerequisites: make two columns, record the color selected at each step, and watch two experiments with identical starts drift apart. The example echoes Pólya's fascination with questions in which the surface procedure looks elementary while the mathematical consequences are rich. The formulas come later. First comes the recognition that replacing one rule by another may change the problem completely.

Another of Pólya's interests was the counting of objects when some arrangements are considered equivalent by symmetry. Imagine coloring the corners of a square with two colors. If two colorings differ only because the square has been rotated, should they count as different? That depends on what we want to model. If the square represents a necklace or a molecule, rotations may not create new objects. Pólya developed powerful enumeration tools that count classes of objects under symmetry groups. His work joined permutations, generating functions and concrete applications, including the counting of chemical structures. It was a way to avoid examining every apparent arrangement one by one.

The counting idea is worth lingering over. Start with all possible colorings, then notice that some are merely rotated views of the same object. We can ask, for each symmetry, how many colorings remain unchanged by that symmetry. Combining those fixed-point counts gives the number of genuinely different types. The general mathematical machinery is more complicated, but the guiding thought is accessible: when symmetry makes several descriptions equivalent, count the equivalence classes rather than the raw descriptions. Pólya's enumeration theorem became a central result in combinatorics. It was not an appendix to his teaching career; it helped shape the subject he would later explain to students.

The collaboration with Szegő is particularly revealing. Their book of problems in analysis did not merely collect questions in order of textbook topic. They arranged material so that a reader could discover connections among methods of solution. Suppose a problem initially looks like an inequality. The intended lesson might be to recognize convexity, to change variables, to use symmetry, or to study the worst possible case. Different-looking problems can have the same hidden structure, while two superficially similar exercises may demand different insights. This was pedagogy built out of research experience. It asked the reader to notice a tool, not just reproduce an answer.

Work with Hardy and Littlewood showed another part of his range. Inequalities are sometimes treated as the unglamorous business of estimating quantities that cannot be calculated exactly. For Pólya and his collaborators, sharp inequalities were a creative subject in their own right. An inequality can reveal when equality is possible, which configuration is extremal, or what hidden symmetry governs an expression. In complex analysis he investigated entire functions, coefficients, zeros and transforms. Those results belong to a large mathematical history that cannot be reduced to a few classroom tricks. The more of his research one reads, the more it becomes clear that the author of How to Solve It was not merely a charismatic teacher who happened to know some mathematics. He was a major working mathematician who had thought carefully about the movement from conjecture to proof.

Pólya also worked extensively in complex analysis and mathematical physics. With Szegő he investigated inequalities and geometric problems involving regions, boundaries and potential functions. With G. H. Hardy and J. E. Littlewood he contributed to the classical book Inequalities. He could move from a precise technical estimate to a broad question about how one sees a proof. This range helps explain why his educational writing was respected by mathematicians rather than dismissed as mere classroom advice. He had faced difficult original problems and wanted to make the craft of facing them more visible.

By the nineteen thirties his work had taken him to American mathematical institutions, including a visit to Stanford. In nineteen forty, with Europe again at war and the political situation threatening, he moved to the United States. After shorter appointments, he joined Stanford, where he continued research and teaching. Migration was a decisive break. It changed his professional language, audience and publishing opportunities. His most famous book existed in German draft form before its English publication. It was in America that the manuscript found a publisher and reached a far larger readership.

His move to the United States also altered the classroom in which these questions were posed. European seminar culture and American universities differed in language, student preparation and institutional expectations. Pólya had to communicate strategies of thought to readers who did not share all the assumptions of his earlier mathematical circles. That helps explain why his best-known book proceeds through repeated questions rather than a grand theory of creativity. What is unknown? What has been given? Have you met a related problem? Could you solve a simpler one? Can you check the result by a different route? Each question is modest, but the questions together change the student's role from passive recipient to active investigator.

A particularly useful strategy is to work backward from the desired result. Imagine that you need to prove some complicated relation. Instead of trying random manipulations from the assumptions, ask what would make the conclusion true. Perhaps a simpler inequality would suffice, or a geometric construction would make a relation obvious. You can then try to build a path from the original data to that intermediate goal. Working backward does not replace working forward; it creates a meeting point. Pólya wanted students to develop this flexible movement between goal and resources. It resembles the practice of experienced researchers who temporarily assume a conjecture and explore what its truth would require.

Another strategy is to change the problem without losing its essence. If a formula has too many variables, try a two-variable case. If a diagram is crowded, study an extreme configuration. If you cannot guess a general pattern, compute a few examples and look for an invariant. These methods do not guarantee discovery. A misleading example can create a false conjecture, and a proof still has to justify the general claim. But a student with a repertoire of intelligent questions is in a better position than a student who only knows how to recognize the finished solution in a textbook.

How to Solve It appeared in nineteen forty-five. Several publishers had passed on it, yet the book became widely read. Its advice was organized around a small set of stages: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, and look back. These are not four commands that magically solve every mathematical question. A student can understand a problem and remain stuck. A proposed plan may fail. Looking back is not simply a polite afterthought; it asks whether the solution could be improved, generalized or transferred to another situation. The stages make the thinking process discussable, not automatic.

Take a simple example. Suppose we want to know how many handshakes occur if every pair among ten people shakes hands once. Counting person by person gives nine handshakes for each person, apparently ninety in total. But every handshake is counted twice, once for each participant. So the true number is forty-five. A teacher might ask the student to try three people or four people first. Or draw dots and lines. Or ask what happens when an eleventh person joins. In doing so the teacher is not replacing mathematics with encouragement. The student is learning the structural idea behind the answer: count ordered pairs, then correct for the two ways of naming the same unordered pair.

The value of Pólya's approach is not that every problem has a trick waiting in a drawer. It is that the habits of mathematical discovery can be discussed, practiced and improved. He argued for teaching that gives students a chance to discover something rather than requiring them only to imitate procedures. Later educators developed and criticized his work, and not every classroom can reproduce the full freedom of mathematical research. His book nevertheless changed how teachers talked about the gap between knowing a theorem and finding a proof.

His later audience was far wider than professional mathematics. Teachers in secondary schools could use his questions in classrooms where students had no intention of pursuing research. Engineers and scientists could recognize the same habits in design problems. That reach has sometimes produced a mistaken understanding of Pólya as the originator of all reflective problem solving. He never owned the human practices of drawing a picture, guessing, or checking an answer. What he did was to organize and explain them with unusual clarity, while grounding that explanation in a career of difficult original mathematics.

A danger attends any famous list of heuristics. If we repeat it as a checklist that always works, we lose the uncertainty that motivated it. A real researcher may try five related problems and fail at all of them. A good teacher may need to ask a different question for a different student. Pólya understood the practice of mathematics as exploratory and revisable. That is why his legacy is more durable than a set of four headings: he treated the learner as someone who can participate in the work of finding a method, not merely follow the traces of the teacher's previous discovery.

After his formal retirement from Stanford in the nineteen fifties, Pólya remained active as a teacher and mathematical writer. He was still teaching courses in old age. His students and colleagues remembered his curiosity and generosity with time, qualities that fit his educational philosophy without proving that every interaction was effortless. He returned to Hungary after many decades, when the old political obstacles were no longer the same. He died in California in nineteen eighty-five, leaving both major research results and a distinctive account of mathematical practice.

It is sometimes said that Pólya taught people to think. That is too broad a claim, and it can sound as if mathematics had been taught without thought before him. His achievement was more precise. He treated the search for a proof as something worth studying in its own right, while continuing to make discoveries across several fields. The mathematician who counted colorings by symmetry and investigated whether a random walk returns home was also asking what a solver should do at the moment a problem has no obvious solution. His answer was not a universal formula. It was an invitation to experiment intelligently, learn from the result, and ask a better question.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1887–1914:** born in Budapest; law, languages, philosophy and mathematical studies; Göttingen; appointment to Zürich with Hurwitz's support.
- **1914–1940:** Swiss career and citizenship; pacifism and dispute over Hungarian wartime service; marriage to Stella Weber in 1918; work with Szegő, Hardy and Littlewood; contributions in analysis, enumeration, probability and physics.
- **1940–1985:** emigrated to the US; appointments preceding Stanford; 1945 *How to Solve It*; research and teaching into old age.

### Mathematical companion

**Recurrence of simple random walk.** The nearest-neighbor symmetric random walk on \(\mathbb Z^d\) returns to the origin with probability one for \(d=1,2\), but a strictly smaller probability for \(d\ge3\). This is a property of a specific walk model; random walks with drift or different transition rules require separate analysis.

**Counting colorings by symmetry.** Burnside's orbit-counting lemma gives the number of orbits of a finite group \(G\) acting on a finite set \(X\) as \(\lvert G\rvert^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert\mathrm{Fix}(g)\rvert\). Pólya's enumeration theorem refines the method with cycle indices and color inventories. Distinguish Pólya's contribution from the prior orbit-counting result.

**Problem-solving heuristic.** The four stages are pedagogical guidance, not a complete algorithm. The sample ten-person handshake problem has \(10\cdot9/2=45\) edges of the complete graph on ten vertices.

**Historical cautions:** Pólya's research program predates his teaching book. His enumeration theorem is not the same as Burnside's lemma. The random-walk recurrence theorem applies to the simple symmetric lattice walk. His emigration and Swiss citizenship should be understood against wartime politics and military conscription, not only personal preference.
