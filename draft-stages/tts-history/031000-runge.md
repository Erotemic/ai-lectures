# Carl Runge: How can a differential equation become a reliable calculation?

Suppose you want to predict the cooling of a metal rod. At this moment the rod is hot, and you know a rule describing how fast its temperature changes. A moment later it is cooler, so the rate of cooling has changed. A moment after that, the rate has changed again. A differential equation tells you what happens at every instant, but the equation itself may not give you a formula for the temperature at a chosen future time. You need a way to turn a continuous law into a sequence of numbers you can actually compute.

Now suppose you are looking at a beam of light through a spectroscope. You see fine bright lines, each at a precisely measured wavelength. There are hundreds of measurements, small experimental errors, and hints of a pattern. Here again the problem is to extract a trustworthy calculation from an object that cannot be handled exactly. The mathematician Carl David Tolmé Runge worked on both kinds of question. He was a pure mathematician, an experimental spectroscopist, an advocate of graphical computation, and a founder of applied mathematics as a university discipline in Germany.

Runge lived from eighteen fifty-six to nineteen twenty-seven. He is remembered most often as one half of the name Runge–Kutta. But his mathematical life was much wider. His approximation theorem, his warnings about polynomial interpolation, and his detailed laboratory work reveal a consistent question: how can finite observations and finite computations faithfully represent something continuous? The answer depends as much on the choice of representation as on the amount of arithmetic we are willing to perform.

## A childhood between languages and disciplines

Runge was born in Bremen on August thirtieth, eighteen fifty-six, in a family whose business and personal connections reached across the Atlantic. His father Julius was a merchant in Havana, where he had lived for years and where Carl spent part of his early childhood. His mother Fanny came from an English-speaking merchant family. English was a home language for the Runge children. When Julius died in eighteen sixty-four, Fanny was left to raise a large family. Carl attended school in Bremen and grew up with the sense that intellectual and cultural life was not contained within one national tradition.

The Runge household had an international character long before Carl ever traveled for scientific work. His father, Julius, had lived for years as a merchant in Havana; his mother, Fanny Tolmé, came from a family with British connections. English was spoken at home, and Runge spent part of his childhood in Cuba. When his father died in eighteen sixty-four, his mother was left to raise a large family. Later recollections describe Carl as close to her and marked by a household that valued self-reliance and fair play. His life as a university mathematician did not emerge from a narrowly mathematical childhood. It came out of languages, family responsibilities, travel, and a curiosity that shifted directions more than once.

He entered the University of Munich in eighteen seventy-six intending to study literature and philosophy. Mathematics won him over quickly. He became friends with another student, Max Planck, who would later revolutionize physics. The two went to Berlin, where Runge encountered some of the most influential mathematicians of the century. Ernst Kummer, Karl Weierstrass, and Leopold Kronecker represented different kinds of mathematical rigor and different views of what should count as a worthwhile question. Runge listened closely to all of them. He also continued reading philosophy and retained an interest in music, languages, and physical exercise.

In his youth he was also known for skating, and as a young man in Berlin he became an accomplished figure on the ice. The image is worth keeping beside the mathematician at his desk: tall, physically active, and conspicuous at a fashionable city pastime. His interests in philosophy were serious too. He admired the philosopher Friedrich Paulsen's lectures and kept reading beyond mathematics. This mixture of bodily energy and wide intellectual taste helps explain how readily he moved between pure theory and experimental work when an interesting problem demanded it.

His eighteen eighty doctoral dissertation concerned the geometry of curves drawn on surfaces. A curve has an ordinary bending in space, but it also has a bending measured relative to the surface on which it lies. Studying that difference leads naturally to curvature, torsion, and geodesics. This was technically serious pure mathematics. In the years that followed, Runge investigated the numerical solution of algebraic equations. His habilitation in eighteen eighty-three found a way to encompass several earlier root-finding procedures within a broader construction. Before he became identified with differential equations, he was already asking how a theoretical relation could be converted into an effective computation.

## What counts as solving an equation?

His early investigation of algebraic equations deserves a closer look because it explains why he did not regard computation as secondary to proof. Suppose we must find a root of a polynomial. For a quadratic there is a general formula. For a higher-degree polynomial we may have no practical expression for the desired root, or an exact expression too complicated to evaluate usefully. Yet an engineer still needs an answer. Newton's method takes a current guess, draws the tangent line to the polynomial graph at that guess, and uses the tangent's intersection with the horizontal axis as the next guess. In favorable circumstances, the guesses converge very rapidly. But if the derivative is small, the tangent can send the next guess far away; and a poor starting point may lead to a different root or no convergence at all.

Runge's early habilitation placed several established algebraic solution procedures in a common framework. For a listener, the detail to retain is that finding a root involves both algebraic identity and the behavior of an iterative process. We can prove that a root exists in an interval because the polynomial changes sign there. That existence statement does not tell us whether Newton's particular starting guess will approach it. We can also bound the error of a calculated root by evaluating how accurately the equation is satisfied and by knowing how sharply the polynomial changes nearby. These are related but distinct questions. Runge kept pursuing that distinction in problems much more complicated than polynomial roots.

Consider a polynomial whose graph nearly touches the horizontal axis without crossing it sharply. A tiny error in the polynomial coefficients can shift a root by a noticeable amount. An algorithm may solve the perturbed polynomial perfectly and still return a root far from the ideal one. This is a conditioning problem in the mathematical task itself. Another algorithm might introduce large errors even when the root is well conditioned; that is a method problem. Runge's later career repeatedly brought him back to the question of how to tell those two sources of error apart.

## An early theorem about approximation

There is another Runge result that belongs to complex analysis, and it is important not to confuse it with his later numerical integration scheme. In eighteen eighty-five he published work that became the foundation of Runge's approximation theorem. The setting is a complex variable, a number with real and imaginary parts. A holomorphic function is one that is complex differentiable on an open region. Such functions obey strikingly strong constraints; they are represented locally by convergent power series, and their values on a small part of a region restrict what can happen elsewhere.

Imagine a function defined near a compact shape in the complex plane. Can we approximate it, uniformly across that entire shape, by simpler functions? For instance, can we use polynomials? The geometry of the region determines the answer. In a common version of Runge's theorem, if the complement of the compact shape is connected, every function holomorphic on a neighborhood of that shape can be approximated uniformly there by polynomials. If the complement contains holes, rational functions with poles placed in the appropriate complementary regions provide the more flexible class of approximants.

A small example makes the statement concrete. Consider one divided by two minus z, where z lies in the closed unit disk. The denominator never vanishes inside that disk. Write the function as one half times one divided by one minus z over two. Its geometric-series expansion begins one half, then z over four, then z squared over eight, and so on. Take the first few terms and you have a polynomial. Because the size of z over two is at most one half, the discarded tail shrinks uniformly. The error is small everywhere on the disk, not only at a few sample points.

This might sound like an exercise in infinite series, but the conclusion is broader. The proof connects a property of a region in the plane to the possibility of computing an approximation. A hole in the region can obstruct approximation by polynomials, because a polynomial cannot imitate the singularity hiding in the hole while satisfying the analytic constraints across the boundary. The theorem identifies what kind of approximating objects are powerful enough for the geometry at hand.

It is easy to hear the word approximation and suppose that every approximation procedure will therefore work. That would be a serious error. A theorem guaranteeing the existence of good polynomials does not say that a polynomial chosen by an arbitrary algorithm will be good. Much later, Runge would help make that distinction unforgettable.

## Hannover: the mathematician at the spectroscope

In eighteen eighty-six Runge became a professor at the technical university in Hannover. The appointment enabled him to marry Aimée du Bois-Reymond the following year. Her father, the physiologist Émile du Bois-Reymond, had already helped direct Runge's attention to a new experimental problem: the spectral lines emitted by chemical elements. Runge joined the physicist Heinrich Kayser in a sustained investigation of the patterns hiding among those lines. After Kayser departed, Runge worked closely with Friedrich Paschen.

There was a personal story behind that appointment. Runge had become close to the family of physiologist Émile du Bois-Reymond and was engaged to his daughter Aimée. Her father wanted Runge to have a professorship before they married. The position in Hannover therefore meant more than a new title: it removed a practical obstacle to their life together. They married in eighteen eighty-seven and would raise four daughters and two sons. Their home became a center of music and science as well as family life. Paschen, a later collaborator, remembered the children joining Runge at the piano for performances of works such as Bach's Saint Matthew Passion. The link between mathematical work and domestic life was personal: relatives, colleagues, and instruments occupied the same crowded world.

A spectroscope separates light by wavelength. A chemical element does not produce a simple smear of every color; under suitable conditions it gives a distinctive series of narrow lines. The problem was urgent because spectroscopy promised a route into the structure of matter at a time when the internal architecture of atoms was unknown. Runge and Kayser measured spectra, developed empirical formulas, and organized large bodies of data. Runge and Paschen later studied helium and the spectra of oxygen, sulfur, and selenium. Their work helped show that a spectrum might contain several related series rather than a single sequence.

Runge's daily working habits had a physical momentum to match the household. Paschen recalled his cycling about eight kilometers between his house and the technical school, sometimes four times a day, and his enjoyment of swimming, gymnastics, and other sports. Those details make the long stretches of spectroscopy more imaginable. The measurements were painstaking, but their maker was no recluse who sat motionless among tables. He could spend hours reducing observations and then travel across Hannover on a bicycle, returning to a home where music was as natural as mathematical conversation.

Picture the labor involved. A researcher must identify which narrow marks correspond to the same element, measure their positions, transform wavelengths into useful numerical quantities, compare groups, and search for a rule. Small differences may be the clue; small errors may also be enough to create a false pattern. Runge did not simply hand an experiment to someone else and supply an equation. He was a working spectroscopist. The mathematical and laboratory sides of his career fed each other. Measuring actual physical systems made numerical reduction an intellectual problem rather than a clerical afterthought.

His collaborations also crossed borders. On visits to Britain and America he became acquainted with Lord Rayleigh and Albert Michelson. After a visit to Yerkes Observatory, George Ellery Hale offered him a position that he declined. Runge spent many years at Hannover, even while colleagues whom others considered more clearly physicists or mathematicians moved to better-established university chairs. The boundary between pure mathematics and experimental physics was often institutionally rigid, and his work fit neither category neatly.

## Patterns in spectral lines

The spectral investigations deserve more than a reference to laboratory measurement. Why would a mathematician spend years studying rows of bright lines? Consider the spectral lines of hydrogen. Their frequencies are arranged in families that can be described by formulas involving integers. The details of the modern quantum explanation came later, but the discovery that an empirical list of measurements could be organized into numerical series was already a major clue to atomic structure. In experiments on other elements, Runge and his collaborators searched for comparable regularity while avoiding the assumption that every element had to follow exactly the same simple formula.

If a series is described by a rule, it predicts where an unmeasured line should lie. A spectroscopist can then return to the instrument and look near that location. A successful prediction supports the classification. A mismatch may reveal an observational error, an incorrect assignment of lines, or a genuine limitation of the proposed formula. This creates a loop between calculation and observation. The numbers do not simply summarize experiments that have already ended; they suggest the next experiment.

With Paschen, Runge found that helium's spectrum included distinct series systems. For a time, some researchers interpreted this as evidence for a mixture of elements, an interpretation later corrected as spectroscopy progressed. That episode illustrates the danger of over-reading a pattern. A good numerical organization is a discovery, but the physical story used to explain it can still be wrong. Runge's actual contribution was the careful measurement and classification of the spectra. It would be misleading to credit him with the later quantum-mechanical explanation of why the atomic lines occur.

The work also depended on colleagues with different abilities. Kayser helped establish the laboratory program. Paschen brought experimental precision. Runge's strength included calculation, pattern recognition, and the ability to make the competing interpretations explicit enough to test. The collaboration demonstrates applied mathematics as a working relationship among instruments, mathematics, and physical hypotheses.

## The differential-equation problem returns

Now return to the cooling rod. Suppose its temperature above the surrounding air is y, and the simplest cooling law says that the rate of change of y is minus two times y. If the initial excess temperature is one, there is an exact formula: y at time t equals the exponential of minus two t. But we will pretend for a moment that we know only the rule for the derivative, because real scientific models rarely come with a convenient elementary answer.

Leonhard Euler had given a natural method long before Runge. Begin at a known state. Calculate the derivative there. Assume that derivative holds for a short time h. Move by h times the derivative. Then repeat. For a general equation y prime equals f of t and y, Euler's update is simply the old y plus h times f at the old time and old state. This is easy to compute and often useful. But it predicts a whole step using the slope from only its beginning.

Take an even friendlier equation: the derivative equals y, with initial value one. Its exact solution after one unit of time is the number e, about two point seven one eight. Euler with a single step of length one begins with slope one and predicts two. The result is visibly too small. Why? Because the function is growing. The slope becomes larger during the step, while Euler pretends it remains equal to the starting slope. Making the step smaller helps, but that requires more steps. Can we do more intelligent work within each step?

This is the question addressed by Runge's eighteen ninety-five paper on numerical integration of differential equations. The idea is to calculate additional slopes at trial positions inside the interval, using evaluations of the same derivative rule. Those intermediate calculations can reveal how the slope is changing without requiring us to differentiate the equation into a long Taylor expansion. The method remains a one-step method: once the current state is known, the method can produce the next state without storing a whole sequence of past states.

## Looking at the middle of a step

Suppose we calculate a slope at the beginning of a step and use it to predict where the solution would be halfway through. At that provisional midpoint, we calculate a new slope. Then, instead of advancing by the original slope, we advance over the whole step using the midpoint slope. That is the explicit midpoint method, one of the central examples in the Runge tradition. The first slope helps locate a trial point. The second slope determines the final advance.

For the equation y prime equals y, begin at one and take h equal to one. The starting slope is one. The provisional midpoint value is one plus one half, or one point five. The slope there is one point five. Advance the original value by one full step at that slope. The prediction is two point five. Compare Euler's two and the exact two point seven one eight. We have improved the approximation without taking additional full steps. The improvement comes from where we sampled the derivative, not from knowing the exact solution.

What order of improvement should we expect? Expand the exact solution in a Taylor series over a short interval. Its increment begins with h times the initial derivative, followed by one half h squared times the derivative of that derivative. The midpoint construction also produces these first two contributions. The error committed during one step begins at order h cubed, provided the differential equation is sufficiently smooth. Across a fixed interval with approximately one over h steps, the accumulated error is generally of order h squared. We call it a second-order method.

That language can be confusing. Second order does not mean that we take two steps, or that we square the answer, or that an error is exactly h squared at every point. It means that in the small-step limit, the leading global error typically decreases in proportion to the square of the step length. Halve the step and, once the asymptotic regime has begun, the error should decrease by about a factor of four. This is a predictive statement about a method, not an assertion that every problem behaves benignly at every step size.

An alternative is to sample the slope at the beginning, predict the value at the end using Euler, sample the slope again at that predicted endpoint, and take the average of the two slopes. This is sometimes called the explicit trapezoidal or improved Euler method. The midpoint and trapezoidal versions look different but both achieve second-order accuracy for smooth problems. They demonstrate a general lesson: the time at which a derivative is evaluated, and the weights assigned to different evaluations, can be designed to cancel leading errors.

## A second pass at the midpoint calculation

The midpoint method becomes more convincing when we vary the step size and see whether the predicted error behavior actually appears. Use again the growing equation y prime equals y, with initial value one, and predict the value at time one. One midpoint step of length one gives two point five. Now divide the interval into two steps, each of length one half. Each step multiplies the current value by one plus one half plus one eighth. That is one point six two five. After two steps, the approximation is two point six four zero six two five. We are closer to the exact value, about two point seven one eight, but still below it.

Try four steps of length one quarter. The multiplier for each step is one plus one quarter plus one thirty-second, or one point two eight one two five. Apply it four times. The result approaches the exact exponential more closely. This is what convergence looks like in an audible numerical experiment. We are not looking for a magical formula that predicts the future in one move. We are creating a controlled family of calculations that can be refined and compared.

There is another reason to repeat a calculation with a shorter step. Often we do not know the exact solution, so we cannot calculate the true error by subtraction. We can, however, compare a computation with step length h to a computation with half that step length. If the difference changes in a manner consistent with the method's order, that gives information about the remaining error. Such comparisons are the basis for practical error estimation and adaptive integration. They are useful, though not infallible: two wrong calculations can agree when both are affected by the same modeling mistake or instability.

## From a clever update to a family of methods

It is tempting to call the four-stage formula used today simply Runge's algorithm. Historically that would give the wrong impression. Runge's eighteen ninety-five paper developed the one-step, internal-stage idea. Karl Heun contributed further schemes in nineteen hundred. Martin Wilhelm Kutta systematized and extended the construction in work completed around nineteen hundred and published in nineteen oh one. Kutta produced higher-order formulas, including the family from which the familiar fourth-order scheme emerged. Later mathematicians, notably John Butcher, developed the general algebraic order conditions that make this family easier to understand and extend.

Runge and Kutta did not produce a single object that was permanently complete. They helped initiate a way of designing algorithms. We can choose a number of internal slope evaluations, where to place them in time, how to construct their trial states, and how to combine them. Then we derive algebraic conditions that force the update to match the true solution through a desired order. A fourth-order method can be built so that the one-step defect begins at order h to the fifth and the global error is typically order h to the fourth. That design is the heart of what many people now mean by Runge–Kutta.

This development also changed who could carry out a useful computation. A researcher did not need a closed-form solution to predict motion or temperature; a table of a few carefully chosen derivative evaluations could be enough. In Runge's lifetime such calculations meant tables, slide rules, plotting instruments, and human numerical labor. Today a computer performs them very quickly, but the governing mathematical problem has not vanished. If the step is too long, or the model is unstable, fast arithmetic produces a wrong answer fast.

## Several equations at once

The numerical problem is richer when the unknown is not a single number. A planetary trajectory needs a position and a velocity, each with several components. Newton's second law gives a second derivative of position, while the Runge framework is usually presented for first derivatives. The conversion is straightforward: treat the velocities as additional unknowns. Their derivatives are the accelerations; the position derivatives are the velocities. Collect these variables into one state vector and apply the same stepping idea to the complete system.

This conversion makes clear what it means to evaluate a slope inside a step. We do not merely sample one scalar derivative; we estimate an entire future state, including its position and velocity, and evaluate the physical law there. A poorly chosen trial state may be inconsistent with a constraint, such as motion confined to a surface. A well-designed integrator has to take such physical structure seriously. For a pendulum, for example, the angle and its angular velocity are enough to form a first-order system, but a long simulation may still drift in energy when computed with a generic method. The Runge tradition provided broadly applicable stepping tools, while later geometric integration examined how to protect special invariants during very long calculations.

## The paradox of polynomial interpolation

In nineteen oh one Runge examined another common numerical habit: estimating a function between measured points by fitting one polynomial through all the observations. Imagine that we sample a smooth curve at equally spaced positions. There is exactly one polynomial of sufficiently low degree passing through all the samples. If we increase the number of sample points, should the polynomial always become a better description of the curve? A plausible answer is yes. Runge showed that the answer can be no.

The now-standard example is one divided by one plus twenty-five times x squared, on the interval from minus one to one. It is a perfectly smooth, positive curve. At x equal to zero, its value is one. Toward either end, its value approaches one twenty-sixth. There is no sharp discontinuity, no violent jump, no suspicious input data. Fit a polynomial through a modest collection of equally spaced samples, and the approximation may look reasonable near the center. Raise the degree while keeping the samples equally spaced, and the polynomial can develop large oscillations near the interval ends. At points between samples it may be wildly wrong, even though it agrees exactly at every sample point.

A listener might object: does a polynomial ever approximate that smooth function well? Absolutely. The Weierstrass approximation theorem guarantees arbitrarily good polynomial approximations to every continuous function on a closed interval. There is no contradiction. The existence theorem says that some polynomials are good. Runge's example says that the particular polynomial selected by high-degree interpolation at equally spaced nodes can be bad. An algorithm is a rule for choosing one approximation from a large class; the class may contain excellent answers while the rule systematically chooses poor ones.

Why do the ends suffer? To fit all the point values, a high-degree polynomial must balance many constraints simultaneously. With equally spaced nodes, the polynomial basis can amplify the effects of values near the boundaries, and the interpolation error depends on a product of distances from all the nodes as well as on high derivatives of the target function. For this example the complex singularities, located off the real axis, also limit the analytic behavior that such an interpolation rule can exploit. The problem is structural, not merely roundoff from an old calculating machine.

One remedy is to place more interpolation points near the endpoints, as Chebyshev nodes do. Another is to use low-degree polynomials on small subintervals, the idea behind splines. We can also approximate in a different basis or use a least-squares fit rather than insist on exactly passing through every sample. Each choice answers a different mathematical question. Runge's counterexample remains part of numerical education because it separates approximation in principle from approximation by a particular computational procedure.

## Göttingen and a new academic home for computation

In nineteen oh four Felix Klein helped bring Runge to Göttingen to occupy a chair in applied mathematics. This was an important institutional development. Mathematics departments had long housed pure theory, while technical schools had used formulas in mechanics and engineering. Runge's appointment gave sustained mathematical computation a clearer place in university research and teaching. He brought a combination of rigorous analysis, practical methods, physical examples, and an insistence that students learn to calculate.

His Göttingen work included numerical algorithms, graphical solutions, and lectures on applied subjects. He published on analytic geometry and later coauthored lectures on numerical calculation with Hermann König. In a nineteen oh nine to nineteen ten visit to Columbia University, he lectured on graphical methods. Think of plotting an equation on graph paper and using intersections or slopes to estimate a solution. A graph is not merely a decoration after the algebra is finished. With appropriate care, graphical constructions can be part of the method itself. They were particularly valuable before widespread digital computation.

Runge also taught in an environment rich with physicists and mathematicians. Göttingen's scientific network included Hilbert, Klein, and researchers in mechanics and aerodynamics such as Ludwig Prandtl. Runge's archived lecture materials include mathematics, mechanics, photogrammetry, aerodynamics, and even discussions of relativity. One can see why a rigid label such as pure mathematician or physicist had failed to capture his work. The calculation was often the bridge between those disciplines.

The scientific and family circles did not disappear when Runge moved to Göttingen. His children grew up among people for whom mathematics, music, and experiments were ordinary conversations. His daughter Nerina, called Nina, later married Richard Courant, connecting the household to another major figure in the history of applied mathematics. His daughter Iris pursued physics and mathematical work of her own. Such relationships do not explain a theorem, but they explain how a scientific culture was lived and carried forward: through students, colleagues, children, and everyday conversation as well as through journals.

## What a good numerical result actually promises

Before we leave Runge's mathematics, try a final calculation. Suppose the equation is y prime equals minus two y and the step length is one. At the starting value y equals one, Euler predicts minus one after the step: a negative value for a physical quantity representing positive excess heat. The midpoint method computes an initial slope minus two, predicts a midpoint value of zero, and finds the midpoint slope to be zero. It therefore returns one, as though the rod had not cooled at all. That is also a poor prediction. The exact value is the exponential of minus two, about zero point one three five.

The midpoint calculation was more accurate for the growing exponential, but with this large step on the decaying exponential it is worse in a different way. A method's order describes the result as the step size tends toward zero. It does not guarantee plausible behavior for an arbitrary large step. Numerical stability is a separate subject: when errors or perturbations arise, does the chosen update magnify them or suppress them? For the test equation y prime equals lambda times y, a method produces an amplification factor depending on h times lambda. If its magnitude is greater than one on a truly decaying mode, the computed sequence can grow while the physical solution shrinks.

That distinction brings the two sides of Runge's mathematical career together. In spectroscopy, the best-fit formula had to respect what noisy measurements could actually establish. In interpolation, the existence of a good polynomial did not certify the one chosen by equally spaced interpolation. In differential equations, a formally high-order update did not guarantee trustworthy results at every step size. The essential question was always the same: which numerical representation preserves the property we need, and under what conditions?

The First World War pressed on this energetic family life. One of Runge and Aimée's sons was killed in the war, a loss recorded by Paschen. Runge opposed German annexationist ambitions, according to biographical accounts, and after the war took part in efforts toward international reconciliation. The same mathematician remembered for athletic pleasure and a musical household now had to contend with the destruction of a generation. This belongs near the end of his Göttingen years because it changes how we hear the cheerful reminiscences from Hannover. The man whose mathematical method computed small steps through a changing world had experienced changes no calculation could make manageable.

Runge died in Göttingen on January third, nineteen twenty-seven. By then the city he had helped make a center of applied mathematics had endured war and political rupture. His name survives on an approximation theorem, an interpolation failure, and the Runge–Kutta family. Those names describe more than an inventory of papers. His life connected exact function theory, careful observation at the spectroscope, and the teaching of useful calculation. His contemporaries also remembered a lively colleague, a cyclist, a musician, and a member of a family deeply involved in intellectual life. Those are connected parts of the same story: Runge wanted mathematics that could enter the world and survive contact with real measurements.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Runge approximation theorem (standard compact formulation)

Let \(K\subset\mathbb C\) be compact and \(f\) holomorphic on a neighborhood of \(K\). If \(\mathbb C\setminus K\) is connected, then for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists a polynomial \(p\) with \(\sup_{z\in K}|f(z)-p(z)|<\varepsilon\). For general \(K\), allow rational approximants with poles in chosen complementary components. One elementary illustration for \(|z|\le1\):
\[
\frac1{2-z}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}},\qquad
\left|\frac1{2-z}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}\right|\le 2^{-N-1}.
\]
This is a concrete instance, not the general proof of Runge's theorem.

### 2. Euler and explicit midpoint methods

For \(y'=f(t,y)\), step length \(h\),
\[
\text{Euler: } y_{n+1}=y_n+h f(t_n,y_n).
\]
\[
\text{Midpoint: } k_1=f(t_n,y_n),\quad
k_2=f(t_n+h/2,y_n+hk_1/2),\quad y_{n+1}=y_n+hk_2.
\]
If \(f\) is sufficiently smooth and locally Lipschitz, midpoint is order two globally over a fixed finite interval. For \(y'=y\), \(y(0)=1\), one step with \(h=1\) gives Euler 2, midpoint 2.5, exact \(e\approx2.718281828\). The explicit trapezoidal method has \(k_2=f(t_n+h,y_n+hk_1)\), \(y_{n+1}=y_n+h(k_1+k_2)/2\).

### 3. Scalar stability diagnostic

For \(y'=\lambda y\), explicit midpoint has \(y_{n+1}=R(z)y_n\), \(z=h\lambda\), \(R(z)=1+z+z^2/2\). At \(z=-2\), \(R=1\), although the true decay multiplier is \(e^{-2}\approx0.135335\). Euler has \(R(-2)=-1\). This demonstrates poor behavior at a large step and does not contradict either method's formal order.

### 4. Runge phenomenon and representation

For \(f(x)=1/(1+25x^2)\) on \([-1,1]\), high-degree interpolation at equispaced nodes can exhibit divergent endpoint oscillations. The existence of good polynomial approximations (Weierstrass theorem) is logically independent of convergence of this particular sequence of interpolants. Chebyshev-like node placement and piecewise interpolation give alternatives. Original paper: Runge (1901), *Über empirische Funktionen und die Interpolation zwischen äquidistanten Ordinaten*, Z. Math. Phys. **46**, 224–243.

### 5. Chronology and attributions

- 1856 Bremen birth; childhood partly in Havana; mathematics training in Munich and Berlin; 1880 Berlin doctorate in differential geometry; 1883 habilitation on algebraic root methods.
- 1885 papers in complex approximation; 1886 Hannover appointment; 1887 marriage to Aimée du Bois-Reymond.
- Collaboration with Heinrich Kayser and Friedrich Paschen on line spectra, with laboratory work on helium, oxygen, sulfur, and selenium; 1895 numerical ODE paper; 1901 interpolation warning.
- 1904 Göttingen applied mathematics chair promoted by Felix Klein; 1909–1910 Columbia lectures on graphical methods; 1924 book on numerical calculation with Hermann König; retired from leadership in 1925; died in 1927.
- The two-stage schemes are associated with Runge's 1895 one-step method; Kutta's 1900/1901 work extended the framework. It is historically inaccurate to credit Runge alone with the modern classical four-stage fourth-order formula.
