# 041000 — Joseph Fourier: Can an irregular temperature become a sum of simple waves?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** mathematical physics, heat diffusion, trigonometric series, and scientific institutions. **Background:** elementary calculus and trigonometry are explained through examples.

Take a metal bar. Heat its left half and leave its right half cold. Now remove the heater and watch. The sharp boundary between hot and cold begins to blur. After enough time the bar approaches a uniform temperature, unless the surroundings keep drawing heat away. How could anyone predict this process from the temperature at the beginning? A single number will not suffice. At every position there is a temperature, and at every moment that entire profile changes. Joseph Fourier found a way to make such a complicated evolution calculable. He represented an initial temperature pattern as a combination of simple oscillating shapes, each of which fades at a predictable rate. The mathematics later reached sound, optics, radio, image processing, and quantum physics. But it began with an ordinary question about how heat moves.

Fourier's life resists the familiar story of a mathematician working undisturbed at a desk. He was born in seventeen sixty-eight in Auxerre, France, the son of a tailor. Orphaned young, he received an education through institutions that recognized his talent. As a teenager he took to mathematics with formidable energy. The revolutionary years then pulled him between scholarship, teaching, politics, and personal danger. He lived through the French Revolution, joined scientific institutions associated with the new republic, traveled with Napoleon's Egyptian expedition, and became prefect of a mountainous French department. Much of his most consequential mathematics took shape while he was responsible for roads, drainage, and civil administration. To understand Fourier, we should keep both lives in view: the man of public upheaval and the man who worked out the mathematics of diffusion.

Before turning to his biography in detail, let us make the central physical observation. Imagine three adjacent pieces of a metal rod. The left piece is hotter than the middle, and the middle hotter than the right. Heat flows from hotter regions toward colder ones. If the temperature changes very rapidly across a short distance, the flow tends to be stronger. Fourier gave this empirical relation mathematical form: the rate of heat flow is proportional to the spatial temperature gradient, with a negative sign indicating flow toward lower temperature. It sounds simple, but it changes the problem from an anecdote about hot metal into an equation that could be measured, tested, and solved.

A temperature gradient is just a slope. If the temperature is eighty degrees at one point and sixty degrees one centimeter farther right, the average slope is minus twenty degrees per centimeter. A steep negative slope drives heat to the right. If temperatures differ by only one degree over the same distance, the slope and expected heat flux are much smaller. Fourier's law concerns the local slope, which may vary from point to point. The proportionality constant depends on the material. Copper conducts heat very readily; a poor insulator does not. We can calculate even when we do not understand every microscopic motion of the substance.

Conservation supplies the second ingredient. If a small segment of the rod gains more heat through its left face than it loses through its right, its internal energy increases, and its temperature rises. If it loses more than it gains, it cools. In symbols kept for the mathematical companion, the rate of energy accumulation equals incoming flux minus outgoing flux. Combining that accounting identity with Fourier's local law yields the heat equation. For a uniform rod with constant material properties, the change in temperature per unit time is proportional to the second spatial derivative of temperature. That second derivative measures curvature, or how the slope itself changes from one position to another.

Why curvature? Picture a point colder than its two immediate neighbors. The graph of temperature bends upward around that point, so its second derivative is positive. Heat arrives from both sides, and the point warms. Now picture a point hotter than both neighbors. The graph bends downward, the second derivative is negative, and the point cools. The heat equation expresses this smoothing mechanism in one line. No complicated molecular story is needed for the continuum calculation. It predicts the tendency of a temperature profile to flatten by transferring energy from locally hotter places to colder ones.

This was the field Fourier entered after a turbulent beginning. In the seventeen nineties he taught and participated in the intellectual life reorganized by revolutionary France. He became associated with the École Normale and the École Polytechnique, where Gaspard Monge and other major mathematicians helped shape a new culture of scientific education. The politics of the Revolution did not remain outside his work. Fourier was caught in local factional conflict and at one stage imprisoned; the political reversals of the period placed him in genuine danger. The same society that expanded scientific opportunity could threaten the lives of those serving it.

In seventeen ninety-eight Fourier joined the French expedition to Egypt. This was a military invasion accompanied by a remarkable, and politically compromised, scientific enterprise. Mathematicians, engineers, naturalists, artists, and surveyors studied Egyptian landscapes, antiquities, and institutions while French troops occupied the country. Fourier worked with the Institut d'Égypte and later helped assemble the monumental Description de l'Égypte. The expedition widened his scientific responsibilities and his administrative experience. It also requires us to remember that the production of scholarly knowledge occurred inside imperial conquest, not apart from it.

Returning to France, Fourier was appointed prefect of the department of Isère in eighteen oh two, based at Grenoble. A prefect represented the central government and supervised many practical affairs. He oversaw projects including the draining of marshes near Bourgoin and work on the road connecting Grenoble toward Turin through the mountains. Those tasks involved real materials, terrain, water, and weather. They also consumed time. Yet in Grenoble he pursued the propagation of heat with sustained effort, carrying out experiments and calculations while managing the demands of office. His first major memoir on heat was submitted to the Academy in eighteen oh seven.

Here the mathematical problem becomes more interesting than a simple finite-difference estimate. Suppose the temperature of a rod is zero at both ends, because its ends touch large reservoirs maintained at zero. At the beginning, the middle is warmer than the boundaries. Which initial shapes evolve without changing their shape, except for an overall decrease in amplitude? If a temperature profile is a sine arch that vanishes at both ends, then the second derivative of that sine arch is a negative constant times the same arch. The heat equation therefore says that its amplitude shrinks exponentially while the spatial shape remains a sine arch.

Take the first sine arch. Its value is zero at the left end, rises smoothly to a maximum at the midpoint, and returns to zero at the right end. At any later time it is still the same arch, only lower. Now imagine two arches placed along the rod, one positive and one negative. This second sine pattern has twice the spatial frequency. The second derivative multiplies it by a negative factor four times as large. Its amplitude therefore falls four times as quickly on the exponential time scale. A pattern with three half-waves falls nine times as quickly. The higher the spatial frequency, the faster heat destroys that fine structure.

That gives us a remarkable clue. If an initial temperature could be assembled from sine arches of many frequencies, then every component would have its own simple decay law. We could solve a complicated partial differential equation by solving many independent elementary problems and adding their solutions. This is the heart of Fourier's method. Its audacity was the proposal that broad classes of initial functions could be represented by trigonometric series, even functions with corners or jumps that do not look like smooth waves.

Try a simple example that the ear can follow. Imagine a rod whose temperature is arranged like a tent: zero at both ends, rising linearly to a peak at the center, and falling linearly back to zero. The graph has a sharp corner at the peak. It is obviously not a single sine curve. Yet we can represent the tent shape as a weighted sum of sine curves. The first sine curve gives the broad hump. The third sine curve corrects its outline, with a smaller amplitude and opposite sign. The fifth makes a finer correction, and so on. Even frequencies vanish because of the symmetry and the particular shape. The magnitudes of the remaining coefficients decrease as one over the square of the frequency.

We can even calculate what the first few waves contribute to the tent. Choose a rod two units long, so its temperature rises to one at the center and falls back to zero. The first sine coefficient is eight divided by pi squared, about zero point eight one. The third coefficient is negative eight divided by nine pi squared, about negative zero point zero nine. At the center, the third sine wave itself is negative one, so multiplying the two negatives adds about zero point zero nine to the first approximation. With just those two modes, the center temperature is already about zero point nine zero. The fifth contribution adds only about zero point zero three. Successive terms refine the pointed tent. When heat flow begins, the third and fifth contributions fade much faster than the first, and the point becomes rounded.

This calculation teaches the mechanics of an infinite series without requiring us to recite every term. The first coefficient captures the largest spatial shape. The next nonzero coefficients correct its detail. Their signs do not directly tell us whether they raise or lower the temperature at the middle, because the sine waves themselves can be positive or negative there. Each contribution is a coefficient multiplied by a spatial shape. Keeping those two roles separate is essential when reconstructing a function from its modes.

Why does that representation help physically? Heat evolution acts on each sine component separately. The first broad wave decays relatively slowly. The third decays nine times as quickly in its exponential rate. The fifth decays twenty-five times as quickly. A few moments after heating stops, the small-scale corrections are largely gone and the temperature resembles the smooth first sine arch. Much later, even that arch fades. Thus a formula about trigonometric expansions explains what we actually see: thermal diffusion erases small-scale structure before large-scale structure.

It is possible to hear this as a purely technical trick, but Fourier was challenging assumptions about what a function could be. A jagged profile assembled from simple smooth waves looks paradoxical. How can infinitely many smooth functions add up to a shape with a corner, or even a jump? The answer requires care with limits. The partial sums are smooth, but the limiting function need not be smooth in the same way. At a discontinuity, a Fourier series often converges to the midpoint of its left and right limits rather than to a value arbitrarily assigned at the jump. This subtlety was not fully settled in Fourier's initial arguments. It helped provoke nineteenth-century debates about convergence, integrability, and the very definition of a function.

The most striking standard example is a square wave. On one half of a repeating cycle it has value positive one; on the other half it has value negative one. Instead of tracing a smooth oscillation, it switches abruptly. The Fourier series uses only odd sine frequencies, with amplitudes proportional to one over one, one over three, one over five, and so forth. The first sine wave gives a rough rounded approximation. Add a third-frequency wave and the flat regions improve. Add a fifth and seventh, and the transitions sharpen. Near a jump, overshoots remain as the approximation becomes narrower. That is the Gibbs phenomenon, studied later and not something Fourier himself formulated in modern terms.

The square-wave example raises a question about convergence. The individual sine terms are smooth and bounded, but infinite summation is an operation that must be justified. A formula that works at ordinary points may behave differently at discontinuities. Modern analysis has precise theorems stating when convergence holds pointwise, in mean square, or in other senses. Those are different promises. For physical heat flow, the situation is especially forgiving: at positive time the exponential damping of high frequencies smooths the series, even when the initial distribution is relatively rough. One lesson of Fourier's method is that the evolution equation can improve regularity.

Fourier did not invent every trigonometric technique he used. Euler, Daniel Bernoulli, d'Alembert, and Lagrange had argued about vibrating strings and the ability of trigonometric series to represent arbitrary initial shapes. Bernoulli emphasized sums of normal modes; others objected that such expressions seemed too narrow to describe arbitrary curves. Fourier expanded the idea into a systematic treatment of heat and pushed it toward a general procedure for coefficients. His contribution lies in the program he assembled, its physical application, and the force with which it moved questions of representability to the center of analysis.

How are coefficients found? Consider two sine waves with different whole-number frequencies on the same interval, each vanishing at the endpoints. Multiply them together and average over the interval. Their positive and negative regions cancel exactly, giving zero. Multiply a sine wave by itself and the average is positive. This orthogonality means that, to discover how much of a particular sine wave is present in an initial temperature, we multiply the profile by that wave, integrate, and divide by its squared size. The other frequencies disappear from the calculation because they are orthogonal.

In finite-dimensional geometry this is just a projection. If a vector points partly east and partly north, take its dot product with the east unit vector to extract the east coordinate. Fourier coefficients do the corresponding job for functions. The function is being treated like a vector with infinitely many possible directions. This connection eventually feeds into Hilbert spaces, spectral theory, signal processing, and the mathematical theory of operators. Fourier's physical problem helped make infinite-dimensional geometry feel like something one could calculate with.

There is another way to check the physical meaning of these modes without solving any elaborate equation. Consider a rod twice as long as the first, made from the same material. The largest spatial scale is now doubled. Because the slowest thermal decay rate is proportional to one over length squared, its decay time becomes four times as long. A short thin object can approach thermal equilibrium quickly, while a massive or extended object can retain a broad temperature difference much longer. The exact geometry and boundary conditions modify the numerical factor, but this square-of-length principle is a practical consequence of the heat equation. It tells an engineer how a change of size can change the response time even before the detailed calculation is complete.

Suppose we have two rods, each with both ends fixed at zero temperature. One is ten centimeters long, the other twenty. Give both the shape of their fundamental sine mode and the same maximum initial temperature. If the material diffusivity is equal, then at the moment the shorter rod's maximum has fallen to about thirty-seven percent of its initial value, the longer rod's maximum has fallen only to about seventy-eight percent. The numbers come from exponential decay: when one exponent reaches minus one, the other reaches minus one quarter. The example reveals that a decay time is a physical scale and that spatial size can be more important than the amount of heat initially supplied.

Consider also a steady temperature profile rather than a cooling one. If the left end of a uniform rod is kept at one hundred degrees and the right end at zero, and no heat is generated inside, then the steady solution has zero second derivative. Zero curvature means a straight temperature graph. Halfway along the rod, the temperature is fifty degrees in the ideal one-dimensional constant-conductivity model. This is a distinct use of the same mathematics: before asking how a temperature changes in time, determine the equilibrium that the boundary conditions enforce. If we subtract that equilibrium from an arbitrary initial state, the remaining difference satisfies the zero-end heat equation. Then the sine modes tell us how rapidly the rod approaches the steady profile.

This technique of subtracting a simple reference solution appears constantly in mathematics. Start with the part determined by the sustained forcing or boundary values. Then express the leftover deviation in modes that decay or oscillate independently. Such a method is often easier to teach because each object has a clear job. The steady profile satisfies the imposed endpoint temperatures. The transient profiles record the temporary memory of the starting state. Confusing the two can lead to an apparently correct series that violates the actual boundary conditions.

The Academy's reaction to Fourier's memoir was mixed. Reviewers recognized the originality of the problem and the importance of his techniques, yet demanded stronger justification for claims about trigonometric expansions. Fourier submitted a substantially developed version to an Academy competition and received the prize associated with the eighteen eleven submission, awarded in eighteen twelve. The history of this episode should not be compressed into a simple victory over foolish opponents. Fourier's broad ideas were enormously productive, and some of his mathematical claims needed exactly the rigor later analysts supplied. Skepticism about convergence was a legitimate mathematical concern even if individual criticisms varied in quality.

One further mathematical advance follows from considering the total size of a temperature fluctuation. Square the deviation from equilibrium and add those squared values along the rod. This gives a measure of the fluctuation's energy-like magnitude. For a rod with fixed zero-temperature ends, the heat equation makes that quantity decrease. The reason is that the derivative in time can be converted, by integration by parts, into minus twice the diffusivity times the sum of squared spatial slopes. A sum of squares cannot be negative, so its negative cannot be positive. This provides a monotonicity principle that does not require knowing the exact coefficients of every sine wave.

The calculation also explains uniqueness. Suppose two temperature functions satisfy the same initial state, the same heat equation, and the same boundary conditions. Subtract them. The difference has zero initial value and obeys the homogeneous equation. Its squared magnitude starts at zero and can only decrease, but squared magnitude is never negative. It must stay zero. The two solutions coincide. In modern language, this is an energy argument. Fourier's work helped establish the framework in which later analysts developed such proofs, and we should distinguish the modern proof from the arguments of his original memoir.

This separation between finding an expression and proving that it is the correct unique answer is central to mathematical physics. A formal series may be brilliant and physically plausible. A rigorous solution also requires conditions under which the sum converges, differentiations can be justified, boundary values are attained, and no second solution is hiding. The history of the heat equation contains both kinds of progress: the discovery of useful formulas and the later organization of a theory that says exactly when they work.

The resulting book, Théorie analytique de la chaleur, appeared in eighteen twenty-two. It was the mature statement of his mathematical physics. The book begins from observed relations among heat, temperature, conductivity, and boundaries, then develops differential equations and methods of solution. It showed that physical processes outside the classical mechanics of moving bodies could be mathematized in their own terms. Heat did not need to be reduced first to a complete mechanical theory of microscopic particles before one could formulate accurate mathematical laws for its spread.

Fourier's administrative career left marks on his scientific persona. After Napoleon's fortunes shifted, Fourier had to navigate another dangerous political transition. He later held positions in Paris, including work in statistical administration. He joined the Academy of Sciences and became its permanent secretary in eighteen twenty-two. He had written about Egypt, governed Isère, and worked as a leading mathematician. Such a wide-ranging career helps explain both his access to institutions and the costs of sustaining theoretical research through political change.

Let us broaden the mathematics from a finite rod to an infinite one. If the rod has no ends, sine waves are not restricted to integer frequencies fixed by boundary conditions. Any spatial frequency can contribute. Instead of summing a discrete set of modes, we integrate over a continuum of frequencies. This is the basic transition from Fourier series to the Fourier transform. A complicated signal is described by how strongly it contains oscillations at different frequencies. For heat diffusion, each frequency is multiplied by an exponential damping factor depending on frequency squared and elapsed time.

There is a precise physical meaning here. A tiny wiggle in temperature needs energy to move only a short distance before neighboring hot and cold spots cancel. It disappears quickly. A broad temperature wave spans a longer distance and takes longer to flatten. Because the decay rate scales as frequency squared, doubling the spatial frequency multiplies the rate by four. This is a scaling law, not merely a property of a convenient formula. It tells us how the physics responds when we shrink the spatial scale.

The Fourier transform has a second interpretation through the heat kernel. Suppose heat is concentrated very near one point on a very long rod at time zero. The temperature at later times takes the shape of a bell curve that widens with time and lowers in height while conserving total heat. Its width grows in proportion to the square root of elapsed time. That square-root scaling is characteristic of diffusion. The Gaussian bell curve is therefore more than a statistical ornament. It is the fundamental spatial response of the heat equation on an unbounded uniform line.

If two separate small heat sources are introduced, the later temperature is the sum of two such spreading bells, provided the ideal linear model applies. This is superposition. The system is linear, so complex initial states can be assembled from simple responses, either as frequency modes or as point-source contributions. These are two views of the same process, linked by Fourier transformation. One viewpoint decomposes a shape into oscillations; the other decomposes it into localized sources.

There is a further payoff. Heat conduction is mathematically related to the diffusion of particles, the spread of dissolved chemicals, and a class of probabilistic random walks. Imagine particles taking many independent tiny steps to the left or right. After many steps their distribution approaches a Gaussian under suitable assumptions. The same bell-shaped kernel appears in heat flow. The microscopic stories are different, but the large-scale equations share a structure. Fourier's method became a prototype for recognizing such cross-domain mathematical similarities.

A modern application offers a way to hear what the frequency viewpoint accomplishes. Think of a musical recording. A waveform over time can be represented as a combination of frequencies. Bass notes contribute lower-frequency components; high harmonics add brightness and sharpness. A Fourier transform exposes those ingredients. In a digital image, the analogous decomposition separates broad gradients from fine edges and texture. Compression, filtering, noise removal, and spectral analysis all use this family of ideas. None of these technologies was envisioned in Fourier's eighteenth-century childhood, but they illustrate the reach of a method devised for heat.

Fourier also studied the temperature of the Earth. In a paper published in eighteen twenty-four, he asked how solar heating, terrestrial heat, and the surrounding environment combine to determine the planet's temperature. He recognized that an atmosphere can alter the exchange of incoming light and outgoing heat. This is part of the prehistory of the greenhouse effect. Fourier did not identify carbon dioxide as the agent driving modern warming or calculate the contemporary radiative forcing formulas. Those developments depended on later measurements and theory, especially the study of absorption by atmospheric gases. His significance here is his effort to put the Earth's thermal balance into a mathematical physical framework.

Notice the difference between the rod and a planet. In the rod, we can usually ignore heat carried by moving air and treat the material properties as constant. The Earth receives solar radiation distributed unevenly across latitude and time; exchanges energy with atmosphere and space; and transports heat through oceans and winds. A global energy budget is already a much more complicated problem. Fourier knew enough to see that different sources and flows must be distinguished, even while a full modern climate model lay far beyond the scientific tools of his century. His interest in terrestrial temperatures shows how his mathematical theory was connected to a wider physical question from the beginning.

The atmospheric history is also a useful test of accurate attribution. Fourier's 1824 argument formed an early framework. John Tyndall later measured selective infrared absorption by gases. Eunice Foote had reported experiments involving carbon dioxide and warming in eighteen fifty-six. Svante Arrhenius made quantitative calculations of the effect of atmospheric carbon dioxide toward the end of the century. A lecture that compresses all of that into a single inventor of the greenhouse effect conceals how scientific understanding actually developed. Fourier deserves his share of the history without acquiring the work of scientists who followed him.

There are limits to the story. Not every function admits every kind of Fourier expansion with pointwise convergence. Not every physical system has constant conductivity or linear material response. Boundary conditions can be mixed, irregular, or time-dependent. An equation might couple temperature with fluid motion or chemical reactions. Fourier's simplest modes no longer solve the entire problem in one step. Yet his method remains valuable because the linear case supplies a vocabulary of modes, projections, time scales, and approximations for more complex situations.

We can make the bridge to modern numerical computation even more concrete. Suppose a temperature profile has been sampled at a hundred evenly spaced points. A discrete transform can express the sampled values through a finite collection of oscillating patterns. Instead of repeatedly applying a large matrix that approximates heat flow, we can transform into the frequency basis, multiply each frequency component by the appropriate decay factor, and transform back. For a suitable periodic discretization, the modes are exact eigenvectors of the discrete diffusion operator. The same idea underlies spectral methods for differential equations and some efficient digital filtering algorithms.

That finite computational story is not a claim that Fourier wrote computer code. It shows that his organizing principle remains useful when the unknown is represented by an array rather than a continuous function. Find coordinates in which the operator acts simply. Perform the easy operations there. Reconstruct the original representation. In linear algebra this is diagonalization. In harmonic analysis it becomes frequency decomposition. In physics it is often called normal-mode analysis.

Another important distinction concerns the history of rigor. Fourier's confidence in trigonometric representation drove later advances in analysis, including the work of Dirichlet, Riemann, Lebesgue, and many others. The mathematics became more precise by distinguishing convergence in an integral sense from convergence at each point. That progression did not diminish Fourier's insight. It revealed how many separate questions his program had opened. A physically motivated method had forced mathematicians to rethink the foundations of functions and infinite sums.

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries produced many great theories in mathematical physics, but Fourier's is distinctive in its starting point. He did not begin with a particle trajectory and a force law. He began with an entire field of temperature distributed through space. The unknown was itself a function. The equation governed the evolution of that function. Solutions came from an infinite collection of spatial modes. In that sense his work prepared mathematics for a world in which whole distributions, waves, and fields become the fundamental objects of reasoning.

Return now to the metal bar. At the beginning, the temperature may have a flat plateau, a sharp corner, and a few irregular hot spots. We could attempt to track every feature directly. Fourier gives a better organizing principle: discover the elementary spatial patterns that the heat operator treats independently, measure how much of each is present, and evolve each at its own rate. What appeared to be a disorderly physical curve becomes a sum of intelligible motions. The higher frequencies vanish first; the lower ones persist. The evolving temperature can then be reconstructed by adding them.

Joseph Fourier's achievement was both a scientific theory and a way of thinking. Choose physical quantities that can be measured. Translate local transport and conservation into a differential equation. Find the simple patterns that the equation preserves. Use those patterns to understand complicated initial states. And investigate carefully what is meant by the infinite sum. That method changed the mathematics of heat, but its influence reached far beyond the hot bar that first made the problem urgent.

## Display-only mathematical companion

### Heat equation from conservation and Fourier's law

For a rod with conductivity \(k>0\), density \(\rho>0\), specific heat \(c>0\), and temperature \(u(x,t)\), heat flux density is \(q=-k u_x\). Conservation over a small segment gives

\[
\rho c u_t=-q_x= k u_{xx},\qquad u_t=\alpha u_{xx},\quad \alpha=\frac{k}{\rho c}.
\]

### Separated modes for fixed zero-temperature endpoints

For \(0<x<L\), \(u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0\), the normalized spatial eigenmodes are \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\), and

\[
u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n e^{-\alpha (n\pi/L)^2t}\sin(n\pi x/L), \qquad
b_n=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^L f(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx.
\]

For the triangular tent \(f(x)=\min(x,L-x)\),

\[
b_n=\frac{4L}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{2}\right).
\]

The even coefficients vanish, and odd coefficients alternate in sign. This is a **modern reconstructed example**, not a claim about a figure in Fourier's memoir.

### Square wave and convergence

Let \(f(x)=1\) for \(0<x<\pi\) and \(f(x)=-1\) for \(\pi<x<2\pi\), extended with period \(2\pi\). Its series is

\[
f(x)\sim\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin x+\frac{\sin 3x}{3}+\frac{\sin5x}{5}+\cdots\right).
\]

At a jump the series converges to the average of the one-sided limits under the standard piecewise-smooth hypotheses; the Gibbs overshoot concerns partial sums.

### Infinite-line heat kernel

\[
G(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\alpha t}}e^{-x^2/(4\alpha t)},\qquad
u(x,t)=(G(\cdot,t)*f)(x).
\]

On the whole line \(\widehat u(\xi,t)=e^{-\alpha\xi^2t}\widehat f(\xi)\) for the standard transform convention \(\widehat f(\xi)=\int f(x)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx\). These formulas describe the ideal constant-diffusivity model.

### Historical distinctions

- Fourier submitted a heat memoir in 1807; the prize associated with a revised memoir was awarded in 1812 for the competition announced for 1811. His major book was published in 1822.
- Trigonometric normal-mode expansions had important antecedents in work on vibrating strings. Fourier's distinct contribution included a systematic heat theory and a far-reaching representability program.
- Strong general convergence and functional-analytic formulations came later. Do not attribute modern full rigor or transform theory in its present form to the 1807 manuscript.
