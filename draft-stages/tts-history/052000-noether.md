# 052000 — Emmy Noether: A mathematician in a world that would not employ her

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** family, university exclusion, mathematical friendship, Göttingen between war and dictatorship, and scholarly exile. **Background:** none beyond general curiosity about science and history.

When Emmy Noether arrived at the University of Göttingen in nineteen fifteen, she was already a respected mathematician. She had a doctorate, published research, addressed professional meetings, and helped younger scholars with their work. David Hilbert and Felix Klein had invited her because they needed precisely the kind of mathematical knowledge she possessed. Yet the university could not simply give her the kind of appointment a man of similar standing might have sought. It was willing to use her expertise while its rules stood in the way of recognizing her as an independent teacher.

The contradiction is a useful place to begin her life. Emmy Noether did not become important because famous men eventually noticed a person who otherwise had no intellectual community. She had spent years building her skills and relationships. Nor is her story adequately captured by the label of a neglected genius. She had colleagues, students, friends, disagreements, ambitions, and an appetite for discussion. Her work helped change mathematics and physics, but her life was shaped just as decisively by the laws and institutions of Germany, by the First World War, by the short-lived freedoms of the Weimar Republic, and finally by Nazi persecution. To see all of that is to understand how much it can take for a mathematical idea to find a place in the world.

She was born Amalie Emmy Noether in Erlangen, Bavaria, on March twenty-third, eighteen eighty-two. Her father, Max Noether, was a professor of mathematics at the university. Her mother, Ida Amalia Kaufmann, came from a merchant family in Cologne. The household was part of the educated Jewish middle class of the German Empire. The empire's universities could offer substantial intellectual opportunities to men such as Max Noether, while the routes open to their daughters remained much narrower. Emmy had three younger brothers, and family relationships would continue to matter long after she left Erlangen.

A schoolgirl in that household did not necessarily grow up expecting a research career. Emmy attended a higher girls' school, studied German, French, English, and music, and enjoyed dancing. She prepared for a socially recognized occupation available to educated women: teaching languages. In nineteen hundred she qualified to teach English and French in Bavarian girls' schools. She had done well on the examinations. If she had followed that path, she might have had a profession and an income considerably sooner than she did. Instead she chose mathematics, even though the institutions surrounding her could offer no straightforward career within it.

At the time, women could not simply enroll at Erlangen in the ordinary way. To attend lectures, Noether needed the consent of individual professors. For a period she was an auditor, physically present in rooms where she was not yet granted the full status of a student. She also spent a semester in Göttingen in nineteen oh three and nineteen oh four, hearing courses by Hilbert, Klein, and other leading mathematicians. In nineteen oh four, after the rules changed, she was able to matriculate formally at Erlangen. The distinction between sitting in a classroom by permission and belonging to a university as a matter of right was not trivial. It affected examinations, credentials, and every later stage of academic employment.

Under Paul Gordan, Noether completed her doctorate in nineteen oh seven. Gordan was famous for an exacting, computational approach to questions about algebraic expressions and the patterns that remain when those expressions are transformed. Her dissertation followed that tradition and involved a large and laborious investigation. It was not yet the style of mathematics most closely associated with her name. It is tempting to describe the dissertation as a mistaken beginning before her real genius arrived. A more useful interpretation is that she learned one powerful working tradition deeply enough to recognize both what it could do and what it could not easily express.

A doctorate did not solve her employment problem. The next usual German academic qualification, the habilitation, remained effectively closed to women. Noether stayed at Erlangen, helped her father, worked on her research, and participated in the mathematical life of the university without receiving the secure appointment that would have made this activity a conventional career. She advised younger researchers, some of whom were formally supervised by her father because university rules did not give her the appropriate standing. An institutional record might therefore understate her contribution even when the people involved knew whose guidance they had received.

Ernst Fischer, who succeeded Gordan at Erlangen, became especially important to her development. Noether later credited him with encouraging her to look at algebra through more general structural ideas. This was not a case of an established professor handing a finished theory to a passive pupil. It was a sustained mathematical relationship, nourished through conversation and correspondence, in which a researcher with formidable skills was changing the questions she considered worth asking. The names in a bibliography are only part of a mathematical education; another part happens when someone challenges the way a problem has always been framed.

By the time Hilbert and Klein invited her back to Göttingen in nineteen fifteen, Noether had a professional reputation of her own. Europe was at war. Göttingen was an important German university and a center for mathematics and theoretical physics, but it was not insulated from the upheaval outside. Researchers in Germany, France, Britain, and other countries had long exchanged ideas across borders that had suddenly become battle lines. At Göttingen, Hilbert was struggling with fundamental problems in the new theory of gravitation. Einstein's general relativity was emerging, and the question of what could properly count as a symmetry or a conservation law was becoming urgent. Noether was asked to join these discussions because her expertise was relevant to a problem the leading men in the room could not simply settle without her.

The famous result that followed is often compressed to a slogan: symmetry gives conservation. It is worth understanding what that means as a piece of history rather than stopping to develop the mathematics. Physicists had long relied on laws such as the conservation of energy and momentum. Noether showed that these principles could be understood through a general relationship between the continuous symmetries of a theory and the equations governing its motion. If the relevant physical laws are unchanged when we shift the origin of time, for example, an energy conservation principle is associated with that invariance. The theory is much broader and more precise than this illustration. Its historical power lay in revealing a common structure beneath results that had often been treated separately.

Noether's nineteen eighteen paper also addressed another kind of symmetry, with consequences for identities connecting field equations. Its two theorems matter in different ways, particularly in the setting of relativity. Recent historical scholarship, drawing on surviving working papers, reconstructs an active exchange among Noether, Hilbert, and Klein during these years. They argued, corrected one another, and pursued related questions. That makes the work collaborative in its intellectual setting, but it does not dissolve the identity of the person who formulated and proved the general theorems. The paper is Noether's. Her colleagues' involvement helps explain how the problem arose, not why her authorship should be diminished.

The university still resisted recognizing her. The difficulty was not that she lacked the necessary mathematics. Regulations in Prussia had explicitly restricted the habilitation of women. Earlier efforts to secure her admission failed. For some courses, Hilbert listed himself as the formal lecturer while Noether did the teaching. This arrangement permitted students to learn from her while leaving the broader exclusion in place. It is hard to find a clearer illustration of the gap between the actual production of knowledge and the institutional record of who is allowed to produce it.

The First World War ended in nineteen eighteen, and Germany entered a period of revolution and constitutional change. The monarchy fell; the Weimar Republic began. Among the many consequences was a change in the conditions under which women could seek university teaching rights. In nineteen nineteen Noether finally obtained her habilitation at Göttingen. It was a meaningful change, but not the end of her financial difficulties. The new status did not immediately bring a salary. Göttingen's own institutional history records that she received a small fixed payment for teaching only in nineteen twenty-three. A person could become a central figure in her field and remain materially insecure for years.

Noether's mathematical interests were also changing. Around this period she increasingly pursued abstract algebra. To a listener outside the subject, the word abstract can suggest withdrawal from the world. For Noether it meant searching for relationships that apply to many different mathematical objects at once. Instead of finding a clever trick for one particular polynomial or number system, she sought language for common patterns of operations and substructures. Her work on ideals in rings helped make modern algebra a field organized around general concepts rather than isolated computational recipes. This was an intellectual choice with a social life: students and collaborators took it up, tested it against problems, and carried it into new areas.

Her teaching was central to that spread. Those who worked with Noether remembered an animated, talkative mathematician who often thought through ideas in conversation. Her circle became known informally as the Noether school; some accounts use the phrase 'Noether boys' for her students and followers. We should not let that familiar label make the participants interchangeable or erase the distinct work they contributed. Bartel van der Waerden, for example, helped communicate the new structural algebra through his influential textbook *Modern Algebra*, informed by the Göttingen mathematical environment and by Noether's ideas. Her influence reached beyond what appeared under her own name on a journal title page.

Conversation was not always comfortable. Historical recollections describe lectures that could be difficult for listeners accustomed to calculations leading quickly to a numerical answer. Noether was interested in the general structure that explained why several apparently different calculations belonged together. Some colleagues admired the shift; others found her approach demanding or too remote from concrete examples. Neither reaction is surprising. A new mathematical style has to be learned by people trained in older habits, and a teacher may be both intellectually generous and challenging to follow.

She also had a life and temperament outside any formal description of her methods. Recollections by Hermann Weyl portray someone with a direct, open manner and strong convictions. In the turmoil following the war, he remembered her taking an active interest in the political discussions around her and sympathizing with the Social Democrats without becoming a party official. He described her as a serious pacifist. These are a colleague's memories, not a diary that lets us recover every private thought. They help, however, to place her inside the arguments of the Weimar years rather than pretending she existed in an apolitical realm of pure symbols.

In nineteen thirty-two Noether gave an invited address at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Zurich. International recognition now existed alongside a career that had been denied ordinary forms of recognition at home. A photograph from that period shows a gathering of mathematicians outside a Göttingen inn, a reminder that much of a research community's life happened in informal conversations as well as in lecture halls. This was a world of friends, visitors, letters, and meals, held together by shared work. It was also a world whose security depended on political decisions made well beyond the university.

In January nineteen thirty-three Hitler became chancellor. The Nazi regime rapidly moved to purge Jewish people and political opponents from public employment and academic life. Noether, who was Jewish, lost her Göttingen position that year. There is a temptation to turn every account of her dismissal into a scene in which mathematics triumphs over politics because the persecuted mathematician remains personally brave. That would misunderstand the event. The government took away her livelihood and access to an institution she had helped make important. The fact that she continued to think and teach could not undo that loss. Across Germany, the destruction of universities as places of open inquiry was carried out through laws, dismissals, intimidation, and the expulsion of people whose work had built those institutions.

Noether's response, as recorded in contemporary and later testimony, was strikingly measured toward individuals around her, including some whose political choices others found unforgivable. Weyl later recalled her openness even toward students associated with the Nazi movement. We should not make this into a general moral prescription for victims of persecution, or confuse personal temperament with agreement. The documented fact is that she maintained relationships and mathematical discussions amid hostility. Her colleagues also saw clearly that the political disaster was not merely a quarrel inside a faculty; it was a government asserting the power to decide who could belong to German intellectual life.

Help came from across the Atlantic. Anna Pell Wheeler, who led mathematics at Bryn Mawr College in Pennsylvania, was instrumental in bringing Noether to the United States. Bryn Mawr was a women's college with an established tradition of higher mathematical education, not an accidental refuge invented only because a famous European arrived. Wheeler had her own distinguished career and knew what it meant to build serious mathematics in institutions that could offer women a place. The support that brought Noether there was part of a larger effort to help scholars displaced by the Nazi regime, although the opportunities were limited and many others never found safety.

Noether sailed to the United States in the autumn of nineteen thirty-three. At Bryn Mawr she taught small groups of advanced students and worked in a setting different in scale and organization from Göttingen. An archival record preserves materials concerning her teaching and correspondence. She assigned work from van der Waerden's *Modern Algebra*, a book whose intellectual origins led back to the seminars she had helped shape in Germany. It is a moving continuity, but not a sentimental one: the mathematics could travel in books, correspondence, and people even after the institution that nurtured it had expelled its teacher.

She also traveled to Princeton to give lectures at the Institute for Advanced Study, meeting mathematicians who had arrived from several different national traditions. She could be humorous about the challenge of teaching an audience accustomed to explicit computations when she wanted to lead them toward abstract structures. Her letters reveal someone still experimenting with how to reach particular listeners, not a monument who had completed a life's work and stopped changing. At Bryn Mawr, faculty and students were learning from her; she in turn was learning the habits of a new academic setting.

Then, in April nineteen thirty-five, she underwent an operation for a tumor. She initially seemed to be recovering but died suddenly from complications, on April fourteenth. She was fifty-three. Her death ended a career that had recently found a more welcoming institutional home after so many obstacles. It was not the inevitable ending of an exile story, and it should not be narrated as a sacrifice for mathematics. It was the death of a person who had more work, conversations, and relationships she might have pursued.

Her colleagues' tributes help us distinguish the different kinds of legacy she left. Weyl emphasized the way she stimulated others, how many ideas took form through students and co-workers rather than only in her own publications. Albert Einstein praised her after her death, but she did not need a famous physicist's endorsement to become a major mathematician. The notebooks, articles, students, and working relationships are evidence of her achievement in their own right. If we remember only the symmetry theorem, we miss the transformation she helped bring to algebra; if we remember only the algebra, we miss her contributions to physics. And if we remember either without the unpaid labor, the rules that restricted her, the political conversations, and the displacement of nineteen thirty-three, we miss the conditions in which those contributions were made.

There is a larger historical lesson in the gap between her work and her official position. The achievements of a university are often attributed to its famous institutions and professors, yet those institutions may fail to recognize the people who make the achievements possible. A person can be central to a field and peripheral to the salary ledger. The mathematical record preserves some contributions under names other than the names of the people who first shaped them in a seminar. Political power can close a classroom even when the people inside have produced knowledge valuable to the world. Noether's biography does not ask us to admire perseverance in the abstract. It asks us to see a gifted, sociable, difficult, generous, politically situated researcher making a career under constraints that were real, then rebuilding it in exile.

Emmy Noether's life began in an educated household in imperial Bavaria and ended at a women's college in Pennsylvania. Between those places lay Erlangen, Göttingen, a world war, a democratic republic, a dictatorship, and an ocean crossing. Her mathematics traveled across them, but it did not travel by itself. It moved through students, friends, books, invitations, arguments, and the institutions that first denied and then welcomed her. That is the history of the person behind the theorems.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and place

- **1882:** Born in Erlangen, Bavaria, to Max Noether and Ida Amalia Kaufmann.
- **1900–1904:** Qualifies to teach languages; audits courses in Erlangen and Göttingen before formal admission to Erlangen in 1904.
- **1907:** Doctorate under Paul Gordan; subsequent research and teaching work at Erlangen without a standard university appointment.
- **1915:** Invited to Göttingen by David Hilbert and Felix Klein; contributes to discussions of invariants and relativity.
- **1918:** Publishes *Invariante Variationsprobleme* (Invariant Variational Problems), containing the two results known as Noether's theorems.
- **1919:** Receives habilitation after previous failed applications; later gains professorial title, but salary for teaching only in 1923.
- **1921:** Publishes *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen*, a major contribution to structural algebra.
- **1932:** Invited address at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Zurich.
- **1933:** Dismissed by the Nazi regime and relocates to Bryn Mawr College, Pennsylvania.
- **1934–1935:** Teaches at Bryn Mawr and visits the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton.
- **1935:** Dies in Bryn Mawr, aged fifty-three, after complications following an operation.

### Scientific orientation

Noether's **first theorem** connects continuous global variational symmetries with conservation laws; her **second theorem** concerns local symmetries depending on arbitrary functions and identities among the Euler–Lagrange equations. These are not interchangeable claims. Her work in **abstract algebra**, especially on chains of ideals and the structure of rings, helped turn algebra toward general relationships rather than catalogues of special calculations. The full statements and proofs belong in dedicated mathematics and physics lectures.

### Evidence and attribution cautions

Her invitations, exclusions, appointments, and eventual displacement are backed by university histories and archival holdings. Tilman Sauer's 2025 research reconstructs the 1915–1918 collaboration using working papers and corrects over-simple priority narratives; authorship of *Invariante Variationsprobleme* remains Noether's. Recollections of personality, politics, and teaching are identified as testimony of colleagues, not access to her private thoughts. Bryn Mawr and Institute for Advanced Study records document her American teaching. The many important figures around her are collaborators and institutions, not substitutes for her agency.
