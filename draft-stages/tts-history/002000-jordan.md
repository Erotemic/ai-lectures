# Camille Jordan: How can two higher-dimensional spaces have several angles?

Two lines have one angle. Two planes in a space of four or five dimensions can meet along some directions and disagree along others. Describing their relative position takes more than one number.

Camille Jordan was a French mathematician whose work helped build the geometry and algebra of higher-dimensional spaces. He is also associated with canonical forms, permutation groups, and the program of classifying mathematical objects by invariants. In an 1875 essay on geometry in several dimensions, Jordan studied the relative positions of subspaces. Principal angles belong to this geometric tradition.

To build a mental picture, take a plane spanned by the first two coordinate axes in four-dimensional space. Keep its first direction fixed, and rotate its second direction toward a new perpendicular axis. One principal angle is zero: there is a shared direction. The other is the angle of the rotating direction. Now rotate the first direction too, but in a different perpendicular two-dimensional plane. The two principal angles can be different.

The construction gives a disciplined way to choose corresponding directions. Start by finding a pair of unit vectors, one in each subspace, with the largest possible inner product. Their angle is the first principal angle. Then look for another pair orthogonal to the first choices and repeat. In finite dimension this produces a list of angles, ordered from smallest to largest. The cosine values also appear as singular values of an overlap matrix between orthonormal frames.

This is more informative than comparing arbitrary bases. Change a basis inside either subspace and the columns may look completely different, even though the spaces have not moved. Principal angles remain unchanged.

Jordan's place in this story is geometric rather than computational. Later investigators turned the angles into operator norms, projection differences, and error bounds. But the essential observation precedes those estimates: the relative position of two subspaces has an intrinsic set of angular measurements.

When a spectral cluster contains several eigenvectors, we usually care about the subspace they span. Jordan's geometric viewpoint tells us how to describe its movement without relying on a particular choice of eigenvectors.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For orthonormal frames \(E,F\) with the same column count, the singular values
of \(E^*F\) are \(\cos\theta_i\). In \(\mathbb R^4\), take
\(K=\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)\) and
\(L=\operatorname{span}(\cos\alpha\,e_1+\sin\alpha\,e_3,
\cos\beta\,e_2+\sin\beta\,e_4)\).
The principal angles are the unordered pair \(\{\alpha,\beta\}\) for \(0\le\alpha,\beta\le\pi/2\).
