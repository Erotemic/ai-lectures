# Camille Jordan: What survives when coordinates change?

Imagine two people describing the same geometric plane. One chooses a pair of arrows pointing along the edges of a square. The other chooses arrows running diagonally across it. Their coordinate lists look different. Their matrices look different. But neither person has changed the plane. Which properties belong to the mathematical object itself, and which belong only to the way we choose to describe it?

That question connects several of the achievements of the nineteenth-century French mathematician Camille Jordan. It links his work on substitutions and groups, the canonical structure of linear transformations, the geometry of angles between subspaces, and even his investigations of curves in the plane. Those subjects do not form a single theorem, and Jordan did not invent every idea that now carries his name. The recurring mathematical problem is nevertheless productive: we want descriptions that survive a change of viewpoint.

The most direct example comes from comparing two planes in a space with four coordinates. In ordinary three-dimensional space, two distinct planes that meet along a line can be described by one nonzero dihedral angle. In four dimensions, one direction of a plane can move a little, while a second, independent direction moves much more. A single angle cannot describe both motions. Jordan's 1875 work on geometry in several dimensions belongs to the historical development of what we now call principal angles between subspaces. We will calculate those angles carefully. But to understand why Jordan asked geometric questions in this way, it helps to begin with the algebraic world in which he worked.

Camille Jordan was born in Lyon in 1838. His full name was Marie Ennemond Camille Jordan. He entered the École Polytechnique in Paris in 1855, as part of a French tradition in which demanding mathematical training often led to careers in engineering. He worked as a mining engineer while pursuing mathematical research and earned his doctorate in 1861. Eventually he taught analysis at the École Polytechnique and mathematics at the Collège de France. This dual career is more than a colorful biographical detail. It places his mathematics at a time when engineering problems, geometry, algebra, and analysis were exchanging tools, and the boundaries between specialties were less settled than they look in a modern course catalog.

Much of Jordan's early work grew out of a practical algebraic puzzle that had fascinated mathematicians since the development of algebraic equations: why can some polynomial equations be solved systematically using radicals while others resist a comparable formula? Évariste Galois had recognized that the symmetries among a polynomial's roots hold important information about that question. Jordan helped turn those insights into an organized mathematical theory. In 1870 he published the substantial Treatise on Substitutions and Algebraic Equations, a landmark in the development of permutation-group theory.

A substitution, in this historical setting, means a rearrangement. Take three labeled objects: one, two, and three. You can leave them all in place, exchange the first two, or send the first to the second position, the second to the third, and the third back to the first. Each rearrangement is a permutation. Two permutations can be performed in succession, and their combined effect is another permutation. The identity does nothing. Every permutation can be undone. These simple properties are the beginnings of a group.

Notice what we have done. We have stopped describing a polynomial only by a list of coefficients, and started asking which rearrangements of its roots preserve the algebraic relations that matter. Different ways of labeling those roots give different written permutations. But the structure of the allowed rearrangements persists. That is the kind of invariance Jordan's algebra sought to understand.

Consider an ordinary quadratic equation with two distinct roots. There are at most two ways to permute those roots: keep them fixed or exchange them. The familiar quadratic formula uses a square root, and the exchange corresponds to changing the sign of that square root. A cubic equation can have a more elaborate group of permitted root permutations. Its algebraic solution requires understanding more than a single exchange. By the time we reach a general polynomial of degree five, the possible symmetries can obstruct a formula built from radicals. This is the setting in which Galois theory answers a structural question about equations. Jordan's contribution was to systematize and extend that theory, especially for groups represented concretely as permutations.

There is a helpful elementary model of the same idea that avoids solving an actual fifth-degree equation. Suppose we start with the six permutations of three objects. Three of them are even permutations: the identity and the two cyclic rearrangements. Together they form a subgroup with three elements. The whole group has six. The quotient between them has two elements. We have broken a structure of size six into a three-element part and a two-element part. Those smaller pieces are easier to analyze, although the original group is not simply a direct product of the two. Its multiplication law still remembers how the pieces interact.

A composition series carries this idea further. It is a chain of groups, with each successive group normal in the one before it, and with no further proper normal subgroup available between adjacent terms. The successive quotients are simple groups: they have no nontrivial proper normal subgroup. A chain can be chosen in different ways, but the celebrated Jordan–Hölder theorem says that the list of simple quotient groups is determined up to reordering and isomorphism. The familiar modern formulation reflects both Jordan's foundational work on composition series and Otto Hölder's later clarification of the factor-group invariant.

For an audible example, consider addition modulo six. One chain goes from the full six-element group to its three-element subgroup, and then to the identity. Its successive quotient sizes are two and three. Another chain passes through the two-element subgroup instead. Its quotient sizes are three and two. The intermediate groups are different, and the order of the pieces is different, but the essential simple pieces agree. This is a classification principle: instead of demanding that two descriptions look identical step by step, we ask which structural parts both descriptions must share.

Jordan's 1870 treatise helped make that style of reasoning central. It also included work related to a second classification problem: how should a linear transformation be represented when a clever change of coordinates makes its structure visible? In a modern linear algebra course, the answer often begins with eigenvectors. If a transformation has enough independent eigenvectors, we can use them as coordinate axes and write the transformation as a diagonal matrix. Each coordinate then evolves independently. But not every matrix has enough eigenvectors. What, then, can an invariant description look like?

Take a transformation of the plane that sends the first coordinate axis to twice itself and sends the second coordinate axis to twice itself plus one copy of the first. In the standard basis its matrix has twos on the diagonal, a one just above the diagonal, and zero below. Its only eigenvalue is two. The first axis is an eigenvector because that direction is simply doubled. The second axis is not an eigenvector. It is doubled and also pushed sideways. No second independent eigenvector exists, so the matrix cannot be diagonalized.

This is a Jordan block of size two. Its extra off-diagonal one records the fact that the transformation cannot be decomposed into two independent eigendirections. The block is still highly structured. It tells us there is an eigenvector, and another vector that is carried one step toward it when we subtract the eigenvalue times the identity. That second vector is called a generalized eigenvector. The pair forms a chain. Longer blocks contain longer chains of generalized eigenvectors.

The point of the Jordan canonical form is that, over the complex numbers, every finite square matrix is similar to a block-diagonal matrix made of such Jordan blocks. Similarity means that we have changed coordinates in the domain and codomain by the same invertible basis transformation. The original matrix might look complicated, but the collection of eigenvalues and sizes of Jordan blocks is intrinsic, apart from the order in which the blocks are arranged. Jordan's 1870 work contributed to this canonical-form tradition, while related work of Weierstrass and later authors is an essential part of the full historical development. The compact modern theorem should not be projected unchanged into one nineteenth-century publication.

Why is the form useful? In the two-by-two block with eigenvalue two, multiply the matrix by itself. The diagonal scaling multiplies by two each time, but the sideways push accumulates. After two applications, the off-diagonal entry becomes four. After three, it becomes twelve. In general, after a positive whole number of applications, the sideways contribution is proportional to that whole number times a power of two. A pure diagonal matrix would give only exponential scaling along independent directions. The Jordan block reveals a polynomial factor accompanying the exponential growth.

The same pattern appears in differential equations. Suppose the rate of change of a two-coordinate state equals our Jordan block acting on that state. The solution involves an exponential factor from the eigenvalue and an additional factor growing linearly in time from the generalized-eigenvector chain. This explains why repeated eigenvalues alone cannot predict every feature of a linear dynamical system. We must know whether the matrix is diagonalizable and, if it is not, how its Jordan chains are arranged.

There is a limit to the usefulness of this classification in numerical work. Consider a matrix with ones on the diagonal and a tiny positive number just above it. No matter how tiny that number is, as long as it is nonzero, the matrix has a nontrivial Jordan block. If the number becomes exactly zero, the matrix turns into the identity and becomes diagonalizable. A microscopic change in the entries has changed the exact block structure. Thus Jordan form is a powerful algebraic invariant but an unstable numerical target. Reliable algorithms usually prefer decompositions based on orthogonal or unitary changes of basis. That distinction will be important when we turn from canonical forms to the geometry of subspaces.

Jordan's algebraic and geometric work were connected by his interest in transformations. In the nineteenth century, groups of motions, substitutions, and changes of coordinates offered different ways to classify geometric situations. Jordan studied groups of Euclidean motions, and the questions attracted visitors such as Sophus Lie and Felix Klein to Paris around 1870. Lie's continuous transformation groups and Klein's geometric program developed in their own directions. Jordan's work was part of a larger shift toward treating symmetry as a mathematical object worthy of study in its own right.

Now return to the question about two planes in four dimensions. The source plane is spanned by the first and second coordinate axes. For the target plane, rotate the first axis toward the third axis by thirty degrees. Separately rotate the second axis toward the fourth axis by sixty degrees. These rotations take place in perpendicular two-dimensional coordinate planes. The resulting two target vectors are still perpendicular, and together they span a plane. Neither target basis vector needs to line up with an original coordinate axis.

What is the angle between the source plane and the target plane? We cannot answer with only thirty degrees, because the second direction has moved by sixty degrees. We cannot answer with only sixty degrees, because another direction has moved less. And taking an ordinary average of the two would discard the fact that one direction is more closely aligned than the other. The natural answer is a pair of principal angles: thirty degrees and sixty degrees.

Here is how to obtain them without depending on the original coordinate labels. Pick one unit vector in the source plane and one in the target plane. Ask for the largest possible dot product. The largest dot product corresponds to the smallest angle between such vectors. Once we have found that best-aligned pair, restrict attention to vectors perpendicular to it within each plane. Find the best-aligned pair again. Continue for as many independent directions as the smaller subspace permits. In our example, the best first overlap is the cosine of thirty degrees. The remaining overlap is the cosine of sixty degrees. The angle list reports two independent ways in which the planes can be relatively positioned.

That successive optimization is easy to state, but it raises an immediate concern. If the best-aligned directions have several equally good choices, do we get different answers? And how can we compute the list without searching over infinitely many unit vectors? The modern answer uses orthonormal coordinate frames and singular values. The individual principal vectors may not be unique when angles repeat, but the ordered principal angles are.

Choose any orthonormal basis of the source plane and any orthonormal basis of the target plane. Form a small overlap matrix whose entries are dot products between source basis vectors and target basis vectors. In our example, using the natural bases, it is a diagonal two-by-two matrix with entries approximately zero point eight six six and zero point five. These are the cosines of thirty and sixty degrees. If somebody rotates the source basis within its own plane, the rows are mixed by an orthogonal matrix. If somebody rotates the target basis, the columns are mixed by another orthogonal matrix. Individual overlap entries change. But the singular values of the overlap matrix do not change under those orthogonal basis transformations. Therefore the angle list stays the same.

That is a genuinely coordinate-free answer. A singular value measures a size that remains unchanged by orthonormal rotations of the coordinates on the left and right. The principal angles are obtained by applying the inverse cosine to those singular values, ordered from smallest angle to largest. One does not need to preserve the original choice of arrows within either plane. Modern numerical methods compute this invariant efficiently with a singular-value decomposition. That computational method came long after Jordan's nineteenth-century essay, and its later development belongs to a history including many other people.

One more calculation shows the geometry behind the abstract formula. Instead of asking which directions are closest, we can ask how far the source projection differs from the target projection. A projection takes any vector and keeps only its component lying in a chosen subspace. With two subspaces, their projections make the same decision on a direction they share; they make different decisions on a direction that has rotated away. In the first of our two independent coordinate planes, the relevant difference has size equal to the sine of thirty degrees, or one half. In the second coordinate plane it has size equal to the sine of sixty degrees, about zero point eight six six. The largest possible projection difference is therefore the latter number. The two principal angles tell us both where the greatest disagreement occurs and how much total squared disagreement there is.

There is a second, more cumulative way to measure that disagreement. Square the sine of thirty degrees and the sine of sixty degrees. We obtain one quarter and three quarters. Their sum is one. Each independent rotation contributes twice to the sum of squared entries in the difference between the two full projection matrices, once for each side of its two-dimensional plane. Thus the total squared Frobenius distance between the projections is two. This is a useful modern connection between Jordan's geometric angle viewpoint and matrix norm estimates. The largest sine governs the operator norm of the projection difference; the sum of squared sines governs the Frobenius norm. Different questions demand different summaries of the same angle list.

What happens if the source and target planes share a direction? Set the first rotation to zero. Then the first principal angle is zero and its cosine is one. That does not mean the whole planes agree. The other angle can still be sixty degrees. What happens if a direction of the source plane is perpendicular to every direction of the target plane? One principal angle is a right angle, and its cosine is zero. Thus principal angles distinguish exact overlap, partial alignment, and complete orthogonality in different directions. A statement about two subspaces should not confuse one shared direction with equality of the full spaces.

These distinctions become valuable in spectral perturbation theory, which studies how eigenvalues and invariant subspaces move when an operator changes. Suppose a matrix has two nearly equal eigenvalues corresponding to a two-dimensional spectral cluster. Small perturbations can dramatically change which single vector is selected as the eigenvector for the smaller eigenvalue. Yet the two-dimensional span of both eigenvectors may remain fixed. Principal angles give a way to measure the stable subspace as a whole. The modern Davis–Kahan inequalities quantify this behavior using spectral separation, residuals, and operator norms. Those theorems are twentieth-century work; Jordan's earlier geometric contribution provides one of the relevant measuring tools.

A concrete case makes the point. Start with a diagonal three-by-three matrix. Its first two diagonal entries are one and one plus a tiny positive number. The third is ten. Now exchange the first two entries. The matrix has changed by only the tiny amount, measured in operator norm. But the eigenvector for the smallest eigenvalue has switched from the first axis to the second: a right-angle change. If instead we treat the two eigenvalues near one as a cluster, the corresponding two-dimensional subspace is the first two coordinate axes before and after the exchange. Its two principal angles with itself are both zero. The relevant gap for the cluster is the large separation from ten. The small internal gap does not make the entire cluster geometrically unstable.

Jordan's 1875 essay on geometry in several dimensions is an important historical source for the idea of such intrinsic angles. It does not contain the modern singular-value algorithm in the language we use today, nor the later perturbation bounds that connect gaps and angle estimates. The history is a chain of mathematical developments, rather than a single person discovering all the subsequent applications. In this recording, the overlap-matrix derivation is a modern way of making the geometry intelligible. It should be understood as an explanation of a connection, not a claim that Jordan used the exact matrix notation or algorithms now familiar to numerical analysts.

The same distinction between classification and computation appeared elsewhere in Jordan's work. In the 1870s he investigated finite groups of invertible complex matrices. Imagine a finite collection of such matrices that is closed under multiplication and inversion. If the matrices are all diagonal, they commute: multiplying two diagonal matrices gives the same result in either order. They are easy to describe through the independent scaling factors on each coordinate. But finite matrix groups need not be diagonal or even abelian. How far can they be from such a simple case?

Jordan proved a remarkable answer in 1878. Fix the size of the matrices. Every finite subgroup of invertible complex matrices has a normal abelian subgroup whose index is bounded by a constant depending only on that matrix size. The index counts how many cosets of the simpler subgroup are required to cover the whole group. The bound does not depend on how many elements the original finite group has. The structural message is strong: in any fixed matrix dimension, a finite linear group contains a large commuting part, in a precise relative sense. This is a classification theorem, not a claim that every finite matrix group is itself abelian.

To see why one cannot hope for a bound on the total number of matrices, consider all scalar matrices obtained by multiplying the identity by an mth root of unity. For any positive integer m, these form a finite group of order m. The dimension can stay at two while m grows as large as we please. So the group size is unbounded. But this example is itself abelian: all its matrices commute. It is consistent with Jordan's theorem because the whole group may serve as the normal abelian subgroup. The theorem bounds the complexity remaining after we account for commuting structure, rather than bounding the number of possible symmetries.

For a less trivial picture, take matrices that independently change coordinate signs and also exchange the two coordinate axes. The diagonal sign changes commute with each other. Exchanging the axes need not commute with a particular sign change. Nevertheless the diagonal sign-change subgroup is normal and has index two in the full group. We can see both features: a manageable commuting core and a small number of additional transformations that permute its coordinates. In higher dimensions, Jordan's theorem says a controlled version of this phenomenon survives for every finite complex linear group, even when its construction is much less transparent.

The motivation for the theorem was connected to linear differential equations with algebraic solutions and the groups arising from their analytic continuation. In modern language, following a solution around a closed path may transform a chosen basis of solutions by an invertible matrix. Collecting these transformations gives a monodromy group. If the group is finite, Jordan's theorem restricts its possible structure. Emmanuel Breuillard's modern exposition of Jordan's original proof emphasizes this differential-equation motivation and how different Jordan's algebraic reasoning was from many later geometric proofs. Once more, the right invariant gives information about a problem whose original appearance can be quite different.

But Jordan's influence reached beyond algebra and high-dimensional geometry. He also helped make the foundations of analysis more rigorous. Consider a closed curve drawn in a sheet of paper: begin at one point, move continuously along the curve, and return to the start without crossing yourself. It is natural to call the bounded region inside and the rest of the plane outside. For a circle or a simple polygon, the picture is immediate. For a wildly twisting continuous curve, the picture no longer supplies a proof. What exactly does it mean for the curve to separate the plane, and how can we be certain that there are precisely two connected regions outside the curve?

The Jordan curve theorem answers that question. A simple closed curve in the plane divides the complement of the curve into exactly two connected components, with the curve forming the common boundary. Simple means that the curve has no self-intersections, aside from its initial and terminal point being identified. The curve may bend and wiggle in complicated ways. What counts is its continuous, non-self-crossing structure. A figure eight, by contrast, has a crossing and does not satisfy that hypothesis. Its regions can have a different topology.

Try to turn the intuitive claim into an argument. If the curve is a polygon, one can track intersections between the boundary and a ray extending from a point toward infinity. Crossing the boundary changes whether the point is treated as inside or outside. For a very irregular continuous curve, however, a ray may touch the boundary at many points or behave in ways that defeat naive crossing counts. One needs a carefully defined topological invariant and a proof that the classification behaves as intended. The difficulty is precisely why a claim that seems obvious in a drawing became an important theorem in analysis and topology.

Jordan presented a proof in his analysis text, and the history of its rigor is more complicated than the stock story that it simply failed and was later repaired. Oswald Veblen's 1905 proof became a standard rigorous treatment. Jordan's proof was criticized for omitted details, including how it handled polygonal curves. Much later, Thomas Hales examined Jordan's argument and argued that its core reasoning had been undervalued, and that its defects were largely expository rather than a fatal mathematical mistake. The fair historical point is that establishing the theorem with complete, explicit assumptions required work, and that the evaluation of Jordan's original proof has itself been studied. We should resist turning a subtle episode into a confident one-sentence verdict.

Jordan's analysis also treated the variation of functions. There is a useful elementary example. Imagine walking along the graph of a function, moving from left to right. Count the total amount by which its height rises or falls, regardless of direction. If the function rises by two units and later falls by one, that part of the journey contributes three units of variation. We can divide the horizontal interval into finer and finer pieces and add the absolute height changes from one piece to the next. The total variation is the supremum over all finite partitions. A function has bounded variation when that supremum is finite.

This idea measures accumulated oscillation, not merely the difference between the two endpoints. A function can start and finish at the same height after moving up and down many times. Endpoint difference would report zero; total variation reports the movement. Bounded variation is part of a rigorous language for curves, integration, and convergence. Jordan's contributions to the subject are among the reasons his name also appears in analysis, separate from the canonical matrices and the subspace angles.

The separation theorem and bounded variation belong to a wider intellectual change visible in his textbooks. His Cours d'analyse de l'École Polytechnique was developed over several editions beginning in the 1880s. Its attention to precise definitions and rigorous arguments influenced later generations of analysts. It is revealing that Jordan, trained for engineering, contributed to a tradition in which an apparently elementary geometric fact was not considered satisfactory until the assumptions and proof had been made exact. A plausible picture is a starting point; mathematics asks what survives when the picture is stretched to the edge of the definition.

Jordan held major positions in French mathematics. He became a professor at the École Polytechnique, joined the Académie des Sciences, taught at the Collège de France, and took over the editorship of the Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées after Liouville's era. The journal was an important channel for mathematics across national and disciplinary boundaries. Jordan's personal life was also marked by the losses of the First World War, including the deaths of three sons. He died in 1922. His name is attached to so many theorems today that it is easy to lose sight of the long career and the several mathematical communities in which he worked.

It is worth separating two names that routinely cause confusion. The Jordan of Jordan canonical form and Jordan curve theorem is Camille Jordan, the French mathematician of this lecture. The Jordan of Gauss–Jordan elimination is Wilhelm Jordan, a German engineer and geodesist. Jordan algebras are named for the twentieth-century physicist Pascual Jordan. Similar surnames do not mean the same mathematician or the same historical contribution. Even for Camille Jordan's own results, the words Jordan normal form, Jordan–Hölder theorem, and Jordan curve theorem refer to different objects and different collaborators or later proof traditions.

How should we put his contributions together without pretending they are identical? In group theory, rearranging labels changes the description of a permutation group but not its underlying composition factors. In linear algebra, changing a basis changes the entries of a matrix but not the eigenvalues and Jordan block sizes of its similarity class. In subspace geometry, rotating orthonormal frames changes the overlap matrix but leaves its singular values, and hence the principal angles, fixed. In topology, deforming a simple closed curve without destroying its defining properties does not erase the distinction between inside and outside. Each subject has its own notion of equivalence and its own invariant. One must specify which transformations are allowed before claiming that a quantity stays the same.

There is also an important difference among those invariants. A Jordan block classification is exact but can jump under arbitrarily small changes in matrix entries. Principal angles, by contrast, are geometric measurements that vary continuously as their subspaces move. A composition-factor list gives a discrete algebraic signature. A curve-separation theorem gives a topological statement under precise assumptions. None is automatically the right measurement for every problem. The art is to identify which structure a particular question preserves, and which apparent details are artifacts of a chosen representation.

Return one last time to our two planes in four dimensions. The first direction tilts thirty degrees and the second tilts sixty. Another observer chooses different orthonormal bases inside each plane and obtains a different-looking matrix of dot products. We do not need to argue about which observer has selected the correct arrows. We compute the singular values, recover the same two principal angles, and see the same one-half and approximately zero point eight six six projection disagreements. The geometry survives the coordinate change. That is the lasting mathematical lesson of this part of Camille Jordan's work, and a useful thread through the rest of his career: a good classification tells us which features belong to the object, provided we understand exactly what counts as changing its description.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Main running example: two planes in four dimensions

Work in Euclidean \(\mathbb R^4\) with standard orthonormal basis \(e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4\), and set

\[
K=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2\},\quad
L=\operatorname{span}\{u_1,u_2\},
\quad u_1=\cos\alpha\,e_1+\sin\alpha\,e_3,\quad
u_2=\cos\beta\,e_2+\sin\beta\,e_4.
\]

For \(0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\pi/2\), the overlap matrix of the displayed
orthonormal frames is

\[
E^\top F=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&0\\0&\cos\beta\end{pmatrix}.
\]

If \(E'=ER\) and \(F'=FS\) for orthogonal \(R,S\in O(2)\), then
\((E')^\top F'=R^\top(E^\top F)S\). The singular values, and therefore
principal angles, are invariant. The minimum-angle definition is equivalent
to the singular-value characterization by successive singular-vector
maximization with orthogonality constraints.

With \(\alpha=\pi/6\), \(\beta=\pi/3\), one obtains
\(\sigma(E^\top F)=(\sqrt3/2,1/2)\), and principal angles
\((\pi/6,\pi/3)\). For orthogonal projections \(P_K,P_L\),

\[
\|P_K-P_L\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\max_i\sin\theta_i=\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad
\|P_K-P_L\|_F^2=2\sum_i\sin^2\theta_i=2.
\]

The displayed projection identities are for equal-rank subspaces in finite
dimension; for unequal ranks, perpendicular unmatched directions require
additional care.

### Why a spectral cluster can be stable despite an unstable selected eigenvector

Let \(0<\varepsilon\ll 1\) and

\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(1,1+\varepsilon,10),\qquad
B=\operatorname{diag}(1+\varepsilon,1,10).
\]

Then \(\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\varepsilon\). The lowest one-dimensional
eigenspaces are orthogonal, while the two-dimensional low-energy cluster is
\(\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)\) for both operators. Its projection does not
move, and both cluster principal angles equal zero. This illustrates a
modern application of the subspace metric, not a theorem by Jordan about
eigenspace perturbations.

### Jordan block and generalized eigenvector chain

\[
J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=2I+N,\quad
N^2=0,\quad Ne_2=e_1,\quad Ne_1=0.
\]

For integer \(k\geq1\) and real \(t\),

\[
J^k=2^kI+k2^{k-1}N,\qquad e^{tJ}=e^{2t}(I+tN).
\]

\(J\) has algebraic multiplicity two at eigenvalue two and geometric
multiplicity one. Its nontrivial Jordan chain supplies the additional
polynomial factor. The family
\(J_\varepsilon=\begin{psmallmatrix}1&\varepsilon\\0&1\end{psmallmatrix}\)
is defective for every \(\varepsilon\neq0\), but equals \(I\) at
\(\varepsilon=0\): exact block structure need not be numerically stable.

### Composition series: same factors, different order

For \(G=\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\), two composition series are

\[
G\triangleright \{0,2,4\}\triangleright\{0\},\qquad
G\triangleright \{0,3\}\triangleright\{0\}.
\]

Their successive simple factors are \((C_2,C_3)\) and \((C_3,C_2)\),
respectively. Modern Jordan–Hölder says the isomorphism classes of the
simple factors are independent of composition series up to permutation.
Jordan's composition-series results and Hölder's later quotient-group
refinement should be distinguished in historical attribution.

### Jordan's theorem on finite complex linear groups

For each \(n\), there is a finite bound \(J(n)\) such that every finite
\(G\leq\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb C)\) admits an abelian normal subgroup
\(A\triangleleft G\) with

\[
[G:A]\leq J(n).
\]

The theorem places no bound on \(|G|\) itself. The finite cyclic scalar
groups \(\{\zeta I:\zeta^m=1\}\) have arbitrarily large order at fixed
\(n\) and are already abelian. In dimension two, the eight signed
permutation matrices contain the normal abelian subgroup of four diagonal
sign matrices, with index two. Jordan's 1878 work arose from the study of
finite linear monodromy associated with algebraic solutions of differential
equations; see Breuillard's modern exposition of the original proof.

### A topological result and its proof history

A **simple closed curve** is an embedding of the circle in the plane.
The complement of its image has precisely two connected components, both
with the curve as boundary. A figure-eight path fails the embedding
hypothesis. Jordan's historical proof prompted critical discussion;
Veblen's 1905 proof became a standard rigorous account, and Hales (2007)
revisited the assessment of Jordan's argument. The narration does not
claim that Jordan's proof was either flawless or irreparably invalid.

### Bounded variation

For \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\), its total variation is

\[
V_a^b(f)=\sup_{a=x_0<\dots<x_m=b}
\sum_{j=1}^{m}|f(x_j)-f(x_{j-1})|.
\]

The distinction from \(|f(b)-f(a)|\) is substantial: endpoint
cancellation does not erase accumulated oscillation.

### Historical attribution and source notes

- **Biography and institutions:** MacTutor's Camille Jordan biography and Henri Lebesgue's 1923 memorial support the training, research trajectory, appointments, and personal history. Accounts differ on the exact day of death, so the spoken lecture states only the year, 1922.
- **Algebra:** Jordan's *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* (1870) systematized permutation-group methods. His canonical-form result occurred in a nineteenth-century algebraic setting; Weierstrass's elementary-divisor work and later linear algebra contributed to the familiar modern theorem.
- **Higher-dimensional geometry:** Jordan, *Essai sur la géométrie à n dimensions*, *Bulletin de la Société Mathématique de France* **3** (1875), 103–174, DOI `10.24033/bsmf.90`. The modern SVD calculation is an exposition of a later formulation, not Jordan's original algorithm.
- **Finite matrix groups:** Jordan's 1878 theorem and its original differential-equation motivation are discussed in E. Breuillard, *An exposition of Jordan's original proof of his theorem on finite subgroups of GL_n(C)*, *Model Theory* **2** (2023), 429–458.
- **Topology:** The historical appraisal of Jordan's curve proof is nuanced. See T. C. Hales, *Jordan's Proof of the Jordan Curve Theorem* (2007), and *The Jordan Curve Theorem, Formally and Informally* (2007).
- **Names:** Gauss–Jordan elimination is associated with Wilhelm Jordan, not Camille Jordan; Jordan algebras are named for Pascual Jordan.

## Reading notes (non-spoken)

MacTutor and Lebesgue give the biographical narrative; Jordan's 1870 and
1875 publications supply the contemporary algebraic and geometric setting.
The modern mathematical readings make the canonical-form, principal-angle,
finite-group, and topological statements precise. These are separate
mathematical achievements, so a reader interested chiefly in subspace
geometry can begin with the 1875 article and the angles-between-flats
reference, whereas a reader interested in algebra can begin with the 1870
treatise and the group-theory sources.
