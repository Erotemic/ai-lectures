# Walther Ritz: How do a vibrating plate and an atom become calculable?

Support a thin metal plate at its center and draw a bow along an edge. A complicated pattern of vibration follows. Some areas move up as others move down, and certain lines barely move at all. Those lines are called nodal lines. If fine sand lies on the plate, vibrations can gather the grains along them, producing the patterns associated with Ernst Chladni. Even a square plate can support many different patterns, each with its own natural frequency. The full mathematical description asks for a function of two spatial coordinates, together with derivatives, boundary conditions, and a frequency. There are infinitely many possible functions. But a laboratory needs a number, or at least a good approximation, not an instruction to search infinitely many possibilities.

How could a calculation with only a handful of unknown numbers reveal an otherwise inaccessible vibration? This problem occupied Walther Ritz, a Swiss theoretical physicist whose career ended in 1909, when he was thirty-one. Ritz found an effective way to replace an infinite-dimensional energy problem with a finite system of equations. It became one of the lasting routes from mathematical physics to computational engineering. That achievement alone could support a history of his work. Yet Ritz was also studying the spectral lines emitted by atoms, and he found a remarkable numerical pattern connecting those lines. He pursued a radical alternative to the emerging theories of radiation and relativity. His life therefore connects three questions: how to approximate a physical motion, how to recognize structure in experimental numbers, and how to decide what a physical theory should explain.

Begin with the plate, but simplify it to a string stretched between two fixed supports. If we pull the string into an arch and release it, its shape changes with time. A vibration is easiest to describe when every point follows the same time rhythm, while different positions have different amplitudes. The spatial shape is then a mode. There are many modes: one broad arch, two alternating arches, three, and so on. A perfectly uniform string has exact sine-shaped modes. A plate with a complicated geometry usually does not give us such convenient formulas. We want a method that still works when no exact mode shape is known.

There are two energetic questions we can ask about any guessed shape. How much does it stretch the string? And how much moving material does it involve? Stretching depends on how quickly the shape changes along the string; a steep slope is energetically expensive. Inertia depends on the square of the displacement across the string. Divide the total stretching contribution by the inertia contribution, and you obtain an estimate of the square of a vibration frequency. The physical constants, tension and mass per unit length, supply an overall scale. For the moment choose units in which that scale is one.

Suppose the string has length one and both ends are fixed. A natural first guess is a parabola: start at zero, rise smoothly, then return to zero. At a position called x, take the height to be x times one minus x. Its slope decreases linearly from positive one to negative one. Square the slope and integrate along the string; the result is one third. Square the height and integrate; the result is one thirtieth. Dividing gives ten. That means our trial shape predicts a squared frequency of ten. The exact fundamental mode gives pi squared, about nine point eight seven. Our guess is not exact, yet it is strikingly close.

Notice what we learned without solving the differential equation. The trial parabola obeys the fixed-end conditions, so it is a physically permitted shape. Its energy calculation gives a number. And the general variational principle tells us that the number is at least as large as the lowest true squared frequency. Here the estimate is ten and the exact value is about nine point eight seven. The trial shape has already given a certified upper bound for this ideal string problem. It does not tell us the exact mode, and it says nothing by itself about every higher frequency. To obtain more information, we need to let the guessed shape change in a controlled way.

This is where the central contribution of Ritz becomes visible. Instead of committing to one guessed shape, choose several simple shapes that all respect the fixed endpoints. Multiply each by an adjustable coefficient and add them together. The coefficients are ordinary numbers. They let the guess bend, flatten, or acquire more complicated curvature while remaining inside a family whose boundary conditions are built in. We have traded the endless collection of possible string shapes for a small collection of coefficients. The problem is now to choose those coefficients by minimizing or making stationary the appropriate energy expression.

Try two shapes. Keep the parabola as the first. For the second, use the square of that parabola. Both vanish at the endpoints, but the second concentrates more strongly near the string's center. One coefficient controls a broad arch; another changes how peaked or flat the arch is. Neither coefficient alone is the answer. The true approximation comes from their best combination. The energy of a combination has three kinds of contribution: energy from the first shape, energy from the second, and a cross term recording how they interact. The inertia has the same pattern. Those interactions are why the method becomes a matrix calculation. The matrix entries are simply integrals formed from pairs of trial shapes.

For this particular pair of shapes, the smallest squared-frequency estimate improves from ten to about nine point eight six nine seven five. The exact value, pi squared, is about nine point eight six nine six zero. The second degree of freedom changes the answer by just over one tenth, but the remaining error is now a tiny fraction of a percent. The point is less about the impressive decimal places than about the mechanism. We did not insert the exact sine shape by hand. We let a finite family find a better shape by varying only two numbers. Adding well-chosen trial directions gives the method a way to repair an initial guess.

There is a useful geometric picture of that repair. Imagine all possible admissible string shapes as points in an enormous space. A single parabola gives one line of candidate shapes, because scaling it changes only the amplitude. Two independent trial functions give a plane. As we add independent functions, the trial spaces grow. Minimizing over a bigger set cannot give a worse minimum than minimizing over a smaller set. For a conforming approximation to the smallest eigenvalue, the estimated squared frequencies therefore descend toward the true value, provided the trial spaces become sufficiently rich in the relevant energy norm. In symbols this is a min–max statement; in the string picture it says we are searching more permitted shapes, so the best one cannot become worse.

This description needs one qualification. A small matrix does not automatically make an accurate method. If the trial shapes are almost the same, their coefficients can be poorly determined. If the shapes fail to satisfy an essential boundary condition, the upper-bound guarantee may disappear. And if the family of trial functions cannot approximate the real solution in the energy sense, then even a long sequence of computations can settle on the wrong limit. Ritz's insight was to make approximation systematic. It was never a license to substitute arbitrary functions and trust the resulting numbers.

It helps to see the variational principle in a problem without frequencies. Imagine a string under a static load, pulled down by a uniform force along its length. Its equilibrium shape is the one that balances elastic energy against the work done by the force. Call the height of the string u. The elastic part penalizes slopes; the load rewards displacement in its direction. The total energy is a quadratic expression in u minus a linear expression in u. The correct equilibrium is a minimizer among admissible shapes.

Choose the same parabolic trial shape. Multiply it by an unknown amplitude. Differentiate the one-variable energy with respect to that amplitude and set the derivative to zero. The result is one half. The approximate equilibrium is therefore one half times the parabola. For this specially chosen uniform-load problem, that is actually the exact solution. We can verify it directly: its second derivative is the constant negative one, which balances the constant load in the chosen units, and its endpoints remain fixed. Here a one-dimensional trial space succeeds completely because the exact shape happens to lie inside it. In a less convenient loading problem, the answer would be approximate. This distinction—exact inside the chosen space versus exact in the full physical problem—is essential to understanding any Ritz computation.

Ritz's procedure is sometimes described as reducing a differential equation to an algebraic system. That is accurate, but the order of reasoning is important. The method begins with the physical energy and the allowed class of functions. Only then does it choose a finite-dimensional subspace of candidates. The algebra follows from asking the energy to be stationary with respect to every coefficient. It does not start by sampling a differential equation at arbitrary points. This energy-first approach is especially useful for complicated boundaries and differential operators whose direct solutions are difficult to find.

The historical setting made this a consequential idea. Ritz was born in Sion in the Swiss canton of Valais in 1878, into a family better known for art than for mathematics. His father Raphael Ritz was a landscape and genre painter, and several relatives were artists. The visual arts were part of his family background, although his own ambitions moved toward a different sort of exact description. Ritz initially prepared for engineering in Zürich, then increasingly devoted himself to mathematics and theoretical physics. Among his fellow students was Albert Einstein. Their later paths would meet again through a disagreement about radiation, but they began in the same unsettled world of turn-of-the-century physics.

An earlier experience also appears in a detailed historical biography. While climbing in the Alps with friends in 1897, Ritz witnessed members of the party fall on a snowy slope. The shock of the accident and the exhausting rescue effort stayed in his own later explanation of his ill health. That is a report of how he remembered the event, not evidence that the accident medically caused his later tuberculosis. It nevertheless gives a glimpse of a young man whose life contained bodily risk and outdoor experience alongside abstract study. Soon afterward his attention shifted from practical engineering toward the more speculative problem of explaining physical laws.

In 1903 Ritz completed a doctorate under Voigt on the theory of spectral series. The problem was apparently far from vibrating plates: atoms emit light at discrete frequencies, and physicists wanted to know why those frequencies followed numerical patterns. Yet the two interests had something in common. Both involved characteristic frequencies; both asked how a physical system selects special numbers from an enormous range of possibilities. Ritz initially tried to explain atomic spectra through classical mechanical models, including elastic oscillations. Later he developed models involving atomic magnets. Those physical models were eventually superseded, but the numerical relationships he uncovered became fundamental.

The move from engineering to atomic spectra was not a retreat from concrete reality. The spectroscope offered an extraordinarily exact set of numbers and almost no satisfactory model for why those numbers occurred. Ritz could put observations and proposed mechanisms beside each other and discover where they failed to align. The same determination to extract useful mathematics from a difficult physical problem would later lead him from vibrating plates to finite-dimensional variational equations.

Serious illness repeatedly interrupted his work. Between 1904 and 1907 he spent long periods seeking treatment for tuberculosis. He moved among places intended to help him recover, rather than settling into the steady timetable of a laboratory appointment. In 1908, after giving up hope of a full recovery, he returned to research in Göttingen. The interruptions left him with little prospect of an uninterrupted academic career. He was trying to develop theories in spectroscopy, electromagnetism, and mathematical physics while the continuity of his working life remained precarious. The variational method gained special significance in those last productive years.

His movement among research centers and places of treatment also meant a scientific life sustained by correspondence. Surviving letters to Carl Runge, including one written shortly before Ritz's death, are a reminder that the equations came from a person maintaining professional relationships across periods of illness. Runge later spoke at his funeral. Such connections mattered in a field where work could continue through letters and manuscripts even when continuous attendance at a laboratory or university was impossible.

The attribution requires care. Lord Rayleigh had long used an energy quotient to estimate a vibration frequency from a guessed mode. Ritz extended the strategy into a systematic finite-dimensional search using independently adjustable trial functions. The standard name Rayleigh–Ritz acknowledges a connection, but it can conceal the difference between making one energy estimate and minimizing over an entire family of approximations. Even the historical boundary between the two has been debated. A detailed modern study by Arthur Leissa argues that the direct procedure Ritz developed deserves to be distinguished more sharply from Rayleigh's earlier calculations. What we can say without settling every priority dispute is that Ritz's work became a concrete, widely reusable method for computing approximate solutions from variational principles.

Ritz did more than write an abstract prescription. His 1909 study of a square elastic plate with free edges confronted a notoriously difficult vibration problem. A freely vibrating plate cannot simply be given the same boundary conditions as a clamped string. The edges are allowed to move, while the bending moments and shear forces must satisfy the appropriate natural boundary conditions. A fourth-order differential equation describes the plate's bending, and the modes are genuinely two-dimensional. Ritz chose suitable trial functions, assembled an energy calculation, and used the resulting finite system to estimate the frequencies and shapes. The older Chladni patterns became a testing ground for variational mathematics.

There is another distinction here that is easy to miss if we jump straight to the matrix. A string's elastic energy involves the square of its first derivative. A bending plate's energy involves second derivatives and a combination of curvatures. Consequently, the space of admissible functions is different, and the boundary conditions need different treatment. We can use the same Ritz philosophy for both, but we cannot reuse the same energy formula unchanged. This is how the method scales across physics: keep the principle of trial spaces and stationary energy; rebuild the underlying energy and function space for the physical system at hand.

The method's modern mathematical form is worth developing because it makes an otherwise mysterious error estimate intuitive. Suppose a symmetric operator acts on vectors, or on functions in a Hilbert space. We select a finite-dimensional trial space and choose an orthonormal basis for it. Applying the operator to a trial vector generally pushes part of the result outside that space. The Ritz equation demands that, after projecting the result back into the trial space, the vector behaves like an eigenvector. That produces a small symmetric matrix formed by taking inner products of the operator-applied basis vectors with the basis vectors themselves. An eigenvector of this smaller matrix supplies the coefficients of a candidate mode in the original space.

But the projected equation is only part of the full equation. Apply the original operator to the candidate and subtract the estimated eigenvalue times the candidate. The remaining vector is called the residual. The Ritz construction makes it orthogonal to every direction in the trial space. Why? Because the finite coefficients were chosen to cancel the part of the equation visible to that space. The residual therefore points into directions we did not include. This orthogonality is the modern Galerkin principle. It also explains why the coefficients cannot be improved merely by adjusting them inside the same trial space while keeping the chosen Ritz value and mode relation. A new correction generally requires new trial directions.

Picture a three-dimensional room and a flat sheet running through it. The sheet represents the trial space. An actual operator can push a vector on the sheet upward, away from the sheet, as well as along it. The Ritz calculation adjusts the vector until the along-sheet mismatch vanishes. What remains is a displacement perpendicular to the sheet. Knowing that this mismatch is perpendicular does not mean it is small. It means we have localized the error in a geometrically useful way. That distinction becomes important when we decide whether an approximate eigenvector should be trusted.

Take a symmetric matrix whose exact eigenvalues are one, four, and nine. If we select a trial space containing the first and second eigenvectors, the Ritz values are exactly one and four. There is no residual: the operator preserves that space. Now tilt the first trial direction a little toward the third eigenvector. The compressed matrix may still produce a plausible small eigenvalue. Yet the resulting vector is not an exact eigenvector of the full matrix. When the operator acts, the component along the high-eigenvalue direction is magnified more strongly, and a residual emerges. The residual exposes the disagreement between the local trial calculation and the ambient operator equation.

A bound on the angle to the true eigenspace needs one more ingredient: a spectral gap. Suppose the approximate eigenvalue is close to one, while every other eigenvalue lies at least several units away. Expand the residual in exact eigenvector directions. In each unwanted direction, the residual coefficient equals the trial-vector coefficient multiplied by the difference between that direction's eigenvalue and the proposed one. A substantial difference forces the unwanted coefficient to be small whenever the residual is small. Divide by the minimum such separation and you obtain an angular error bound. If two eigenvalues almost coincide, a small residual can coexist with a large change of eigenvector direction. The Ritz method produces an approximation; perturbation theory explains what can be inferred from its residual.

This framework also explains a subtle numerical fact. When the trial space is enlarged, the smallest Ritz value in a conforming symmetric problem moves downward or stays the same. But that statement concerns the computed eigenvalue, not the geometric closeness of every approximate eigenvector. Near a cluster of equal or nearly equal eigenvalues, individual eigenvectors may turn freely, while the span of several eigenvectors is well determined. It can be more meaningful to estimate an entire invariant subspace than to insist on identifying a particular vector. This is one reason Ritz approximations connect to the principal-angle geometry and subspace-perturbation bounds used throughout modern numerical linear algebra.

Another practical issue is the choice of basis. Our two trial functions for the string were not orthogonal. Their inner-product matrix therefore had off-diagonal entries. In a well-conditioned basis that is only a routine complication. But if two functions are nearly linearly dependent, the matrix that measures their inner products becomes nearly singular. Solving for the coefficients can amplify roundoff errors. One can orthogonalize or otherwise stabilize the basis without changing the underlying trial space. The mathematical approximation space and the numerical coordinate system used to represent it are different things. Separating them lets us preserve the physics while improving the computation.

The same idea extends into the finite element method, although Ritz himself did not develop the later finite element software or its full theory. Break a long string, a beam, or a plate into small pieces. On each piece, choose simple local functions whose values fit together as required. Instead of one global polynomial, the trial space is assembled from many local shapes. The energy calculation again produces matrices, now often sparse because a local function interacts directly with only nearby ones. Refining the pieces enriches the trial space. For elliptic problems with the appropriate coercivity, admissibility, and approximation properties, variational analysis provides convergence results. The modern computational framework owes much to the finite-dimensional variational viewpoint Ritz helped establish.

Ritz arrived at his results through the needs of physics, and those needs were not confined to the mechanics of vibrating metal. Spectral lines were another problem in which a scientist possessed measurements more reliable than the proposed microscopic pictures. The combination principle is easiest to understand when one imagines Ritz confronting a table of frequencies and trying to discover whether the numbers fit together as parts of a larger system.

Yet there is another version of Ritz's work that begins, not with a plate, but with a spectroscope. In the nineteenth century, physicists passed light from heated gases through prisms and diffraction gratings. Instead of a continuous rainbow, each chemical element produced a collection of bright spectral lines. A line represents light at a particular frequency, or equivalently a particular wavelength. Spectroscopists learned to measure those lines with increasing precision. The challenge was to find a rule connecting them: an atom could produce many lines, and no simple classical picture of an atom explained the whole arrangement.

Hydrogen presented a celebrated early clue. Johann Balmer discovered a compact numerical formula for a family of visible hydrogen wavelengths. Johannes Rydberg developed broader series relationships. Ritz, looking for a physical theory behind such data, investigated how line frequencies could be organized. The most enduring outcome is the Ritz combination principle: express the frequency of a spectral line as the difference between two numbers called spectral terms. A collection of terms can generate many possible differences. Some observed lines share one term; other lines share another. The apparent catalog of unrelated frequencies begins to look like a connected network of differences.

An example makes that rule audible. Imagine three spectral terms, measured in the same convenient frequency units, at twelve thousand, nine thousand, and three thousand five hundred. The difference between the first two is three thousand. Between the second and third it is five thousand five hundred. Between the first and third it is eight thousand five hundred. The longest difference is exactly the sum of the other two. If the first two line frequencies have been measured and the relevant terms belong to the same system, the third frequency is numerically determined. Such relations give a way to check assignments, look for missing lines, and organize what otherwise seems to be a bewildering list of observations.

That example is a mathematical model of the combination structure, not a promise that every possible difference will appear as a bright spectral line. Quantum selection rules later explain why some transitions are forbidden or extremely weak. And the numerical terms do not, by themselves, constitute a successful theory of the atom. What made Ritz's result so powerful was that it separated a reproducible relation among observed frequencies from the mechanical picture proposed to explain it. A physical model can be rejected while a measured numerical relation survives and is reinterpreted more deeply.

The reinterpretation became central to early quantum theory. In the Bohr model and its successors, an atom has allowed energy states. When it changes from one state to another, the energy difference is carried by emitted or absorbed light. Frequency multiplied by Planck's constant equals that difference in energy. Spectral terms can then be understood as energy levels expressed in frequency units. The combination principle looks almost inevitable once that picture is available: differences between levels generate transition frequencies. But Ritz formulated the relationship before the later quantum explanation, while working within classical models. Explaining the modern theory must not erase the historical problem he was actually trying to solve.

Ritz's classical models of spectra were inventive and demanding. His doctoral work explored elastic atomic oscillations. Later he proposed magnetic arrangements intended to account for spectral series. The theories did not become accepted atomic physics. They nevertheless helped him reason toward line relations that survived. A model can be productive even when its underlying ontology is mistaken, provided it leads to testable structure and leaves a record of what observation actually supports. That lesson appears in both branches of Ritz's work. In vibrations, an intentionally imperfect trial shape yields a useful bound. In spectroscopy, an imperfect mechanical explanation accompanies a reliable numerical relationship. The standards of validity are different, but the discipline of extracting what the calculation truly establishes is shared.

In his last years Ritz also challenged the prevailing formulation of electrodynamics. A wave equation can admit solutions describing radiation spreading away from a source, and mathematical solutions that look like radiation converging toward it. In everyday experience we see antennas, stars, and lamps emitting outward-going waves. We do not usually see coherent radiation arriving from all directions and concentrating into a source. Why is there an asymmetry between outward and inward radiation if the equations themselves permit both time directions? Ritz regarded the asymmetry as requiring a fundamental restriction in the laws governing radiation. Einstein argued that statistical or boundary-condition considerations could play a central role. Their public exchange in 1909 is a small but important chapter in the history of the arrow of time.

It is tempting to turn their discussion into a simple contest in which a single sentence proves that one scientist understood nature and the other did not. The historical record resists that simplification. Ritz criticized the Maxwell–Lorentz framework and considered an emission-based account that differed from Einstein's special relativity. The precise radiation-asymmetry dispute concerned whether the observed preference for retarded effects should be placed in fundamental dynamics or explained through the physical circumstances and statistical behavior of systems. A later historical reassessment has shown that both positions were more nuanced than the familiar short account of their disagreement. The main enduring question is still worth asking: where, in a theory that mathematically allows multiple possibilities, do the physical restrictions enter that select the behavior we actually observe?

The exchange with Einstein also brings their student years into a new light. They had once entered theoretical physics amid similar questions; by 1909 they were willing to disagree publicly about a fundamental assumption of the subject. Ritz's argument had a precise theoretical target. He was asking where the arrow of time belongs in a physical theory, while Einstein gave a different account. The fact that both positions could be put in print and compared shows the importance of intellectual disagreement among colleagues in a period when the basic structure of modern physics was being rebuilt.

Ritz did not have the time to pursue these debates for another decade. He completed his habilitation in Göttingen in 1909 and died there that July, only thirty-one years old. He left a collection of published work that colleagues would soon gather into a memorial edition. The brevity of his career is striking because he left two mathematical ideas that entered different branches of twentieth-century science. Spectroscopists still use Ritz wavelengths derived from differences of measured energy levels. Numerical analysts still use Ritz values, Ritz vectors, and Ritz approximations to turn complicated operator problems into finite calculations. The same name marks a method for finding approximate modes and a principle for organizing atomic frequencies.

After his death, the Swiss Physical Society published his collected works, preserving articles that otherwise might have remained scattered across journals. The difference between the number of years he worked and the persistence of his methods is striking. He left behind useful equations and unresolved theories, along with a correspondence network and colleagues who understood what had been lost. That mixture of finished mathematics and unfinished scientific ambition should accompany the name Ritz whenever it appears on a numerical approximation.

To appreciate the first legacy, return to the string. We began with one admissible parabola and obtained a squared-frequency estimate of ten. We then allowed a second shape to modify the first and obtained a value very close to pi squared. The calculation used no knowledge of the exact sine mode; it used only trial functions, energy integrals, and a small eigenvalue problem. Enlarging the admissible space gives the search room to improve. The residual measures what still lies outside that space, and a spectral gap can translate some of that residual information into an angle bound. These are distinct steps, each with its own mathematical conditions, that form a coherent route from a physical guess to a controlled approximation.

To appreciate the second legacy, imagine an old spectroscope table covered in measured numbers. What seems at first to be a list of unrelated colored lines begins to acquire a structure of terms and differences. A missing frequency can sometimes be anticipated from the relations among known ones. Later physics identifies the spectral terms with differences between energy levels. But the observational pattern was there for Ritz to discover and investigate before that interpretation was available. It is a reminder that mathematical organization can clarify a phenomenon before we have the right physical model for it.

Walther Ritz worked at a boundary where exact solution was often impossible and physical interpretation was changing quickly. His variational method offered a disciplined way to calculate without pretending that a trial space was the entire universe of possibilities. His combination principle found a stable numerical structure amid competing theories of the atom. His final controversies showed how seriously he took the demand that a physical theory explain not just equations, but observable behavior. The lasting result is a pair of mathematical tools with clear meanings: a finite trial space that makes an infinite problem manageable, and a family of spectral terms that makes a complicated line spectrum intelligible. Both reward us for asking carefully what our calculations actually reveal.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### A precise one-dimensional model

Let the string occupy \(0\le x\le1\), with uniform tension and mass density normalized so that \(T=\mu=1\). The weak vibration eigenproblem is
\[
 a(u,v)=\lambda\,m(u,v),\qquad
 a(u,v)=\int_0^1u'(x)v'(x)\,dx,\quad
 m(u,v)=\int_0^1u(x)v(x)\,dx,
 \quad u,v\in H_0^1(0,1).
\]
The associated self-adjoint Dirichlet operator is \(A=-d^2/dx^2\) on \(H^2(0,1)\cap H_0^1(0,1)\) in \(L^2(0,1)\). Its eigenvalues are \(\lambda_k=k^2\pi^2\), with \(\lambda_1=\pi^2\approx9.869604401\).

For \(\phi_1=x(1-x)\),
\[
 a(\phi_1,\phi_1)=\tfrac13,\qquad
 m(\phi_1,\phi_1)=\tfrac1{30},\qquad
 \mathcal R(\phi_1)=10.
\]
With the second admissible, independent trial function \(\phi_2=x^2(1-x)^2\), use the two-dimensional conforming space \(V_2=\operatorname{span}\{\phi_1,\phi_2\}\subset H_0^1\). The stiffness and mass matrices are
\[
 K=\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/15\\1/15&2/105\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 M=\begin{pmatrix}1/30&1/140\\1/140&1/630\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The generalized eigenvalue equation \(Kc=\lambda Mc\) has characteristic polynomial proportional to
\[
 \lambda^2-112\lambda+1008=0,\quad
 \lambda_{\pm}=56\pm4\sqrt{133},\quad
 \lambda_-=9.869749621\ldots.
\]
Thus \(\pi^2<\lambda_-<10\). The improved result comes from stationarity over the *same* trial space of dimension two, not by imposing the known exact eigenfunction.

For \(V_m\subset V_{m+1}\subset H_0^1\), the variational principle gives
\[
 \lambda_1\le\lambda_1^{(m+1)}\le\lambda_1^{(m)}.
\]
Convergence to \(\lambda_1\) requires that the trial spaces approximate the true first eigenspace in the relevant energy topology (for example, their union is dense in \(H_0^1\)); nestedness alone is insufficient. The upper-bound assertion requires a conforming subspace of the proper form domain and the symmetric, semibounded eigenvalue problem.

A simple counterpoint: \(\phi_3=x(1-x)(x-\tfrac12)\), odd about the midpoint, has
\[
 a(\phi_3,\phi_3)=\frac1{20},\quad m(\phi_3,\phi_3)=\frac1{840},\quad
 \mathcal R(\phi_3)=42.
\]
An extra arbitrary trial direction need not resemble the lowest mode; only a **minimization over the enlarged space** guarantees nonworsening of its smallest Ritz value.

### Static equilibrium versus free vibration

For \(-u''=f\) with zero endpoint values, the energy functional is
\[
 J(u)=\frac12\int_0^1 |u'|^2\,dx-\int_0^1fu\,dx.
\]
Taking \(f=1\) and restricting to \(u=a\phi_1\) gives
\[
 J(a\phi_1)=\frac{a^2}{6}-\frac a6,\qquad J'(a)=\frac a3-\frac16.
\]
The minimizer is \(a=\tfrac12\), and \(u=\tfrac12x(1-x)\) happens to solve the original boundary-value equation exactly. For a general source \(f\), stationarity over \(V_m\) yields \(Kc=b\), with \(b_i=\int f\phi_i\), whereas free vibration yields \(Kc=\lambda Mc\). These are distinct finite-dimensional systems derived from related energy principles.

### Galerkin orthogonality and residuals

For a bounded Hermitian matrix \(A\) and an isometric trial frame \(Q\) (\(Q^*Q=I\)), define \(H=Q^*AQ\). A Ritz pair \(Hy=\theta y\) gives \(x=Qy\) and the **full** residual \(r=Ax-\theta x\) satisfying
\[
 Q^*r=Q^*AQy-\theta Q^*Qy=0.
\]
For a finite-dimensional Hermitian \(A\), if the target eigenspace \(\mathcal E\) is separated from the other eigenvalues by \(\delta=\min_{\mu\in\sigma(A|_{\mathcal E^\perp})}|\mu-\theta|>0\), then
\[
 \|(I-P_{\mathcal E})x\|\le\frac{\|r\|}{\delta}
\]
for a normalized \(x\). This follows by expanding \(x\) in an orthonormal eigenbasis: every unwanted component gets multiplied by \(\mu-\theta\) in the residual. For a PDE in an energy form domain, the *weak residual functional* always makes sense, but an \(L^2\) residual \(Au-\theta u\) requires \(u\in D(A)\). A trial function in \(H_0^1\) need not belong to \(H^2\); one must not silently substitute a strong residual into a weak statement.

### Bending plates require different energies

A Kirchhoff–Love isotropic thin-plate model with bending stiffness \(D\) and Poisson ratio \(\nu\) uses (under standard smoothness and physical assumptions) a bending energy proportional to
\[
 \frac D2\int_\Omega\left(w_{xx}^2+w_{yy}^2+2\nu w_{xx}w_{yy}
 +2(1-\nu)w_{xy}^2\right)\,dx\,dy.
\]
The model's natural free-edge conditions are not clamped Dirichlet conditions. A conforming trial family must live in the appropriate energy domain (often \(H^2\) for the classical primal formulation) and impose any *essential* constraints. Rigid motions may create zero modes. The finite-dimensional Ritz logic survives, but the differential operator, energy, nullspace, and boundary terms all change. Ritz's 1909 free-square-plate study is the appropriate primary historical reference.

### Atomic spectral terms and the combination principle

Spectral terms may be expressed in wavenumbers \(\tilde\nu_i=E_i/(hc)\) in a later quantum-energy interpretation; a transition has vacuum wavenumber
\[
 \sigma_{ij}=|\tilde\nu_i-\tilde\nu_j|,
 \qquad \nu_{ij}=c\sigma_{ij},\qquad \lambda_{ij}=1/\sigma_{ij}.
\]
A fabricated arithmetic demonstration uses terms \(12000,9000,3500\) in common units; the differences are \(3000,5500,8500\), with \(8500=3000+5500\). No claim is made that these are measured lines of a specific atom, or that every term pair produces an allowed strong transition. NIST defines Ritz wavelengths from energy-level differences in this way (with unit conversions, for example \(\lambda_{\mathrm{nm}}=10^7/\sigma_{\mathrm{cm}^{-1}}\)).

A historical precursor for hydrogen is the Rydberg formula
\[
 \frac1\lambda=R\left(\frac1{n_1^2}-\frac1{n_2^2}\right),\quad n_2>n_1,
\]
which later acquires an energy-level interpretation. Ritz's combination principle (1908) generalized the empirical organization of lines, while his classical atom models were not retained. The Bohr-frequency relation \(h\nu=|E_i-E_j|\) is a *later* interpretation, not a claim that Ritz worked with a mature quantum spectrum in 1908.

### Radiation-time asymmetry: what the historical discussion actually concerned

For a wave equation, retarded Green-function solutions describe fields responding to past sources and advanced solutions fields associated mathematically with future source data under different boundary choices. The existence of two solution families does not by itself settle which physical conditions select observed radiation. Ritz's 1909 disagreement with Einstein concerned whether the observed radiation asymmetry should be built into elementary laws or understood through other physical/statistical conditions. Ritz also pursued a separate emission-based alternative to Maxwell–Lorentz electrodynamics. The views expressed in 1909 have a more involved historical record than a simple winner/loser summary; consult Frisch and Pietsch (2016) and the Stanford Encyclopedia discussion.

### Historical readings (non-spoken)

Ritz was born in Sion on 22 February 1878, studied initially at the Swiss Federal Polytechnic, obtained his doctorate in Göttingen in 1903, and died there on 7 July 1909. The *Historical Dictionary of Switzerland* and *Deutsche Biographie* provide the principal biographical chronology. Ritz's 1908 Göttingen paper and the long 1909 Crelle journal paper document the variational method. His 1909 *Annalen der Physik* paper addresses the square plate. Leissa (2005) compares Rayleigh's earlier trial-frequency work with the Ritz method and disputes a simple assimilation of their procedures. The 1995 *Physics–Uspekhi* historical study is especially useful for Ritz's spectroscopy and classical models; the original 1908 spectral-series paper provides a primary source. NIST offers a current practical interpretation of Ritz wavelengths. Frisch and Pietsch (2016) examine the 1909 radiation-asymmetry debate and the limitations of abbreviated historical retellings. Introductory Wikipedia pages on Walther Ritz, Rayleigh–Ritz, and the Rydberg–Ritz principle are useful entry points, with the specialist works retained for technical and historical detail.
