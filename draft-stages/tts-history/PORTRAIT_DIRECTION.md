# Historical portraits: editorial direction and next revisions

**Agent D · 16 November 2026**

## Purpose

These are historical lives of mathematicians, scientists, inventors and related figures, not a second pass through the technical mathematics curriculum. The central narrative is what a person experienced, whom they worked with, what institutions and resources they could access, and how the social and political setting influenced what they could do. Their mathematics or science remains essential evidence of what they worked on, not the sole organizing principle.

**Detailed mathematics is welcome in the spoken narration.** When an existing draft has a valuable proof, numerical example, derivation or discussion of mathematical structure, preserve it. In a mathematics-first portrait, move the technical treatment toward the later part of the narration, after the listener understands why the person worked on the problem and what was at stake. A particular life may call for more interleaving; there is no mandated mathematical fraction, chapter count or duration. Do not cut substance to meet a time target.

## Narrative and evidence criteria

- Establish the geography and era concretely: city, language, education, war, political institutions, travel, money, professions, networks, sources of support. Avoid timeless biographies consisting chiefly of discoveries and awards.
- Show choices, interests, friendships, patrons, collaborators, rivals, teachers, students, and family when the documentary record supports them. Do not invent domestic dialogue, private motives, or psychological diagnoses.
- Explain barriers without making the individual's achievement into a morality play. Consider restrictions on university entry, appointments, religious participation, economic means, political persecution, migration and health insofar as evidence warrants.
- Treat original publications, correspondence, recollections, memoirs and later scholarship according to what each can establish. A well-known anecdote is not automatically contemporary evidence. Name uncertainty when the record is sparse.
- Describe the scientific or mathematical ideas at the points where they affected the life story. Once the historical arc is clear, sustained technical explanation may follow. Mark a modern reconstruction as such instead of attributing modern notation or complete proofs to the historical author.
- Preserve existing lecture IDs, sources, claim records and display-only companions. Update spoken word metadata descriptively when narration changes. Keep Wikipedia or institutional reading links in the companion; substantive chronology and disputed attributions should be supported by strong sources.
- Let the material determine length. The listener should feel they met a person in a particular world, not simply attended a technical lesson with a short biographical introduction.
