# 104000 - Euclid: The life we cannot see, and the book that survived

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** mathematical Alexandria under the early Ptolemies; how much and how little can be known about Euclid; the thirteen books of the *Elements*; geometry, number, ratio, and proof; later editors and translators; and the long afterlife of a method. **Background:** no geometry beyond a line, a circle, and counting numbers is assumed.

Imagine arriving at a city and discovering that its most influential resident has left almost no account of his own life. You know his work better than the streets in which he lived. You can describe the order of his arguments, the questions he thought important, and what he expected a reader to accept before beginning a proof. But you cannot reliably describe his face, his parents, his childhood or his daily habits. This is the predicament of anyone trying to tell the life of Euclid of Alexandria. It is also a useful introduction to a question at the heart of historical mathematics: what can a mathematical book reveal about the person who made it, and what does it conceal?

Euclid worked around three hundred years before the common era, in the Greek-speaking intellectual world of the eastern Mediterranean. He is associated with Alexandria in Egypt, a city founded in the wake of Alexander the Great's conquests and developed into a center of the Ptolemaic kingdom. The famous museum and library belong to the larger story of intellectual patronage in Alexandria, although surviving evidence does not permit us to give Euclid a job title in either institution. His book, known as the Elements, was already regarded in antiquity as a work of extraordinary importance. Thirteen books of arranged definitions, constructions, propositions and proofs made mathematical knowledge into a sequence a student could traverse.

There are biographies of other ancient scholars in which a narrator can follow a family through illness, travel and professional rivalry. With Euclid, restraint is necessary. Even the familiar dates sometimes printed beside his name are modern approximations, not a birth and death registered by contemporaries. We do not know his birthplace. The claim that he was born in Alexandria should not be repeated as a documented fact, nor should one confidently describe his teachers, spouse, children or pupils. We are not even certain how much of the material in the Elements originated with Euclid rather than with mathematicians whose results he assembled, clarified and proved anew.

One of the main ancient testimonies comes not from a colleague but from Proclus, a philosopher who wrote centuries later, around the fifth century of the common era. Proclus placed Euclid in the reign of the first Ptolemy. He said that Euclid drew on Eudoxus and Theaetetus, organizing and refining earlier results. He also repeated the story that a ruler asked whether geometry could be learned by a shorter route, and Euclid replied that no royal road led to it. That is a revealing story about how later intellectuals imagined the discipline of mathematics. It is not a stenographic record of a conversation. We should not build an entire personality from it.

The same caution applies to the famous picture of Euclid leaning over a diagram in a Renaissance painting. It tells us about the painter's understanding of ancient mathematical authority, not what Euclid looked like. Even the name Euclid has been confused in historical writing with that of Euclid of Megara, a philosopher associated with Socrates who lived earlier. What remains secure is the book and the mathematical culture to which it belongs.

Let us enter that book. The Elements is not a modern textbook divided into unrelated subjects. It starts with points, lines, circles and the elementary constructions of plane geometry. In its opening definitions, a point is said to have no part, a line to have length without breadth. These formulations aim to identify what counts as the object of reasoning. They are not descriptions of ink strokes, because a real stroke has thickness. Already the reader is being asked to move from a visible mark to an ideal thing.

Euclid then gives basic requests, traditionally called postulates, and general principles often called common notions. Among the postulates is permission to draw a straight line from one point to another, to extend a finite line, and to construct a circle with a chosen center and radius. Their apparent simplicity disguises a profound choice. Instead of arguing each time about whether drawing a circle is allowed, the writer declares the operations from which the argument may begin.

Consider a task near the start of Book One: construct an equilateral triangle on a given segment. Put a point at each end of the segment. Draw a circle centered on the first point with radius equal to the segment. Draw another centered on the second. At an intersection of the circles, place the third vertex. Because that point belongs to each circle, it is the same distance from either endpoint as the endpoints are from one another. All three sides are equal. A diagram makes the result plausible, but the proof explains why the equality follows from the construction.

There is a complication that modern readers can miss. Euclid's stated postulates alone do not spell out every assumption needed to guarantee that the two circles intersect. Many arguments in the Elements rely on the configuration displayed in the figure, and modern axiom systems add explicit conditions of continuity and order. This is not a reason to dismiss the ancient work as careless. It is a reason to read it historically: Euclid's method of proof was extraordinarily influential, and later mathematicians learned to examine its own unstated background more closely.

The first book moves through triangles, parallel lines, areas and the theorem often associated with Pythagoras. Euclid did not invent all of those topics. We know that mathematical cultures before him had investigated right triangles, and the Greek tradition already included sophisticated geometric arguments. The distinctive achievement of the Elements was to place familiar and difficult results inside a shared structure of dependence. A later proposition could cite an earlier one. A reader could locate the point where agreement had ended. A proof became a public object that another person could challenge line by line.

One of the notorious starting assumptions concerns parallel lines. If a straight line crosses two other lines and the interior angles on one side add to less than two right angles, the two lines, extended far enough, will meet on that side. The wording is awkward to a modern ear. For centuries mathematicians tried to deduce this parallel postulate from principles they considered simpler. The attempts generated beautiful results, but no such deduction succeeded within the intended framework. In the nineteenth century, work by Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Gauss and others helped establish coherent geometries with different parallel behavior. That history did not erase Euclid. It revealed that different systems of assumptions support different mathematical worlds.

The Elements also goes far beyond pictures of triangles. Books Five and Six develop a theory of ratios and proportionality. The theory traditionally associated with Eudoxus was powerful because ratios of magnitudes need not always be ratios of whole numbers. The diagonal of a unit square, for example, has a length whose ratio to the side cannot be written exactly as one integer divided by another. A theory limited to fractions would fail just where geometry becomes interesting.

Suppose two rectangles have areas that seem to stand in the same proportion as two other areas. It is not enough to test one pair of measurements with a ruler. Eudoxian proportion is framed by comparing all positive whole-number multiples of the magnitudes: if one multiple exceeds another, the corresponding comparison must agree. The idea is subtle, and Euclid's use of it enabled reasoning about quantities that resist expression through ordinary fractions. It is one of the places where the book's ancient intellectual inheritance is most visible.

Books Seven through Nine take up numbers, divisibility, common measures and primes. Here is a fact worth hearing slowly. Choose any collection of prime numbers, however large, and suppose someone claims the collection contains them all. Multiply the listed primes together and add one. The new number leaves remainder one on division by each prime in the collection. It may itself be prime, or it may be composite. Either way it has a prime divisor, and none of the listed primes can be that divisor. The list was not complete. Therefore there can be no finite list of every prime number.

Euclid's Proposition Nine, Twenty is traditionally described as the infinitude of primes. The modern product-plus-one argument captures its essential force, though one should not confuse the convenient contemporary presentation with every step of Euclid's original construction. Also notice the precision of the result. We do not claim that multiplying primes together and adding one always makes a prime. We claim it produces a number not divisible by the primes we started with. That is enough.

Number theory in the Elements includes a method of finding a greatest common divisor by repeated subtraction of smaller magnitudes from larger ones. In a modern classroom the efficient version uses division and remainders. Take eighty-four and thirty: eighty-four leaves remainder twenty-four when divided by thirty. Thirty leaves remainder six when divided by twenty-four. Twenty-four is divisible by six, so six is the greatest common divisor. The underlying reasoning is that replacing a larger number by its remainder does not change the common divisors. The method became attached to Euclid's name, but the book should not be mistaken for a record of his personal invention of every operation it contains.

Book Ten examines different kinds of irrational magnitudes in demanding detail. Books Eleven through Thirteen move into solid geometry, ending with constructions of the five regular polyhedra. There are worlds of mathematics inside this volume that are almost invisible when Euclid is remembered only for the parallel postulate or the Pythagorean theorem. Its organization gave readers a route from a small collection of permitted constructions to an astonishing range of results.

A life of Euclid can also be told through the readers who kept the Elements alive. Ancient commentators studied and altered the text. Theon of Alexandria, working in the fourth century of the common era, prepared an edition influential in the surviving Greek manuscript tradition. His daughter Hypatia is associated with the intellectual circle in which mathematical texts were studied and edited, though individual claims about her direct editorial work need care. Much of what readers later called Euclid arrived through copies of copies. The original manuscript has not survived.

Arabic-language scholarship was central to the book's later movement. Translators and mathematicians working across the Abbasid world rendered and studied Greek mathematical texts; names associated with the Arabic transmission of the Elements include al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf ibn Matar, Hunayn ibn Ishaq and Thabit ibn Qurra. Their versions were not merely a pipe carrying a sealed package westward. Translation forced choices about technical terms; commentators tested proofs and extended mathematical arguments. Latin scholars later encountered Euclid in part through Arabic translations, and additional Greek traditions entered the learned world of medieval and Renaissance Europe.

The first printed edition of the Elements appeared in Venice in fourteen eighty-two in a Latin version. It did not suddenly make everybody a mathematician, but printing changed the economics of repeating a text with diagrams. Printers had to find ways to set figures, letters and explanations in a stable relation on a page. Teachers could assign the same proposition to many students using copies that were more consistent than separately handwritten manuscripts. The Elements became not only a mathematical work but also an institution of education, recitation, examination and discipline.

This history creates a problem of authorship. Suppose a sentence in a ninth-century manuscript differs from one in a later copy. Is it a scribal mistake, a deliberate clarification, or a trace of a different ancient source? Scholars including Johan Ludvig Heiberg compared manuscript families to identify earlier readings. The work resembles mathematical inference in one respect: evidence is examined for patterns, and hypotheses are tested against what copies share or fail to share. Yet textual criticism does not produce absolute access to the author's desk. It produces better-supported reconstructions of a book with a long, complicated life.

Euclid is associated with works beyond the Elements, including Data, Optics and Phaenomena. Some other writings named by ancient authors survive only in testimony or fragments, and attributions can be disputed. These works help resist the image of an author who cared only about ruler-and-compass geometry. Mathematical deduction could be applied to perspective, astronomy and questions about what information is sufficient to determine an unknown figure.

What, then, can we say about Euclid as a human being? We can say that the Elements reflects sustained attention to how another mind might be led through an argument. It reveals a preference for organizing results so their dependencies can be examined. We can say that Euclid was part of a tradition that inherited Greek mathematics while operating in Ptolemaic Egypt. We cannot say he was kind, severe, patient, eccentric or lonely on the evidence of a textbook. The authorial voice of a proof is not a diary.

A few ideas of the Elements now appear so elementary that their historical novelty can be hard to feel. A student may be told: define your terms, state your assumptions, prove your claim. But these instructions are not automatic properties of human thinking. They require a shared practice: someone must agree to explain why a conclusion follows and must be willing to have that explanation inspected by another person. The practice has transformed itself many times since antiquity. Modern geometry, arithmetic, logic and proof assistants use different formal standards. They can nevertheless recognize the problem Euclid's book helped make famous.

In an ordinary biography, we might conclude with the subject's death. In Euclid's, we do not know when that happened. The only honest ending is the long survival of the work. The student drawing two circles today is not looking over Euclid's shoulder. The student is participating in a chain of teaching, disagreement, copying and reconstruction that reaches across more than two thousand years. Euclid's individual life is mostly hidden. His book made an idea of mathematical reasoning visible, and generations of readers have continued arguing with it ever since.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence

- **Late fourth to early third century BCE:** conventional period for Euclid's activity, associated with Alexandria under Ptolemy I. Birthplace, dates, teachers and family are unknown.
- **About 300 BCE:** conventional dating of the *Elements*, a compilation and reworking of existing Greek mathematical traditions in thirteen books.
- **Fifth century CE:** Proclus writes the surviving influential historical testimony and repeats later anecdotes.
- **Fourth century CE onward:** Theon of Alexandria's editorial tradition profoundly affects the Greek witnesses.
- **Ninth to twelfth centuries CE:** Arabic translation and commentary, then Latin translations from Arabic, carry the work through new institutions.
- **1482:** first printed Latin edition in Venice; later philological work compared independent manuscript branches.

### Mathematical companion

**Infinitely many primes.** Given finitely many distinct primes \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\), the integer \(N=p_1p_2\cdots p_n+1\) is not divisible by any of them. A prime divisor of \(N\) therefore lies outside the list. **Do not assert that \(N\) itself is prime.** Proposition IX.20 is an ancient argument with somewhat different presentation.

**Greatest common divisor.** \(\gcd(84,30)=\gcd(30,24)=\gcd(24,6)=6\). Modern remainder notation compresses the repeated subtraction found in Greek treatments of common measure.

**Parallel assumptions.** The fifth postulate concerns a specific angle condition. Non-Euclidean geometries challenge the uniqueness of that parallel behavior under altered assumptions; they do not retroactively make every proposition of Euclid's system false.

**Axiomatic omissions.** Ordinary diagrams silently supply existence and betweenness facts that Euclid's stated postulates do not always secure. Modern axiom systems formalize additional hypotheses; the historical achievement should not be described as a fully modern formalization.

**Historical cautions:** The king-and-royal-road dialogue is a late anecdote; no reliable birthdate, birthplace, private life or personal appearance is known. Do not confuse Euclid of Alexandria with Euclid of Megara. Earlier results of Eudoxus and Theaetetus are explicitly part of the story. The medieval text is transmitted through editorial and translation layers, not a surviving authorial original.
