# Ky Fan: How can a whole list of singular values be compared at once?

Imagine two machines, each of which transforms a vector into another vector. You are allowed to test them with inputs of length one. The first machine can stretch some direction by a factor of four. The second never stretches a direction by more than three. If all you care about is the largest possible stretch, you have your answer: the first machine is more extreme.

But suppose these machines represent the same quantity measured across three independent directions. The first has stretching factors four, one, and one. The second has factors three, two, and one. The first has a larger worst-case effect. Yet the total of its three stretching factors is six, exactly the same as the second. And if you add only the two largest factors, you get five in both cases. One machine is more concentrated in its strongest direction; the other distributes more of its effect across two directions.

Which machine should we say is larger? The answer depends on what the measurement is intended to capture. A single maximum or a single total cannot describe every aspect of a list of stretching factors. A whole family of partial sums can.

This was a central theme in the mathematical work of Ky Fan, a twentieth-century mathematician whose career ran through China, France, and the United States. In the years just after the Second World War he developed inequalities that made it possible to compare several spectral quantities at once. His name is now attached to the sum of the largest singular values of a matrix, to a dominance theorem covering every unitarily invariant matrix norm, and to a set of extremal principles that make those sums computable by optimization.

Fan's work reached considerably further. He also made important contributions to fixed-point and minimax theory, questions about whether a mathematical process must settle somewhere and whether competing choices admit a stable solution. These topics seem far from matrix singular values. The shared mathematical habit is to replace a difficult global question with carefully stated conditions under which an extremum or a common point must exist.

We will follow the person and the mathematics together. The basic question throughout is this: when information is distributed among many directions, how can we preserve enough of that distribution to make reliable comparisons?

## From Hangzhou to Paris

Ky Fan was born in Hangzhou, China, on September nineteenth, nineteen fourteen. He entered Peking University in nineteen thirty-two and completed his bachelor's degree in nineteen thirty-six. According to the obituary written by Fuzhen Zhang with help from the mathematics department at the University of California, Santa Barbara, Fan originally considered engineering. A mathematician uncle, who chaired the mathematics department at Peking University, helped draw him toward mathematics instead.

There is an important difference between such an account and a story we might invent. The obituary supplies an identifiable source for the change of direction; we do not need to manufacture a dramatic conversion scene. The documentary record already gives a meaningful picture: a young student choosing mathematics during a period when universities in China were developing strong modern mathematical programs, with family connections to that professional world.

In nineteen thirty-nine, Fan went to France. He received a doctorate from the University of Paris in nineteen forty-one, working under Maurice Fréchet. Fréchet was a leading figure in the development of metric spaces and functional analysis. One of his great contributions was to make it possible to study convergence and continuity in settings far more general than ordinary Euclidean coordinates. That environment was a natural place for someone who would later work fluently with matrices, abstract operators, topological vector spaces, and fixed-point principles.

The dates deserve to be heard together: Fan arrived in France in nineteen thirty-nine and completed his doctorate in nineteen forty-one, during a European war. The public record establishes the dates and the academic relationship. It does not tell us exactly how he experienced every disruption surrounding them. After completing the doctorate he worked at the French National Centre for Scientific Research. In nineteen forty-five he moved to the United States, and from nineteen forty-five to nineteen forty-seven he was a member of the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton.

The Institute brought together researchers who were changing functional analysis and spectral theory. Among the mathematicians influencing Fan were John von Neumann and Hermann Weyl. The chronology alone does not tell us who discussed which theorem in a particular room, but the papers tell us something firmer. Weyl published a paper about the relationship between eigenvalues and singular values in nineteen forty-nine. Fan wrote a direct response in the same journal later that year, followed by a second installment in nineteen fifty. Here we can see a genuine exchange of mathematical questions in print.

Fan later taught at the University of Notre Dame, where he became a full professor. He also held positions at Wayne State University and Northwestern University. In nineteen sixty-five he joined the University of California, Santa Barbara, where he remained through his retirement in nineteen eighty-five. His career included service to mathematical institutions in East Asia. Elected to Academia Sinica in nineteen sixty-four, he directed its Institute of Mathematics from nineteen seventy-eight to nineteen eighty-four.

These institutions give a rough itinerary, but they also suggest something about the kind of mathematics Fan practiced. A statement about matrices might arise from finite-dimensional algebra, be proved using geometric optimization, and then extend to compact operators on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. His career crossed some of the same mathematical boundaries as his results.

## The shape hidden behind a matrix

We should understand exactly what the three stretching factors in the opening example mean. A matrix sends input vectors to output vectors. It might stretch, rotate, shear, or even flatten some directions. An eigenvector is a direction that the matrix sends along itself. But many matrices do not possess a full orthogonal basis of eigenvectors; eigenvalues can also be complex, and their sizes do not always tell us how the matrix amplifies ordinary lengths.

Singular values solve a related but more stable measurement problem. They describe the lengths of the principal semiaxes obtained when a matrix maps the unit sphere into an ellipsoid, allowing degenerate semiaxes when some directions are flattened. They are always nonnegative. For a real matrix, you can calculate their squares as the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix formed by multiplying the transpose of the original matrix by the matrix itself. For a complex matrix, use the conjugate transpose instead.

Consider a diagonal matrix that multiplies the first coordinate by four, the second by one, and the third by one. It turns the unit sphere into an ellipsoid with one long axis and two short axes. Its singular values are four, one, and one. A second diagonal matrix with entries three, two, and one produces a more balanced ellipsoid. Its singular values are three, two, and one.

The maximum stretching factor of a matrix is its largest singular value. That quantity is called the operator norm. Another measurement, the trace norm, adds every singular value. The Frobenius norm squares the singular values, adds them, and takes a square root. For our two examples, the first operator norm is four, while the second is three. Their trace norms are both six. Their Frobenius norms are different: the first is the square root of eighteen; the second is the square root of fourteen.

So the operator norm distinguishes the strongest effect, the trace norm does not distinguish these examples, and the Frobenius norm gives greater weight to a concentrated large stretch. None of those observations makes one norm defective. They measure different properties. Fan's great insight in this part of matrix analysis was that there is a useful intermediate vocabulary which lets us compare all these measurements systematically.

For each positive integer k, add the largest k singular values. When k is one, you get the operator norm. When k is the full dimension, you get the trace norm. The quantities in between measure how much stretching is concentrated in the strongest k directions. These are now called the Ky Fan k norms.

For our first matrix, the sequence of partial sums is four, five, six. For our second, it is three, five, six. The first dominates the second at every stage. A statement about every stage is stronger than a statement about either endpoint alone. We can see that the larger Frobenius norm of the first matrix fits the same pattern.

It is worth looking at a different pair before we give the theory a name. Compare singular values four, one, one against three, three, zero. Both totals are six. The first has the larger single maximum: four instead of three. But the first two terms of the second add to six, whereas the first two terms of the first add only to five. There is no uniform winner across the partial sums. One ellipsoid is more extreme in one direction; the other spends more of its stretching budget in a two-dimensional plane. If we insist on deciding which is larger without specifying a measurement, we are asking a question that has no single answer.

## Ordering a whole list: majorization

Suppose we have two decreasing lists of nonnegative numbers. We say that the first list weakly majorizes the second if, for every possible length of prefix, the sum of the largest numbers in the first list is at least as large as the corresponding sum in the second. The word weakly is important. We do not require the total sums to be equal. Requiring equality of total sums produces the more restrictive relation usually called majorization.

Our lists four, one, one and three, two, one have equal totals, and the first has every prefix sum at least as large as the second. So the first majorizes the second, and therefore also weakly majorizes it. The lists four, one, one and three, three, zero are incomparable by weak majorization: the order of the first partial sum disagrees with the order of the second.

What is gained by keeping the whole collection of inequalities? Imagine checking whether the output of a matrix is well controlled under any norm that is unaffected by rotating the input or the output. Such a norm is called unitarily invariant. Rotating the coordinate frame should not change the measured size of a transformation. The operator norm, Frobenius norm, and trace norm all satisfy this requirement. So do many other norms, including the intermediate Ky Fan norms.

The Fan dominance theorem gives an exact answer for finite matrices of the same size. If every Ky Fan partial sum for matrix A is no greater than the corresponding sum for matrix B, then every unitarily invariant norm of A is no greater than the same norm of B. And conversely, if the inequality holds for every unitarily invariant norm, it must hold for each Ky Fan norm, because the Ky Fan norms are among them.

This is an extraordinarily efficient way to organize a family of inequalities. The hypothesis does not specify a particular norm. It specifies the ordered singular-value information from which all those norm comparisons follow. Once we establish the partial-sum comparison, we can choose a norm appropriate to the application without repeating the spectral argument.

We should be exact about what the theorem does not say. Knowing only the largest singular value is not enough. Knowing only the sum of all singular values is not enough. Even knowing both endpoints can leave the intermediate partial sums undetermined. A matrix whose singular values are five, two, two, and one has the same largest singular value and same total as a matrix with singular values five, three, one, and one. Their second partial sums differ: seven versus eight. Their third partial sums differ too: nine versus nine, which happens to agree. The missing information is concentrated at that intermediate comparison.

There is one more historical distinction. The modern theorem carries Fan's name, but the general study of unitarily invariant norms also rests on work by von Neumann and on the older theory of majorization developed by Hardy, Littlewood, and Pólya. Attaching a name to a result can be useful for navigation. It must not collapse an entire mathematical lineage into one person's achievement. Fan's contribution is concrete: extremal spectral results and inequalities that helped make partial sums a central tool in matrix and operator theory.

## Why those sums are extrema

A family of numbers is most powerful when it also has a geometric interpretation. Fan's work connected the singular-value sums to maxima over orthogonal vectors. That means we can recognize a Ky Fan norm without first calculating every singular value explicitly.

For a matrix A, imagine choosing k input directions that are mutually perpendicular and each have length one. Also choose k mutually perpendicular output directions. Pair the first input direction with the first output direction, the second with the second, and so on. For each pair, apply A to the input, take its inner product with the chosen output direction, and add the results. In the complex case, take the real part of the total. How large can that number be?

If we are free to choose both orthonormal families, the maximum is precisely the sum of the largest k singular values of A. For k equal to one, the assertion is familiar. We are asking for the strongest possible alignment of A applied to a unit input with another unit vector. First align the output test vector with A times the input; then choose the input that is stretched the most. The maximum is the largest singular value.

When k is two, we cannot simply choose the strongest direction twice. The chosen input directions have to be orthogonal, and so do the output test directions. That constraint forces the optimization to account for a second independent direction. The best total is the largest singular value plus the second largest. With three directions, we add the third. Orthogonality is the mechanism that prevents one exceptional direction from being counted repeatedly.

Let's try this with the diagonal matrix whose singular values are four, one, one. If we select the first two coordinate directions for both the inputs and the output tests, the two contributions are four and one. Their total is five. We cannot do better with a different pair of orthogonal inputs, because every alternative distributes the available stretching across the same principal axes. A pair that concentrates more heavily on the long first axis necessarily leaves less room for an independent second direction. The exact inequality follows from the singular-value decomposition and its variational characterization.

For a Hermitian matrix, whose eigenvalues are real and whose eigenvectors can be taken orthogonal, there is a closely related maximum principle. Add the Rayleigh quotients of k orthonormal vectors. Their maximum is the sum of the k largest eigenvalues. Here the same vectors appear on both sides of the inner product. For general matrices and singular values, we allow separate input and output vectors. The distinction reflects the difference between eigenvalues and singular values.

Take a diagonal Hermitian matrix with entries five, minus four, and one. Its two largest eigenvalues are five and one, so the Hermitian maximum for two orthonormal test vectors is six. But its two largest singular values are five and four, so the singular-value maximum is nine. The negative eigenvalue contributes its magnitude to stretching, even though it does not belong among the two largest algebraic eigenvalues. If we confuse those two extremal statements, we will make a false claim about a very simple matrix.

This type of maximum property is sometimes called a variational characterization. The spectral quantity appears as the answer to an optimization problem over vectors or subspaces. The optimization form is often better suited to a proof than the explicit singular-value formula. We can compare two operators by comparing what their test vectors can achieve. We can bound a sum of singular values by evaluating or estimating inner products. And if a matrix is formed from several pieces, the variational form makes the triangle inequality almost visible.

## The triangle inequality and the limits of easy proofs

Take matrices A and B, and ask about their sum. The triangle inequality for the Ky Fan k norm says that the total of the k largest singular values of A plus B is at most the total of the k largest singular values of A, plus the corresponding total for B. This is true even if the singular directions of A and B do not align.

The maximum principle gives the reason. Fix any pair of orthonormal input and output families. The sum of the k test inner products for A plus B separates into the corresponding sum for A and the corresponding sum for B. The first part is at most the Ky Fan k norm of A. The second is at most the Ky Fan k norm of B. Because this is true for every choice of the test families, it is true for the maximizing choice. That is the triangle inequality.

We should not infer that the individual singular values satisfy a term-by-term triangle inequality. They generally do not. Consider two diagonal two-by-two matrices. The first has entries one and zero. The second has entries zero and one. Each has singular values one and zero. Their sum is the identity matrix, whose singular values are one and one. Its second singular value is one, although the second singular values of the summands add to zero. The attempted term-by-term inequality fails.

The Ky Fan partial sums behave better. For k equal to one, the identity's leading singular value is one, no greater than one plus one. For k equal to two, the identity's sum is two, exactly equal to the sum of the two separate trace norms. The partial sums keep a level of control which the individual entries do not have.

This is one reason Fan's spectral inequalities are more than a convenient way to name matrix norms. The sums have a variational structure. They survive addition, comparison, and changes of coordinates. Those are precisely the operations that come up in perturbation theory, where a known transformation is modified by an error and we want a bound on what moves.

## How one family controls all the invariant norms

The dominance theorem has a deeper idea behind it. If you give me a list of nonnegative numbers arranged from largest to smallest, I can calculate a weighted sum. Suppose I weight the first number by three, the second by two, and the third by one. For our list four, one, one, this makes twelve plus two plus one, or fifteen. For the list three, two, one, it makes nine plus four plus one, or fourteen.

Now rewrite that same calculation without multiplying the individual numbers. Take the first prefix sum, add the first two prefix sum, and add the full sum. For four, one, one, that is four plus five plus six, again fifteen. For three, two, one, it is three plus five plus six, again fourteen. The two descriptions are exactly equivalent.

The same rearrangement works for any decreasing sequence of nonnegative weights. A weighted sum can be built by adding prefix sums with nonnegative coefficients. Each coefficient is the drop from one weight to the next. Thus, if every prefix sum of one list is bounded by the corresponding prefix sum of another, every such decreasing weighted sum is bounded as well.

That explains much of why weak majorization is the right language. A norm that is unchanged by permutations and sign changes can be represented through its dual as a maximum of suitable weighted scalar products. After sorting the absolute values, the optimizing weights can be taken nonnegative and decreasing. Control of all prefix sums therefore controls every such symmetric norm on vectors. And the singular-value characterization of unitarily invariant matrix norms transfers that fact from vectors to matrices.

There are two ingredients here, with different historical roots: the theory of symmetric gauge functions describing rotation-invariant matrix measurements, and the prefix-sum comparison developed in majorization and Fan's matrix inequalities. Von Neumann's nineteen thirty-seven work is a key predecessor in the first ingredient. Fan's inequalities and extremal principles are central to the second. A proof that combines them is a mathematical synthesis, and it is useful to know which parts it combines.

Consider what this theorem lets a researcher do. Suppose an error matrix E has a singular-value list weakly dominated by that of a reference matrix R. The largest singular value of E cannot exceed that of R. Its Frobenius norm cannot exceed that of R. Its trace norm cannot exceed that of R. Every intermediate Ky Fan norm is controlled too. In fact every unitarily invariant norm is controlled. We have established a statement strong enough to answer many later questions about the same two matrices.

But the quantifier remains essential. Checking the operator norm and Frobenius norm is not a substitute for checking all prefix sums. The dominant matrix cannot be chosen by taking whichever of those two familiar norms is convenient. The result is uniform only because its hypothesis contains the whole ordered family of comparisons.

## The nineteen forty-nine to nineteen fifty-one papers

We can now place the mathematics into a precise historical setting. In July nineteen forty-nine, Hermann Weyl published a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences titled Inequalities between the Two Kinds of Eigenvalues of a Linear Transformation. The two kinds were the ordinary characteristic roots and the nonnegative spectral values related to the magnitude of a transformation. Such comparisons help distinguish algebraic behavior from geometric stretching.

In November of the same year, Fan published On a Theorem of Weyl Concerning Eigenvalues of Linear Transformations, Part One, in that journal. Part Two appeared in January nineteen fifty. The titles and dates show a concrete mathematical exchange: Fan was investigating and extending problems raised by Weyl's work. We should not picture this as a single theorem suddenly discovered in a vacuum. Matrix inequality theory was developing through the interaction of several researchers and through increasingly sophisticated ways of organizing spectral information.

In November nineteen fifty-one, Fan published Maximum Properties and Inequalities for the Eigenvalues of Completely Continuous Operators. The paper appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences while he was affiliated with Notre Dame. It treats operators in Hilbert space and gives extremal statements involving more than one operator. The term completely continuous in that historical setting refers to what we now call compact operators.

A compact operator in infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is a transformation that can be approximated in operator norm by transformations of finite rank. For such an operator, the nonzero singular values can be arranged in a sequence tending to zero, with multiplicities included. The first largest singular value is well defined. So are the first two, the first three, and every fixed finite partial sum. That makes Fan's finite-prefix perspective particularly natural for compact operators. We can study a limited amount of spectral mass even if summing the entire infinite list would diverge.

For example, imagine a diagonal operator that scales coordinate number n by one divided by n. It is compact because those scale factors approach zero. Its first k singular values have a finite sum for every fixed k, but as k grows those sums become the harmonic series and diverge. The operator is compact but not trace class. Here the partial sums remain useful when a global trace norm is unavailable.

The original nineteen fifty-one paper studies maximum properties and inequalities for compact operators, including multi-operator extremal problems. The familiar finite-matrix Ky Fan norms and dominance theorem are a modern way to organize part of the resulting legacy. One should not assume that every later textbook proof, every extension to a particular operator ideal, or every application to subspace perturbation appeared verbatim in that seven-page original paper.

This distinction matters especially in infinite dimensions. The statement that one matrix norm controls another is easy to write when every matrix has a finite number of singular values. On an infinite-dimensional space, we must ask which operators lie in the domain of the chosen norm, whether that norm can take infinite values, and which kind of convergence is being used. Fan's approach makes finite partial sums available, but passing to an infinite norm requires additional hypotheses. This is where the operator-ideal theory associated with Robert Schatten and other functional analysts meets the partial-sum theory associated with Fan.

## A second mathematical life: fixed points and minimax

It would be misleading to tell Fan's biography entirely through spectral theory. His work in fixed-point and minimax mathematics was extensive and influential in its own right. To understand why, begin with a situation that does not involve a matrix at all.

Imagine choosing a number between zero and one. A rule transforms your chosen number into a new number between zero and one. A fixed point is a choice that the rule leaves unchanged. For the rule that sends x to one minus x, one half is a fixed point. For the rule that sends every x to the constant one third, one third is a fixed point. The question is whether broad assumptions on a rule can guarantee such a point without requiring us to solve its equation directly.

There is a classical answer for continuous maps of a closed interval into itself: at least one fixed point exists. In more dimensions, Brouwer's fixed-point theorem gives a corresponding conclusion for a continuous map from a compact convex region of Euclidean space into itself. Generalizing such conclusions to more abstract spaces requires the kind of topology and convex geometry that Fréchet and his contemporaries had helped develop.

Fan published Fixed-point and Minimax Theorems in Locally Convex Topological Linear Spaces in nineteen fifty-two. He went on to develop further fixed-point results, including a nineteen sixty-one generalization of Tychonoff's fixed-point theorem. These works belong to a different branch of his research from the nineteen forty-nine spectral inequalities. They also show the breadth of his approach: abstract hypotheses about continuity, convexity, or set-valued correspondences can force the existence of a solution.

One important principle associated with this part of his work is the Knaster–Kuratowski–Mazurkiewicz covering principle, often shortened to the KKM principle. Its original roots go back to those three mathematicians in nineteen twenty-nine. Fan later supplied powerful extensions, so the name Fan–KKM theorem is also encountered. Here is a one-dimensional picture of the underlying idea.

Suppose we have the interval from zero to one, and we assign a closed set to each endpoint. The first set contains zero and reaches through seven tenths. The second begins at four tenths and contains one. These two sets together cover the whole interval, and they overlap from four tenths to seven tenths. In one dimension, connectedness and closedness make a covering like this behave predictably. The higher-dimensional KKM condition asks for analogous coverage of the convex hulls of finite collections of points. With suitable closedness and compactness assumptions, one can conclude that all the assigned sets share a point, even when the collection is infinite.

The theorem is an existence machine. It can take a family of local conditions and establish that there is one point satisfying every condition simultaneously. An optimization or equilibrium problem often asks exactly that. We want a decision that survives every possible competing choice, and checking each competitor separately is not sufficient unless we can also show those requirements intersect.

A related idea appears in a minimax inequality associated with Fan. In one common formulation, consider a compact convex set of possible choices and a function measuring the advantage of one choice against another. Assume its value when a choice is compared with itself is never positive. Assume continuity in the appropriate variable and a form of concavity in the other, so that upper-level sets of the competing choices are convex. Under those hypotheses, there is a point whose comparison value is nonpositive against every competitor.

For a simple picture, let both choices range from zero to one and measure the advantage of y against x by y minus x. If x equals one, no competing y in the interval can make that expression positive. This is a trivial example to calculate, but it identifies the quantifier in the more general theorem: one x must work for all y. The theorem is valuable because it gives such a common choice when direct calculation is impossible.

These fixed-point and minimax ideas found uses in nonlinear analysis, mathematical economics, and game theory. That application history does not mean Fan invented every equilibrium problem that later used his methods. It means his results became part of the toolkit for demonstrating that equilibria or solutions exist under suitable convexity and continuity assumptions.

There is a family resemblance between the two sides of his career. The singular-value problem asks us to control every prefix of a spectrum. The minimax problem asks for one decision that satisfies every comparison. Both involve a carefully organized collection of inequalities. But they are genuinely different mathematical programs, with different theorems and different hypotheses. A biographical portrait gains accuracy by showing the range instead of trying to force every result into a single slogan.

## Teaching, institutions, and a long professional life

By the nineteen sixties, Fan was established internationally in operator theory, nonlinear functional analysis, and matrix inequalities. His career took him from Notre Dame through several other American institutions to Santa Barbara, where he became a central figure in the mathematics department. His work continued well beyond his early spectral papers. The number of areas represented in his publications is substantial: matrix theory, topological groups, approximation, convex analysis, and nonlinear problems.

Accounts of his teaching describe a demanding advisor. Fuzhen Zhang's memorial includes a story about Fan's reaction to a graduate student who reported spending eight hours each day on mathematics; the story eventually became the basis of a humorous T-shirt made by students. It conveys the reputation he had for intense commitment to mathematical work. As with many classroom anecdotes, it should be read as a recollection passed on by colleagues, rather than as a verified verbatim conversation. There is no need to put an invented voice or private thoughts into his mouth to understand what colleagues were remembering.

Fan also invested time and resources in mathematical exchange. In nineteen eighty-nine, after many years abroad, he returned to mainland China on a visit. He had already served as director of the Institute of Mathematics at Academia Sinica in Taiwan from nineteen seventy-eight to nineteen eighty-four. These affiliations place him within a mathematical community that crossed geographical and institutional boundaries even when those crossings required considerable effort.

In nineteen ninety-nine, Fan and his wife Yu-Fen made a gift of approximately one million dollars to the American Mathematical Society. The resulting Ky and Yu-Fen Fan Endowment was intended to foster collaboration between Chinese and North American mathematicians, improve mathematical resources, and support talented students. The endowment reflects a part of his career that cannot be captured by an eponymous matrix norm: helping mathematicians obtain the conditions in which they could do their own work.

Fan died in Santa Barbara on March twenty-second, twenty ten, at the age of ninety-five. The obituary's account of his career and the American Mathematical Society's description of the endowment together give a clearer portrait than the theorem names alone. He was a researcher moving between fields, a teacher with a demanding reputation, and a supporter of the mathematical communities that had shaped his life.

## What can move, and what can be controlled?

Let us return to the machines from the opening. One has singular values four, one, one. The other has three, two, one. We now know how to say exactly what the comparison means. The first has larger or equal sums for every leading group of singular values. Consequently, every unitarily invariant norm of the first is at least as large as that norm of the second. The conclusion is uniform because the hypothesis checked every partial sum.

If we change the second machine to have singular values three, three, zero, the comparison breaks. The first dominates at k equal to one, but the second dominates at k equal to two. A researcher who needs a bound on the strongest direction will rank those machines differently from a researcher who needs a bound on the two strongest directions together. Neither is making a mathematical mistake. They are asking different questions, and the partial sums reveal where the questions diverge.

One place this information is useful is the geometry of subspaces. Suppose two spaces of the same finite dimension are close but are not identical. Their principal angles describe how much the spaces rotate relative to each other. A certain operator measuring the part of one subspace that leaks outside the other has singular values equal to the sines of those angles. If a perturbation argument bounds the leading sums of those singular values, it controls the movement of the subspace in several senses at once. The largest-angle bound, the Frobenius-type bound, and many other norm bounds then become consequences of a common spectral comparison.

That application belongs to the later development of perturbation theory. It should not be credited as a complete Davis–Kahan theorem authored by Fan. Fan supplied important machinery for comparing ordered spectral quantities; other mathematicians developed separate hypotheses and arguments that bound the spectral movement caused by changing an operator.

The lasting idea is broader than any one application. A complex object may have many directions in which it can behave differently. Its largest effect can be informative, but it is rarely a complete description. Fan showed how sums of the largest effects can serve as a bridge between the full spectrum and a broad family of geometric measurements. His variational results give those sums a meaning in terms of independent test directions. His dominance principle explains why the whole sequence, rather than one preferred norm, can be the right object to control.

And the same career reminds us that an enduring mathematical contribution need not live in only one subject. Ky Fan worked on spectra, but also on the existence of fixed points and solutions to minimax problems; he taught across institutions and supported cooperation across borders. His portrait is a history of rigorous ways to turn many simultaneous constraints into a theorem.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Singular values and Ky Fan norms

For a complex matrix \(A\in\mathbb C^{m\times n}\), write its singular values in nonincreasing order, padded by zeros when required:
\(s_1(A)\ge\cdots\ge s_r(A)\ge0\), \(r=\min(m,n)\). Define

\[
\|A\|_{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_j(A),\qquad 1\le k\le r.
\]

This definition extends to \(k>r\) by zero-padding. For square matrices,
\(\|A\|_{(1)}=\|A\|_{\mathrm{op}}\) and \(\|A\|_{(n)}=\|A\|_{S_1}\).
For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(4,1,1)\) and \(B=\operatorname{diag}(3,2,1)\):

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccc|ccc}
 & s_1&s_2&s_3&\|\cdot\|_{(1)}&\|\cdot\|_{(2)}&\|\cdot\|_{(3)}\\\hline
A&4&1&1&4&5&6\\
B&3&2&1&3&5&6
\end{array}
\]

Thus \(s(B)\prec_w s(A)\), with \(\|A\|_{S_2}=\sqrt{18}\) and
\(\|B\|_{S_2}=\sqrt{14}\). For \(C=\operatorname{diag}(3,3,0)\),
\((4,5,6)\) and \((3,6,6)\) cross; neither \(s(A)\prec_w s(C)\) nor
\(s(C)\prec_w s(A)\).

### 2. Variational characterizations

For \(A\in\mathbb C^{m\times n}\) and \(k\le\min(m,n)\):

\[
\sum_{j=1}^k s_j(A)
=\max_{\substack{U\in\mathbb C^{m\times k},\ U^*U=I_k\\
                   V\in\mathbb C^{n\times k},\ V^*V=I_k}}
  \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(U^*AV).
\]

One maximizing choice consists of corresponding left and right singular
vectors. If \(H=H^*\in\mathbb C^{n\times n}\) has eigenvalues
\(\lambda_1(H)\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n(H)\), the separate Hermitian
maximum principle is

\[
\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda_j(H)
=\max_{U^*U=I_k}\operatorname{Tr}(U^*HU).
\]

They must not be confused: for \(H=\operatorname{diag}(5,-4,1)\) the
leading two eigenvalues sum to \(6\), while the leading two singular
values sum to \(9\). The singular-value characterization yields
\(\|A+B\|_{(k)}\le\|A\|_{(k)}+\|B\|_{(k)}\).
An individual bound \(s_k(A+B)\le s_k(A)+s_k(B)\) is false, as the
two complementary rank-one diagonal projections show at \(k=2\).

### 3. Weak majorization and dominance

For decreasing nonnegative vectors \(x,y\in\mathbb R^n\), \(x\prec_w y\)
means \(\sum_{j=1}^k x_j\le\sum_{j=1}^k y_j\) for each \(1\le k\le n\).
The Fan dominance equivalence in finite dimensions is

\[
 s(A)\prec_w s(B)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 |||A|||\le|||B|||\quad\text{for every unitarily invariant matrix norm.}
\]

Sketch: unitarily invariant norms correspond to symmetric gauge norms of
singular-value vectors. For a nonnegative descending weight vector
\(w_1\ge\cdots\ge w_n\ge0\), summation by parts gives

\[
 \sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_j
 =\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(w_k-w_{k+1})\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_j\right)
  +w_n\sum_{j=1}^n x_j.
\]

All coefficients are nonnegative. Hence weak majorization controls every
ordered weighted sum, and dual representations transfer this to symmetric
gauge norms. The reverse implication follows by testing each Ky Fan norm.
Von Neumann's 1937 gauge representation and older majorization theory
supply distinct historical ingredients in this modern proof.

### 4. Compact operators and domain care

If \(T\) is compact on a separable Hilbert space, its singular values
\(s_j(T)\downarrow0\) define finite prefix sums \(\sum_{j\le k}s_j(T)\)
for each finite \(k\). The diagonal operator \(Te_j=j^{-1}e_j\) is compact,
with \(\sum_{j\le k}s_j(T)<\infty\) for each fixed \(k\), while
\(\sum_{j\ge1}s_j(T)=\infty\). Therefore \(T\notin S_1\), though
\(T\in S_2\). Infinite-dimensional norm comparison needs the specific
norm ideal and domain conditions; a finite-dimensional dominance statement
should not be extended to all operators and all norms without those conditions.

### 5. KKM and minimax, a distinct research program

The KKM covering condition (Knaster, Kuratowski, Mazurkiewicz, 1929) for a
set-valued map \(F\) on a convex set \(X\) demands

\[
 \operatorname{conv}\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}
 \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^m F(x_i)
\]

for every finite choice of points. Under appropriate closedness and compactness
conditions, the KKM intersection principle guarantees
\(\bigcap_{x\in X}F(x)\ne\varnothing\). Fan's subsequent
extensions are distinct from the original 1929 theorem.

One standard compact-convex form of the Ky Fan minimax inequality states:
let \(X\) be a nonempty compact convex subset of a Hausdorff topological vector
space, and \(f:X\times X\to\mathbb R\). Assume \(f(x,x)\le0\) for all
\(x\), \(x\mapsto f(x,y)\) lower semicontinuous for each fixed \(y\), and
\(y\mapsto f(x,y)\) quasi-concave for each fixed \(x\). Then

\[
 \exists x_0\in X\ \forall y\in X:\quad f(x_0,y)\le0.
\]

Here quasi-concavity means upper-level sets are convex. On \(X=[0,1]\),
\(f(x,y)=y-x\) satisfies these hypotheses and \(x_0=1\) works. The
mathematics of fixed points, set-valued maps, and minimax equilibria is
substantial in its own right; the lecture's brief presentation is an entry
point rather than a proof of Fan's most general theorem.

### 6. Historical chronology and research attribution

- **1914–1936:** Born September 19, 1914, Hangzhou. Entered Peking University
  in 1932; completed a mathematics B.S. in 1936. The family influence and
  initial engineering interest are documented in the 2010 ILAS obituary.
- **1939–1947:** Studied in France beginning 1939, D.Sc. University of Paris
  under Maurice Fréchet in 1941, CNRS employment; moved to the United States
  in 1945, Institute for Advanced Study member 1945–47.
- **1949–1951:** Weyl's July 1949 PNAS paper; Fan's November 1949 and January
  1950 responses; Fan's November 1951 PNAS paper on compact-operator maximum
  properties. These are distinct publications, not a single joint paper.
- **1952 onward:** Fixed-point and minimax results in locally convex spaces
  (1952), subsequent minimax work (1953), and the 1961 generalization of
  Tychonoff's fixed point theorem, among other contributions.
- **1964–1985:** Elected Academia Sinica in 1964, joined UCSB in 1965,
  directed Academia Sinica's Institute of Mathematics 1978–84, retired UCSB
  1985. His other American appointments included Notre Dame, Wayne State,
  and Northwestern.
- **1989–2010:** Visit to mainland China in 1989; Ky and Yu-Fen Fan's AMS
  endowment gift in 1999. Died March 22, 2010, age 95.
- **Priority:** Ky Fan norms and dominance reflect Fan's spectral inequality
  program; matrix gauge theory also builds on von Neumann (1937) and
  majorization theory. KKM originates with Knaster–Kuratowski–Mazurkiewicz
  (1929); Fan's extensions and minimax results are later. The Davis–Kahan
  subspace application is a later mathematical connection, not a claim that
  Fan originated the 1970 theorem.
