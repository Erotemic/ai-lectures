# 051000 — Felix Klein: A mathematician who built a world for mathematics

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** a life between the revolutions of 1848 and the First World War; teaching, institutions, geometry, nationalism, and the Göttingen community. **Background:** none beyond general curiosity about science and history.

In the spring of eighteen forty-nine, when Felix Klein was born in Düsseldorf, the political future of the German lands was unsettled. The revolutions of eighteen forty-eight had challenged the kings and governments of central Europe. In the Rhineland, protesters resisted Prussian authority; the promise of a more constitutional and unified Germany had not disappeared, even as the uprisings were being defeated. Klein's father was a secretary to the provincial government. A later obituary placed the birth against the sound of fighting outside, an image that may have been sharpened for dramatic effect but that points to a genuine historical circumstance: this child entered a household connected to a state under challenge.

Seventy-six years later, Klein died in Göttingen, in a Germany transformed by unification, industrial expansion, a world war, military defeat, and the end of the monarchy. In between, he helped transform mathematics from work conducted by comparatively small circles of specialists into a more organized international enterprise, with research schools, journals, laboratories, meetings, textbooks, and plans for educating schoolchildren. His name is attached to important ideas in geometry. But if we want to understand his life, it is at least as important to ask how he became the kind of mathematician who thought the whole organization of learning could be changed.

Klein grew up in the Prussian Rhineland, a region where industry, commerce, and the political aftermath of revolution were close at hand. His father, Caspar Klein, served the provincial administration; his mother, Sophie Elise, came from an Aachen merchant family. Felix attended the Gymnasium in Düsseldorf. We should not picture the German schools of his day as simply places of Latin recitation from which a solitary genius escaped. In Prussia, mathematics and natural science already held a place in some secondary schools, although the balance of subjects varied greatly. Klein benefited from that educational world before he tried to reform it.

He went to the University of Bonn intending at first to study physics. There he became an assistant to Julius Plücker, a professor whose work crossed experimental physics and geometry. Plücker did not offer the sharp division between practical apparatus and abstract reasoning that we sometimes impose on the sciences. His laboratory work and his geometrical investigations belonged to the same intellectual career. For a young student, this was formative. Klein did not have to choose forever between an interest in the physical world and an interest in mathematical structure.

Plücker died in eighteen sixty-eight, the year Klein completed his doctorate. The death left unfinished work on line geometry, and Klein became involved in preparing its continuation. This is one of the less celebrated ways a scientific career begins: not with a lone revelation, but with the labor of continuing another person's research, learning its language, and meeting the people who care about it. Through this work Klein came into contact with Alfred Clebsch and the mathematical circles of Göttingen. He also visited Berlin and Paris, seeking the wider scientific world that an ambitious young German mathematician could not find within a single institution.

Then political history entered the itinerary. Klein was in Paris in July eighteen seventy, when the crisis between France and Prussia became war. He returned to Germany and served for a time as a medical orderly. A detailed historical account also records that he contracted typhus and left the medical corps to recover. A few years earlier he had been moving among French and German mathematicians; now those states were fighting. The contrast matters. Nineteenth-century mathematics was increasingly international, but the institutions that paid, promoted, and mobilized its practitioners were national. Klein would live within that tension for the rest of his career.

After the war he began teaching at Göttingen and, in eighteen seventy-two, became a full professor at Erlangen at the age of twenty-three. The young man's appointment was a striking vote of confidence, strongly supported by Clebsch. Erlangen was not a major center for the sort of research school Klein hoped to lead. It did, however, give him an occasion to articulate an idea that helped shape his reputation. In the document now called the Erlangen Program, he proposed that geometries could be understood in terms of the transformations that preserve their characteristic properties. Instead of treating Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry as unrelated collections of facts, one could ask what each geometry regards as unchanged.

The historical importance of that proposal does not require us to work through transformation groups here. Think of a map that can be moved without changing the distances between places. Now think of a drawing seen in perspective, where apparent lengths change but other relations survive. Different questions lead us to care about different kinds of sameness. Klein supplied a powerful organizing language for this problem. The program was not a claim that one person had invented all those geometries; it was a synthesis of a field that included work by many others. Nor was the famous text simply a transcript of the inaugural speech he gave. Its publication had a life beyond that occasion, acquiring influence as mathematicians learned to read different subjects through the language of transformations.

Klein did not remain at Erlangen for long. In eighteen seventy-five he moved to the technical university in Munich, where the number and caliber of students gave him opportunities unavailable in his previous position. His students and associates included people who would later be important in mathematics and physics, among them Adolf Hurwitz, Carl Runge, and Max Planck. These names are sometimes printed as a list of future notables. The more interesting fact is that Klein was learning to create a place where several kinds of ability could be put to use. A gifted student might thrive because a teacher made unfamiliar research feel accessible. An institution might gain stature because younger scholars found reasons to stay and work together.

That year he married Anne Hegel, a granddaughter of the philosopher G. W. F. Hegel. The marriage joined Klein to a family with its own intellectual history, although we should not invent domestic conversations between mathematics and philosophy for which there is no record. What the surviving biographies show clearly is a career that moved repeatedly across cities, chairs, and responsibilities while he and Anne built their family life.

Klein moved to Leipzig in eighteen eighty. He was still only thirty-one, already established, and increasingly interested in questions linking geometry, complex analysis, and the study of functions. Sophus Lie, Henri Poincaré, and other contemporaries were exploring overlapping terrain from different directions. The mathematical world was not a quiet procession of discoveries assigned to single names. It involved ambitious competing programs, correspondence, argument, and the urgency of publishing an approach while it was still new. Klein worked intensely, and he also wanted a school around him: collaborators, students, a community in which ideas could be tested and developed.

During the early eighteen eighties his health deteriorated seriously. Historical accounts describe a collapse in eighteen eighty-two and a period of depression in eighteen eighty-three and eighteen eighty-four. We should not treat this as a neat moral fable about overwork, or diagnose a person from a distance. It is enough to recognize that an energetic research career had encountered limitations that ambition could not simply sweep aside. Klein continued to work and publish, sometimes through extended collaborations, but his role changed. A major body of work on automorphic and elliptic modular functions was developed with Robert Fricke over the following decades. He did not stop being a mathematician. Yet the center of gravity in his life moved toward teaching, editing, institution-building, and the organization of research.

In eighteen eighty-six he accepted a chair in Göttingen. The town was much smaller than Berlin or Munich, but its university had a formidable scientific tradition. Gauss and Riemann had worked there. Klein wanted Göttingen to become a leading center again, and he was willing to make practical arrangements in service of that aim. He supported regular mathematical meetings, a reading room and library, closer contact among mathematicians and physicists, and a style of university life in which teaching and active research could reinforce one another. These were not spectacular theorems. They were the ordinary structures that allow research to accumulate across generations.

He also worked as an editor. The journal *Mathematische Annalen*, founded by Clebsch and Carl Neumann, became internationally prominent under Klein's influence. A journal can seem like a neutral container for papers, but editing has a history of its own. Choosing what to publish, recognizing a developing field, and helping researchers communicate across institutional boundaries can alter the direction of an entire subject. Klein took that responsibility seriously. He helped connect the mathematical networks of Germany with researchers elsewhere, including in the United States, where the organization of graduate mathematics was rapidly changing.

One of his consequential appointments was David Hilbert, whom he brought to Göttingen in eighteen ninety-five. Klein and Hilbert were not duplicates. Hilbert's interests and ways of reasoning differed from Klein's; the later fame of the Göttingen school owed much to that combination of people rather than to one leader's method. Other scholars made the institution what it became. A history told only through a succession of great professors leaves out students, assistants, librarians, seminar organizers, and people who had to struggle to gain admission at all.

The exclusion of women from university study was one of the facts Klein confronted. German universities had long treated women as exceptions, or kept them outside ordinary enrollment. Klein supported opening Göttingen's mathematical training to women. Grace Chisholm, who had studied at Girton College, Cambridge, earned a doctorate at Göttingen under his supervision in eighteen ninety-five and later recalled his support. This was real advocacy, but it did not mean the system had become equitable. Permission to study, permission to earn a degree, eligibility to teach, and a salaried appointment were separate barriers. One could pass through the first and still be blocked by the others.

That distinction became especially important when Emmy Noether entered the story. Noether, born in Erlangen in eighteen eighty-two, had become an accomplished mathematician without obtaining the academic position ordinarily associated with her work. In nineteen fifteen Klein and Hilbert invited her to Göttingen to participate in research connected with the developing theory of relativity. The university's rules still obstructed her habilitation, the qualification required for independent university teaching. She lectured for a period under Hilbert's name and did not receive a small fixed salary for teaching until nineteen twenty-three. Klein supported her participation and collaborated with her, but the contrast between her intellectual authority and her institutional status remained stark.

Why did these people work together? Einstein's general theory of relativity was forcing mathematicians and physicists to reconsider concepts that had seemed secure. In physics, conservation of energy had been a central principle. But in a theory where space and time were no longer fixed background coordinates, the familiar language of conserved quantities became more difficult. Klein and Hilbert brought their mathematical methods to these problems, and Noether supplied insight of a different order. Her nineteen eighteen paper explained, in a general setting, how continuous symmetries of a physical theory are connected with conservation laws and with identities among the equations of motion. Recent historical research based on the participants' papers and working notes emphasizes their active discussion and mutual influence. It does not justify assigning Noether's theorem to either of the men who invited her. The authorship and generality of her result are hers.

Klein's interest in the connection between mathematical research and physical science extended beyond relativity. He worked on mechanics, including the mathematics of the gyroscope, and collaborated with Arnold Sommerfeld. He helped edit a large *Encyclopedia of Mathematical Sciences* intended to bring together the expanding mathematical knowledge of the period. A project like that has almost no chance of being completed by a lone author. It depends on finding contributors, defining topics, and mediating between specialties. Klein was increasingly the person who could imagine and organize those connections.

He also cared about secondary schools. Around the turn of the century, he argued that school mathematics should not remain isolated from the concepts shaping contemporary research and scientific practice. In particular he supported giving students an earlier sense of functions and the ideas of calculus. Such a change was not merely about putting advanced material into a younger student's timetable. It concerned what kind of mathematical thinking education should cultivate: static rules and finished results, or an ability to see quantities varying and connected. In nineteen oh eight he became the first president of the International Commission on Mathematical Instruction, created at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Rome. Its work took place on a scale far beyond his own classroom.

The organizer and reformer also belonged to the politics of his country. When the First World War began in nineteen fourteen, Klein signed the public declaration known as the Manifesto of the Ninety-Three. Its signatories defended Germany's conduct of the war and rejected allegations concerning the invasion of Belgium. Those assertions cannot be treated as a neutral description of what happened; the document was part of wartime propaganda, and its denial of abuses stands against the historical record. Klein's signature is a documented choice. His support for educational openness, his friendships with scholars abroad, and his work to build international mathematical institutions do not erase it. Nor should a biographer explain it away by assuming that intellectual achievement necessarily creates political wisdom.

This is one reason to understand scientists as people rather than as titles attached to discoveries. A mathematician can defend the intellectual freedom of a colleague and also endorse a government's war claims. A reformer can recognize the potential of women students while participating in a system that grants them only conditional permission. A man who helped build international scientific ties can place his prestige behind national propaganda. Klein's historical importance survives these contradictions, but an account that removes them would be less truthful about the person and about his age.

Klein retired from his professorship in nineteen thirteen, shortly before the war. He lived until nineteen twenty-five. By then the German empire he had known was gone. Göttingen remained important, though the war and its aftermath had changed European academic life. In the following decade, the rise of National Socialism would do catastrophic damage to the mathematical community that Klein and his colleagues had helped assemble. Noether and other Jewish scholars would be expelled or forced into exile. Klein did not live to see that final destruction, and it would be wrong to pretend he could have foreseen its precise course. But the later fate of Göttingen reveals how dependent a brilliant research community is on the political conditions that allow its members to work.

What, finally, should we remember of Felix Klein? His name survives on geometrical objects and in an influential program for understanding geometries through transformations. He also helped make Göttingen a place where mathematical conversations could endure across generations. He treated journals, classrooms, libraries, scientific collaboration, and even school curricula as serious parts of a mathematician's responsibility. His life moved through war, illness, ambition, political compromise, institutional success, and intellectual friendship. It is the story of a man who enlarged the world available to mathematicians, while remaining unmistakably a man of his own contested time.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and place

- **1849:** Born in Düsseldorf, in the Prussian Rhineland, amid the aftermath of the 1848 revolutions.
- **1865–1868:** Bonn; studies of mathematics and physics, assistance to Julius Plücker, doctorate and work on his unfinished geometry.
- **1870–1871:** Interrupted stay in Paris; medical service during the Franco-Prussian War; later teaching at Göttingen.
- **1872–1875:** Professor in Erlangen; Erlangen Program published as an organizing proposal for geometry.
- **1875–1880:** Munich; marriage to Anne Hegel and a growing teaching circle.
- **1880–1886:** Leipzig; major research activity and period of severe ill health.
- **1886–1913:** Göttingen chair; research-school building, teaching, journals, mathematical physics, and education reform.
- **1908:** First president of the International Commission on Mathematical Instruction.
- **1914:** Signatory of the pro-German war declaration now called the Manifesto of the Ninety-Three.
- **1915–1918:** Mathematical collaboration and discussion with David Hilbert and Emmy Noether concerning physical invariants.
- **1925:** Died in Göttingen.

### The science in context

The **Erlangen Program** organizes geometries through transformations and invariant properties; it was a unifying historical proposal rather than the personal discovery of every geometry it discussed. The **Klein bottle** is a nonorientable closed surface, embeddable without self-intersection in four-dimensional Euclidean space but not in three-dimensional Euclidean space. The different models, group structures, and mathematical details belong in the geometry curriculum.

### Evidence and attribution cautions

The account of cannon fire at Klein's birth comes through a later obituary, not an independently verified minute-by-minute family diary. Accounts of his health crises should not become a retrospective clinical diagnosis. The 1914 manifesto documents Klein's political endorsement of its claims, not proof that those claims were true. Recent research on Klein, Hilbert, and Noether emphasizes collaborative discussion; Noether's 1918 published theorems are not credited to Klein or Hilbert by association.
