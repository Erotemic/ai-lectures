# Tosio Kato: How can a spectral subspace be followed while an operator changes?

An operator depends on a parameter. At one value it has a clearly separated cluster of eigenvalues; as the parameter changes, that cluster moves. We would like to follow the corresponding subspace without repeatedly guessing which eigenvector is which.

Tosio Kato was a Japanese mathematician whose work gave perturbation theory for linear operators a systematic functional-analytic foundation. His 1966 book Perturbation Theory for Linear Operators remains a standard reference. It treats problems ranging from finite matrices to unbounded operators and analytic families.

A spectral projection provides a useful answer to the tracking problem. Surround the desired cluster by a closed contour that does not meet the spectrum. Integrate the resolvent around that contour. The result is the projection onto the chosen spectral subspace. As long as the contour remains safely away from the changing spectrum, the projection can vary continuously with the operator under suitable hypotheses.

This is preferable to following individual eigenvectors through a collision. If two eigenvalues inside the cluster approach each other and exchange labels, the cluster projection can remain well behaved. The object we track is the whole invariant subspace.

The resolvent is the inverse of the operator minus a spectral parameter. Its norm measures how close that parameter is to spectral trouble, especially for self-adjoint operators. This is why a contour separated from the spectrum gives a stable construction and why the gap is a quantitative resource.

In the unbounded case, one must be careful. The operator may act only on a dense domain, and changing domains can make naive subtraction or multiplication invalid. Kato's framework treats such conditions as part of the mathematics, not a minor technical inconvenience.

Kato's role in the larger story is to make perturbation a theory of operators and their spectral data. Davis–Kahan estimates supply particularly concrete, often sharp geometric bounds on the movement of subspaces. Kato's spectral projection language explains why those subspaces are natural objects to follow in the first place.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For a bounded operator \(A\) and a contour \(\Gamma\) enclosing an isolated
part of its spectrum,
\(P=(2\pi i)^{-1}\int_\Gamma(zI-A)^{-1}\,dz\).
The contour must remain in the resolvent set. The same formula applies to
suitable closed operators with domain-aware resolvents.
