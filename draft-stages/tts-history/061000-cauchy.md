# 061000 — Augustin-Louis Cauchy: Revolution, rigor, and loyalty

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** Napoleonic engineering, French mathematical schools, the Restoration, the Revolution of 1830, exile, academic judgment, religious commitments, and the foundations of analysis. **Background:** no advanced analysis assumed.

Augustin-Louis Cauchy was born in Paris in August seventeen eighty-nine, barely a month after the storming of the Bastille. His life would be repeatedly redirected by changes of French government. He trained as an engineer under Napoleon, gained professional authority during the Bourbon Restoration, left France after the July Revolution of eighteen thirty, and returned to find that mathematical eminence did not eliminate the demand to declare political loyalty. The language of mathematical rigor that he helped establish is now taught as if it belongs outside history. His career offers a different picture: a brilliant analyst working inside state schools, royal appointments, scientific rivalries, religious commitments, and a succession of regimes that demanded to know on whose side he stood.

Cauchy's father Louis-François Cauchy had held administrative posts in the old royal order. In the dangerous first years of the Revolution, the family moved for a time to Arcueil outside Paris. Food was scarce, and later family letters describe conditions that were difficult even for a household with education and connections. The child's birth in revolutionary Paris was not simply a convenient date printed above a portrait. It shaped his family's security, his father's professional prospects, and a lasting view of politics. When the family returned to Paris, Louis-François took an active interest in his son's education and maintained friendships with scientists and mathematicians.

Among those visitors were Joseph-Louis Lagrange and Pierre-Simon Laplace. The later biographical tradition records Lagrange's advice that the boy should be educated thoroughly in classical languages before devoting himself to mathematics. Whether repeated word for word or not, the story fits the schooling Cauchy received. He studied Greek and Latin at the École Centrale du Panthéon and then moved toward advanced mathematics. The early French republic and the Napoleonic regime built technical schools that became powerful routes to government service. The best students could enter elite institutions through competitive examinations, but access was still shaped by family preparation, finances, gender, and social connection.

In eighteen oh five Cauchy entered the École Polytechnique. He trained amid the mathematical programs associated with Monge, Laplace, Lagrange, and their successors. The school's objective was not only abstract learning. The state required officers, surveyors, engineers, and people able to calculate the quantities on which infrastructure and war depended. Cauchy continued at the École des Ponts et Chaussées, the school of bridges and roads, and received practical assignments. On the Ourcq Canal near Paris, a student of high mathematics encountered earthworks, measurement, public expense, and schedules. His first major employment would put him still closer to the imperial military machine.

In eighteen ten he went to Cherbourg to work on harbor construction connected with Napoleon's plans for naval operations against Britain. Imagine the conditions: a busy coastal construction project, problems of labor and engineering, and a young mathematician trying to write research after his official obligations. Cauchy took serious mathematical books with him and corresponded with his family. He produced early work in geometry and continued his research on polyhedra and symmetric functions. His academic aspirations increasingly competed with his engineering responsibilities. After becoming unwell he returned to Paris and sought positions that would allow more time for mathematics. His return was not a clean switch from practical to pure science: it was a negotiation among illness, appointments, projects, and influential colleagues.

Cauchy's path into the Academy of Sciences was neither immediate nor free of politics. He failed in several competitions and elections even while his papers attracted attention. In eighteen sixteen, after Napoleon's defeat and the restoration of the Bourbons, the government removed Lazare Carnot and Gaspard Monge from the Academy. Cauchy was appointed to one of the resulting vacancies. He had already produced substantial mathematics and won recognition for research on waves. Nevertheless, the political circumstances of his admission mattered, particularly to colleagues whose careers had been bound to the Revolution and Empire. We should hold two facts together: Cauchy had earned mathematical respect, and the institutional opportunity through which he entered was shaped by a purge.

In the restored monarchy he became an influential teacher at the École Polytechnique. The classroom was a place where his most enduring ideas were tested. His students were future engineers; many needed computational methods as well as theorems. Cauchy insisted that calculus and series should not rest simply on suggestive manipulations. What does it mean for a sequence of values to approach a limit? Under what conditions does a sum of infinitely many terms converge? When can a limit be exchanged with an operation such as integration? The questions may sound like technical housekeeping, but they determined whether the impressive calculations of previous generations could be used without hidden contradictions.

Earlier mathematical writing often used an infinite series successfully while leaving implicit what the calculations required. A physicist could manipulate an expansion and obtain a good prediction; an analyst could still ask why the procedure was permitted. These concerns were not an attack on useful applications. Cauchy was helping to create a set of standards that made the boundary between a successful computation and a justified conclusion more visible. His classroom work also exposed a tension: military and engineering students did not necessarily welcome the abstract care that their teacher regarded as indispensable. A foundational reform changes not only research papers but also what a student is expected to learn and prove before using a technique.

His *Cours d'analyse*, published in eighteen twenty-one, began with basic ideas about numbers, functions, limits, continuity, and series. It did not contain today's completely standardized foundations, and it inherited assumptions and habits from earlier analysis. Yet it made the demand for careful definitions and proofs unusually public in the education of the period. Cauchy's lectures and books circulated beyond France, reaching readers who would continue the process of clarifying real analysis. The result was not one man ending every debate about infinity. It was a major stage in the slow transformation of what counted as an acceptable proof.

His mathematical range was astonishing. He worked on differential equations, complex functions, determinants, permutations, infinite series, mechanics, elasticity, optics, and the propagation of waves. In eighteen fourteen he submitted an important memoir on definite integrals and complex variables. Later he developed the calculus of residues, which allows certain integrals to be evaluated through information about a function's singularities. He established theorems about analytic functions that would become standard parts of complex analysis. We should remember that techniques now collected under one course heading were then emerging from several projects, including attempts to justify calculations used by physicists and astronomers.

His private life and public commitments were not incidental. In eighteen eighteen he married Aloïse de Bure; they had two daughters. Cauchy was an observant Catholic and a supporter of the Bourbon monarchy. These loyalties were evident to colleagues and could create conflict in academic settings. His strong religious convictions were sometimes expressed in judgments about scientific writings, including questions not strictly required by their mathematical contents. At the same time, later accounts describe substantial charitable involvement. A biography that treats him either as a saint of rigor or merely as a partisan intruder into science fails to explain how different commitments could coexist in a single professional life.

Cauchy's relations with other mathematicians could be strained. He could be a demanding teacher and an abrasive reviewer. Jean-Victor Poncelet, a major figure in projective geometry, later described frustrating attempts to obtain a fair hearing. Most consequentially, the Paris Academy's handling of research by younger mathematicians has left a troubling historical record. Niels Henrik Abel submitted a major memoir in eighteen twenty-six and awaited an assessment that did not arrive while he lived. Cauchy and Legendre were assigned to review it. Cauchy's delayed report came after Abel's death in eighteen twenty-nine. The manuscript then had a complicated publishing and archival history. It would be excessive to blame a single man's judgment for every part of that story, but professional delays had consequences for someone without Cauchy's established position.

The case of Évariste Galois is frequently told in a still simpler form: Cauchy lost Galois's work, so the young genius was silenced. The documentary record is more complicated. Galois submitted papers through the Academy in eighteen twenty-nine, and there is evidence that he may have withdrawn or reworked part of the material before later submissions. Fourier's death affected another competition manuscript. Reviews failed, papers moved or disappeared, and the sequence cannot be converted confidently into one villain's single act. It remains important to ask how evaluation procedures treated precarious young researchers. Cauchy's story offers a reason to study institutional authority with the same care we bring to mathematical credit.

Then came the Revolution of July eighteen thirty. The Bourbons were overthrown and Louis-Philippe became king. Cauchy left Paris and refused to swear allegiance to the new regime. He lost his official teaching positions. This was a choice made by a person whose reputation was already substantial and whose family and income were affected by the consequences. He traveled first to Switzerland and then to Turin, where he taught mathematical physics. The language, institutional expectations, and community differed from Paris. A student's later recollection of the Turin lectures describes dazzling insight mixed with disorganized delivery. Such testimony should be recognized as one observer's judgment, not a complete verdict on the teacher.

In eighteen thirty-three Cauchy joined the exiled royal household in Prague as a tutor to the young Bourbon heir, the Duke of Bordeaux. It was a political and personal commitment, not the route one would choose to maximize opportunities to publish in Paris. The experience did not make him less mathematical. He continued to work, but he was removed from the central French institutions that had supported much of his earlier career. The period throws into relief the difference between a mathematical object, which may be studied anywhere, and a professional mathematical life, which depends on salaries, courses, libraries, colleagues, and access to journals.

He returned to Paris in eighteen thirty-eight and recovered his place in the Academy, but the oath requirement remained an obstacle to teaching appointments. In eighteen thirty-nine he was elected to the Bureau des Longitudes but could not assume the position after refusing the required oath. Four years later the chair at the Collège de France went to Guglielmo Libri amid a disputed competition involving Cauchy and Joseph Liouville. Elections for prestigious posts were never only exercises in ranking research by an abstract measure. They were influenced by networks, personalities, political allegiances, and institutional rules. Cauchy's principled refusal had professional costs; it also did not place him beyond criticism for the judgments he himself had made about others.

The Revolution of eighteen forty-eight altered the situation again. Cauchy resumed teaching at the Faculty of Sciences in Paris. When the Second Empire later imposed new political conditions, an exception permitted him to continue without making the same oath demanded of others. The irony is revealing. A mathematician could refuse an oath as a matter of conscience but still benefit from the discretionary power of a government to excuse him. Meanwhile his mathematical productivity continued. He published work through academies and journals and developed new treatments of differential equations and complex analysis well into his later years.

His Catholic commitments also had a practical institutional expression. In his later years Cauchy was associated with charitable and educational causes, including support for religious schooling. This was not an activity that can be dismissed as a hobby outside his real career, nor did it excuse his difficult conduct in mathematical judgment. At a time when French political life repeatedly contested the role of the Church, charitable organization, allegiance, and mathematics were connected through the same networks of people. His marriage, daughters, home in Sceaux, church affiliations, and teaching appointments all placed claims on his time. They help explain why a refusal to sign an oath was not an empty symbolic gesture.

Cauchy died in eighteen fifty-seven at Sceaux, near Paris. By then the transformations in mathematics with which he was associated had already begun to spread among younger scholars. The schools that had educated him under Napoleon had survived multiple changes of regime. His mathematical writing continued to be studied by people who did not share his faith or political allegiances. That is one way scientific institutions acquire continuity: results remain available for criticism and development even when the political circumstances of their author are no longer those of their readers.

Let us now look at a few of the ideas for which his name appears in mathematics courses. The first is a sequence approaching a limit. Consider the numbers one, one half, one third, one fourth, and so on. As the denominator grows, the values become smaller. To say the sequence tends to zero is to say that however small a positive tolerance you choose, eventually every remaining term is within that tolerance of zero. Choose one hundredth, for example. From the term one over one hundred and one onward, the values are smaller than that. The idea is that no fixed positive gap remains. Cauchy worked with the language of infinitely small variable quantities; later mathematicians made the quantifier-based definition standard. We should credit his contribution without silently attributing all modern notation to him.

A second idea is that sequences can be tested for internal coherence without knowing the limiting value in advance. Imagine a long sequence of measurements that supposedly settles toward a result. A *Cauchy sequence* is one in which, given any desired tolerance, all sufficiently late terms lie within that tolerance of each other. For ordinary real numbers, the completeness of the real line ensures that such a sequence converges to a real number. In the rational numbers alone, the analogous conclusion can fail: rational approximations to the square root of two can get as close to one another as desired while no rational number equals their limit. This difference helps explain why foundational work on the real numbers remained necessary after Cauchy.

A third idea involves infinite sums. Take one half plus one quarter plus one eighth and keep adding powers of one half. The partial sums approach one. Now compare the harmonic series: one plus one half plus one third plus one fourth and so on. Its individual terms approach zero, but its total grows without bound. A useful argument groups terms: after the initial one, the next two include at least one half in total; the next four include at least one half; the next eight do likewise. The sequence of partial sums therefore keeps gaining at least another half from each successive group. The fact that individual additions become small does not, by itself, guarantee convergence. This distinction was central to the rigorous treatment of series.

Complex analysis offers another glimpse of Cauchy's power. A complex number has a real and an imaginary component. In a setting where a function is differentiable in the complex sense throughout a suitable region, its values are tied together far more rigidly than for an arbitrary function of two real variables. Under appropriate hypotheses, integrating such a function around a closed curve gives zero. When a function has isolated singularities, a related theorem says that an integral around a curve can be determined by the residues at those singularities, with the orientation and enclosure of the curve handled carefully. This is not a recipe that applies without assumptions; the region, singularities, and curve matter. It shows why an analyst would want a theory in which the conditions of a formula are as visible as its conclusion.

A particularly revealing issue concerned infinite sums of functions. If every individual term in a series is continuous, must the sum also be continuous? Not always. The answer depends on how the partial sums approach their limiting function. Convergence at each separate point can be too weak to preserve continuity; a stronger control over the errors across an entire region is often needed. Cauchy's formulations of this question in eighteen twenty-one and eighteen fifty-three have generated sustained historical debate. Historians disagree about how precisely his terms correspond to what would later be called uniform convergence, and the shift in definitions from his time to ours makes simple translation risky. The episode shows mathematical rigor as a continuing practice of refining claims, not a monument built once in eighteen twenty-one.

There are also limits to Cauchy's own rigor. Some arguments involving the passage from convergence of functions to continuity or integration required conditions later formulated with greater precision, including uniform convergence. Historians have debated how to interpret his varying formulations, and we should not turn that debate into the claim that Cauchy never understood the issue, or that his textbooks were already equivalent to today's analysis texts. The historical achievement was to insist that such distinctions mattered enough to become central objects of inquiry. Later analysts, including Weierstrass and others, revised and sharpened the standards.

Cauchy's biography teaches us not to separate mathematics from judgments about people. A theorem about a convergent sequence can be stated without reference to a king. But the time available to prove it, the students who heard it, the colleagues who accepted or rejected a paper, the salaries that supported the author, and the conditions under which younger mathematicians were assessed all belonged to political and institutional history. Cauchy moved between Napoleon's engineering state, a royal academy, exile, and a restored teaching career while continuing to build mathematical structures that remained useful to political opponents and distant successors. The rigor he pursued is not less rigorous because he lived a contested life. Understanding that life helps explain how the rigor became part of mathematics.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology and cautions
- **1789–1810:** Born during the French Revolution; family refuge in Arcueil; mathematics under Lagrange and Laplace's influence; École Polytechnique and Ponts et Chaussées education; Ourcq Canal and Cherbourg projects.
- **1814–1829:** Research on integrals, analysis, and wave motion; Academy membership in 1816 through a vacancy affected by political dismissal; *Cours d'analyse* (1821); reviews of papers by younger mathematicians including Abel and Galois.
- **1830–1848:** After the July Revolution refuses the new oath; Switzerland, Turin and Prague; returns to Paris but oath requirements complicate teaching and Bureau des Longitudes appointments; political change in 1848 enables renewed teaching.
- **1848–1857:** Further mathematical and charitable work, exception from the Second Empire oath requirement, death at Sceaux.

The fate of Abel's Paris memoir involved delayed reports, archive losses, and eventual posthumous publication; do not equate every episode with a single deliberate loss by Cauchy. The Galois-Cauchy manuscript story is contested by the Academy registers and historical scholarship, and the commonplace assertion that Cauchy simply lost every Galois submission is unsupported. Cauchy's mathematical standards were influential but did not finish the modern foundations of real analysis. Early modern accounts of his health or personality do not justify retrospective diagnoses.

### Mathematical display companion
- **Limit definition (modern):** \(a_n\to 0\) iff for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(N\) such that \(n\ge N\implies |a_n|<\varepsilon\).
- **Cauchy property:** \(\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\ \forall m,n\ge N,\ |a_m-a_n|<\varepsilon\). Convergence of every such sequence is a *completeness* property of the ambient metric space; \(\mathbb Q\) fails it.
- **Geometric versus harmonic series:** \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty 2^{-n}=1\) but \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty 1/n\) diverges.
- **Complex contour theorem:** Under suitable analytic and domain hypotheses, \(\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0\); residue methods apply when isolated singularities are enclosed by an appropriately oriented contour.
