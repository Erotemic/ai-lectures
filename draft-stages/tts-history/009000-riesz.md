# Frigyes Riesz: How do inner products represent the measurements we make?

Suppose someone gives you a procedure that assigns a number to every vector. The procedure is linear and continuous, but it is not presented as an inner product with a known vector. Can that hidden measuring direction be recovered?

Frigyes Riesz helped establish the foundations of functional analysis and Hilbert-space operator theory. His work connected linear functionals, convergence, and function spaces. Riesz and Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy later wrote influential lectures on functional analysis. Several theorems bear the name Riesz; the one we need here is the representation of continuous linear functionals in a Hilbert space.

In the ordinary plane, a linear measurement can look like twice the first coordinate plus three times the second. This is just the inner product with the vector whose coordinates are two and three. The Riesz representation theorem says that the same phenomenon holds for every bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space: there is a unique vector that performs the measurement by inner product, with the appropriate convention in the complex case.

Why does this help spectral theory? To understand an operator, we constantly measure its output against trial vectors. We write an inner product involving the operator and ask how it changes when the input changes. Representing bounded linear measurements by vectors makes adjoints and orthogonal projections natural. The adjoint is the operation that transfers the measurement from the output side to the input side.

The representation theorem also shows why the norm of a functional is not an abstract extra number. It equals the length of the representing vector. A bound on all measurements is a bound on a concrete geometric object.

There is an important limit. In a general Banach space, not every bounded linear functional is given by an inner product, because there may be no inner product at all. And in Hilbert space an unbounded functional does not fit the bounded representation theorem.

Riesz's place in the story is the identification of analytic data with geometry. Residuals, projections, and spectral estimates can be expressed as inner products, and these expressions have quantitative content. A theorem about the movement of subspaces uses that geometry at every stage.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For a real Hilbert space \(H\), every bounded linear functional
\(\ell:H\to\mathbb R\) has a unique representing vector \(y\in H\) with
\(\ell(x)=\langle x,y\rangle\) and \(\|\ell\|=\|y\|\).
For \(\ell(x_1,x_2)=2x_1+3x_2\), the representing vector is \((2,3)\).
