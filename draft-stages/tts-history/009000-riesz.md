# Frigyes Riesz: How Can a Function Become a Vector?

Imagine hearing a note played on a violin. You can describe the sound as a mixture of pure frequencies. There is a number for the first tone, another for the second, and so on. In principle there are infinitely many. Suppose a mathematical calculation hands you this infinite list, without handing you a sound. How can you tell whether the list describes an actual waveform? And if it does, in what sense does the sum of all those tones approach that waveform?

Now imagine a laboratory that never shows you a waveform directly. Instead, it lets you make measurements of it. For every possible waveform, you can ask for a weighted average, or the strength of one harmonic. The measurement is linear: adding two signals adds their measurements. Must every well-behaved linear measurement have some concrete mathematical object behind it? Can you reconstruct the hidden rule by knowing how it acts on all signals?

A third question is waiting. An integral equation can have infinitely many possible solutions, and the operation defined by its integral may not look like a finite matrix. Yet certain operators behave strikingly like matrices: away from zero, their spectral values are isolated, and a failure of invertibility can be described in terms of a finite-dimensional eigenspace. Why should infinite-dimensional analysis recover finite-dimensional behavior at all?

These questions were central to the work of Frigyes Riesz, the Hungarian mathematician who lived from 1880 to 1956. He helped turn Fourier series, functions, and linear equations into subjects of a common geometry. His name is attached to several different representation theorems, to the Riesz–Fischer theorem, to parts of the spectral theory of compact operators, and to ordered vector spaces. The challenge in telling his story is to understand how those results fit together without pretending that they are all the same theorem.

There is another reason to look closely at his life. Riesz built mathematical institutions during the political rearrangement of Central Europe after the First World War, and then faced anti-Jewish persecution during the Second. His career shows both the portability of mathematical ideas and the vulnerability of the institutions and people who sustain them. We will begin with a calculation he helped make rigorous: taking an infinite set of coefficients and finding the mathematical object they describe.

## From Győr to the problem of infinite series

Riesz was born in January 1880 in Győr, in the Kingdom of Hungary, then part of Austria-Hungary. His father, Ignácz Riesz, was a physician active in the city's Jewish educational community. His younger brother Marcell, often called Marcel in international publications, also became a distinguished mathematician. Their research careers later took different paths. Marcell became especially known for work in harmonic analysis and interpolation, while Frigyes became a central figure in functional analysis. When you encounter a result attributed simply to Riesz, it is worth asking which brother, which theorem, and which historical setting are meant.

Frigyes studied in Zürich, Budapest, and Göttingen. Göttingen was one of the places where abstract questions about functions and integral equations were becoming central to mathematics. David Hilbert was investigating ways to solve equations whose unknown was itself a function. A differential equation or an integral equation might be approximated with finitely many numbers, but its actual solution generally required infinitely many degrees of freedom. A familiar matrix could no longer be the whole story.

The early career helps explain an aspect of Riesz that students later remembered: he was comfortable developing deep mathematics away from a fashionable center. He could produce influential papers while teaching in a secondary school, at a time when ideas about measure and infinite-dimensional space were moving rapidly through Paris and Göttingen. His work was neither a provincial imitation nor an isolated discovery. He used the available new tools to ask his own question: when can a collection of numerical coefficients be treated as a real mathematical object, rather than a formal promise?

Riesz received his doctorate in Budapest in 1902. He then taught in secondary schools, including positions in Lőcse and Budapest, while continuing his research. His early breakthroughs did not require a prestigious university chair. They arose from the need to justify an operation analysts already performed: reconstructing functions by summing infinitely many orthogonal contributions.

We should remember what had changed by the beginning of the twentieth century. The idea of an integral had been extended beyond the Riemann integral. Lebesgue integration made it possible to handle functions with complicated sets of discontinuities and to state convergence theorems suited to analysis. Meanwhile, the theory of orthogonal systems offered a way to break functions into independent pieces. What was missing was a reliable account of how the infinite collection of pieces and the original function related to one another.

## A waveform made from numbers

Take the interval from zero to one. Think of a string fixed at both endpoints, with its possible displacement described by a function of position. One simple family of shapes consists of sine waves: the first completes half an oscillation across the string, the second completes a full oscillation, the third one and a half, and so on. Normalize each sine wave so that its squared integral over the interval is one. Distinct normalized sine waves are orthogonal: multiply two different ones, integrate, and you get zero.

Orthogonality gives us an exact accounting rule. Suppose we combine the first sine wave with coefficient one and the second with coefficient one half. Because the waves are perpendicular in the geometry defined by integration, the squared size of their sum is one squared plus one half squared. That is one and one quarter. There is no cross term to worry about. This is the Pythagorean theorem, but the two perpendicular directions are functions, not arrows drawn on a page.

Now continue. Give the third wave coefficient one third, the fourth coefficient one fourth, and the nth wave coefficient one divided by n. We can always make a finite partial sum: use only the first ten waves, or the first thousand. The question is whether those partial sums converge to a function in a useful sense as we keep adding frequencies.

Measure the distance between two partial sums by squaring their difference and integrating over the interval. Orthogonality makes the squared distance equal to the sum of the squares of the coefficients in the omitted tail. The coefficients are one over n, so their squares are one over n squared. That numerical series has a finite sum. Consequently, once we have included enough waves, the remaining total squared contribution can be made as small as we wish. The partial sums form what mathematicians call a Cauchy sequence: later approximations become uniformly close to one another in this particular measure of size.

But a Cauchy sequence does not automatically come with a limit in whichever collection of functions we happened to choose. Consider the collection of finite sums of sine waves. Every partial sum belongs to it. Their limit need not be a finite sum. If we insist that admissible objects have only finitely many frequencies, the limit may fall outside our space. We need a space that has room for the limit.

The appropriate setting is the space of square-integrable functions, usually called L two. Its distance is defined by integrating the square of the difference. Two functions that differ only on a set of measure zero represent the same element. The important property is completeness: a Cauchy sequence in this norm converges to an element of L two. Once completeness is established, our increasingly long Fourier sums do indeed converge to a legitimate square-integrable function.

This does not promise that the functions converge at every point. It does not promise that their maximum pointwise error becomes small. It promises convergence in mean square. For a listener, the distinction is between controlling the average squared error across the whole string and controlling what happens at every single location. The former is exactly what the orthogonality calculation supplies.

## What Riesz and Fischer each contributed

In 1907, Riesz published several short notes in the proceedings of the French Academy of Sciences. In the same period Ernst Fischer published related work on square-integrable functions. Their names became joined in the Riesz–Fischer theorem, but a single modern formulation can conceal the original distinctions.

One version focuses on completeness. If approximations become Cauchy in mean-square distance, there exists a square-integrable function that they approach. This is closely associated with Fischer's formulation. Another version starts from numbers. Given coefficients whose squares have a finite total, and an orthonormal family of functions, one can find a square-integrable function having exactly those coefficients as measurements against the orthonormal family. This is closely associated with Riesz's formulation.

Here is why the second statement goes beyond merely manipulating a series of symbols. If a function exists with the prescribed coefficients, we can test it against each normalized sine wave. The measurement against the first gives one. Against the second it gives one half. Against the hundredth it gives one hundredth. It is the same function answering all those infinitely many measurement questions. The theorem says that square-summability is sufficient for this collection of answers to be realized, in the square-integrable setting.

It is important that the orthonormal family need not contain every possible function direction. If the family is incomplete, the coefficients determine only the projection of the function onto its closed span. We could add a function perpendicular to every member of the family without changing any of the recorded coefficients. Existence is guaranteed; uniqueness requires an additional completeness condition on the orthonormal system.

Historian of mathematics John Horváth examined the original papers and showed how much is lost when the two historical formulations are simply treated as interchangeable slogans. In the modern space L two, both statements hold, and one can relate them using Hilbert-space structure. But Riesz's route through representation of functionals, the questions he actually posed, and the mathematical concepts available in 1907 deserve attention in their own right.

The result connects two seemingly different kinds of object: a function, which varies continuously across an interval, and a sequence of numerical coordinates. It is one of the bridges by which infinite-dimensional spaces acquired a geometric language. The same bridge later helped mathematicians translate between function descriptions and sequence descriptions of quantum states. The subsequent physical theories required much more than a single theorem, but the mathematical correspondence was indispensable.

## The first representation problem: a hidden measuring direction

Now return to the laboratory that gives a number for each possible signal. A linear functional is just such a rule: its input is a vector, its output is one number, and it respects addition and scalar multiplication. A bounded linear functional has an additional property. There is a fixed constant such that the magnitude of its output is at most that constant times the length of its input. A tiny signal, in the norm of our space, cannot produce an arbitrarily large measurement.

In ordinary two-dimensional geometry, let the measurement of a vector with coordinates x and y be twice x plus three times y. There is a simple way to understand the rule. It is the inner product of the input vector with the fixed vector whose coordinates are two and three. Instead of seeing the measurement as an arbitrary formula, we can see it as taking a shadow in a particular direction. The length of the measuring vector, the square root of thirteen, tells us the maximum output obtainable from a unit input.

In a Hilbert space the same statement holds. Every bounded linear functional can be represented by an inner product with one unique vector. The norm of the functional is the norm of that representing vector. This is often called the Fréchet–Riesz representation theorem, honoring an overlapping development of the idea. The Hilbert-space formulation is not the same historical result as Riesz's later representation of functionals on spaces of continuous functions.

We can see the Hilbert-space result in infinitely many coordinates. Suppose the inputs are square-summable sequences and our measurement is the first coordinate plus half the second plus a third of the third, continuing with weights one over n. The weights themselves form a square-summable sequence. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, multiplying input entries by those weights and summing gives a well-defined bounded measurement. Its representing vector is simply the sequence of weights.

Why must the weights be square-summable? Imagine using weights equal to one for every coordinate. On any sequence with only finitely many nonzero entries, we could define a measurement by adding them. But choose an input with its first N entries all equal to one divided by the square root of N. Its total squared length is exactly one. The measurement adds N copies of one over the square root of N, producing the square root of N. That grows without bound. So the proposed measurement cannot extend to a bounded functional on all of square-summable sequence space.

That is the role of boundedness. It rules out measurement procedures that become infinitely sensitive to increasingly distributed unit-length inputs. The representing vector is not an optional decoration. Its finite length is precisely the quantitative condition that makes the measurement stable.

A geometric proof can be pictured without writing an infinite matrix. Take a nonzero bounded functional and look at the set of vectors on which it gives zero. That set is a closed hyperplane. The direction perpendicular to this hyperplane is the only direction the functional really measures. By scaling a vector in that direction, we recover both the functional and its norm. Completeness and the projection theorem ensure that the perpendicular direction exists. The algebraic formula emerges from the geometry.

## A second representation theorem, with a different answer

There is another theorem bearing Riesz's name, published in 1909, that begins with a different space and arrives at a different kind of representing object. Instead of square-integrable functions, consider all continuous functions on the interval from zero to one. Measure a function's size by its largest absolute value anywhere on that interval. This is the supremum norm.

A linear functional on that space might take an ordinary average: integrate the input from zero to one. Or it might evaluate the function at its midpoint. The midpoint measurement is especially simple. Give it a function, and it returns that function's value at one half. It is bounded in the supremum norm: the value at the midpoint can never exceed the largest absolute value anywhere.

Could the midpoint measurement always be written as an ordinary integral against some fixed, integrable weight function? Suppose the weight were an ordinary integrable function. Now choose a narrow triangular spike centered at one half. Give the spike height one, but make its base narrower and narrower. The midpoint measurement always returns one. An ordinary integral of that spike against a fixed integrable weight tends to zero as the spike narrows. So no such ordinary density can represent point evaluation on all continuous functions.

The representing object must allow concentrated mass at a point. In modern language, it is a measure: the point mass at one half. Integrating a continuous function against that measure returns its value at the midpoint. Ordinary averaging corresponds to the uniform measure on the interval. A measurement that adds the average of a function and twice its midpoint value corresponds to the sum of those two measures, with weight two on the point mass.

Riesz's 1909 result expressed continuous linear functionals on continuous functions through Stieltjes integrals against functions of bounded variation. The later measure-theoretic form makes the interpretation especially transparent: bounded linear measurements correspond to finite signed or complex measures, under the appropriate hypotheses. The modern representation theorem for spaces of continuous functions on locally compact spaces also incorporates work by Markov and Kakutani. The names mark a sequence of developments, rather than a single statement proved once in its final form.

The contrast with the Hilbert-space theorem is essential. For L two, a functional is represented by an inner product with another L two function. But evaluating an L two function at one point is not even a well-defined operation on its equivalence class, because changing the value at one point does not change the L two element. For continuous functions in the supremum norm, point evaluation is perfectly well defined and bounded, and a point mass represents it. Different norms lead to different notions of continuity, and therefore different types of admissible measurement.

This distinction is a fine example of what functional analysis accomplished. It asked not only what formula a transformation had, but what space it acted on, what convergence meant there, and which measurements were continuous. Once those choices were explicit, the correct representation object could be found.

## A weaker notion of getting close

There is a further distinction that Riesz helped develop: weak convergence. Suppose we work again with square-summable sequences. Let the first vector point entirely along the first coordinate, the second entirely along the second, and so on. Each vector has length one. None gets closer to the zero vector in ordinary norm. Their lengths remain one forever.

But give an observer a fixed bounded linear measurement. We now know that the observer can be represented by a square-summable sequence of weights. When the nth unit coordinate vector arrives, the measurement returns only the nth weight. Because the weights are square-summable, those individual weights must approach zero. Thus every fixed bounded measurement reports a value tending to zero, even though the vectors themselves never approach zero in norm.

We say the coordinate vectors converge weakly to zero. This is a precise claim about the behavior of all bounded linear measurements. It is weaker than convergence in norm. Strong convergence implies weak convergence; the coordinate example shows that the reverse fails in infinite dimensions. A weakly convergent sequence can keep its full norm while its energy travels into directions that every fixed measurement eventually sees less and less.

The word weak should not suggest that nothing useful is known. When trying to solve an equation, a sequence of approximate solutions may be bounded but fail to converge in norm. Weak convergence can still supply a candidate limiting object, because all bounded linear tests behave consistently. Additional properties, such as compactness of a relevant operator or convexity of an energy, may then allow an existence argument to proceed. We must identify which convergence mode each step requires; weak convergence alone does not justify every nonlinear operation or guarantee convergence of norms.

This language became central to the development of abstract analysis. Riesz's explorations of functionals, convergence, and spaces belonged to the movement that later received a systematic axiomatic treatment from mathematicians such as Stefan Banach. The collaboration was not that one individual had already written a finished modern theory. Different researchers isolated different structural properties, tested their scope, and slowly assembled the common language.

## From integral equations to compact operators

Consider an integral operator. It takes an input function, mixes its values according to a kernel, and produces a new function. Many such operators smooth their inputs. An oscillatory input may be transformed into a calmer output. That smoothing effect suggests that a very large collection of possible inputs might map into a comparatively constrained family of outputs.

In functional analysis, a compact operator is one for which the image of the unit ball has compact closure. In a metric setting, this means that every sequence of outputs from unit-length inputs has a convergent subsequence. The definition sounds abstract, so let us build a model using infinite sequences.

Take an input with coordinates x one, x two, x three, and so forth. Define an operator that leaves the first coordinate alone, divides the second by two, the third by three, and the nth by n. This is a diagonal operator with diagonal entries one, one half, one third, and so on. Every high-numbered coordinate is strongly suppressed.

To approximate this operator, keep its action on the first N coordinates and erase all later output coordinates. That approximation has finite-dimensional range. For an input of length at most one, the total length of the discarded output is at most one divided by N plus one. So the finite-rank operators approach the original operator in operator norm. In a Hilbert space, norm limits of finite-rank operators are compact. We have constructed a compact operator by suppressing infinitely many distant directions.

Compare that with the identity operator, which leaves every coordinate unchanged. The identity sends the unit coordinate vectors to themselves. Those vectors stay separated from one another by the same positive distance, so they have no convergent subsequence. The identity on infinite-dimensional square-summable sequence space is not compact. The difference is exactly what the diagonal entries did: when they tended to zero, far-away directions no longer survived at full size.

For a compact operator, nonzero spectral values behave in a way reminiscent of finite matrices. On an infinite-dimensional complex Banach space, every nonzero spectral point is an isolated eigenvalue of finite algebraic multiplicity, and zero is the only possible accumulation point of the spectrum. Zero itself requires care. It may be in the spectrum without being an eigenvalue, as happens for the diagonal example: none of its diagonal entries is zero, yet no bounded inverse exists because division by entries approaching zero would be unbounded.

This is a major part of Riesz's legacy in operator theory. In his 1918 work on linear functional equations, he treated questions of solvability and spectrum abstractly, in the tradition of Fredholm's theory of integral equations. The structure was useful precisely because the operator need not be presented as a symmetric matrix or admit an orthogonal eigenbasis. Compactness supplied enough control to recover a finite-dimensional obstruction to solving many equations.

## A small Fredholm calculation

We can see the obstruction without developing the entire theory. Use the diagonal compact operator just described, and consider an equation that asks for a vector x whose image under the operator called identity minus twice the compact operator equals a given vector y.

Look at the second coordinate. The compact operator divides it by two. Twice that is exactly the original second coordinate. When we subtract, the second coordinate disappears. No matter what number we choose for the second coordinate of x, the output's second coordinate is zero.

Two consequences follow. If the given vector y has a nonzero second coordinate, there can be no solution. If y has zero second coordinate, the second coordinate of x can be chosen freely, so a solution, when it exists, is not unique. The same one-dimensional eigendirection explains both the failure of existence for certain inputs and the failure of uniqueness for others.

In all the other coordinates, the corresponding diagonal multiplier is nonzero and bounded away from zero once we exclude the second coordinate. So those coordinates can be recovered by division, with a bounded inverse on the complementary subspace. The entire difficulty has been isolated in one finite-dimensional direction.

That is the flavor of the Fredholm alternative: for a compact perturbation of the identity, either the inverse exists or there is a nontrivial finite-dimensional obstruction, accompanied by precise solvability conditions. The formal theorem has hypotheses and an adjoint formulation that the companion spells out. The point of this calculation is to hear how an infinite system can fail for essentially finite-dimensional reasons.

It also explains why nonzero isolated spectral values are valuable. Around an isolated spectral point one can integrate the resolvent, the inverse of the operator shifted by a complex parameter, along a contour. The resulting spectral projection selects the associated invariant part. Such a projection provides a coordinate-independent way to discuss the spectral cluster, even when the operator is not normal. The modern Riesz projection construction is one of the tools that turns separation in the complex plane into a geometric decomposition of an operator.

## A university in motion

While these mathematical ideas were developing, Riesz's academic life moved through a changing Central Europe. In 1911 he sought a position at the University of Budapest. A committee ranked him highly, but the position went to another candidate. The surviving account of that appointment does not supply a simple verdict about every person's motive. The outcome, however, was clear: Riesz continued his career elsewhere, in Kolozsvár, the city now known as Cluj-Napoca in Romania.

Kolozsvár was part of Hungary when Riesz took his position. After the First World War, borders changed and the city became part of Romania. The Hungarian-language university was displaced and reorganized within the new Hungarian borders, ultimately settling in Szeged. This was much more than a change in a mathematician's office address. Universities carry libraries, journals, teaching appointments, students, and informal networks. War and political settlement disrupted all of them.

In Szeged, Riesz worked with Alfréd Haar to establish the János Bolyai Mathematical Institute and the journal Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum. The journal began publication in 1922 and became an international venue for mathematics. An institution of this kind gives a research community continuity. Results can be circulated, criticized, refined, and transmitted to students who have never met their authors. The mathematics of functional analysis did not develop only through isolated statements in famous papers; it also developed through the places that made a long conversation possible.

The Szeged environment had a personality of its own. Edgar Lorch, an American mathematician who visited in 1934, expected to speak French with Riesz. Instead, Riesz had spent years teaching himself English, reading grammar and literature without a conversation partner. Lorch recalled that Riesz sometimes corrected his pronunciation, with a little satisfaction when the correction finally made a sentence understood. Such a recollection does not prove a grand character theory. It shows a quiet scholar with a taste for thorough preparation and a dry sense of humor. The same patient attention appeared in his lectures, which students remembered as mathematically lucid even when they wandered away from a prepared outline.

Other recollections complicate the portrait. Riesz liked the measured social world of rowing and fencing clubs, bridge, and the routines of Szeged, while remaining intensely absorbed in mathematics. He supported the younger László Kalmár and encouraged his development. István Vincze remembered a lecturer who could turn a digression into a lesson about how the field itself had grown. One should not confuse a quiet manner with a lack of temperament. Riesz could be amused, demanding, and occasionally impatient, traits that became particularly visible when younger colleagues tried to keep pace with his changing ideas.

The institutional story has a personal dimension. Riesz trained and influenced mathematicians in Hungary, and later worked with Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy. Riesz and Szőkefalvi-Nagy jointly authored a major functional analysis textbook, first published in French in 1952. Its two parts combined integration, integral equations, linear functionals, and operator theory. The book drew on teaching carried out over years in Szeged and Budapest. It continued to appear in editions and translations, helping the subject acquire a common curriculum.

A story Lorch attributed to Tibor Radó makes the point vividly. Radó would spend the summer turning Riesz's lecture notes into a large manuscript. When he brought the completed pages back, Riesz would welcome the effort and then announce that he had developed a new way to organize the material. The young assistant would have to begin again. Lorch told the episode with humor, and it is best heard as a recollection of a working relationship rather than a literal schedule for every year. The later book with Szőkefalvi-Nagy gave that restless search for a clearer organization a more lasting form. In functional analysis, the order of the ideas can be as important to learning as their truth.

The contribution of a mathematical book is easy to underestimate. The original breakthrough paper may establish a theorem, but a good book tells readers why the hypotheses belong together, which examples demonstrate the boundaries, and how a proof can be adapted. Riesz's legacy includes that work of synthesis. His partnership with Szőkefalvi-Nagy also shows why histories centered entirely on eponyms miss part of how mathematics advances.

## Order as another kind of structure

Riesz explored structures that did not begin with an inner product. Consider two real-valued functions on the same interval. We may compare them point by point. One lies below another if its value is never greater at any point. We can also form the function that takes the larger of the two values at each point, and the function that takes the smaller.

For a simple example, take the function whose value at position t is t, and another whose value is one minus t. On the left side of the interval, one minus t is larger. On the right side, t is larger. Their pointwise maximum makes a V-shaped corner at the midpoint, turned upward. It is still continuous. The pointwise minimum makes the complementary corner. These operations organize functions by order, without requiring a notion of angle.

A vector lattice, often called a Riesz space, is a vector space with a compatible order in which pairs of elements have a supremum and an infimum. It permits constructions such as positive and negative parts and absolute value within an ordered algebraic setting. Such structure is useful when inequalities, positivity, and monotone approximation are more natural than orthogonality. Riesz developed ideas leading to these spaces in the late 1920s and 1930s; further development involved other researchers, including Kantorovich and Freudenthal.

This is another reason to resist the idea that functional analysis is only Hilbert-space geometry. Some questions concern distance and perpendicularity. Others concern positive operators, monotonicity, or the order of functions. The same linear language can support different complementary structures. Knowing which structure a proof uses is often the key to understanding why a theorem is valid in one space but fails in another.

## Averages that settle down

Later in his career, Riesz also worked on ergodic theorems. Here the question changes from reconstructing a function to understanding repeated action over time. Suppose a transformation takes a vector to another vector of the same length. Apply it again and again. The individual states may keep moving forever. But perhaps their average stabilizes.

Picture a vector in the plane, rotated by a quarter turn each time. After four steps it returns to its starting position. It never approaches zero; its length is constant. Yet average its first four positions. The four arrows cancel exactly. If you average a very large number of successive positions, the result is close to zero because almost all terms can be grouped into complete four-step cycles.

Now add a third direction perpendicular to that plane, and suppose the transformation leaves this third direction fixed. Every rotated vector splits into two parts: a component in the rotating plane and a component along the fixed axis. The rotating part averages away. The fixed part survives unchanged. The long-time average is the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of fixed vectors.

For a unitary operator on a Hilbert space, the mean ergodic theorem makes this picture rigorous. The average of the first N iterates converges in norm to the projection onto the vectors unchanged by the operator. One way to understand the proof is to consider a vector of the form z minus its transform. When we average its successive images, the intermediate terms cancel. Only the first z and the final transformed z remain, divided by N. Because unitary transformations preserve length, that remainder tends to zero.

Vectors that can be approximated by such differences therefore have averages tending to zero. Vectors fixed by the transformation keep their value under averaging. A Hilbert-space decomposition then shows that these two kinds of behavior account for the whole space. The conclusion is a statement about average behavior; it does not say that the individual iterates converge.

The lecture room was not immune to politics. In 1936 a vacant Budapest professorship seemed to offer Riesz a long-desired move to the capital. Lipót Fejér submitted an extended recommendation placing him above a field of distinguished candidates. The faculty nevertheless rejected the proposal without discussion. The surviving vote establishes the rejection, but not an explicit explanation; historians have pointed to anti-Jewish prejudice as the most plausible context. Riesz continued to work in Szeged, where his international reputation and his distance from the capital existed side by side. It is a revealing contrast between the influence of his mathematics and the vulnerability of his career.

John von Neumann established the mean ergodic theorem in the early 1930s. Riesz later contributed notably direct arguments and extensions, including work published in 1938. A theorem can gain new power from a proof that reveals which assumptions are essential. The rotating-vector picture shows why the argument is mathematical analysis rather than a claim that every orbit eventually settles down.

## Persecution and survival

We must place the later institutional history alongside the mathematical achievements. Riesz came from a Jewish family, and the political changes of the 1930s and 1940s placed him under direct threat. In 1936 there was another attempt to bring him to a Budapest professorship. Lipót Fejér offered an emphatic recommendation, yet the faculty rejected the appointment. The recorded vote is evidence of rejection, but the absence of a recorded rationale means that attributing a precise motive to every voter would go beyond the surviving document. Anti-Jewish exclusion was a serious feature of the surrounding environment; the historical record supports discussing that context without inventing private conversations.

The wartime record is more painful than that earlier disappointment. Riesz had been invited to lecture in Geneva, but the German occupation of Hungary made travel impossible. He was forced into retirement and faced confinement. Friends and interventions by the Swedish legation helped protect him from deportation; the exact sequence varies in some later reminiscences, so a neat rescue anecdote would be misleading. The essential fact is that a mathematician who had helped create a major school of analysis was made to depend on appeals and exceptions for his survival. Afterward he returned to the university, but his family and community had suffered losses that a list of publications cannot convey.

In March 1944 German troops occupied Hungary. Hungarian Jewish people were subjected to escalating persecution, and Riesz was forced to wear a yellow star and to retire. He faced the threat of confinement in a ghetto. The Swedish legation intervened and he was permitted to remain in his apartment. Those events are documented in his biographical record. They are part of the history of the mathematician and of the university system in which he worked.

The violence reached his family. His sister Margit, her husband, and their daughter were murdered at Auschwitz. A history that lists postwar honors and scientific results without acknowledging this loss leaves out facts needed to understand the life in which that work occurred. At the same time, it would be wrong to turn those facts into an invented private testimony or claim to know his thoughts on particular days. The documented events are sufficient.

As the war situation changed, Riesz resumed university responsibilities. He served as rector at Szeged in the 1944–45 academic year, and after the war took a professorship in Budapest. He continued working, teaching, and writing. In 1952 the textbook with Szőkefalvi-Nagy appeared. Riesz died in Budapest in February 1956, after a period of declining health. His life crossed the last decades of the Habsburg world, the upheaval following the First World War, the Holocaust, and the postwar reconstruction of mathematical institutions.

## What the representation idea made possible

We can now bring the mathematical threads together without collapsing their distinctions. In the Fourier problem, we began with a list of coefficients and asked for a function. Square-summability and completeness let us realize the data as a genuine object. In the Hilbert-space representation theorem, we began with a bounded measurement and found the vector hidden behind it. In the representation of functionals on continuous functions, we found that the correct representing object could instead be a measure. In compact-operator theory, a condition on the image of bounded sets helped us recover discrete spectral structure from an infinite-dimensional transformation.

Each step asks a similar type of question: what kind of object can be recovered from partial information, and what condition ensures that recovery is legitimate? But the hypotheses are different. The coefficient theorem uses orthogonality and square-summability. The Hilbert-space functional theorem uses a complete inner-product geometry and boundedness. The measure representation theorem uses the topology of continuous functions under the supremum norm. Compact-operator theory uses a form of finite-dimensional approximation. They belong to a common intellectual program without being special cases of one elementary formula.

These tools are still visible in contemporary spectral computation. An algorithm may represent a function by finitely many coordinates, approximate an operator by a matrix, and compute an approximate invariant subspace. The coordinate description is useful because of the correspondence with a larger function space. Bounded functionals allow residuals and outputs to be measured. Compactness or another spectral hypothesis may explain why particular eigenvalues are isolated. Orthogonal projection gives meaning to approximation error. And convergence in the appropriate norm is needed before any finite computation is allowed to speak for an infinite model.

The lesson is not that every modern perturbation bound follows from Riesz's papers. The historical connections are more specific. Riesz helped clarify what a vector may be, how an infinite family of coefficients can determine one, how linear measurements can be represented, and when infinite operators recover some of the tractability of matrices. His theorems gave later mathematicians reliable objects on which to formulate sharper questions about eigenvalues, invariant subspaces, and stability.

Return to the violin. At the beginning we had only an infinite list of tone strengths and the hope that it described a sound. We can now say what must be checked, what kind of convergence is promised, and what is left unresolved. We can construct a square-integrable waveform from square-summable orthogonal coefficients. We can measure it through bounded linear functionals. We can recognize that pointwise questions may require a different space and different tools. And when an operator acting on those objects has the necessary compactness, much of its nonzero spectrum can be studied through isolated, finite-dimensional pieces.

Frigyes Riesz's achievement was to make those transitions rigorous. His work showed how an infinite calculation can produce an actual function, how an abstract measurement can have a concrete representative, and how an infinite-dimensional operator can be understood through structural hypotheses rather than wishful analogy with finite matrices. The result was a language in which geometry, integration, and linear equations could be studied together—and a body of mathematics that endured even while the world containing its institutions was repeatedly disrupted.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Orthonormal synthesis and the Riesz–Fischer distinction

On \(L^2(0,1)\) use \(\phi_n(t)=\sqrt{2}\sin(n\pi t)\). Then

\[
\langle \phi_m,\phi_n\rangle=\delta_{mn},\qquad
s_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}\phi_n,
\qquad
\|s_M-s_N\|_2^2=\sum_{n=N+1}^{M}\frac{1}{n^2}.
\]

Since \(\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=\pi^2/6\), the partial sums are Cauchy in \(L^2\), hence converge in \(L^2\) to some \(f\). Continuity of the inner product implies
\(\langle f,\phi_k\rangle=1/k\). The conclusion is **norm convergence**, not pointwise or uniform convergence. For a noncomplete orthonormal system, coefficient data determine the projection onto its closed span, not an arbitrary orthogonal complement.

Historical distinction (Horváth): in modern retellings Fischer is associated with completeness of \(L^2\), Riesz with realization of square-summable prescribed coefficients. Horváth's examination of the original 1907 and 1910 texts shows why their formulations and proof routes should not be identified without qualification.

### 2. The Hilbert-space representation theorem

With \(\langle x,y\rangle\) linear in the **first** variable, every bounded complex-linear functional \(L:H\to\mathbb C\) admits a unique \(y\in H\) such that

\[
L(x)=\langle x,y\rangle,\qquad \|L\|=\|y\|.
\]

For \(H=\ell^2\), \(a=(1,\frac12,\frac13,\dots)\), let
\(L(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}x_n\overline{a_n}\). Then \(\|L\|=\|a\|_2=\pi/\sqrt6\). The formal summation \(\sum_n x_n\) on finitely supported sequences cannot extend to a bounded functional on \(\ell^2\), as \(x^{(N)}=(N^{-1/2},\ldots,N^{-1/2},0,\ldots)\) has \(\|x^{(N)}\|=1\) and \(\sum_n x^{(N)}_n=\sqrt N\).

For nonzero \(L\), \(\ker L\) is closed; the projection theorem provides a unit normal to it. Scaling the normal yields the unique representer. This is the **Fréchet–Riesz Hilbert-space theorem**; it differs in domain and representing objects from the 1909 continuous-function representation.

### 3. Functionals on continuous functions and point masses

On \(C([0,1])\) with the supremum norm,

\[
\Lambda(f)=\int_0^1 f(t)\,dt+2f(1/2)
=\int_{[0,1]}f\,d(\lambda+2\delta_{1/2}).
\]

Here \(\Lambda\) is positive and \(\|\Lambda\|=\Lambda(1)=3\). If point evaluation were \(f\mapsto\int gf\,d\lambda\) for an ordinary \(L^1\) density \(g\), choose continuous triangular bumps \(f_\varepsilon\), with \(0\le f_\varepsilon\le1\), \(f_\varepsilon(1/2)=1\), support in \([1/2-\varepsilon,1/2+\varepsilon]\). Absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral yields \(\int gf_\varepsilon\to0\), while evaluation stays 1. A measure with an atom is required. In \(L^2\), a pointwise value is not a well-defined functional on an equivalence class.

Riesz's 1909 original used a Stieltjes integral with a function of bounded variation. The modern measure representation on broader spaces bears later contributors' names as well.

### 4. Weak versus norm convergence

For the standard basis \(e_n\) of \(\ell^2\):
\(\|e_n\|=1\), but for every fixed \(y=(y_k)\in\ell^2\), \(\langle e_n,y\rangle=\overline{y_n}\to0\). Hence \(e_n\rightharpoonup0\) weakly but \(e_n\not\to0\) in norm.

### 5. Compact diagonal operator and Fredholm obstruction

Define \(T:\ell^2\to\ell^2\) by \((Tx)_n=x_n/n\). Its truncations \(T_N\) satisfy

\[
\|T-T_N\|=\sup_{n>N}\frac1n=\frac{1}{N+1}\longrightarrow0.
\]

Thus \(T\) is compact, with \(\sigma(T)=\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\ge1\}\). The point 0 is spectral but is not an eigenvalue for this \(T\). For the equation \((I-2T)x=y\), the second coordinate requires \(y_2=0\). When this holds, \(x_2\) is arbitrary and the remaining coordinates have unique bounded recovery:

\[
x_n=\frac{y_n}{1-2/n}\quad(n\ne2).
\]

The denominators for \(n\ne2\) stay bounded away from zero, so this defines an element of \(\ell^2\). The example illustrates the finite-dimensional kernel/cokernel of a compact perturbation of the identity. It does not assert that an arbitrary bounded operator has discrete nonzero spectrum.

For a compact operator on a complex infinite-dimensional Banach space, nonzero spectral points are isolated eigenvalues of finite algebraic multiplicity, and zero is the only possible accumulation point. For an isolated spectral subset, the associated Riesz projection is defined by a contour integral of the resolvent; contour orientation and resolvent convention must be kept consistent:

\[
P=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\Gamma(zI-T)^{-1}\,dz.
\]

### 6. Order structure

In \(C([0,1],\mathbb R)\), set \(f(t)=t\) and \(g(t)=1-t\). The supremum in the pointwise order is
\((f\vee g)(t)=\max(t,1-t)\), and the infimum is \((f\wedge g)(t)=\min(t,1-t)\). Both are continuous. Lattice-ordered vector spaces (Riesz spaces) encode this structure without requiring inner products.

### 7. Mean ergodic averaging

For a unitary \(U\) on a Hilbert space,
\[
A_N=\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}U^k,
\qquad
A_N(I-U)z=\frac{z-U^N z}{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.
\]

On \(\operatorname{Fix}(U)\), \(A_N\) is the identity. Since
\(H=\operatorname{Fix}(U)\oplus\overline{\operatorname{Ran}(I-U)}\), the averages converge **in norm for each vector** to \(P_{\operatorname{Fix}(U)}\). This is a modern Hilbert-space exposition of the mean ergodic theorem; Riesz gave later elementary arguments and extensions, following von Neumann's earlier result.

### 8. Historical chronology and attribution

- **1880–1902:** Born in Győr, educated at Zürich, Budapest, Göttingen; doctorate in Budapest (1902). Frigyes Riesz and his younger brother Marcell Riesz had distinct research careers.
- **1907:** Three Riesz notes in *Comptes rendus* concerning orthogonal systems and \(L^2\), alongside Fischer's contemporary work. Horváth documents differences in formulation and inference.
- **1909:** Representation of functionals on continuous functions via bounded-variation/Stieltjes integration.
- **1911–1922:** Kolozsvár appointment; after geopolitical upheaval the university relocates to Szeged, where Riesz and Haar establish the Bolyai institute and *Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum* (1922).
- **1918:** *Über lineare Funktionalgleichungen*, *Acta Mathematica* 41, 71–98, landmark abstract operator/Fredholm analysis.
- **Late 1920s–1930s:** Contributions to ordered vector spaces and ergodic analysis; 1938 mean-ergodic work.
- **1936–1945:** Budapest appointment rejected despite Fejér's strong recommendation (individual motives not documented); under 1944 German occupation Riesz is forced to retire and faces anti-Jewish restrictions. His sister Margit and her family are murdered in Auschwitz. He survives and resumes institutional service.
- **1952–1956:** *Leçons d'analyse fonctionnelle* with Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy (1952); death in Budapest in February 1956.
