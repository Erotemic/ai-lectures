# Grace Hopper: How did mathematics become a language a machine could understand?

Imagine being handed a complicated calculation with this instruction: make a machine perform it, and make certain that another person can use your work next month. You know the mathematics. You know the answer should be the same whether a person, a gear-driven machine, or an electronic device carries out the arithmetic. But the machine cannot read your intentions. Every operation must be placed in an exact order, every value stored somewhere, and every assumption about the calculation made explicit. Now imagine that the machine is so new that nobody has agreed on how its instructions should be written. The engineers are still learning what its failures look like. This was the practical world Grace Hopper entered in nineteen forty-four.

Hopper is remembered as a pioneer of compilers and business programming languages, and as a naval officer whose career lasted through several generations of computers. That familiar description is true, but it starts the story in the middle. Before she saw a computer, she had completed a doctorate in mathematics. Her research asked when a polynomial could be factored, a problem that requires reasoning about the structure behind a formula. Later she worked on a different kind of factorization: how to break a program into reusable procedures that a machine could assemble and execute. There is no theorem saying that the first task automatically leads to the second. The connection is the habit of asking which parts of a calculation can be named, understood, and composed without repeating all the work.

Grace Brewster Murray Hopper lived from nineteen oh six to nineteen ninety-two. She worked as a teacher, an algebraist, a naval programmer, an industrial computing researcher, and an advocate of common standards. Her career makes a central question concrete. How do we move from a correct mathematical operation to a reliable and shareable computational procedure? To answer it, we will begin with her early mathematics, enter the room-sized machinery of wartime calculation, and gradually replace machine-specific instructions with procedures and languages that people can reason about.

## A household that valued mathematics

Grace Murray was born in New York City on December ninth, nineteen oh six. She grew up in a family that expected girls to receive a serious education. Her mother, Mary Van Horne Murray, was interested in mathematics; her father, Walter Murray, worked in insurance. Later accounts describe a mechanically curious child who wanted to know how household devices operated. The story of taking apart alarm clocks has become so widely repeated that it is sometimes treated as the explanation for her whole career. It captures an appetite for mechanisms, but it leaves out the sustained education, professional support, and years of difficult work that followed.

She attended Vassar College and graduated in nineteen twenty-eight with studies in mathematics and physics. At Yale she earned a master's degree in nineteen thirty, and, while teaching at Vassar, continued toward a doctorate. She married Vincent Foster Hopper in nineteen thirty; the marriage later ended. At Vassar she taught mathematics to students in a period when professional opportunities for women were restricted, though many women had already made important contributions in mathematical research and teaching. A university career appeared to be her future. She moved through academic ranks and continued studying because she wanted to solve mathematical problems, not merely apply formulas already understood.

Her doctoral adviser at Yale was the algebraist Øystein Ore. Hopper received her doctorate in nineteen thirty-four for a dissertation titled New Types of Irreducibility Criteria. That title can sound remote from the computers that later made her famous. Let us take the mathematical question seriously. A polynomial is a sum of powers of a variable with numerical coefficients. Some polynomials can be written as products of lower-degree polynomials. Others cannot, if we insist that the coefficients in the factors are rational numbers. That second property is called irreducibility over the rationals. It is a basic question because breaking an object into simpler pieces often exposes its algebraic structure.

## What an irreducibility test actually does

Begin with a polynomial we can factor: x squared minus one. It equals x minus one times x plus one. The factorization is easy to verify by multiplying. Now consider x squared minus two. It has roots at the positive and negative square root of two, but those roots are irrational. A quadratic polynomial with rational coefficients factors into rational linear factors exactly when its roots are rational. So x squared minus two is irreducible over the rational numbers. This example separates two ideas that students sometimes confuse: the existence of roots in the real numbers and the existence of factors in the chosen coefficient field.

For larger degrees, guessing factors quickly becomes difficult. An irreducibility criterion gives a certificate: a finite collection of conditions that, if satisfied, rules out every nontrivial factorization. One familiar example is Eisenstein's criterion. Suppose there is a prime number dividing every coefficient except the leading coefficient, while its square does not divide the constant term. Then the polynomial is irreducible over the rationals. Consider x cubed plus six x plus three. The prime three divides the coefficients of x squared, x, and the constant term. It does not divide the leading coefficient, which is one. Nine does not divide three. Therefore Eisenstein's criterion applies, and the cubic cannot be factored over the rationals into lower-degree polynomials.

Notice what the criterion achieves. Instead of testing every possible factor, it studies a carefully chosen pattern in the coefficients. The possible factorization would have to obey divisibility constraints, and those constraints cannot all be met. That is the style of reasoning Hopper learned in algebra: transform an intractable search into a structural obstruction. Her dissertation investigated different criteria from this elementary example, using geometric constructions related to Newton polygons. The name requires explanation because these diagrams are easy to mistake for pictures of a function. A Newton polygon is built from information about a polynomial's coefficients and exponents. Geometry becomes a bookkeeping device for algebraic possibilities.

For a simple geometric illustration, imagine marking a point for each nonzero term of a polynomial. Its horizontal position tells us the exponent of x. Its vertical position tells us something about the coefficient, such as how many times a chosen prime divides it. The lower convex boundary of those points forms a polygonal path. When polynomials multiply, coefficient exponents combine, and the resulting geometry constrains how that polygon can arise from factors. The relationship involves addition of sets of points, called a Minkowski sum. If one set consists of two locations and another consists of three, their Minkowski sum contains the positions obtained by adding one point from each set. It is a geometric version of combining exponents in a product.

Hopper's thesis used a particular archimedean version of this polygonal reasoning. Modern analysis of the dissertation emphasizes her bounds on how the polygon of a product relates to the Minkowski sum of the polygons associated with its factors. The familiar prime-adic Newton polygon is a useful doorway into the idea, but we should not pretend that Eisenstein's criterion or the simple picture we just drew is her original result. What the research shows is that her mathematical background was substantial and technically specific. She had spent years developing tools to infer what could and could not be hidden inside an algebraic expression.

## War and the sudden demand for calculation

In December nineteen forty-three Hopper entered the United States Naval Reserve and trained at the women's midshipman school in Northampton, Massachusetts. The war had made large calculations urgent: firing tables, engineering problems, logistics, and scientific models were needed on a scale that exceeded routine manual computation. The military already relied on human computers and mechanical calculators. New machines promised speed, but they also required new kinds of skilled labor. Hopper wanted to serve and eventually secured the assignment that redirected her career.

In nineteen forty-four she arrived at Harvard's Cruft Laboratory, where Howard Aiken's team used the IBM-built Automatic Sequence Controlled Calculator, commonly called the Harvard Mark I. It was an electromechanical machine, with switches, relays, counters, and a long sequence of operations that could be controlled automatically. It was large and intricate, and its moving parts made computation a physical event. Unlike a modern general-purpose laptop, it did not let someone type an expression into a friendly language and immediately see a result. The programmer had to understand the representation of numbers, the available operations, and the instruction sequence.

Hopper became one of the first programmers of the Mark I. She worked on calculations for the Navy and later wrote a detailed manual explaining how the machine operated. A manual may seem less dramatic than inventing a machine, yet it answers a problem central to the history of computing. A device can be powerful and still be unusable to people who do not know its precise instruction conventions. Documentation turns a collection of electrical and mechanical capabilities into a system that others can operate, diagnose, and trust.

Consider the problem of evaluating a polynomial, such as three x squared plus two x plus five, for many values of x. On paper we might say: square x, multiply by three, multiply x by two, and add everything. On a machine with a limited collection of registers, the program must decide where x is stored, which register receives the square, where each intermediate value goes, and which operation follows. A more efficient plan rewrites the polynomial as x times the quantity three x plus two, plus five. Now a sequence of two multiplications and two additions is enough. This is an instance of Horner's rule. It has the same mathematical result but a different computational cost and a different flow of intermediate values.

To a wartime programmer, such choices were concrete. A calculation might run for hours. A misplaced instruction could waste a day, or worse, produce a plausible incorrect number. Debugging was not an afterthought; it was part of understanding a new physical machine. The famous moth preserved in the Mark II team's logbook in nineteen forty-seven illustrates that reality. Engineers discovered an insect interfering with a relay and recorded the incident. Hopper helped make the story famous, but the term bug had been used for technical faults before that day. The episode belongs to the history of practical troubleshooting, rather than to the invention of the word itself.

## From a mathematical expression to executable instructions

There is a second difficulty in early programming that is easy to overlook. A mathematician can rearrange a formula and know that its value remains the same. A computer needs a disciplined procedure for deciding whether the rearrangement is permitted. Consider the expression two times the quantity x plus three. In exact arithmetic, it equals two x plus six. On a machine that stores only a limited number of digits, intermediate calculations can be rounded, and two algebraically equivalent forms can sometimes give slightly different computed answers. The programmer must decide whether equality in mathematics is the same as interchangeability in the chosen machine representation.

The issue becomes more serious when the data are not numbers but records. A postal code such as zero one two three is a label; performing arithmetic on it would lose the first zero. A date may look like a number but requires a calendar. An inventory count and an account balance may both be stored as sequences of digits, while obeying different rules. A language that treats every bit pattern as an undifferentiated number forces the programmer to carry those meanings mentally. A language with clear data declarations can represent the difference between a quantity, an identifier, and a fixed-width financial field.

We can give a mathematical definition of a correct translation. Let a high-level program specify a result for every permitted input. Let the compiled machine program also define a result for those inputs. If both computations agree for every permitted input, and they agree on the specified behavior when an error occurs, then the translation preserves the program's meaning. That statement is simple; proving it for real languages is difficult because a program can read and write data, repeat indefinitely, encounter invalid input, or depend on machine limits. Nevertheless it explains why compiler development eventually became its own branch of computer science. The translator is part of the proof that an intended computation will occur.

Hopper's early work preceded the formal semantics and compiler-verification methods that grew in later decades. We should not credit her with the entire subsequent theory. But her effort to automate the assembly of programs helped create the practical need for it. Once a machine constructs another machine program, the human must trust an intermediate system as well as the original calculation. That is an important change in the organization of computational responsibility.

## The same calculation, again and again

After the war Hopper remained at Harvard as a research fellow and worked on the Mark computers. In nineteen forty-nine she joined the Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corporation, which was developing the UNIVAC electronic computer. The technology had changed: electronic circuitry could perform operations much faster than relay-based machines. Yet faster arithmetic did not automatically solve the problem of programming. If each new calculation required laboriously preparing a machine-specific sequence, the cost of human preparation could dominate the benefit of faster hardware.

Suppose a laboratory needs to solve a standard mathematical task, such as computing a square root to an acceptable precision. Someone can write and test a square-root routine once. Another program may need that same calculation repeatedly. If the routine is copied by hand into each program, every copy invites transcription errors and every change must be repeated. It is better to store a tested subroutine and refer to it. This sounds natural today. In the early years of electronic computers, the practical challenge was getting a machine to locate the right instructions, insert them at the correct addresses, and connect the pieces so that control and data moved where they were supposed to go.

Imagine three reusable procedures: read a batch of data, calculate a total, and print the result. Each procedure has instructions and perhaps an agreed place where it expects input. The user's program specifies which procedures are needed and in what order. A tool can then locate the corresponding instruction sequences in a library, arrange them in memory, and adjust references when their final addresses are known. The procedure for calculating the total might have been written by another programmer months earlier. The new program still executes the same underlying machine operations, but the human can work with larger units of meaning.

That was the essential setting of Hopper's A-zero system, developed in the early nineteen fifties. It was historically called a compiler and was a major advance in automatic programming. Its operation was closer to what a modern programmer would call a linker or loader than to a compiler translating a sophisticated source language into machine code. A-zero organized stored routines and automated work that people had previously performed manually. The distinction clarifies her achievement. It did not solve every problem of programming language design in a single leap. It made an important part of program construction mechanical and repeatable.

The early A-zero work grew into an effort to make computing more accessible to people who were experts in a problem domain rather than in a particular machine's instruction set. But there was resistance. Developers who had spent years mastering machine details often assumed those details were unavoidable. Others worried that automation would waste scarce memory or processor time. Those concerns were genuine in an era when computers were costly and constrained. Hopper's case for automatic programming rested on a broader accounting: computer time was expensive, but so were programming labor, delays, errors, and the difficulty of modifying old systems.

## Abstraction and its price

The benefit of reusable subroutines can be explained with a small economic model. Suppose writing and checking a routine takes twenty hours, and copying and adapting it for each new program takes four more. Ten programs cost twenty plus ten times four hours even if the core mathematics is identical. If a library system requires an initial additional ten hours but lets each new program refer to the routine in half an hour, the same ten programs cost thirty plus five hours. The numbers are hypothetical; the logic is independent of any specific machine. Up-front effort can reduce the total cost when the same structure is reused. Standardized interfaces make that reuse safer.

Of course, reuse imposes obligations. A procedure must declare what input it accepts, what output it produces, and which conditions it assumes. A square-root routine over real numbers, for example, needs a policy for negative input. If the caller assumes that the procedure handles all real numbers and the implementation assumes only nonnegative inputs, a correct algorithm can still be embedded in an incorrect system. This is a general lesson from Hopper's work. Automation works well when the hidden details have been turned into explicit contracts rather than forgotten.

The separation between the description of a task and its concrete implementation is called abstraction. At its best, abstraction lets a programmer say what computation is intended and rely on tested machinery to carry out the repetitive details. It has costs: interfaces may be too limited, implementations may hide performance problems, and translation may introduce errors. Hopper did not argue that machine architecture could be ignored. Her career shows sustained attention to making the boundary between human intention and machine execution practical.

## Words on the page: FLOW-MATIC

Hopper's team developed FLOW-MATIC in the nineteen fifties for business data processing. The language used English-like phrases to describe operations such as moving data, comparing records, and producing output. A payroll clerk or inventory specialist already understood records, quantities, and reports. Hopper wanted the instructions for these tasks to reflect their domain, so that a program could be examined by people who understood the work even if they were not machine specialists.

A useful imagined program might read a purchase record, compare its amount with an approved limit, mark the record for review if the amount is too large, and otherwise add it to a running total. In machine terms, that program requires input buffers, numerical comparisons, branches, and updates to stored values. In a business-oriented language, the programmer can express the decision in terms of records and amounts. The machine still executes low-level instructions. A compiler and its supporting tools mediate between the language's statements and those instructions.

Consider a simple conditional calculation. A shop gives a five-percent discount on orders above one hundred dollars. A programmer could express the rule as: if the amount exceeds one hundred, multiply the amount by ninety-five hundredths; otherwise keep the amount. For an order of one hundred twenty dollars, the result is one hundred fourteen. For an order of eighty dollars, the result remains eighty. The mathematics is elementary. What becomes difficult in a large program is keeping the representation of money, the exact comparison rule, and the behavior of every branch consistent. Data declarations and common language conventions help make these assumptions visible.

FLOW-MATIC did not immediately make programming a plain conversation with the machine. English-like statements still obeyed strict grammar, data representations, and operational rules. A computer cannot infer all the unstated conventions of a business. An instruction that sounds obvious to an accountant may be ambiguous without a specified rounding rule or definition of a valid record. Hopper's work is important precisely because it treated that ambiguity as an engineering problem. The language must be understandable enough for humans and exact enough for automatic execution.

## COBOL belonged to a community

By the end of the nineteen fifties, businesses, manufacturers, and government agencies faced a problem of fragmentation. Software written for one vendor's machine could be hard to move to another. Organizations risked tying their operations to proprietary instruction sets and incompatible programming languages. In nineteen fifty-nine the Conference on Data Systems Languages, known as CODASYL, brought together participants to work toward a common business-oriented language. The resulting COBOL specification drew heavily on FLOW-MATIC and other efforts, including contributions from many people and institutions.

It is tempting to tell this story with a single inventor and a single date. Hopper did not personally write every rule of COBOL, and COBOL was created through a committee and a community of implementers. Her role was substantial: she had developed a working predecessor, advocated English-like business instructions, participated in the standardization effort, and supported adoption. The collaborative history is more informative than a simplified claim that one person invented a whole language. It shows how an idea moves from a local demonstration to infrastructure shared by competitors and users.

A standard language is useful only if its words mean enough of the same thing across implementations. Imagine two computers receiving a record containing an amount and a date. If they disagree about how decimal digits are stored, whether a blank is valid, or how an overflow is reported, the same apparent program can produce different outcomes. Standardization tries to make those semantics explicit. It creates a contract among language designers, compiler implementers, organizations, and the people affected by their calculations. That is a mathematical problem in the broad sense that precision, invariants, and equivalence are involved; it is also an institutional and organizational problem.

One simple way to think about portability is to imagine a program as an expression in a language, and an implementation as a rule that gives the expression meaning on a particular machine. If two implementations agree on the specified meaning for all permitted inputs, the program is portable for the behavior covered by the standard. Achieving that agreement is harder than writing similar-looking keywords. Numeric precision, input/output conventions, error handling, and machine limits can all cause divergence. Hopper spent a remarkable part of her later career promoting precisely this kind of disciplined agreement.

## Reliability at the scale of institutions

Many of the first commercial computer users were banks, insurers, manufacturers, and government agencies. Their programs handled money, personnel, inventory, and public administration. A bug in a laboratory calculation might corrupt a research result. A bug in a payroll or benefits program could affect thousands of people. The larger the deployment, the more important it became that a system could be inspected, tested, and modified by engineers who had not written its first version. Hopper's work helped make computer programming a durable organizational practice rather than an isolated feat performed by individual specialists.

Suppose a business program handles a million transactions. An arithmetic error that changes one transaction by one cent may appear small. If the same error occurs on every transaction, the total discrepancy can reach ten thousand dollars. The multiplication is easy: a million times one cent is ten thousand dollars. More subtle faults may occur only on boundary cases, such as a transaction at exactly the discount threshold or a date on the last day of a month. Reusable routines and language standards reduce certain classes of mistake, but they do not replace validation. Program design has to identify the boundaries of its own correctness.

Hopper's famous demonstrations of a nanosecond made another constraint tangible. Light in a vacuum travels about thirty centimeters in a billionth of a second. In a real conductor a signal travels more slowly, but the short piece of wire she held up gave audiences a physical sense of how fast modern electronics operated and how tiny timing intervals could become. Her point was pedagogical: a computing system has material limits, even when its instructions look abstract. Many popular accounts preserve the joke and forget the teaching lesson. A numerical quantity becomes easier to reason about when it is attached to a concrete scale.

The Navy called Hopper back to active duty in nineteen sixty-seven, after an earlier retirement, to help coordinate and standardize programming systems. She remained in active service until nineteen eighty-six, retiring as a rear admiral. Her uniform and the later honors she received made her a visible representative of computing, but her day-to-day mission continued to involve programming languages, testing, interoperability, and education. She spoke widely to engineers, students, business people, and military audiences. She treated explanation as a part of technical leadership, not an activity reserved for ceremonies.

## The programmer as a teacher

Hopper understood that tools change behavior only when people can learn to use them. Teaching a machine language could produce a small class of expert operators. Teaching a higher-level language, supported by shared documentation and reliable implementations, could widen the group of people able to express useful calculations. That did not eliminate specialized expertise. It moved the boundary of what a person needed to know before making progress. A student could begin with records and computations, then learn about machine implementation when the problem demanded it.

This educational approach echoes her earlier work at Vassar. A mathematics instructor must choose which details a beginner can reasonably handle, while preserving enough precision for the result to be correct. A programmer creating a library routine faces a comparable design choice. Expose too much and every caller must manage irrelevant details. Hide too much and the caller cannot tell whether the routine is safe for the task. A successful interface offers a useful set of operations with a clear statement of their behavior. The connection is not a biographical slogan. It is a practical similarity between designing a lesson and designing a computational tool.

There is also a reason to be careful with stories about genius. Hopper worked within teams at Harvard, Eckert-Mauchly, Remington Rand, Sperry Rand, and the Navy. Their work depended on engineers, operators, programmers, administrators, and the organizations that financed machines. She achieved recognition in a field where women encountered professional barriers, and her advocacy helped other people enter and remain in technical careers. The record is richer when we acknowledge both the individual initiative and the collaborative labor that made large computing systems function.

## A worked picture of translation

Let us collect the central ideas in one final example. We want a procedure that accepts a list of purchase amounts, counts how many exceed one hundred dollars, and returns the total of all accepted amounts. The input contract says that the list is finite and every amount is a nonnegative number with two decimal places. The output contract says that the count is an integer and the total is represented exactly in cents. At the human level, the algorithm is simple. Start with count zero and total zero. Examine each amount. If it exceeds one hundred dollars, increase count. In either case, add the amount to the total. Return both values.

Now take the three amounts eighty, one hundred twenty, and forty. The total after the first is eighty, after the second two hundred, and after the third two hundred forty. Only the middle amount exceeds one hundred, so the count is one. This small example fixes the meaning of the procedure independently of a particular computer. A machine-language programmer must express comparisons, additions, loops, and memory operations with a machine's limited instructions. A system of reusable subroutines can supply standard operations such as reading the records and formatting a report. A higher-level language lets the human express the loop and conditional in terms close to the business task. A standard specifies how those language constructs are supposed to behave across machines.

Each layer can be checked. The algorithm can be checked against the example and against a general loop invariant: after processing the first k entries, total equals their sum and count equals the number among them that exceed the threshold. The compiler can be checked to preserve the specified behavior of the program. The runtime can be checked to read and write valid records. The output can be checked against independent accounting totals. One layer does not excuse errors in another. The power comes from isolating responsibilities and giving each an intelligible contract.

## How standards preserve a calculation across time

A final thought experiment takes us beyond moving a program between two computers. Imagine a payroll program written when an institution had two hundred employees. Ten years later it has twenty thousand. The program has been modified many times, its original authors have left, and several hardware generations have passed. The essential business rule remains: each employee's pay is computed according to a contract, a calendar, and a set of deductions. Keeping that rule stable requires more than replacing a broken machine. The institution must preserve the meaning of its data and the logic of its procedure as the surrounding technology changes.

This is where clear records and language conventions become historical assets. If a calculation has a defined input type and an invariant that can be tested, a later maintainer can compare a new implementation against the old one on representative cases. Consider a pay amount rounded to the nearest cent. If one implementation rounds at every intermediate step and another rounds only at the end, they may disagree. Neither can be declared correct until the contract tells us which rule is intended. Portability therefore depends on the preservation of meaning as well as the preservation of text.

A standard can freeze mistakes along with good ideas, so standards need revision. Hopper advocated mechanisms by which languages and implementations could be tested and improved. The difficult balance is to allow new machinery without requiring every organization to rewrite its work from first principles. That challenge remains with us whenever a format, a programming interface, or an institutional database outlives the hardware for which it was first designed. Hopper's career provides a historical example of treating long-lived compatibility as a design responsibility.

## A career measured in what others could build

Grace Hopper died on January first, nineteen ninety-two, after a career that crossed academic mathematics, wartime calculation, commercial computing, and public service. She had seen computing change from long electromechanical sequences to standardized languages used across enormous institutions. Her 1934 research on irreducibility remains part of the mathematical record, while her work in automatic programming became one of the foundations of everyday software development. Both deserve to be remembered on their own terms.

If we return to the original task—make a machine perform a calculation, and let another person use it next month—the difficulty is clearer now. We need a correct mathematical idea. We need an unambiguous sequence of operations. We need reusable parts, an implementation that respects their contracts, and a common language for people who were not present when the program was written. Hopper's most enduring contribution was to help move those responsibilities out of the head of a single programmer and into systems that could be examined, shared, and improved. Computing became more powerful when people could build reliably on work they had not written themselves.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. A simple irreducibility certificate

For \(f(x)=x^3+6x+3\in\mathbb Z[x]\), Eisenstein with \(p=3\) gives irreducibility over \(\mathbb Q\) because \(3\nmid 1\), \(3\mid0,6,3\), and \(9\nmid3\). This illustrates structural certification, **not** Hopper's original 1934 theorem.

### 2. Newton polygons and multiplication

For a polynomial \(f=\sum_i a_ix^i\) over a valued field, the usual lower Newton polygon is the lower convex hull of \((i,v(a_i))\) for nonzero coefficients. Newton polygons and Minkowski sums relate coefficient geometry to possible factors. Hopper's 1934 dissertation treats an **archimedean** construction and quantitative bounds on its behavior under multiplication. The exact formulation and historical distinction are documented in Asher Auel's 2019 study. Do not substitute the ordinary \(p\)-adic Newton polygon theorem as a statement of Hopper's result.

### 3. Horner's rule and a concrete computation

\[
3x^2+2x+5=x(3x+2)+5.
\]
At \(x=4\), the sequence \(3x+2=14\), \(x\cdot14=56\), \(56+5=61\). Horner's rule avoids explicitly forming \(x^2\) and generalizes to a degree-\(n\) polynomial with \(n\) multiplications and \(n\) additions.

### 4. Reuse versus copying

Hypothetical labor model: write/test reusable routine \(20\) h; manual adaptation \(4\) h per application; automated library setup \(10\) h then \(0.5\) h per application. For ten uses: \(20+10\cdot4=60\) h manual versus \(20+10+10\cdot0.5=35\) h automated. This models incentives, not actual historical accounting.

### 5. Loop invariant example

Given nonnegative decimal amounts \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\), maintain \(S_k=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i\) and \(C_k=\sum_{i=1}^k\mathbf1\{a_i>100\}\). Initialize \(S_0=C_0=0\); increment using \(a_{k+1}\). Induction proves both postconditions. For \((80,120,40)\), \(S_3=240\), \(C_3=1\). To ensure decimal exactness represent amounts as integer cents, not floating-point approximations.

### 6. One nanosecond

Vacuum speed of light is exactly \(299{,}792{,}458\) m/s, so in \(10^{-9}\) s it travels \(0.299792458\) m. Propagation in cables is slower; the common short-wire demonstration is a scale illustration, not a universal signal-propagation length.

### 7. Chronology and attribution

- 1906: Born in New York City; 1928 Vassar graduation; 1930 Yale MA; 1934 Yale PhD advised by Øystein Ore, *New Types of Irreducibility Criteria*.
- 1943–44: Entered Naval Reserve; assigned to Harvard's Mark I project; contributed to programming and documentation; the Mark I was electromechanical.
- 1947: Mark II moth incident. The word *bug* for a technical fault predates the incident.
- 1949: Joined Eckert-Mauchly; 1951–52 A-0 automatic programming system, historically called compiler, functionally closer to a linker/loader.
- Mid-1950s: FLOW-MATIC developed by Hopper's team; 1959: participated in CODASYL process resulting in COBOL specification, a collective achievement.
- 1967: Recalled to active Navy service for standardization; 1986: retired as rear admiral; 1992: death.
