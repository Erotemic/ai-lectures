# 053000 — Leonhard Euler: A mathematician between three cities and two academies

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** Basel, St Petersburg, Berlin, family, scholarly networks, academies, and the practical work behind Euler's mathematics. **Background:** ordinary arithmetic and curiosity about European history.

In the middle of the eighteenth century, a mathematician could spend years helping to make maps, evaluating ship designs, arranging an academy's finances, advising on canals, and teaching people he might never meet through printed letters. All this could be part of the same job as discovering a new theorem about infinite series. Leonhard Euler's working life makes little sense if we detach the mathematics from those duties. The equations familiar to modern students are the surviving shorthand for a much larger career carried on through households, letters, experiments, institutions, and the changing patronage of European rulers.

Euler lived from seventeen oh seven to seventeen eighty-three. He was born in Basel, grew up nearby in Riehen, worked for many years in Saint Petersburg and Berlin, and ended his life back in Saint Petersburg. Those cities occupied different political worlds. Basel was a Swiss republic with a distinguished mathematical tradition. Saint Petersburg was a new imperial capital that recruited foreign specialists. Berlin was becoming an ambitious royal center of learning under Frederick the Second of Prussia. None was a neutral backdrop. At every move Euler faced questions about money, family, security, academic loyalty, and the sort of intellectual work a ruler would support.

His father, Paul Euler, was a Protestant minister who had studied mathematics while preparing for the church. His mother, Margaretha Brucker, came from another minister's family. The household expected Leonhard to acquire the education appropriate for religious service. Paul could also teach him mathematics, and the family had a connection with the Bernoullis, one of the most influential mathematical families in Europe. Johann Bernoulli had studied alongside Paul, and later became Leonhard's most important early guide.

When Leonhard entered the University of Basel in seventeen twenty, the boundary between general philosophy, theology, and mathematics was still quite different from the modern division into academic departments. The boy was preparing for a profession his family knew and respected. He completed work in philosophy, including a comparison of the views of Descartes and Newton, and began the theological course. But the mathematics took hold. Johann Bernoulli was too busy to offer a conventional private course. In Euler's own later account, the arrangement was more demanding: Bernoulli told him to read difficult works independently and come on Sundays with the places he could not understand. That account, preserved as an autobiographical recollection, gives us something more useful than an origin legend. It shows a student being taught how to read, struggle with, and question advanced mathematics rather than waiting for every step to be dictated.

Euler's father eventually consented to the change in direction. By the time the young man finished his studies, he had written on mechanics and submitted a prize essay about how ships should be rigged. The subject may look far from pure mathematics, but navigation and shipbuilding drew on the same new methods of motion, forces, and calculation that interested leading analysts. Euler did not win the Paris Academy's first prize for the essay; he placed second. He also failed to obtain a professorship at Basel. His first major professional opening was therefore not at home.

The invitation came from Saint Petersburg. Peter the Great had tried to remake Russia's technical and administrative institutions, and the academy established under his successors was part of that larger enterprise. It sought scientists from abroad to train people, investigate practical problems, and help give imperial prestige a scholarly form. Daniel Bernoulli and Jakob Hermann were already connected with the Petersburg academy. Their presence mattered: Euler was entering a community, not simply accepting a lonely appointment in a faraway country. In seventeen twenty-seven he traveled across Europe and arrived in the Russian capital. His assigned duties shifted toward mathematics and physics, and for a time his academy position was supplemented by a naval appointment.

It is easy to imagine his Petersburg work as uninterrupted research. The academy was more complicated. Its scholars needed salaries, printing facilities, instruments, contacts in other countries, and enough stability to pursue questions that did not immediately interest administrators. Euler worked with Daniel Bernoulli, the geographer Joseph-Nicolas Delisle, and the mathematician and correspondent Christian Goldbach. Letters could sustain an argument long after a colleague had moved away. Goldbach, for example, posed number-theoretic questions whose significance survived the particular correspondence. When Daniel Bernoulli returned to Basel in seventeen thirty-three, Euler inherited the senior mathematics position. This was not merely an honor. The improved income changed what he could afford to do in his private life.

In January seventeen thirty-four, Euler married Katharina Gsell, whose family also had Swiss connections and whose father was a painter working in Saint Petersburg. Their home grew busy. Thirteen children were born to the couple, although only five survived infancy. These facts belong in the story of a working mathematician. The academy did not provide the modern separation between a laboratory career and a family sphere. Education, illness, domestic support, and successive bereavements were part of the conditions under which work was written. Later recollections describe Euler thinking through mathematics while children were around him. That should not be turned into a claim that family life presented no demands; it shows a household and a scholarly practice occupying the same time and space.

Euler helped prepare maps of the Russian Empire, studied mechanics and ship design, wrote on astronomical and physical problems, and developed techniques in infinite series and differential equations. His work had a special ambition: to make problems of motion and changing quantities systematically calculable. Newton had provided powerful principles of mechanics. Euler and his contemporaries increasingly expressed those principles in an analytical language that could be adapted to many different physical settings. His book on mechanics in the seventeen thirties was one important step. Meanwhile the questions being exchanged with colleagues ranged from the distribution of numbers to vibrating strings, from astronomical orbits to the mathematics of the simplest networks.

The decade also brought serious illness and deteriorating vision. Euler himself associated some eye problems with the strain of cartographic work; modern historians have questioned any simple explanation that attributes a specific loss of sight to overwork. The degree and timing of the damage are not completely recoverable from recollections and portraits. A familiar story portrays blindness as the almost magical price of genius. It is less misleading to say that Euler had significant sight problems at various stages of his career, yet continued to work within a growing circle of colleagues and assistants whose intellectual labor mattered.

By the early seventeen forties his reputation had traveled. He had received prizes from the Paris Academy, and Frederick the Second invited him to Berlin. Political insecurity for foreign scholars in Russia was one factor in his willingness to leave. Euler arrived in Berlin in seventeen forty-one. The king intended to strengthen the academy as an emblem of Prussia's place among European powers. Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis became its president; Euler led its mathematical work. The two men had an important working friendship, though academic alliances could also become political liabilities when disputes reached the royal court.

An academy post in Berlin was not a modern research-only position. Euler supervised an observatory, arranged publications, assisted with maps and calendars that helped finance the institution, and worked on practical questions involving pumps, waterways, navigation, ballistics, insurance, and pensions. A king who supported mathematics expected useful advice. Euler could therefore be summoned to answer a question about the hydraulics of a royal residence and then return to a problem about the motion of the moon. His extensive correspondence and published work were sustained by this surprisingly administrative career.

Berlin was also where Euler wrote some of his most influential textbooks. The Introductio in analysin infinitorum, published in seventeen forty-eight, helped organize the study of functions in a way later generations could learn. His books on differential calculus and the calculus of variations extended that educational enterprise. He developed arguments through series, curves, algebraic transformations, and carefully chosen examples. The symbols now associated with him were not all his inventions, and their modern meanings were not always fixed in his day. What mattered was the way he stabilized and spread a usable mathematical language. Students who never entered his academy could nevertheless learn to reason in a style that owed much to his books.

Euler's teaching did not stop with specialist readers. He wrote a long sequence of explanatory letters to a German princess about the physical world, astronomy, philosophy, and the principles by which knowledge is acquired. They were later published as the Letters to a German Princess. The titles of his technical papers alone would conceal this side of his career. He was not only producing solutions; he was addressing different audiences, from the university-trained analyst to a curious reader at court. His religious convictions and philosophical disagreements were also part of that teaching voice, even when modern readers find their conclusions disputable.

Relationships with rulers and fellow scholars were not always easy. Frederick admired French Enlightenment culture and sometimes preferred the conversation of figures such as d'Alembert. Euler disagreed with d'Alembert on scientific questions. When Maupertuis died, Euler handled important academy business without receiving the presidential title; royal interference increasingly restricted the position. His departure from Berlin in seventeen sixty-six followed years of tension, not a sudden abandonment of a flourishing life. He accepted a new invitation from Catherine the Great and returned to Saint Petersburg, while maintaining the learned contacts that transcended both empires.

By then he was nearly sixty. Near-total blindness followed, but it did not end his research career. Nor did it leave him working alone. His son Johann Albrecht was a mathematician in the academy; colleagues including Anders Lexell and the young Swiss mathematician Nicolas Fuss helped with calculations, drafts, and publication. Historians of the academy have emphasized that these collaborators contributed mathematical development, not mere transcription. This matters to Euler's historical reputation: enormous individual productivity depended in part on a community able to hear, calculate, check, organize, and circulate his ideas.

His personal world suffered further losses. A fire destroyed the family home in seventeen seventy-one. Katharina died in seventeen seventy-three. Euler later married her half-sister Salome Abigail Gsell. He remained attached to his family and the academy until his death in September seventeen eighty-three. An account of his achievement that simply adds up published papers misses this endurance of obligations and changing relationships. It also misses how much of what he wrote became available after his death, as manuscripts were edited and printed by others.

To understand why Euler's name appears in so many branches of mathematics, consider three examples. The first began with a small geographic puzzle. In the Prussian city of Königsberg, paths connected pieces of land by seven bridges. Could someone begin somewhere, cross every bridge exactly once, and finish? It sounds like a question about the precise streets and shapes of the riverbanks. Euler's insight, published in the seventeen thirties, was to discard those measurements. Replace each land region by a point and each bridge by a connection. When you enter an intermediate region across an unused bridge, you must leave by another unused bridge. Except possibly at the start and finish, bridges therefore arrive in pairs. A journey using each bridge once can have at most two regions with an odd number of connecting bridges. In the Königsberg network there were four. The proposed walk was impossible.

The argument is a small demonstration of a large habit. The problem becomes solvable when you identify what remains unchanged after irrelevant details are removed. It anticipates methods of graph theory, although Euler did not inherit the full modern language of vertices and edges and set out to found a subject under that name. A geographic question, pursued by a mathematician whose professional duties involved real maps and routes, became an abstract argument about connections.

A second example came from the sum of squared reciprocals. Consider adding one, then one quarter, then one ninth, then one sixteenth, and so on. The sum clearly grows, but the additions become rapidly smaller. What is its exact value? Euler established that the infinite total is pi squared divided by six. This is striking because pi first appears to belong to circles, whereas the series is built from ordinary whole numbers. Euler's routes to this result used manipulations involving infinite products and power series that require care by modern standards. We should not pretend that every step of his eighteenth-century arguments was written in the later language of uniform convergence. The achievement lies both in the discovery of a precise connection and in the powerful new tools it inspired others to justify and extend.

The third example concerns prime numbers. Every integer greater than one can be factored into primes. Euler used that structure to relate a sum over all positive integers to a product over primes: for a real exponent greater than one, the sum of the reciprocals of integers raised to that exponent equals a product, over primes, of one divided by one minus the reciprocal of that prime raised to the same exponent. To see why, imagine expanding each factor as a geometric series. A term in the resulting product selects a power of two, a power of three, and so on. Unique prime factorization makes every positive integer appear once. The identity joins analysis, with its convergent infinite sums, to arithmetic, with its indivisible building blocks. More than a century later, Riemann would carry related ideas into the complex plane and ask new questions about where a certain function becomes zero. The later questions were not already answered by Euler, but they grew in part from a language he helped develop.

There were many other sides to the work: polyhedra and their vertices, edges, and faces; fluid motion and the equations governing it; differential equations describing vibrations; methods for finding curves that optimize a quantity; and new ways to teach trigonometry through functions. Each belonged to a different problem and a different scholarly conversation. It is misleading to treat them as ornaments attached to an isolated mind. Euler's mathematics developed within a network of Bernoullis, academy correspondents, skilled assistants, printers, patrons, students, and competitors. Even his notation and textbooks were forms of communication designed to outlive a particular calculation.

When Euler died, he had served two courts, crossed several political borders, survived serious illness and private losses, and worked through decades in which European science was being organized around increasingly permanent institutions. What joined his achievements was not a single theorem but a method of thinking: represent a changing physical or mathematical situation clearly enough that relationships can be calculated, tested, and taught. The next time an equation carries Euler's name, it is worth remembering the family in Saint Petersburg, the academy calendars in Berlin, the distant correspondents, and the people who helped his later manuscripts reach print. They were part of how that equation came to exist.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1707–1726:** Born in Basel, raised in Riehen; studies at Basel, guided by Johann Bernoulli while initially preparing for the ministry.
- **1727:** Travels to the Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences; works with Daniel Bernoulli, Jakob Hermann and others.
- **1733–1734:** Takes the senior mathematics post; marries Katharina Gsell.
- **1730s:** Mechanics, cartography, series, and mathematical correspondence; serious illness and changing eyesight.
- **1741–1766:** Berlin Academy under Frederick II; scientific, administrative and state work; textbooks including the *Introductio* (1748).
- **1766:** Returns to Saint Petersburg under Catherine II; late career supported by mathematician collaborators despite severe visual impairment.
- **1771–1783:** Fire, Katharina's death, remarriage, continued work, death in Saint Petersburg.

### Mathematical orientation

The Königsberg bridges problem concerns the degree-parity obstruction to an Euler trail, not a general solution of all routing problems. The Basel sum is \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^2=\pi^2/6\). For real \(s>1\), Euler's product is \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-s}=\prod_{p\,\mathrm{prime}}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}\). The convergence condition is essential for the direct expansions described here. The lecture intentionally distinguishes Euler's historical reasoning from later rigorous analytic frameworks.

### Evidence and interpretation

Euler's autobiographical recollection about Sunday meetings is retrospective; it supports a pedagogical relationship, not invented conversations. His sight problems have disputed timing and causes; avoid the claim that he deliberately made himself blind by working. His late output depended on mathematical collaborators, including Johann Albrecht Euler, Lexell, Krafft and Fuss. The Berlin Academy history records practical and administrative work that a theorem-only biography omits. His household losses should not be compressed into a story of effortless productivity.
