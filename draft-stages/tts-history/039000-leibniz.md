# 039000 — Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: Can reasoning itself become a calculus?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** calculus, mathematical notation, computation, and logic. **Background:** elementary algebra and geometry; all distinctive constructions are explained locally.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine that you are trying to describe a moving object, a curve, or a complicated decision. In every case, the details seem different. The object changes place, the curve changes slope, and the decision depends on conditions. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz wanted to discover whether changing things could be handled by a reliable symbolic method. His most celebrated result was a calculus of changes and sums. But he also built calculating machines, investigated binary numbers, wrote about logic, studied law and diplomacy, and imagined a language in which disagreements could be resolved by calculation. His life is a remarkable experiment in the claim that well-chosen symbols can make difficult thought manageable.

Leibniz was born in Leipzig in sixteen forty-six, near the end of the Thirty Years' War. His father, Friedrich, taught moral philosophy at the university. When his father died, Gottfried was still a child. The family library became a source of education. He learned languages early and read philosophy, history, and theology alongside the formal lessons of school. At Leipzig University he studied subjects that today would be separated: law, philosophy, rhetoric, classical languages, and mathematics. This variety was not a diversion from his later scientific work. It supplied the central problem that followed him for decades: how can knowledge drawn from different fields be organized into a coherent system?

In sixteen sixty-six he published a dissertation on the art of combinations. The title sounds mathematical, and part of it is. One can take a finite collection of elementary things and count or systematically list their combinations. But Leibniz wanted more than the number of possible arrangements. He imagined ideas decomposed into simpler ingredients, then recombined according to rules. If complex thoughts have a structure, perhaps a symbolic language could make that structure inspectable. The dream was premature as a finished logical system. It was nevertheless an important early ambition: turn some kinds of reasoning into something like explicit calculation.

His initial career did not resemble the career of a modern mathematics professor. He obtained a doctorate in law at Altdorf in sixteen sixty-seven and declined an academic position. He entered court service, first through connections with Johann Christian von Boineburg and the Elector of Mainz. Legal reforms, diplomatic projects, and proposals for religious reconciliation occupied his attention. A project intended to redirect French military ambitions toward Egypt was among his political ideas, though it did not become the policy he hoped. These activities gave him a network of correspondents and a reason to travel, while leaving him to pursue mathematics through reading and conversation rather than through a conventional university appointment.

The decisive journey was to Paris in sixteen seventy-two. For several years Leibniz lived among the scientists and philosophers of one of Europe's intellectual centers. He met Christiaan Huygens, whose command of mathematics revealed how much the young diplomat still had to learn. Huygens encouraged him to study modern geometry and the analysis of infinite series. Leibniz also gained access to the work of Descartes and Pascal. His mathematical progress was rapid, but it was not instantaneous. His manuscripts document trial, error, and many revisions of notation before the methods familiar today took recognizable shape.

Consider a very simple puzzle: a sequence of numbers starts at two, then five, then nine, then fourteen. The successive differences are three, four, and five. Instead of looking at the original values, look at how they change. The differences themselves form a simpler pattern. Now run the thought in reverse. If the differences are given, can the original totals be reconstructed? Yes, after choosing the starting value. You add the differences successively. Leibniz came to see a deep connection between taking differences and accumulating them. This discrete picture does not yet prove any theorem about smooth curves, but it gives the listener a model for the relationship between differentiation and integration.

For a smooth curve, the difference between two nearby heights becomes smaller as the horizontal step becomes smaller. Suppose the height is the square of the horizontal coordinate. At the position two, the height is four. Move horizontally by a small amount h. The new height is two plus h, squared. That equals four plus four h plus h squared. The change in height is therefore four h plus h squared. Divide by the horizontal change h and obtain four plus h. As the step shrinks, the ratio approaches four. That is the slope at the original position. In modern notation, the derivative of x squared is two x. Leibniz's distinctive contribution was to organize this sort of local calculation into a manipulable system of differentials.

His symbols d x and d y represent changes in the corresponding variables. The quotient d y over d x expresses their relative change. A listener can regard it initially as the slope measured through smaller and smaller comparisons. Leibniz himself did not define differentiation by modern epsilon-and-delta limits; his infinitesimal language and his metaphysical interpretation were different. Later mathematicians supplied rigorous foundations through limits, and still later through other frameworks. For now, the essential point is the computational role of notation. The symbols invite a calculation while reminding us which variable is changing and which quantity responds.

Take two changing quantities u and v and multiply them. If u changes by a small amount d u and v changes by d v, then the new product is u v plus u times d v plus v times d u plus d u times d v. Subtract the original product. The change is the sum of three terms. The last term is a product of two small changes and is of second order. In the first-order differential calculus, it falls away. What remains is d of u v equals u d v plus v d u. This is the product rule. It sounds like a trick when presented as a formula, but it is simply the result of expanding a changed product and keeping track of which contributions are first order.

An example makes the rule audible. Imagine the area of a rectangle with width three and height four. Increase the width by one hundredth and the height by two hundredths. The extra area has a vertical strip, a horizontal strip, and a tiny corner. The strips contribute three times two hundredths plus four times one hundredth, giving one tenth. The corner contributes one hundredth times two hundredths, only two ten-thousandths. For very small changes the corner is negligible relative to the strips. The product rule is a geometric record of that observation, valid for arbitrary differentiable products under the proper limit interpretation.

The chain rule expresses another relation. Suppose a quantity y depends on u, while u depends on x. Changing x influences u, and the change in u influences y. The overall response is obtained by multiplying the two local rates. In modern notation, d y over d x equals d y over d u times d u over d x. For example, take u equals x squared and y equals u cubed. Then y is x to the sixth power. The first link changes at rate two x. The second at rate three u squared. Multiplying gives six x times x to the fourth, or six x to the fifth. This small calculation illustrates a major advantage of the notation: composition of processes can be differentiated without expanding every expression from scratch.

Calculus also solves optimization problems. Imagine a farmer with a fixed length of fencing who wants to enclose the largest rectangular area. If the perimeter is forty units, the width plus the length must be twenty. Call the width x, so the length is twenty minus x. The area is twenty x minus x squared. Its derivative is twenty minus two x. Set that equal to zero and x equals ten, yielding a square. The derivative identifies a stationary candidate, and the concavity of the area function confirms a maximum. The same method handles tangents, minima, maxima, and the changing relationships that appear throughout physical science.

At first these examples may seem far from Leibniz's actual seventeenth-century research. They are modernized teaching examples, but the methods themselves are central. In October sixteen eighty-four Leibniz published a short paper in the Acta Eruditorum introducing his differential calculus to a public audience. Its title promised a new method for maxima, minima, and tangents. It was brief, sometimes compressed, and not a complete modern textbook. What mattered historically was that mathematicians could now learn a general symbolic procedure and begin applying it to new problems.

Differentiation measures change locally. What about recovering a total from that local rate? Picture a road with a varying speed limit, or a moving body whose velocity changes with time. Divide a duration into short intervals. Over each interval, distance is approximately speed times interval length. Add all these little distances. Refine the subdivision. The sum approaches a total distance, represented by the integral sign. Leibniz chose an elongated letter S, suggestive of a sum, to mark this operation. The symbol was already present in his manuscripts in the sixteen seventies and appeared publicly in his sixteen eighty-six work on integral calculus.

Take the simplest nonconstant example: a body whose velocity rises steadily from zero, with velocity equal to time. Over the first two seconds, how far does it travel? The graph of velocity against time is a straight line from zero to two. The area underneath is a triangle of base two and height two. Its area is two. In integral notation, the integral from zero to two of t d t equals two. The same result follows by finding an antiderivative, one half t squared, and subtracting its values at the endpoints. This is the connection at the center of the fundamental theorem of calculus: accumulating a rate and differentiating the resulting total undo one another, under appropriate hypotheses.

The fundamental theorem is not the achievement of one isolated author. Its precursors include work by Barrow, Newton, and others, and its modern formulation required later analysis. Leibniz's contribution was to furnish a symbolic architecture in which the relationship could be used broadly. The differential and integral signs provided a common language. Once a derivative is identified, finding an integral becomes the reverse problem of recovering a function from its rate. This duality has powered mathematical modeling ever since.

There is a practical reason to care about such notation. Suppose the function being differentiated is a product of three changing quantities nested inside a square root. Without rules, one could expand or manipulate the whole expression by ingenuity each time. With product and chain rules, the calculation is a sequence of local operations. The notation stores information about structure. A good formula is not merely concise; it tells you which step to take next. Leibniz understood this advantage with extraordinary clarity.

There is another way to use the product rule that points toward the integral calculus. Start from the derivative of u times v, which is u prime times v plus u times v prime. Integrate both sides over an interval, then rearrange. The result is integration by parts. In modern notation, the integral of u times the derivative of v equals the product u v minus the integral of the derivative of u times v. Why is this useful? It allows us to trade a difficult integral for a simpler one. For example, the integral of x times the exponential of x can be found by taking u equals x and the derivative of v equals the exponential of x. The answer becomes x times the exponential of x minus the exponential of x, plus a constant. The symbolic operation follows from a rule we have already understood geometrically.

A second application is a differential equation, where the unknown is itself a function. Suppose a population grows at a rate proportional to its current size. Say the constant of proportionality is k. Then its rate of change, d y over d t, equals k times y. Which functions have the property that their rate of change is a fixed multiple of themselves? Exponential functions do. The solution starting from value y naught is y naught multiplied by e to the k t. This is a modernized example rather than a claim about a particular Leibniz manuscript. It illustrates why Leibniz's notation became a language for laws of change: the relation between a function and its derivative can be written independently of knowing the answer in advance.

The publication of his methods also changed the mathematics of a network of correspondents. Jakob and Johann Bernoulli used the new calculus to investigate curves, differential equations, and optimization. Their work, along with contributions from l'Hôpital and others, made the method more systematic and powerful. A celebrated problem asked for the curve along which a bead, sliding without friction under gravity, reaches a lower point in the least time. The answer is a cycloid, not the straight line one might initially expect. The brachistochrone became a proving ground for the new calculus of variations, a subject in which optimal quantities are entire curves rather than single numbers.

The catenary was another famous challenge. Hang a uniform flexible chain from two fixed points and ask what curve it forms. It resembles a parabola, but the exact curve is a hyperbolic cosine. Huygens, Leibniz, and Johann Bernoulli solved the problem around sixteen ninety. It is an elegant reminder that geometry can arise from force balance and that differential equations can determine shapes in nature. The new symbolic language helped make such problems systematic rather than isolated feats of geometric invention.

The discovery of a calculus also produced one of science's most bitter disputes. Isaac Newton had developed methods of fluxions earlier than Leibniz's published papers, though much remained private. Leibniz created and published his own differential notation and methods. Correspondence between them in the sixteen seventies later became evidence in arguments over who had learned what from whom. By the early eighteenth century a controversy over priority had become entangled with national rivalries and institutional power. The Royal Society's inquiry was compromised by Newton's influence. Modern historians generally recognize independent and foundational contributions by both men while distinguishing earlier private work from earlier public publication. Credit is not a race with only one meaningful finish line.

The dispute had consequences beyond reputation. In much of continental Europe, Leibniz's notation and the work of the Bernoullis encouraged rapid development of analysis. In Britain, attachment to Newton's fluxional notation and the priority conflict contributed to a different mathematical culture. The symbols a community chooses can affect how easily discoveries spread. Leibniz had supplied notation that made many operations look natural: products, quotients, composites, and integrals could all be handled through a coordinated syntax.

Leibniz's work on physics also reveals his independent mathematical taste. In disputes about collisions and mechanical effect, he argued for the importance of a quantity he called vis viva, or living force, proportional to mass times velocity squared. That is close to twice the kinetic energy in modern mechanics, although the conceptual setting and exact physical interpretation were different. Imagine a mass of one unit moving at speed two. Its mass times velocity squared is four. Double the speed to four and the quantity becomes sixteen: four times larger. This squares-with-speed dependence is exactly what makes stopping distance and impact energy behave differently from momentum. Leibniz was challenging some accounts of motion that privileged mass times velocity alone. Later mechanics clarified the distinct conservation laws for momentum and energy.

The disagreement was not just philosophical. It prompted precise experiments and calculations about bodies falling from heights and colliding. Huygens's work on impacts was important to this history. Leibniz sought quantities that remained invariant through physical transformations. Here his mathematical instincts were aligned with his philosophical hope that nature had intelligible structure. Yet one should resist treating vis viva as though it were the final modern theory. The road to energy conservation passed through many contributors and new physical assumptions.

Leibniz's work on calculation was not confined to pen and paper. During his time in Paris and later in Hanover, he worked on a mechanical calculator that could perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. Its most distinctive feature was the stepped drum, a cylinder with teeth of different lengths, capable of engaging other gears in a controlled way. An operator could turn a handle and have the machine perform a sequence of digit operations. Making this reliable was difficult. Carries had to propagate correctly; precision engineering and mechanical friction limited what the machine could do. Leibniz improved his design over many years, but his devices did not become widely used commercial machines in his lifetime.

Why did a philosopher of calculus care about mechanical arithmetic? Consider multiplying thirty-seven by twenty-four. One can compute thirty-seven times four, then thirty-seven times twenty, then add the results. The operation is already an algorithm: a finite prescription using simpler repeated steps and positional notation. If a mechanism can represent digits and carry values between positions, much of the work can be transferred from the human calculator to the device. Leibniz saw that arithmetic has structure sufficiently regular to become a machine process. The difference between a symbolic operation and a physical mechanism is less absolute than it first appears.

A second line of work concerned the representation of numbers. In ordinary base ten, the number thirteen means one ten and three units. In base two, thirteen is written one-one-zero-one. Reading from left to right, those digits represent eight, four, zero twos, and one unit. Add eight, four, and one to recover thirteen. Leibniz studied binary arithmetic extensively and published an explanation in seventeen oh three. Because only zero and one are needed, basic operations can be extremely simple, even though the numeral strings become longer. This is not a direct line from Leibniz's paper to the invention of every modern digital computer. Earlier binary ideas existed, and many later developments intervened. Yet it is an instructive precursor to systems that represent information through two states.

Let us calculate a little in binary. One-one in base two is three. One-zero-one is five. Add them as we add decimal digits, carrying when two ones make one zero in the current column and one in the next. Three plus five is eight, written one-zero-zero-zero. The calculation is as exact as its decimal counterpart. The choice of base changes the representation, not the underlying number. Leibniz was fascinated by this separation between what a quantity is and how it is expressed. Once representation is treated as a variable, arithmetic becomes a field for design.

He linked binary notation to an interpretation of the sixty-four hexagrams of the Chinese Book of Changes, which he learned about through Jesuit correspondence, especially with Joachim Bouvet. The correspondence is historically important as an episode of intellectual exchange across cultures. It is equally important not to turn it into a simplistic story in which Leibniz discovered something unknown elsewhere. The Chinese tradition had its own history and purposes, and positional binary representations had appeared in other mathematical contexts. What Leibniz supplied was one particularly clear European exposition of an arithmetic system using just two symbols, along with theological and philosophical interpretations of its significance.

One can take the binary idea further without claiming Leibniz invented modern electronics. In a digital circuit, two stable physical states can represent zero and one. With suitable gates, combinations of bits can represent numbers, text, images, and instructions. The conceptual bridge is the possibility of encoding different contents into a systematic symbolic representation. The engineering bridge, however, required Boolean algebra, electrical circuits, logical design, and electronic devices developed long after Leibniz. A good historical portrait identifies the seed of an idea without pretending the entire future was already contained in it.

His interest in binary arithmetic also gives an opportunity to understand positional algorithms more deeply. Multiply the binary number one-one, meaning three, by one-zero-one, meaning five. The units bit of the second number is one, so write a copy of one-one. The next bit is zero, so add nothing in the twos column. The third bit is one, so add another copy of one-one shifted two places, meaning twelve. The sum is fifteen, written one-one-one-one in binary. Everything is carried out using shifts, additions, and the digits zero and one. The calculation is exact, and the repeated pattern is easy to mechanize. This is one reason positional notation can support machines, even though the historical stepped reckoner operated in decimal arithmetic.

Leibniz also wrote about more familiar decimal arithmetic and calculations with determinants and equations. To solve two linear equations in two unknowns, one can eliminate a variable; the combination a d minus b c, formed from their coefficients, determines whether a unique solution is possible. This is the two-by-two determinant. Leibniz explored determinant-like constructions in the sixteen nineties, but the modern determinant theory was created through many contributions and later systematic notation. The historical point is that he was interested in formulaic patterns across quite different kinds of calculation.

A third line of work involved logic. Leibniz imagined a characteristica universalis, a universal symbolic language in which concepts could be represented clearly, and a calculus ratiocinator in which arguments could be checked by formal rules. His famous vision that disputants might sit down and calculate was a program, not a completed device. Many questions resist conversion into mechanically settled formal propositions; even where formalization succeeds, the choice of assumptions matters. Still, the ambition anticipates later developments in symbolic logic and automated reasoning. The historical connection is a continuity of problems and aspirations, rather than an uninterrupted chain of practical machines.

Here the early study of combinations returns. Suppose two claims can each be true or false. There are four possible assignments: true and true, true and false, false and true, and false and false. An explicit table can show when a compound statement is true. For the logical word AND, only the first assignment yields true. For OR, any assignment with at least one true input yields true. Modern truth tables were developed much later, but the example captures the attraction of Leibniz's program. Once a statement has been given a formal representation, some questions about it become finite calculations. The hard part is choosing a language that captures the reasoning one cares about.

Leibniz also had a distinctive approach to philosophy. He argued for a principle of sufficient reason: there should be an explanation for why things are as they are rather than otherwise. He defended a principle often called the identity of indiscernibles: if two things share all their properties, there is no genuine distinction left between them. He developed a metaphysical picture of simple substances called monads and tried to reconcile free will, divine order, and the laws of nature. These doctrines are not mathematical theorems and should not be presented as if they were. But they make his scientific ambitions intelligible. He hoped that the world's diversity possessed intelligible structure and that reasoning could reveal it.

His philosophical life involved extensive correspondence with thinkers including Antoine Arnauld, Samuel Clarke, and many others. The exchanges with Clarke, who defended aspects of Newton's position, brought questions about absolute space, time, the vacuum, and the foundations of physics into sharp focus. If two universes differed only by shifting every object to the east, would there be a meaningful physical distinction? Leibniz argued against unnecessary absolute distinctions, using his principle of sufficient reason and a relational view of space. These debates belong to philosophy and physics, but they also concern a mathematical habit: attend to the structure that remains unchanged under a transformation.

There was a harder material side to Leibniz's working life. He entered the service of the House of Hanover in the sixteen seventies, serving as librarian, adviser, and historian while pursuing mathematics and philosophy. He tried to improve drainage in the Harz mining region using pumps and wind-driven machinery. The projects proved difficult and largely unsuccessful in their intended economic goals. Years of archival research on dynastic history also occupied his time. These responsibilities explain why his mathematical output often appeared in letters, small journal papers, and manuscripts rather than in a single large systematic work.

Among the correspondents and patrons who helped keep his intellectual projects alive were Sophie of Hanover and her daughter Sophie Charlotte, who later became queen in Prussia. They discussed philosophical and religious questions with him and supported the wider exchange of ideas. In such conversations Leibniz practiced a form of scholarship that crossed academic and courtly life. His papers were not written for a single profession. They addressed diplomats, philosophers, mathematicians, theologians, engineers, and rulers. This diversity was both his strength and a source of constant unfinished business.

He helped found the scientific academy in Berlin, later called the Prussian Academy of Sciences. He encouraged correspondence and the circulation of knowledge across political and religious boundaries. His proposals often outran the institutions prepared to support them. That could make him appear restless or impractical. Yet his activities followed a consistent theme: the organization of knowledge, both in the mind and in public institutions, was itself worth improving.

Another mathematical illustration brings these strands together. Leibniz investigated the alternating series one minus one third plus one fifth minus one seventh and so on. Multiply its infinite sum, under suitable convergence reasoning, by four, and one obtains pi. Today this is commonly called the Gregory–Leibniz series, because James Gregory had found the relation earlier. Its appeal is immediate: a famous geometric constant appears as a sequence of simple fractions. Its weakness is also instructive. The sum converges slowly. Adding thousands of terms still leaves a noticeable error. Symbolic elegance and computational efficiency are different properties. A good numerical method needs more than a beautiful exact identity.

Why does that series produce pi? One route begins with the geometric series for one over one plus x squared. For absolute x less than one, expand it as one minus x squared plus x to the fourth minus x to the sixth and so on. Integrate term by term from zero to one, carefully handling the endpoint through a limiting argument. The result is one minus one third plus one fifth and so forth. But the integral of one over one plus x squared from zero to one is arctangent of one, which is pi over four. The calculation combines algebra, infinite series, and integration in a way characteristic of the new analysis.

A historical complication lies underneath this argument. In Leibniz's time, mathematicians did not possess all the later machinery for uniform convergence and rigorous interchange of limits and integrals. They found powerful results with reasoning that later generations needed to clarify. This is not an indictment. It is a useful reminder of what a new mathematical technique initially looks like. Methods are often productive before their most careful foundations have been written down. The challenge is to improve rigor without losing the insight that made the method fruitful.

The end of Leibniz's life was lonely. The Hanoverian ruler George Ludwig became King George the First of Great Britain in seventeen fourteen, but Leibniz remained in Hanover, increasingly burdened by unfinished court history and diminished standing. He died there in November seventeen sixteen. His fame in philosophy and mathematics grew after his death, while the thousands of letters and manuscripts he left behind continued to reveal the variety of his work. Historians are still careful when dating some of those manuscripts, because the chronology of a discovery can depend on the page on which it was first written, not on the book in which it was later reprinted.

We can now return to the question that motivated this portrait. Can reasoning become a calculus? Leibniz answered part of that question triumphantly through differential and integral notation. He answered another part practically, though imperfectly, by building calculating devices. He described binary arithmetic with a clarity that made representation itself a mathematical subject. His universal logical language remained an aspiration rather than a completed system. The success and limits belong together. We do not honor him by pretending that every ambition became a finished theorem or machine.

What lasts is the principle that the representation of a problem changes what can be done with it. A rectangle's changing area becomes a product rule. A variable velocity becomes an integral. A difficult arithmetic task becomes a sequence of gear movements. A decimal quantity becomes a pattern of zeros and ones. A logical dispute might, when its premises and meanings are made exact, become a proof that can be checked. Leibniz's career shows mathematics advancing through the invention of symbolic tools that preserve structure while making new operations possible. He gave science more than formulas. He helped give it a language in which new questions could be asked and answered.

## Display-only mathematical companion

### Differences and accumulation

For a sequence \(a_n\), let \(\Delta a_n=a_{n+1}-a_n\). Then
\[
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Delta a_n=a_N-a_0.
\]
This discrete telescoping identity motivates the differentiation/integration duality but does not by itself prove the continuous fundamental theorem.

### Leibniz's differential and integral calculus

For differentiable \(u,v\) and a differentiable composition \(y=f(u(x))\),
\[
d(uv)=u\,dv+v\,du,\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx},
\qquad \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a).
\]
These are modern formulations. Leibniz used differentials and infinitesimal arguments rather than nineteenth-century limit definitions.

**Running rectangle:** for \(A=uv\),
\[
(u+\delta u)(v+\delta v)-uv=u\,\delta v+v\,\delta u+\delta u\,\delta v.
\]
The last term has second order in the simultaneous changes; this motivates the differential product rule.

### Optimization and accumulation

At fixed perimeter 40, \(A(x)=x(20-x)\), with \(A'(x)=20-2x\) and \(A''(x)=-2<0\). The unique maximum on \([0,20]\) occurs at \(x=10\), area \(100\).

For \(v(t)=t\),
\[
\int_0^2 t\,dt=\left[\tfrac12t^2\right]_0^2=2.
\]

### Binary calculations

\[
13=8+4+1=(1101)_2,\quad 3=(11)_2,\quad 5=(101)_2,\quad 3+5=(1000)_2=8.
\]

### The Gregory–Leibniz series

For \(|x|<1\),
\[
\frac{1}{1+x^2}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n x^{2n}.
\]
Integration to \(1\), with an appropriate limiting justification, gives
\[
\frac\pi4=\arctan(1)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}.
\]
After \(N\) terms, the alternating-series error has magnitude at most \(1/(2N+1)\), and the corresponding \(\pi\) error at most \(4/(2N+1)\). The series is historically associated with both James Gregory and Leibniz; it is not a sole-priority claim.

### Historical distinctions

- Leibniz published differential calculus in 1684 and integral calculus in 1686. Newton developed fluxions earlier in manuscripts; their major contributions were independent and their priority dispute had significant social consequences.
- The stepped reckoner was a mechanical device employing a stepped drum; it was not a functioning modern programmable computer.
- The 1703 binary paper was an important exposition, but binary representation predated Leibniz in other traditions and mathematical manuscripts. Its discussion of Chinese hexagrams is a distinct historical cross-cultural exchange.
- The characteristica universalis and calculus ratiocinator were proposals, not completed modern formal calculi.
