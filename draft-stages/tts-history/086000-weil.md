# 086000 - André Weil: A life across languages, exile, and algebraic geometry

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Parisian family, sister Simone, European and Indian education, Bourbaki, war, imprisonment, emigration, teaching, and the transformation of number theory and geometry. **Background:** mathematical curiosity; curves, finite fields and conjectures explained intuitively.

André Weil once found himself imprisoned in France while Europe was descending into war. In circumstances no mathematician would choose, he worked on a theorem connecting the number of solutions to equations over finite fields with the geometry of curves. He later emerged as one of the architects of twentieth-century number theory and algebraic geometry. The juxtaposition is dramatic, but the prison episode is not an explanation of all his work. Weil's life crossed languages, countries, political disasters, and institutions. He was born in a cultivated Jewish household in Paris, taught in India, helped create a collective mathematical author with a fictional name, passed through Europe as a refugee, and eventually became a leading figure at the Institute for Advanced Study. The mathematics grew from that intellectual geography.

He was born in May nineteen oh six, the son of Bernard Weil, a physician, and Selma Reinherz Weil. The family had roots in Alsace and in Jewish communities connected to central and eastern Europe. Their home was a place of books, music, languages, and strong educational expectations. André's younger sister Simone, born in nineteen oh nine, would become a philosopher and religious thinker whose life and writings followed a very different course. It is easy to make their relationship into an allegory of mathematics and philosophy. They were first brother and sister, sharing the disruptions of war and a demanding family environment, and exchanging ideas as independent adults.

The First World War began when André was a child. His father served as a military doctor, and the family moved as his assignments changed. Such moves disrupted schooling, but they also exposed the young Weil to different places and ways of life. He read widely, learned languages, and became passionately interested in mathematics. His later memoir is rich with recollections of early reading, travel, and the teachers who encouraged him. Autobiographical testimony is invaluable, but it is also a retrospective construction: the accomplished mathematician selects events that give a shape to an apprenticeship he could not have recognized completely while it was happening.

At the Lycée Saint-Louis in Paris, Weil encountered mathematics beyond ordinary school examinations. Jacques Hadamard, a major figure in analysis and mathematical problem-solving, gave him advice and recommended books. Weil read Camille Jordan's treatise on analysis and became fascinated by questions that connected arithmetic to geometry. He entered the École Normale Supérieure in nineteen twenty-two, joining a cohort for whom mathematics, physics, and philosophy were being reorganized by recent discoveries. The École was one of the institutions where French mathematicians learned not merely technical skills but a common style of argument and a network of colleagues.

Weil did not confine himself to one national tradition. He studied with and read French analysts, traveled to Italy to learn from algebraic geometers, and went to Göttingen, where Emmy Noether and Richard Courant belonged to a remarkably active mathematical community. These journeys mattered because methods that seemed standard in one place were unfamiliar in another. Italian algebraic geometers had developed powerful geometric intuition; German researchers were building structural and algebraic techniques; French universities had their own strengths in analysis and geometry. Weil believed important questions required him to move among these traditions rather than treat the language of any one school as final.

He also made time for the study of Sanskrit, ancient literature, painting, and languages. In later years he wrote about the history of number theory with close attention to older texts. To call these interests decorative would miss something about his intellectual habits. Mathematical cultures are historical cultures: one discovers what an older theorem actually says by learning the notation and problems of its author, not just by reading a modern paraphrase. Weil's respect for historical writing did not make him gentle about the mathematics of his contemporaries. He could be sharply critical of exposition or argument he thought deficient.

His doctoral work, completed in nineteen twenty-eight, concerned the arithmetic of algebraic curves. A curve can be specified by an equation, for instance one relating two variables. In ordinary geometry we may ask what the curve looks like over the real numbers. In number theory we can ask whether its points have rational coordinates, or how many solutions it has when the allowed numbers come from a finite field. These questions are related, but a solution in one setting is not automatically a solution in another. Weil was seeking ways to carry geometric reasoning into arithmetic without losing the precision of the arithmetic question.

His thesis contributed to what became known as the Mordell–Weil theorem, a result about the structure of rational points on certain algebraic varieties. The larger problem known as the Mordell conjecture remained far beyond his reach and would only be proved decades later by Gerd Faltings. Weil's thesis established a different major result. Giving the correct attribution matters: a famous conjecture, a theorem connected to it, and a proof completed half a century later should not be folded into one undifferentiated triumph. The result made the young Weil known to an international mathematical audience.

After completing military service, he taught in India, at Aligarh Muslim University from nineteen thirty to nineteen thirty-two. The appointment arose through contacts that had initially concerned the possibility of teaching French civilization. Instead, a mathematics position was available. Weil took the opportunity to travel, study Sanskrit and Indian culture, and encounter a world outside the European universities that had trained him. His account of the period is enthusiastic about travel and learning, but it belongs to the viewpoint of a French visitor who could leave. Aligarh had its own intellectual and political history under British colonial rule. Its people were not supporting characters in a European mathematical biography.

Returning to France, he joined the University of Strasbourg in the early nineteen thirties. There he worked alongside Henri Cartan, the son of the mathematician Élie Cartan. Their conversations often concerned the dissatisfaction they felt with existing analysis textbooks. They wanted a more coherent presentation of mathematics, one organized by general structures and precise statements rather than by an inherited sequence of disconnected techniques. A group of young scholars began meeting to write a new work. It acquired the name Nicolas Bourbaki, a fictional mathematician whose supposed personal identity became part of the group's elaborate humor.

The joke concealed serious labor. Bourbaki was a collective project with participants such as Henri Cartan, Jean Dieudonné, Claude Chevalley, and others. Authors argued through definitions, rewrote chapters, and tried to make different branches of mathematics fit a shared structural language. Weil was an essential founder, but the books were not secretly written by him alone. The use of a single fictitious name makes historians work harder to recover the collaborative process. It also reveals something about intellectual authority: a theorem printed in a textbook can look inevitable, even if the definitions surrounding it were the outcome of long debate.

Bourbaki's influence was substantial, but it was contested. The attention to structures, abstraction, and rigorous exposition helped unify fields that had sometimes been taught separately. Critics argued that excessive abstraction could obscure the problems that motivated the concepts, or make mathematical education inaccessible. Both experiences occurred in different settings. Weil did not advocate mathematical vagueness, but neither should one assume that every historical problem is best introduced at the maximum available level of generality. The story of Bourbaki is as much a story of teaching and literary form as of mathematical discoveries.

Weil married Éveline de Possel in nineteen thirty-seven. Their lives were soon drawn into the Second World War. In nineteen thirty-nine he was visiting mathematical colleagues in Finland when war began in Europe. Weil opposed military service and attempted to avoid the French army. Finnish authorities arrested him amid wartime suspicions, and his Russian-language correspondence with the mathematician Lev Pontryagin did not help his case. Some later retellings emphasize a dramatic near execution. His own recollections and later biographical accounts differ in the amount of detail they can securely establish. What is beyond doubt is that he was detained and that the political situation made an international mathematical life suddenly dangerous.

After leaving Finland, he was sent back to France and imprisoned because of his failure to comply with military obligations. He corresponded with family and colleagues and continued mathematical work while held at Rouen. Élie Cartan went to testify on his behalf during the legal proceedings in nineteen forty. Weil obtained release on the condition that he serve in the army, but the German advance and the collapse of the French military situation quickly transformed the possibilities before him. He eventually left Europe with members of his family, reaching the United States in early nineteen forty-one.

The war did not affect all members of his family in the same way. Simone Weil pursued her own political, philosophical, and spiritual commitments, and died in England in nineteen forty-three. André's Jewish family background placed him amid dangers that extended far beyond the question of conscription. He lived through a European catastrophe that destroyed institutions, displaced scholars, and permanently changed the map of mathematics. To describe these events simply as adventurous travels from one university to another would be false. They were choices made under constraint, including choices for which he had to answer to others.

He taught at Haverford and Lehigh in the United States, then at the University of São Paulo in Brazil. After the war he joined the University of Chicago, where Marshall Stone was building an energetic mathematical department. Colleagues included Shiing-Shen Chern and other researchers concerned with geometry and analysis. Chicago drew mathematicians with different backgrounds into regular contact. In nineteen fifty-eight Weil joined the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, a place where research fellows could work without the ordinary teaching load of a university department. He remained there through retirement in nineteen seventy-six and continued his mathematical and historical writing afterward.

What had he accomplished mathematically? One of his most important achievements concerned curves over finite fields. In an ordinary graph, we might count the real points lying on a curve only if the curve is finite; most familiar curves have infinitely many. Over a finite field there are only finitely many possible coordinate pairs, so counting solutions becomes a meaningful arithmetic problem. Count the solutions not just over the original field but also over its successive extensions. Form a generating function from those counts. The remarkable fact is that this counting function has a structured algebraic form, and its zeros and poles carry geometric information.

Weil established major results for curves, including an analogue of the Riemann hypothesis in this finite-field setting. He then proposed far-reaching conjectures for higher-dimensional algebraic varieties. These later became known as the Weil conjectures. They required new ideas from several mathematicians. Alexander Grothendieck's program rebuilt much of the necessary geometry in terms of schemes and cohomology; Pierre Deligne eventually completed the most famous remaining part in the nineteen seventies. Weil's role was to recognize the pattern and provide a program with extraordinary reach, not to prove all the conjectures himself.

The power of the program is easier to appreciate when we avoid the language of miraculous insight. An equation can be studied as a geometric object, even when the numbers available for its coordinates form a finite system. A geometric object can have invariants, quantities that do not change under appropriate transformations. Counting arithmetic solutions can then be related to those invariants. That connection brought areas together which older curricula had treated as separate. It also motivated the invention of new mathematical tools, since familiar topological methods from the real or complex numbers did not simply transfer unchanged to finite fields.

Weil worked in other directions as well, including harmonic analysis on groups, abelian varieties, theta functions, and the foundations of algebraic geometry. He wrote influential books and essays on the history of number theory. The historical essays were not a retirement hobby in the trivial sense. They revealed his belief that mathematics has an intellectual continuity stretching across languages and centuries. He could be impatient with professional ritual and exacting about standards. Accounts by friends and former students also preserve humor and generosity, though neither quality was uniformly visible to every person who encountered him.

He died in Princeton in August nineteen ninety-eight, having outlived the Bourbaki founding generation and the institutional world in which he was first educated. His life offers a different picture of twentieth-century mathematical development from a simple sequence of theorems. Parisian schools, Göttingen, Aligarh, Strasbourg, wartime prisons, Brazil, Chicago, and Princeton were not incidental backdrops. They were environments through which ideas traveled and were tested. The fictional Bourbaki author was a response to shared problems of teaching; the Weil conjectures invited others to reconstruct a field. The important lesson is not that a single mathematician unified everything. It is that one mind, working through the resources and crises of a century, saw relationships that required whole communities to understand.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology

- **1906–1928:** born in Paris into a multilingual Jewish family; childhood disrupted by World War I; lycée and École Normale Supérieure; research in Italy and Göttingen; doctorate in 1928.
- **1930–1939:** taught at Aligarh in India, then Strasbourg; co-founded Bourbaki; married Éveline de Possel; war began while he was in Finland.
- **1939–1947:** detention in Finland and prison in France; service and escape during wartime; taught in Pennsylvania and São Paulo.
- **1947–1998:** University of Chicago, then Institute for Advanced Study from 1958; mathematical books, Bourbaki, and historical writing; died in Princeton in 1998.

### Mathematical companion

For a variety \(X\) over \(\mathbb F_q\), its zeta function records counts \(N_r=\#X(\mathbb F_{q^r})\) through

\[Z(X,t)=\exp\!\left(\sum_{r\geq 1}N_r\frac{t^r}{r}\right).\]

For a smooth projective curve of genus \(g\), the numerator in its rational expression has degree \(2g\), and Weil's Riemann-hypothesis analogue says the reciprocal roots have absolute value \(\sqrt q\). The general Weil conjectures concern smooth projective varieties of higher dimension; major parts were proved later through work by Grothendieck and Deligne, among others.

The Mordell–Weil theorem concerns finite generation of certain groups of rational points. The Mordell conjecture, later proved by Faltings, is a distinct statement about finiteness of rational points on curves of genus greater than one.
