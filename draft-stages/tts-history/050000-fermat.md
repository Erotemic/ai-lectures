# 050000 — Pierre de Fermat: What can a mathematical challenge prove?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** number theory, probability, tangents, and the culture of correspondence. **Background:** arithmetic, algebra, fractions, and an elementary idea of proof.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Here is a mathematical claim you can test in less than a minute. Take any whole number, say five, and square it. You get twenty-five. Divide by five, and of course there is no remainder. That is almost too obvious to notice. But now take two to the fifth power. That is thirty-two. Divide by five, and the remainder is two. Take three to the fifth power. That is two hundred forty-three. Divide by five, and the remainder is three. Why should raising a number to the fifth power leave the same remainder after division by five as the original number?

There are only four nonzero remainders to test, so perhaps we could simply check every case. But what if we replace five with a huge prime number? There must be a principle behind the pattern. One of the theorems associated with Pierre de Fermat answers just that question. It says that for a prime number p, raising any integer to the power p leaves the same remainder upon division by p as the integer itself. This property can be explained with a remarkably elegant argument about rearranging a list. And it reveals the kind of reasoning that made Fermat central to modern number theory.

Fermat lived in seventeenth-century France. He did not hold a university appointment as a mathematician. He built a serious career as a lawyer and public official, serving in the Parlement of Toulouse, a regional high court rather than an elected parliament in the modern sense. Historians sometimes call him an amateur mathematician because mathematics was not his paid profession. That label can mislead. He pursued difficult research, corresponded with leading European mathematicians and created methods that scholars have studied for centuries. His professional setting shaped the pace and form of his mathematics, but not its importance.

The basic chronology is itself a lesson in historical caution. A baptismal record has often supported a birth date in sixteen hundred one, but scholarship has raised questions about whether that record belongs to the mathematician or to an earlier child in the family with the same name. We need not pretend that a date disputed by biographers is absolutely settled. What is secure is his upbringing in the region of Beaumont-de-Lomagne and his eventual legal and mathematical activity in Toulouse and Bordeaux. Much less survives about his childhood temperament than about the lives of some later mathematicians. A portrait should not invent a precocious boy staring at the stars merely to make the opening picturesque.

A lawyer's life involved documents, clients, judgments, official duties and relationships with other learned men. Fermat's mathematical community, in contrast, was often assembled through letters. Pierre de Carcavi, a fellow official and mathematical friend, helped connect him with the circle around Marin Mersenne in Paris. Mersenne's correspondence functioned as a network for exchanging problems and results long before modern journals could do that job efficiently. A challenge sent by post might return weeks or months later with an answer, an objection, a counterexample, or an irritated letter.

Fermat seems to have enjoyed posing hard questions. That did not always make collaboration easy. Some contemporaries found his arguments too compressed, and he did not routinely provide full demonstrations of everything he claimed. We should be careful about interpreting the habit as deliberate malice. In a culture where results circulated through private correspondence, stating a challenge and withholding a complete solution could be a way to provoke interest or establish priority. It could also leave colleagues quite reasonably unconvinced. The distinction between having an argument and communicating it well was already important.

Let us return to the remainders of powers. There is a simple proof of the closely related statement that, for a prime p and a number a not divisible by p, the power a to the p minus one leaves remainder one. Choose a small prime such as seven, and choose the number three. The nonzero remainders after division by seven are one, two, three, four, five and six. Multiply every number in this list by three, keeping only the remainder after division by seven. You obtain three, six, two, five, one, four. The order has changed, but the set of remainders is exactly the same.

Why must that happen? Because seven is prime, multiplying by a number not divisible by seven cannot send two different nonzero remainders to the same result. If three times one and three times four had the same remainder, then seven would divide three times their difference. Because seven does not divide three, it would have to divide their difference. But the difference between two distinct numbers from one through six is too small to be a nonzero multiple of seven. Thus multiplication by three permutes the list.

Now multiply all six numbers in the original list together. Multiply all six numbers in the rearranged list together. Since the lists contain the same elements, their products have the same remainder. Yet the rearranged product is three multiplied by itself six times, times the old product. Because the old product is not divisible by seven, it can be cancelled in arithmetic modulo seven. The result is that three to the sixth power has remainder one after division by seven. And it does: seven hundred twenty-nine is one more than seven hundred twenty-eight, a multiple of seven.

This elementary argument shows the theorem's structure for any prime. It does not require us to calculate a gigantic power digit by digit. It uses the fact that multiplication by a nonzero remainder permutes all the nonzero remainders. If a number is divisible by the prime, the original form saying a to the p has the same remainder as a still holds, because both are zero modulo p. The proof also reveals why the condition that p be prime matters. For a composite modulus, some nonzero numbers cannot be cancelled, and multiplication need not rearrange the list without collisions.

The theorem is known today as Fermat's little theorem. The name helps distinguish it from the much more famous claim about sums of powers, but the proof we just used is a modern streamlined presentation. It should not be taken as a verbatim reconstruction of what Fermat wrote in a particular letter. We have learned something mathematical in our own right: primality makes nonzero modular multiplication reversible.

Fermat's career, however, was much wider than number theory. In the sixteen thirties he worked on methods for finding maxima and minima and for drawing tangents to curves. These topics were among the ingredients that later became differential calculus. Fermat's method of adequality has a history and notation of its own; we should not simply credit him with Newton's or Leibniz's fully developed derivative concept. But the family resemblance is real and instructive.

Consider the curve whose height is the square of the horizontal coordinate. At a point called x, the height is x squared. Move a small horizontal distance h. The new height is x squared plus twice x times h plus h squared. The difference between the heights is twice x times h plus h squared. Divide by h, assuming h is not zero, and you obtain twice x plus h. When h becomes smaller and smaller, that expression approaches twice x. In the modern language of derivatives, the slope of the tangent is twice x.

The example contains a subtle logical transition. We divide by h only while it is nonzero. Then we consider what happens as it approaches zero. Simply setting h to zero at the start would make the division invalid. Fermat's own methods anticipated crucial parts of such reasoning while using different justification and notation. This is why early calculus has multiple historical contributors, and why a historical lecture should resist handing all of it to one hero.

Fermat became involved in a pointed disagreement with René Descartes about methods for tangents and the treatment of optical refraction. It was not merely a feud of personalities. They disagreed about the validity of methods for finding geometrical properties of curves. The exchange involved other mathematicians, including Roberval and Étienne Pascal, and the dispute eventually moved toward acknowledgment that Fermat's tangent technique was sound. The detail matters because priority and proof were negotiated among people who could be proud, defensive and intellectually generous at different moments.

Optics gave Fermat another opportunity to reason mathematically about nature. Why should a ray of light bend when it crosses from one medium into another? In a simplified modern version of Fermat's principle, the actual optical path makes the travel time stationary relative to nearby possible paths. Stationary means that the first-order change is zero; it does not always mean an absolute minimum. Fermat originally framed his principle in language about the quickest journey, and it helped recover the law of refraction. Later formulations are more precise about the variational condition. A principle that sounds like a slogan becomes useful only once the quantity being optimized, the admissible paths and the conditions are specified.

Here is a numerical picture. Suppose walking on a beach is quick, but swimming is slow. To reach a point offshore, a traveler may choose a path that spends more distance on the beach and less in the water rather than going in one straight line. Light crossing regions where it moves at different speeds behaves according to a related time-balancing rule. The analogy is imperfect, but it explains why the geometrically shortest path need not be the quickest one. It also connects Fermat's variational intuition to the actual bending of a ray.

In sixteen fifty-four Fermat exchanged letters with Blaise Pascal about a problem from games of chance. Imagine two equally skilled players competing in a match. Each game is independent, and either player has a one-half chance of winning. They agree that the first to reach a target number of wins receives a pot of money, but the match stops early. How should they divide the pot fairly?

Suppose player A needs only one more victory, while player B needs two. A could win the next game and finish immediately. If B wins the next game, each then needs one victory, and the following game decides the match. Thus A wins with probability one half immediately, plus one half times one half by losing first and then winning. That gives three quarters. B has the remaining one quarter. In a fair division under these assumptions, A receives three quarters of the pot and B one quarter. The point is not that gambling creates pure mathematics by itself; it is that a social problem about fairness became a question about enumerating possible future outcomes.

The letters between Pascal and Fermat are often presented as the birth of probability theory. That is too simple if it erases earlier work on counting chances and games. But the correspondence was a major step in creating a systematic mathematical treatment of such problems. Their exchange also illustrates why Fermat benefited from a network of colleagues: another person could set an issue in a form that drew out his talent for exact reasoning.

There is, of course, the claim that made Fermat famous far beyond mathematics. In the margin of a book by Diophantus he stated that no two positive whole-number powers of a given exponent greater than two add to a third power of the same exponent. Two squares can add to another square: three squared plus four squared equals five squared. But the analogous claim for cubes, fourth powers and every higher exponent, if the numbers are positive integers, has no solution. Fermat wrote that he had found a marvelous proof too large for the margin. No valid general proof by Fermat survives.

This distinction is essential. A mathematician may make an assertion confidently, may even believe they have proved it, and still not leave a correct argument. Fermat did establish important special cases and methods. But the general theorem became one of mathematics' longest-running challenges. Andrew Wiles, with a correction developed jointly with Richard Taylor, produced the proof in the nineteen nineties, using ideas from elliptic curves and modular forms far beyond Fermat's surviving techniques. The modern proof does not prove that Fermat had the proof he claimed. It establishes the theorem independently.

Fermat also used infinite descent: the strategy of showing that any supposed smallest positive-integer counterexample would imply a strictly smaller one. Since positive integers cannot decrease forever, the assumed counterexample cannot exist. The challenge is always to construct the smaller object rigorously. A vague assertion that one can descend is not a proof. This is another reason his terse correspondence sometimes frustrated his contemporaries: a missing step might contain almost all the difficulty.

The amount we know about Fermat's private temperament is limited compared with the evidence for his mathematical correspondence. He was married to Louise de Long and had a family, and he continued his legal career while communicating with mathematical peers. His official duties restricted how much time he could devote to assembling full treatises for publication. Those are real historical circumstances, but they should not be made into invented scenes of him secretly scribbling proofs at the dinner table. What survives most vividly is a working intellectual personality visible through challenges, arguments, letters and choices about what to explain.

Fermat died in sixteen sixty-five. His son Samuel helped publish mathematical material after his death, including editions that preserved famous notes. The fragmented way these writings circulated became part of the subsequent history of number theory. Euler and later mathematicians supplied proofs, generalizations and new frameworks for problems associated with Fermat. The history was not simply a list of riddles waiting to be checked off. It was a change in what mathematical methods were available and what counted as a satisfactory explanation.

Return to the small remainders that opened this portrait. Three times every nonzero remainder modulo seven gives the same list in a different order. From that observation we can derive a theorem about powers without testing every case separately. That is the essential mathematical move: replace a pattern with a reason. Fermat's life shows the same move from another angle. An assertion in a letter can provoke work for centuries, but it becomes a shared mathematical result only when the hypotheses, argument and conclusion are made available for scrutiny. His achievements are real, and so are the limits of what his surviving pages prove.

## Mathematical and historical companion (not spoken)

### Fermat's little theorem

For a prime \(p\) and integer \(a\):

\[
a^p\equiv a\pmod p.
\]

For \(p\nmid a\), equivalently \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\). The usual permutation proof multiplies the nonzero residue classes \(1,\ldots,p-1\) by \(a\) and cancels the invertible product \((p-1)!\). The proof assumes **primality**; it is not a general statement for arbitrary composite moduli.

### A tangent by a modern difference quotient

\[
\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=2x+h\quad(h\ne0),\qquad\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h)=2x.
\]

This is a modern illustration of a calculation related to Fermat's adequality, not a transcription of Fermat's original procedure.

### The problem of points

If player A needs one win, player B needs two, and each independent fair game has outcome probability one half, then:

\[
P(A\text{ wins match})=\frac12+\frac12\frac12=\frac34,
\quad P(B\text{ wins match})=\frac14.
\]

### The famous claim

Fermat's Last Theorem states that for integer \(n>2\) there are no positive integers \(x,y,z\) with \(x^n+y^n=z^n\). This theorem was ultimately proved by Wiles with Taylor's assistance on a gap; a correct general proof by Fermat is **not** documented.
