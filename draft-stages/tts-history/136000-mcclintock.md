# 136000 — Barbara McClintock: Reading a changing genome in maize

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Cornell cytogenetics, chromosomes and crossing over, Missouri and Cold Spring Harbor, breakage–fusion–bridge cycles, Ac and Ds elements, the reception of genetic control, collaborative institutions and the 1983 Nobel Prize. **Background:** A gene, chromosome and inheritance are explained in speech; no molecular-biology prerequisite.

Look at an ear of maize. Some kernels are uniformly yellow or purple. Others contain flecks, streaks or islands of color. The pattern can look like a painter's experiment, but it is also a record of cellular history. Each kernel develops through many cell divisions. If a pigment-producing process switches on early, its descendants can form a large colored patch. If it switches on late, the patch can be small. A pattern on a seed can preserve evidence about what happened to genetic material while the seed was growing.

Barbara McClintock learned to read such evidence with extraordinary precision. She worked for decades on the chromosomes of maize, first helping establish that genetic recombination corresponds to actual exchanges of chromosome material, then discovering elements of the genome that can change position and influence other genes. Her work transformed the idea of what a chromosome is: not merely a fixed row of instructions, but a physical system whose pieces can move, break, rejoin and alter their effects depending on context.

The familiar retelling says that nobody believed her, that she was alone for thirty years, and that a Nobel Prize finally proved everyone else wrong. The real history is more instructive. Some of her discoveries were accepted quickly within maize genetics; some of her larger interpretations about control and development were disputed, neglected or difficult for outsiders to assess. She was respected early in her career and faced serious institutional obstacles. Both facts matter. The distinction between her evidence and the full theory she built around it is part of the intellectual drama, not an embarrassment to be edited away.

McClintock was born in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1902 and grew up largely in Brooklyn, New York. Her family valued reading and education, though they did not always agree about her academic future. As a young woman she wanted to study at Cornell University; accounts of the family describe concern that an advanced education might constrain her social and marriage prospects. It was possible for an exceptionally able girl to be encouraged intellectually and still confront expectations that science was not a proper life for a woman. She entered Cornell in 1919, beginning a career that would be tied to agricultural science, microscopy and an organism far removed from the usual human-centered examples of heredity.

Why maize? It was abundant, practical, and genetically informative. Breeders had long selected plants with useful traits. A single ear carries hundreds of kernels; different kernels can display visible color differences. The plant's growth, reproduction and chromosome behavior could be studied across generations. A field of maize was therefore not just farmland. For a geneticist willing to keep careful pedigrees, it was an experimental archive. Each cross between plants created a question: which traits travel together, which separate, and what physically happens inside the cell to make that possible?

At Cornell, McClintock worked among major early maize geneticists including Rollins Emerson, George Beadle, Marcus Rhoades and others. Scientific talent mattered, but so did a department willing to support sustained work with plants and microscopes. She earned her bachelor's degree in 1923, her master's in 1925 and her doctorate in 1927. She was trained in cytology, the study of cells and their structures, at a moment when genetics was becoming something visible rather than only a set of inheritance ratios.

A chromosome is a long physical structure carrying genetic material. In an ordinary plant body cell, chromosomes occur in paired sets: one inherited through each parent. During the formation of reproductive cells, pairs can exchange corresponding sections. This process, called crossing over, creates new combinations of inherited variants. Geneticists had inferred exchanges from breeding results, but the inference was strongest when physical chromosome evidence could be connected directly to the inherited traits.

McClintock developed staining and microscopy techniques that made the ten chromosomes of maize distinguishable. Tiny differences in length, constrictions, and visible markers became information. In 1931 she and her collaborator Harriet Creighton produced a landmark demonstration: offspring showing genetic recombination also showed a corresponding physical exchange of chromosome segments. It was a remarkable joining of two kinds of evidence. A breeder could infer that traits had been reshuffled; the microscope showed the chromosome structures had been reshuffled too. A separate research line in fruit flies, associated with Curt Stern, reached a similar result independently. That is how a broad scientific claim becomes secure: different materials and methods converge.

Imagine two paired chromosomes, each marked at two places. Before a reproductive cell forms, one chromosome carries variants A and B, while its partner carries alternatives a and b. After an exchange between the markers, a resulting chromosome may carry A with b. The genetic combination has changed. If the exchange also transfers a visible structural feature, microscopy can independently confirm the physical event. The example uses modern symbols for clarity; it is not a transcript of McClintock and Creighton's experimental notebook. What matters is the pairing of a breeding prediction with a physical object that can be observed.

Her early work secured her professional reputation. She won fellowships, spent time at the California Institute of Technology and in Germany, and returned to Cornell. But academic work in the Depression was precarious, and opportunities for women were restricted by hiring practices, salaries and expectations about teaching. She accepted a position at the University of Missouri in 1936. There she pursued experiments involving damaged chromosomes and how they behaved across cell divisions. Her interest moved beyond inheritance as a stable pattern toward the response of chromosomes to disturbance.

One important phenomenon was the breakage–fusion–bridge cycle. Imagine that a chromosome has broken and lost an end that normally protects it. When the chromosome is copied and cell division begins, exposed ends may fuse. The fused structure can be pulled toward opposite sides of a dividing cell, forming a bridge that eventually breaks. The new broken ends can fuse again in the next generation of cells. Repetition creates a cycle of rearrangement, loss and duplication. The details depend on the structure of the chromosome and the stage of division; the image of a bridge is not just metaphorical. McClintock saw physical evidence of chromosomes behaving this way.

These experiments gave her a way to investigate what we might call genome instability. The word genome refers to an organism's complete hereditary material. It is not a single solid object untouched by development. When chromosomes break and rejoin, genetic regions can be lost, copied or moved relative to one another. Some consequences show up as visible patches in maize kernels. A mosaic kernel is a kind of developmental time record, because cells share ancestry. A change in one early cell can be inherited by many descendant cells; a change in a late cell reaches fewer descendants.

McClintock moved in 1941 to the Carnegie Institution's genetics research environment at Cold Spring Harbor on Long Island. Milislav Demerec and other colleagues helped create a setting where she could devote herself to experimental work without the same classroom load. She cultivated maize, inspected it, made crosses, and followed unstable inherited traits through multiple generations. This work could not be accelerated simply by sitting longer at a microscope. Maize needs growing seasons. Seeds have to develop, crosses have to be made, pedigrees maintained, and observations checked. A genetic result may be measurable only after a summer or after a series of crosses planned years in advance.

The puzzle that emerged was more radical than a chromosome with a broken end. Certain sites appeared capable of changing position in the chromosome, and those movements could alter the expression of nearby genes. McClintock identified elements she called Activator, or Ac, and Dissociation, or Ds. In the specific system she studied, Ds could cause chromosome breakage or influence a gene's activity under the influence of Ac. The relationship was conditional. The effects of Ds depended on the presence and behavior of Ac; without the relevant Ac activity some changes did not occur.

To understand what she saw, imagine a gene necessary for making purple pigment. Insert a piece of genetic material in a position that disrupts the gene, and a cell may fail to make that pigment. If the inserted piece is removed during the development of a kernel, the gene's function may be restored in that cell and its descendants. A large colored sector suggests the removal happened relatively early; a small spot suggests it happened later. This is one possible way to read a variegated kernel, not a claim that every maize color pattern arises from an identical event. What made McClintock's work powerful was that she linked patterns to pedigrees and chromosome behavior rather than stopping at the visual resemblance.

We now call these mobile DNA sequences transposable elements or transposons. The popular phrase jumping genes is useful but imprecise. Not every mobile element is a protein-coding gene. Elements do not physically leap from one cell to another like insects. Depending on their molecular machinery, some are cut from one DNA position and inserted elsewhere, and others are copied through a different mechanism. The Ac–Ds system is a DNA-transposon system. Ac can supply a transposase, a protein that catalyzes movement; Ds elements in this family generally need Ac activity because they are not autonomous in the same way. This molecular understanding came later than the original cytogenetic discovery.

The modern vocabulary should not flatten what McClintock actually claimed. She spoke of controlling elements, and her attention reached beyond the mere fact that a piece of genetic material could move. She asked whether the genome could regulate the activity of genes through changes in the position or state of elements, particularly under developmental or environmental challenge. Her interpretation had both experimentally grounded parts and broader speculative implications. Explaining a colored kernel by transposition is one claim; asserting a general adaptive program that directs genomic responses is another. The second requires further evidence and can be controversial without invalidating the first.

McClintock published an influential paper on mutable loci in 1950 and presented her research at the Cold Spring Harbor symposium in 1951. She later recalled bafflement and some hostility from listeners. Those recollections deserve attention. A scientist can communicate a result and find that even experts do not see its logic. Yet historian Nathaniel Comfort's archival research has shown that the simple story of thirty years of universal rejection is inaccurate. Many maize geneticists accepted transposition and confirmed it in the 1950s. Wider genetics took much longer to absorb its generality, and McClintock's more expansive theory of genetic control did not command the same agreement. An accurate portrait lets acceptance, misunderstanding and disagreement coexist.

Her work was demanding partly because she had developed a particular way of knowing individual plants and families. She did not treat a field merely as a source of anonymous samples to be averaged. She tracked lineages and exceptions that might seem like noise if considered outside their histories. The method required intuition about what patterns were worth checking, but intuition by itself was not proof. She could design a cross to distinguish explanations, wait for the plants, and inspect the result. When a model predicted a particular combination of color and chromosome behavior, a successful experiment increased confidence; a failure asked for another explanation. In this respect her science was both intensely personal in its attention and deliberately public in its evidence.

It is tempting to say that a kernel with colored spots proves there is no fixed genetic information. That goes too far. Most genetic sequences remain in relatively stable positions during ordinary development, and the regular transmission of chromosomes is essential for heredity. Transposition adds a mechanism by which some sequences can relocate or influence expression; it does not abolish the principles of genetics. Nor does the discovery itself establish that an organism can deliberately alter its genome to satisfy a future need. The modern study of DNA repair, mutation, recombination, regulation and mobile elements separates such processes while also investigating their interactions.

The timing of recognition also has several strands. In 1944 McClintock was elected to the National Academy of Sciences, long before the Nobel Prize, and in 1945 she became president of the Genetics Society of America. Her early chromosome work had already won professional respect. During the 1960s she helped with research and training connected to maize populations in Latin America, where the diversity of the crop itself offered new questions. She continued scientific work at Cold Spring Harbor after the formal retirement date often attached to her career. The portrait of an unsupported hermit misses a scientist embedded in institutions and networks, even when she preferred independent research and sometimes found ordinary departmental expectations constraining.

By the 1970s molecular research in microbes and other organisms had produced abundant examples of mobile genetic elements. Scientists could analyze their DNA sequences and mechanisms, revealing that the maize phenomenon was part of a much wider biological world. The wider recognition brought new ways to ask what mobile elements do. They can disrupt genes, create mutations, alter regulation and participate in evolutionary change. Their consequences range from harmful to neutral to occasionally useful for the organism; there is no single moral or evolutionary role. In some genomes mobile-element-derived sequences constitute a large fraction of DNA, but abundance should not be confused with evidence that every copy is functionally beneficial.

McClintock received the National Medal of Science in 1970, a Wolf Prize in 1981 and, in 1983, an unshared Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery of mobile genetic elements. The Nobel ceremony was a major public event, but the award came to someone whose experimental identity had been established for more than half a century. She did not suddenly become a scientist in 1983. The scientific world had changed enough to appreciate the breadth of a discovery that began in a maize field, and she had continued to pursue questions even when public acclaim was neither immediate nor assured.

Her Nobel lecture was titled *The Significance of Responses of the Genome to Challenge*. It returned to her lifelong concern with what genomes do when ordinary developmental systems are disrupted. She described experiments rather than merely retelling an inspirational story. That choice matters. The point of her career was not to be vindicated as a misunderstood personality. It was to understand phenomena that would otherwise be missed if a researcher insisted the genome must behave according to an oversimplified picture.

Barbara McClintock died in 1992 on Long Island. The science she helped begin is now part of molecular genetics, developmental biology and evolutionary genomics. Researchers know more about the enzymes, sequences and consequences of transposition than she could have known from chromosomes and kernels. Some of her broader ideas invite careful comparison with present evidence, and not every hypothesis survives unchanged. This is how science should work. A major contribution can open a field while leaving later scientists to revise its mechanisms and generalizations.

Return to the spotted ear of maize. Once we understand the lineage of its cells, the patches become more than colors. They can be marks left by events in the genome at different times in development. McClintock did not discover those events because she decided to ignore all the rules. She learned the rules extraordinarily well, refined the instruments for observing them, and noticed when the organism behaved in a way the prevailing rules did not explain. That is the deeper lesson of her life: the patient, disciplined attention needed to see a biological process that the eye can look at for years without learning to read.

## Non-spoken companion: genetics and reception

- **Crossing over:** Creighton and McClintock connected inherited recombination with physical chromosome exchange (1931); Curt Stern independently provided a similar result with *Drosophila*. This is not a claim that McClintock alone discovered all crossing over.
- **Breakage–fusion–bridge:** A chromosome losing a telomeric end can join another broken end, form a dicentric bridge during division, and break again. The particulars vary by cellular context and the interpretation of chromosome structures.
- **Ac and Ds:** Ac can encode the transposase needed for movement of nonautonomous Ds family elements. Insertion, excision and chromosome breakage are experimentally distinct possibilities. The simple color-sector example should not be treated as the explanation of every patterned kernel.
- **Reception:** Nathaniel Comfort's history distinguishes early acceptance of transposition by maize geneticists from widespread skepticism of McClintock's broader theory of controlling elements. The universal-rejection story is not supported by that scholarship.
- **Modern boundaries:** Transposition does not imply purposeful mutation or that every genomic response to challenge is adaptive. Later DNA and molecular evidence supplied mechanisms unavailable to McClintock in 1950.
