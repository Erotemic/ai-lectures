# 057000 — Joseph-Louis Lagrange: From Turin to revolutionary Paris

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Focus:** three political settings, the republic of letters, mathematical mechanics, education and institutional survival. **Background:** historical curiosity; any needed mathematics is introduced in narration.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In the late eighteenth century, a mathematician born in Turin could spend twenty years directing mathematics in Berlin and finish his life teaching in Paris. Joseph-Louis Lagrange followed that route, but the changes were not simply changes of address. He moved among three different political worlds: the kingdom of Sardinia, the Prussian court of Frederick the Great, and a France transformed by revolution and empire. Each offered the possibility of sustained mathematical work; each imposed different expectations on the people who practiced it. His mathematical style, often described as the ambition to turn mechanics into analysis, developed across those changes rather than outside them.

Lagrange was born in Turin in January seventeen thirty-six, into a family connected to the administration of public works and fortifications. Turin was then the capital of the kingdom of Sardinia, not a city in a unified Italy. His baptismal name is recorded in an Italian form, Giuseppe Lodovico Lagrangia, and the family had French ancestry. Neither one national label nor the other captures the cultural setting of his long career. Mathematical correspondence crossed borders, and so did opportunities for employment. His father held a treasury post, but family losses through financial speculation made the prospect of a secure profession particularly important. A legal career was expected for the eldest son.

The young man initially preferred classical studies. His mathematical interests took shape as he read about algebra and optics and encountered the teaching of physics. The turn toward mathematics did not involve joining a renowned university research group. He studied much of the subject independently, tested ideas in letters, and learned how difficult it was to determine whether a calculation was genuinely new. In his first published mathematical work, he noticed a pattern relating the binomial theorem to repeated differentiation of products. Soon afterward he learned that comparable ideas had already appeared in the correspondence of Leibniz and the Bernoullis. The setback was not evidence of dishonesty. It was a lesson about the scattered international literature that an isolated scholar had to find before making a claim to discovery.

At nineteen, in seventeen fifty-five, he became professor of mathematics at the Royal Artillery School in Turin. A military institution could pay for mathematical instruction in a way that few dedicated research chairs could. Fortification, trajectories, surveying, and the preparation of officers gave mathematics an administrative role, even when a teacher's deepest interests lay in questions far removed from the students' immediate needs. Lagrange's published research was already moving toward problems in which the laws of motion and the requirements imposed on a moving system could be expressed through a single analytical method.

A challenge known as the tautochrone problem helped direct his attention. One asks for a curve along which a particle can slide under gravity and arrive at a fixed point in the same time regardless of where it starts. The exact shape is less important for the historical point than the shift in the type of unknown. Instead of solving for an ordinary number, one is searching among possible curves. Change the curve a little and ask how an associated time or other quantity changes. This was the territory of the calculus of variations, a subject being developed through the work of Johann Bernoulli, Euler, and others. Lagrange found a new analytical approach and communicated it to Euler.

Euler's reaction mattered. The celebrated mathematician in Berlin recognized that the younger man's method could make variation problems more systematic. In seventeen fifty-six Lagrange was elected to the Berlin Academy. He did not immediately take a job there. He also helped found a learned society in Turin that would become the Royal Academy of Sciences, and its journal gave his circle a place to publish. A professional community needs more than prodigies. It needs editors, printers, readers able to evaluate an argument, and the social trust required to exchange results before publication.

In Turin he also investigated vibrating strings. Imagine a stretched string held at both ends and set into motion. Earlier mathematicians had written equations for the changing displacement of the string, but disagreements remained over which mathematical forms could describe its motion. Lagrange took a different route: he imagined the string divided into many small masses connected by segments, analyzed the finite system, and then considered what happened as the number of masses increased. This is striking because it begins with a mechanical model simple enough to calculate and moves toward a continuous system through a limiting argument. It did not end debates about functions, convergence, or the interpretation of the string equation. It revealed a fruitful bridge between a discrete collection of moving pieces and a continuous motion.

Paris recognized his achievements through academy prize contests, including work on the Moon and the moons of Jupiter. He became acquainted with d'Alembert, who supported his career and understood his value. Lagrange traveled to Paris during the seventeen sixties and became ill during the journey. This was not the life of a scholar who moved effortlessly between capitals. Professional mobility required patronage, money, travel arrangements, and physical endurance. Meanwhile the Berlin Academy sought a director of mathematics when Euler prepared to leave for Saint Petersburg. Lagrange had earlier declined its invitations, partly because he did not wish to compete with Euler at the same institution. Once Euler's departure became clear, the offer changed.

In seventeen sixty-six he arrived in Berlin and succeeded Euler as director of the mathematical section. The appointment put him among scholars such as Johann Heinrich Lambert and members of the Bernoulli family, within a court whose ruler took an active interest in the academy. It also placed him among older colleagues who had hoped for the position themselves. A court academy could furnish the security needed for deep work and still be troubled by disputes over rank and favor. Lagrange stayed for roughly two decades, publishing on planetary motion, mechanics, algebra, number theory, differential equations, and probability.

He married his cousin Vittoria Conti during the Berlin years. They had no children, and Vittoria's long ill health became part of their shared life. Her death in seventeen eighty-three affected Lagrange deeply. Some biographies emphasize his reserve or periods of discouragement; the dates and letters give firmer ground than an attempt to diagnose a personality across two centuries. Mathematics continued, but his circumstances changed. Frederick died in seventeen eighty-six, and the conditions that had made Berlin attractive were no longer the same. The court and the scholar were both entering different phases.

One of his Berlin results, the four-square theorem, is easy to state and difficult to prove. Every nonnegative whole number can be written as the sum of four whole-number squares. For example, seven is four plus one plus one plus one: the squares of two, one, one, and one. Fifteen is nine plus four plus one plus one. The theorem is not a claim that a mathematician could instantly guess a four-square representation for every huge integer. Its achievement is a general proof that no whole number is excluded. Lagrange's result connected the world of integer arithmetic with a style of reasoning already being honed by Euler and other eighteenth-century mathematicians.

He also examined why polynomial equations of degrees two, three, and four could be solved by formulas involving radicals, and why a general method for higher degrees remained elusive. His approach focused on arrangements of roots rather than merely manipulating coefficients. Permuting several roots can leave some combinations unchanged while changing others. That observation later became important to the development of group theory, through the work of Ruffini, Abel, Galois, and others. Lagrange was not presenting the modern theory of groups or solving all of the later problems himself. His questions created material that later generations transformed into a different kind of algebra.

The work most closely associated with his style was a treatise on mechanics. Written substantially during the Berlin years and published after he moved to Paris in seventeen eighty-eight, the Mécanique analytique treated the behavior of mechanical systems through general mathematical principles. In its preface Lagrange emphasized that his methods needed no geometrical figures. That statement should not be misunderstood to mean the physical systems disappeared. Rather, he wanted to represent their possible motions, constraints, and forces by general equations, so that the same analytical procedure could be applied to many arrangements of bodies.

Think of a pendulum. A weight attached to a rigid rod moves along a circular arc. In a direct force diagram one would draw the weight, gravity, and the pull of the rod. In an analytical treatment one can instead describe the system by a single angle, because the rod's length constrains the weight to move on a circle. The relevant energies can be expressed in terms of that angle and its rate of change. A modern Lagrangian is the kinetic energy minus the potential energy. Taking the variation of the associated action gives the equation of motion. The elegant formula now taught as the Euler–Lagrange equation emerged through a longer historical development; it is not a transcription of one page in Lagrange's book. But it conveys what his analytical program made possible: learn the mechanics by choosing variables suited to the constraints, rather than constructing a separate geometrical argument for every machine.

When Lagrange moved to Paris in seventeen eighty-seven, the old monarchy still governed France. His appointment to the Academy promised support and initially relieved him of an obligation to teach. Less than two years later the Revolution began. To be a scientist in revolutionary Paris was to watch the purposes and authority of public institutions change rapidly. The academy that had sponsored prizes and publications under the monarchy was abolished in seventeen ninety-three. Scientists were expected to contribute to reforms in weights and measures, military engineering, public administration, and education, yet their positions could become politically precarious overnight.

Lagrange served in the commission working toward a decimal metric system. Common measures seem like neutral tools, but weights and measures were also rules for commerce, taxation, trade, and state authority. Standardizing them meant choosing procedures that could be explained, reproduced, and imposed across regions where old local measures varied. Mathematical judgment was necessary, but it did not resolve the political question of who had the power to make a standard binding. Lagrange belonged to this practical history as much as to the history of differential equations.

The dangers of the Revolution came close to him. In seventeen ninety-three a decree against foreigners born in hostile countries threatened his position. The chemist Antoine Lavoisier helped secure an exception. Lavoisier himself was later tried by a revolutionary tribunal and executed in May seventeen ninety-four. Lagrange's survival and Lavoisier's death cannot be reduced to a difference in genius or worth. They expose the unpredictable force of political classification in a period when the state could suppress institutions and destroy individual lives. The remembered lament Lagrange made after Lavoisier's execution is powerful, but its wording comes through historical testimony; it is not a license to invent an extended scene between the men.

The Revolution also created new educational institutions. Lagrange taught analysis at the École Polytechnique and elementary mathematics at the École Normale. The students were not merely apprentices to an individual mathematician; they were to become engineers, teachers, and public servants of a new political order. Fourier, who attended his lectures, remembered a quiet speaking style and an Italian accent. His testimony is a reminder that a major researcher is not automatically an effortless classroom performer. The mathematical textbooks Lagrange wrote from his teaching belong to this period. His attempt to base calculus on algebraic expansions rather than an informal appeal to infinitesimals influenced later discussions, although its treatment of convergence did not resolve every difficulty.

In Paris he married again, to Renée-Françoise-Adélaide Le Monnier, the daughter of an astronomer. He remained an influential mathematician through the Consulate and Empire, held positions in national institutions, and received honors under Napoleon. A biography focused only on his survival might picture an apolitical mind; a biography focused only on court honors might picture a calculating politician. The documentary record gives a more complicated life: a scholar whose vocation required institutions and who continued his work while institutions changed around him.

The significance of Lagrange is therefore neither that he eliminated every physical picture from mechanics nor that he invented all the mathematics bearing his name. He helped make it possible to investigate mechanical systems through general analytical relations. He gave important theorems in number theory and asked powerful questions about algebraic equations. He contributed to education and standard measurements in a revolutionary state. Euler recognized his work when he was young; Parisian institutions carried it into another century. When Lagrange died in April eighteen thirteen, he had witnessed the passage from eighteenth-century court academies to the engineering schools and scientific institutions of modern France. The mathematics was not detached from that passage. It was one of the things those institutions enabled him to create and teach.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and context
- **1736:** Born in Turin in the kingdom of Sardinia; family connected to public administration; early legal studies give way to mathematics.
- **1755–1757:** Teaches at the Royal Artillery School; corresponds with Euler on variations; Berlin Academy election; helps found Turin learned society.
- **1764–1766:** Parisian prizes and journeys; replaces Euler at the Berlin Academy in 1766.
- **1767–1783:** Marriage to Vittoria Conti, then her death; Berlin research includes vibrating strings, number theory, algebra, celestial mechanics and foundations of variations.
- **1787–1788:** Move to Paris; *Mécanique analytique* published.
- **1790–1795:** Metric commission, suppression of the Academy, political threats to foreign-born scientists, Lavoisier's execution; teaching in revolutionary schools.
- **1792 and 1813:** Second marriage to Renée-Françoise-Adélaide Le Monnier; death in Paris.

National classifications differ by period and source; Turin was part of the kingdom of Sardinia, and Lagrange spent much of his scientific life in Prussia and France. Fourier's assessment of his lecturing is a witness's view rather than a universal verdict. Lavoisier helped defend Lagrange in 1793 but was himself executed in 1794; avoid implying the same proceedings.

### Mathematics for display
- Lagrange's four-square theorem: every nonnegative integer \(n\) admits \(n=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2\) with integers \(a,b,c,d\).
- In modern notation the Lagrangian for a simple pendulum of mass \(m\), length \(\ell\), angle \(\theta\), and gravitational acceleration \(g\) can be written \(L=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2-mg\ell(1-\cos\theta)\). Stationary-action reasoning gives \(\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\sin\theta=0\). This is a pedagogical modern formulation, not Lagrange's wording.
- The Euler–Lagrange equation is shared historical development, not a sole-inventor attribution. Likewise, roots and permutations in Lagrange's 1770 paper anticipate, but are not identical with, modern group theory.
