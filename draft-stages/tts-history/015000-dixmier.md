# Jacques Dixmier: What can two infinite-dimensional closed subspaces share?

Take two subspaces of three-dimensional space. Their intersection, the directions orthogonal to each other, and the remaining angle geometry can all be described with a few vectors. Infinite-dimensional closed subspaces are less cooperative. Their relative position may involve a continuous range of angles rather than a finite list.

Jacques Dixmier was a French mathematician whose work included operator algebras and the geometry of Hilbert spaces. His studies of subspaces and operator structures belong to the historical background of the two-projection theory. The recurring issue is how to describe relative position without choosing an unstable list of basis vectors.

Consider two closed subspaces K and L. The intersection K with L contains directions they share exactly. The intersection of K with the orthogonal complement of L contains directions that are entirely missed by L. The corresponding reverse intersection can have different dimension. Those pieces are intrinsic, independent of which bases we use.

Remove them conceptually, and a generic angular relationship remains. In finite dimensions, its principal angles can be collected into a list. In Hilbert space, the angle information is better encoded by operators formed from the projections. The product of a projection onto K with a projection onto L measures how much a K-vector survives when projected into L.

An important distinction appears here. The smallest angle between two closed subspaces may be zero even if their intersection is only the zero vector. There can be unit vectors in the first subspace whose distance to the second tends to zero, without any one nonzero vector belonging to both. Infinite-dimensional geometry permits this limiting behavior.

That observation changes how perturbation results are stated. Subspaces can be closed and still have delicate relative position, so a proof may require spectral separation or a uniform projection estimate rather than relying on finite-dimensional dimension counting.

Dixmier's contribution is part of the transition from a geometric picture of finitely many planes to operator descriptions of arbitrary closed subspaces. The aim is not to abandon angles. It is to encode their content in a language that survives when there are infinitely many of them.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For closed subspaces \(K,L\), the directed sine quantity is
\(\|(I-P_L)P_K\|\). It can equal \(1\) even when the largest
principal angle of a finite-dimensional intuition is not represented by a
single vector. In infinite dimensions \(\inf\{\angle(x,L):x\in K,\|x\|=1\}=0\)
need not imply \(K\cap L\ne\{0\}\).
