# 019000 — William Kahan: What makes a numerical eigenspace trustworthy?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** Davis–Kahan and spectral rotation. **Background:** introductory linear algebra; the lecture defines its local objects.

William Kahan's career brings two kinds of error into the same conversation. One is the mathematical difference between the answer to an exact eigenvalue problem and the answer produced by a trial subspace. The other is the numerical difference between the calculation we intended and the calculation a finite-precision machine actually performed. The two errors can interact, but they are not identical. Kahan contributed to the theory needed to distinguish them. His 1970 paper with Chandler Davis examines how accurately a computed eigenspace follows a separated spectral subspace. His wider career in numerical analysis asks a recurring question: how can a computation provide evidence of its own reliability?

Kahan was born in Toronto in nineteen thirty-three, the son of immigrants whose family histories were marked by antisemitism in Europe. In an extensive oral history recorded in two thousand five, he spoke about relatives who had survived the Holocaust and about how his parents chose English-sounding names for their sons. At home, however, they called him Velvel, the Yiddish diminutive of the name Wolf. He kept the name. To him, someone calling 'Velvel' was likely to know him well; someone calling 'William' was probably farther away from his everyday life. The detail brings us close to the person who would later argue so fiercely about the behavior of arithmetic inside computers.

As a child Kahan liked making things. His parents gave him a Meccano construction set, and he learned to handle mechanical parts and electrical devices. He also loved science and mathematics. He recalled sometimes spending language lessons reading books from shelves at the back of the classroom, including adapted French literature. He had an appetite for learning that did not always follow the assigned lesson. At the University of Toronto, where he studied mathematics, that appetite led him from proofs and numerical methods toward a physical machine.

In the spring of nineteen fifty-three he first learned that the university had a computer. At first the idea of storing instructions and data in the same machine intrigued him so much that he sketched his own design, using an analogy with railway tracks and switching yards. That summer he joined the programming program for FERUT, the University of Toronto's Ferranti computer. The beginners were supposed to spend much of their time on routine jobs such as punching paper tape and sweeping floors. Kahan recalled that good programmers were allowed more programming time. Before that balance shifted for him, he built tape winders from Meccano parts salvaged from a disused differential analyzer. The boy who had made mechanisms at home was now learning where a physical computer could lose, store, or alter a numerical quantity.

One early computation helped engineers design electric transformers. The program combined formulas for magnetic cores, copper windings, energy losses, and heat dissipation. Its floating-point operations were carried out through an interpreter on a machine whose runs were brief and expensive. A small mistake in numerical representation could affect a real design choice. Kahan went on to a doctorate at Toronto under Byron Griffith, work at Cambridge with numerical analysts including Jim Wilkinson, and research at Toronto before moving to Berkeley in nineteen sixty-eight. His later role in the IEEE floating-point standard grew from years of encountering the mismatch between mathematical intention and machine behavior. The Davis–Kahan work on subspaces belongs to this same world of practical mathematical accountability.

Imagine a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues describe vibration frequencies. A computer supplies a unit vector as an approximation to one of its normal modes. Looking at the printed coordinates tells us little. Even a highly precise decimal display can describe the wrong direction. A better first question is whether applying the original matrix to that vector gives something close to a scalar multiple of the same vector. If it does, the vector nearly satisfies an eigenvalue equation. The difference between those two results is called the residual. We can compute it from the proposed vector, the proposed eigenvalue, and the original matrix. It is a measure of how well an equation is satisfied, independent of how many digits were printed.

Take a diagonal two-by-two matrix with entries zero and four. Suppose the trial vector points along a direction that makes an angle of ten degrees with the first coordinate axis. Its coordinates are cosine ten degrees and sine ten degrees. Apply the matrix: the first component becomes zero, and the second becomes four times sine ten degrees. The Rayleigh quotient, which is the scalar that best fits the image of a unit trial vector in the least-squares sense, equals four times sine squared ten degrees. Subtracting that scalar multiple of the trial vector leaves the residual. Because the Rayleigh quotient was chosen from the same trial vector, the residual is perpendicular to the trial vector. Its norm in this example is four times sine ten degrees times cosine ten degrees.

The numerical value of that residual is about zero point six eight four. That number has units of the matrix, not of an angle. It does not immediately tell us that the trial vector is ten degrees from the correct eigenvector. To obtain an angular statement, we need spectral information. In this example the unwanted eigenvalue is four, and the Rayleigh quotient is about zero point one two one. The separation from the Rayleigh quotient to the unwanted spectral point is therefore about three point eight seven nine. Project the residual onto the unwanted eigendirection. Its component has magnitude four times sine ten degrees times cosine squared ten degrees, because projection retains only the unwanted coordinate. The part of the trial vector lying in the unwanted direction has magnitude sine ten degrees. Dividing the unwanted residual component by the spectral separation gives exactly that sine. The gap performs the conversion from equation error to geometric error.

There is a reason to calculate with the projected residual rather than just its full norm. The identity on the unwanted eigenspace is exact: the projected residual equals the unwanted operator minus the trial Rayleigh quotient, acting on the unwanted component of the trial vector. Only then can we invert that factor. The full residual includes additional information that is not itself the unknown leakage. For a single isolated eigenvalue and a clearly identified target, a familiar norm estimate can follow. For a cluster of eigenvalues, however, there may be no distinguished vector to target. The right object is the whole invariant subspace, and the residual becomes a matrix whose columns describe failure of invariance for an orthonormal trial frame.

Consider a three-by-three diagonal matrix with eigenvalues zero, one thousandth, and ten. A computer could rotate its first two eigenvectors through almost any angle while keeping the associated two-dimensional span fixed. If the first two eigenvalues are too close to distinguish reliably, asking that each computed vector match a particular exact vector is a poor test. But the plane containing the first two coordinate axes is sharply separated from the third axis, whose eigenvalue is ten. Any approximate two-dimensional invariant subspace can be assessed by measuring leakage into that third direction. This is why the smallest separation inside a cluster is not always the relevant gap. The desired subspace is stable because the cluster as a whole is isolated from the rest of the spectrum.

Let E be an orthonormal frame for such a trial subspace. The smaller matrix formed by restricting the large operator's quadratic form to E is the Rayleigh–Ritz compression. It is obtained by applying the operator to the frame and taking inner products back with the frame. Call that smaller matrix H. The residual is the large operator applied to E, minus E multiplied by H. By construction, E transposed times the residual is zero in the real case. This is Galerkin orthogonality: the residual is perpendicular to every column of the trial frame. It matters because it tells us which part of the error has already been optimized inside the trial space and which part remains to be controlled outside.

Now introduce the exact spectral subspace that the trial space is meant to approximate. Let Q be the orthogonal projection onto it. Project the frame E onto the orthogonal complement of Q. That projected frame records the unwanted component of every trial vector. Apply the residual relation and use the invariance of the exact spectral complement under the self-adjoint operator. We obtain a Sylvester equation for the projected frame. The operator on the unwanted spectral subspace acts on one side; the small Ritz matrix H acts on the other. Every scalar version of this equation says that an unknown leakage coefficient is a residual coefficient divided by an eigenvalue difference. If the spectra are separated in an appropriate way, these denominators are controlled and the leakage can be bounded.

The words 'appropriate way' carry real mathematical content. Suppose the unwanted eigenvalues lie strictly above all Ritz values. Then every difference has the same sign and is bounded below by a common positive gap. This ordered separation supports one form of norm estimate. If unwanted spectral values lie on both sides of the Ritz spectrum, every absolute difference might still be large, but the operator-norm behavior of the inverse Sylvester map can require a different constant. It is invalid to look at the individual matrix entries, divide by numbers of size at least delta, and infer that every unitarily invariant norm shrinks by exactly the same factor. The Hilbert–Schmidt norm has a special squared-entry structure that makes such division straightforward in spectral coordinates. Other norms need their own argument.

This explains why papers about subspace perturbation can contain several sine and tangent bounds that look almost identical. A sine of a principal angle measures projection leakage. A tangent measures leakage relative to the component retained in the target subspace. The tangent can be much larger when the retained component is small. A bound in operator norm controls the largest principal angle, whereas a Frobenius or Hilbert–Schmidt bound accumulates the squared errors in all principal directions. A theorem about a trial eigenspace may invoke a residual measured against a Ritz matrix, whereas a theorem about two exact spectral subspaces uses a perturbation of the full operator. The differences are substantive, not interchangeable notational choices.

In the two-by-two example, the difference between sine and tangent is easy to hear. At ten degrees, the sine is about zero point one seven four and the tangent is about zero point one seven six. They are close. At sixty degrees, the sine is about zero point eight six six but the tangent is about one point seven three two. Both describe the same angle, yet they answer different questions. The sine is the amount that a unit source direction leaks out of its target. The tangent compares that leaked amount with the part that survives inside the target. This second ratio becomes unstable as the source approaches perpendicularity. It therefore requires a more careful geometric condition before treating the target graph coordinate as bounded.

At Toronto and later Berkeley, Kahan worked among people who made that last calculation an engineering problem as well as a mathematical one. He and colleagues tested machine libraries, questioned manufacturers' claims, and investigated how different architectures rounded results. The concern was personal in the sense that he had repeatedly watched real computations behave in ways programmers did not expect. A machine does not necessarily form the exact residual corresponding to the exact matrix and vector written in the mathematical model. Each matrix-vector product and subtraction incurs rounding error. If a computed residual is small, we must ask whether it is small relative to the operator, the scale of the data, and the rounding uncertainty. There are two possible routes to confidence. One is a forward error estimate: bound the difference between the computed and exact desired results. The other is a backward error statement: show that the computed result solves a nearby problem exactly. A small residual often supports the second statement directly. A spectral gap is one of the ingredients needed to turn an appropriate backward error into an angular forward error.

The distinction becomes tangible with a nearly multiple eigenvalue. Suppose a matrix has eigenvalues one and one plus one billionth. A tiny perturbation comparable to that separation can rotate its eigenvectors considerably. A numerical algorithm might solve a nearby matrix perfectly and still return directions far from the eigenvectors of the original matrix. There is no contradiction. It is the eigenvectors that are ill-conditioned as individual outputs. If the two eigenvalues form an isolated cluster, the plane spanning both directions may be much better conditioned. The correct response is not to demand more digits from an ill-conditioned individual eigenvector. It is to identify the stable subspace, measure its residual, and use the gap to the rest of the spectrum.

Kahan's broader work on floating point supplies another concrete caution. Adding a large number and a tiny number in finite precision can discard the tiny contribution. Repeating that operation many times can accumulate a noticeable discrepancy. A compensated summation algorithm keeps an extra correction so that low-order information lost in one addition can influence later ones. It does not make floating-point arithmetic exact, and its numerical behavior depends on assumptions about rounding and data. Its relevance here is methodological: the computational operation itself must be analyzed. A theorem about exact real matrices is not an automatic guarantee for a program whose inputs and arithmetic obey a different error model.

The IEEE 754 effort turned that experience into a prolonged public negotiation. A numerical standard specifies much of the behavior of basic floating-point operations, including formats and rounding. It makes numerical reasoning more portable across machines, although programs still have to be written and evaluated carefully. Kahan worked with hardware designers, mathematical software specialists, and standards committees that did not always agree about how arithmetic should behave. In his oral history he remembered the meetings as lively enough that other numerical analysts treated them as a spectacle worth visiting. He could be combative in technical argument, particularly when a proposed shortcut threatened the assumptions on which programs relied. His descriptions of those disputes convey a researcher willing to spend years on details many other scientists would have preferred to regard as settled engineering matters.

The 1970 Davis–Kahan paper appeared in the SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis and treated two related questions. How much can an invariant subspace change under a perturbation of a Hermitian operator? And if an algorithm supplies approximate eigenvectors for a cluster, how far might the resulting span lie from the true spectral subspace? The paper characterizes subspace discrepancy using angles and proves bounds involving trigonometric functions of those angles, spectral separation, perturbation, and residual. Its title invokes the rotation of eigenvectors, but its most durable language concerns the geometry of whole subspaces. That is precisely the language a numerical analyst needs when an individual eigenvector is not a stable numerical object.

A single recipe can now summarize how to use the theory in an actual problem, provided we do not skip its hypotheses. First identify the wanted spectral cluster, rather than assuming every computed vector is separately meaningful. Second form an orthonormal trial frame and its Ritz compression. Third calculate or bound the residual using an error model appropriate to the arithmetic. Fourth establish a separation between the Ritz data and the unwanted part of the true spectrum, with the correct ordered or distance-only hypothesis for the theorem chosen. Fifth apply the corresponding Sylvester estimate to obtain the directed sine or tangent bound. Finally interpret that bound in the geometry and norm that the application uses. Every step supplies information needed by the next.

There are limits to what this chain can establish. The residual and gap do not automatically locate the correct cluster if several spectral regions could fit the data. Directed leakage does not by itself certify equality of dimensions unless dimensional information is available. An operator-norm sine bound controls the largest principal angle but does not determine the full distribution of angles. A proof for bounded finite matrices does not immediately cover differential operators on moving domains. A computation with standard floating-point arithmetic does not gain a rigorous forward error bound merely because the exact theorem has a small constant. Mathematical reliability depends on keeping these boundaries visible.

Consider how a numerical residual can be small for the wrong reason. Let A be a diagonal matrix with eigenvalues zero and one millionth. Choose a unit vector halfway between the two eigenvectors. Its Rayleigh quotient is half a millionth. Applying A and subtracting that multiple produces a residual with norm half a millionth. A naive rule saying 'small residual means correct eigenvector' would call this a spectacular approximation. But the trial vector is forty-five degrees from either exact eigendirection. The gap is also only one millionth, and the ratio of residual to separation is not small. If the wanted object is the full two-dimensional invariant subspace, the trial line is too small to represent it. This example exposes two conditions missing from the naive inference: the spectral separation must be appropriate, and the dimension of the target must match the object we are certifying.

There is also an instructive way to derive a residual without computing the eigenvalues at all. Suppose we have an orthonormal matrix of trial vectors E and calculate H from the inner products of E with A applied to E. By construction H is symmetric when A is symmetric. Form the residual R as A applied to E minus E times H. Multiplying R on the left by E transposed yields the small matrix E transposed times A times E minus H, which is zero by the definition of H. The residual is orthogonal to the trial subspace, irrespective of how accurate the trial space happens to be. This identity is sometimes mistaken for proof that the residual must be small. It is not. Orthogonality describes the direction of the error; only the actual residual norm tells us its size.

A simple rank-two trial space makes the distinction concrete. Let A be diagonal with entries zero, one, and ten. Choose E to span the first coordinate vector and a mixture of the second and third coordinate vectors. The first trial direction is an exact eigenvector and contributes zero residual. The mixed direction has a Ritz value between one and ten and a residual perpendicular to the span of both trial directions. The entire residual matrix therefore has one zero column and one potentially large column. Its operator norm measures the worst trial direction; its Hilbert–Schmidt norm measures the combined squared error across columns. If we rotated the trial frame inside its own span, the residual columns would change, but the singular values of the residual operator would not. That is why a subspace theorem should normally be phrased in basis-independent terms.

Now suppose a computer returns approximations to the eigenvalues but has no guaranteed enclosure for the unwanted spectrum. Can we still use a gap theorem? We might estimate a gap from computed eigenvalues, but then those estimates themselves require error control. A circular argument would assume the computed eigenvalues are sufficiently accurate in order to prove that the computed eigenvectors are accurate, while relying on the eigenvectors to justify those eigenvalues. A sound analysis can use interval enclosures, perturbation inequalities, independent spectral information, or a verified backward-error model. The precise technique depends on the problem. The important point is that a gap used in a proof must be established about the relevant true spectral sets, or about certified enclosures that guarantee their separation.

The different faces of the Davis–Kahan theorem are worth separating by an audible example. Suppose a two-dimensional target plane lies almost in a three-dimensional coordinate plane. We can describe the source plane using two orthonormal vectors. Project them into the target complement. The largest singular value of that projected frame controls the worst leakage across all unit combinations of the source vectors. The sum of the two squared singular values measures cumulative leakage. Even if one principal angle is near zero, the other could dominate the operator norm. Conversely, many moderately small angles can produce a sizeable Hilbert–Schmidt norm even when no single angle is particularly large. Choosing one output norm over another expresses what failure the application cares about.

One particularly revealing bridge between the sine and tangent formulations uses graph coordinates. Imagine a trial line that meets the exact target line at a small angle. Its vector can be split into one component along the target and one perpendicular component. Divide the perpendicular amount by the retained amount. This ratio is the tangent of the angle. It is the slope of the trial line when the target line is used as the horizontal axis. The same idea applies to higher-dimensional subspaces as a graph operator, provided the projection from the trial subspace to the target is invertible. If a principal angle reaches a right angle, that projection loses invertibility and the graph description breaks. The sine leakage remains defined; the tangent representation no longer does. That is why tangent theorems must protect the denominator geometrically.

Numerical algorithms add another source of choices. A dense symmetric eigenvalue solver may reduce a matrix to tridiagonal form and then compute eigenpairs; a Krylov method may approximate an invariant subspace using repeated matrix-vector products. Both can return trial bases that are only approximately orthogonal in floating point. If the matrix E is not genuinely orthonormal, the simple Ritz and residual formulas need adjustment, often through a Gram matrix or an explicit reorthogonalization. Otherwise an analysis can accidentally combine exact identities that assume orthonormality with measured quantities produced by nonorthogonal columns. Kahan's broader numerical perspective encourages us to ask what the program actually maintained, rather than infer exact properties from the names of its arrays.

There is a famous simple test for floating-point intuition: compute one plus a small number, then subtract one. In exact arithmetic the answer is the small number. In finite precision it can be zero if the intermediate addition rounds to one. This does not make the machine defective; it reflects the finite set of representable numbers and a specified rounding rule. It also tells us why cancellation deserves attention in residual computation. If A times x and lambda times x are large and almost equal, their subtraction may have substantial relative error, even if the final residual is small. Error analysis must use absolute and relative scales carefully. Sometimes higher precision or a more stable residual evaluation is justified before applying a sharp angular bound.

In compensated summation, the correction variable can be understood as carrying forward information that rounding would otherwise discard. Suppose a sum repeatedly adds a very small increment to a large running total. Standard summation may lose some increments. A compensated method estimates the low-order discrepancy from each addition and includes it in later steps. It reduces a common mechanism of numerical error, although it does not eliminate every problem, such as overflow, underflow, or adversarial cancellation. This is not a direct proof of the Davis–Kahan theorem. It is another worked instance of Kahan's practice: identify the operation where information is lost, build a mathematical model of that loss, and modify the method so that a defensible accuracy statement becomes possible.

Kahan's contribution to floating-point standards is similarly specific. The standard does not make all calculations reliable by decree. It makes the behavior of elementary operations sufficiently systematic that algorithms can be analyzed consistently. Rounding modes, infinities, NaNs, signed zeros, and other details are not merely software trivia when one is proving error bounds. The result is an interface between mathematical idealization and actual computation. In the eigenvector setting, the interface is a residual and a gap. In arithmetic, the interface is a rounding model and a bound on accumulated error. A reliable numerical argument has to cross both interfaces with its assumptions visible.

The historical collaboration also belongs in a larger methodological landscape. Perturbation estimates had antecedents in classical eigenvalue inequalities, variational principles, and operator theory. Davis brought a sustained geometric investigation of subspaces. Kahan brought deep numerical-analysis concerns and a quantitative interest in error bounds. Their joint paper did not invent the idea that eigenvalues change under perturbation, nor did it make individual eigenvectors universally stable. Its lasting value is the sharp treatment of angles and residuals for separated spectral subspaces, in forms that numerical analysts can use. The paper's exact claims must be read with their hypotheses rather than applied as a generic certificate for every eigenproblem.

An especially useful final thought experiment is to change the observable while holding the calculation fixed. Suppose we estimated a two-dimensional eigenspace accurately and then use it to project noisy data. The worst-case projection error may be governed by the largest sine of a principal angle. If instead we care about mean squared loss across an orthonormal family of directions, the Frobenius measure may be more appropriate. If downstream computations use a particular basis inside the subspace, further choices and conditioning enter. None of these quantities is 'the numerical error' in isolation. The application selects the geometric object and the norm, and the theorem then tells us what the residual can certify under the stated gap. This is how one turns a good-looking numerical answer into a meaningful quantitative guarantee.
In later years Kahan taught students who became influential numerical analysts in their own right, including James Demmel and Peter Tang. With students and collaborators he worked on elementary-function libraries and tools for testing floating-point behavior. He also described frustrations with compiler designers: even after an arithmetic standard had been accepted, a compiler could reorganize a computation in ways that undermined expectations about rounding or intermediate precision. The intellectual life he described was collaborative and argumentative, with the classroom, the computer laboratory, and the standards meeting all part of the same profession.

A useful practical refinement is to distinguish exact arithmetic residuals from certified residual bounds. If the computed quantities are stored in floating point, one can sometimes evaluate the residual in higher precision, or bound the discrepancy between its floating-point estimate and its exact value using known rounding-error estimates. The result is an interval or upper bound on the true residual, which can then enter a perturbation theorem. A gap bound established by a separate argument completes the certificate. This route may be more expensive than simply calling an eigenvalue routine, but it answers a different question: not merely what the algorithm returned, but what can be proved about the answer. The same structure appears in verified numerical computing more broadly.

Return to the trial vector in the diagonal matrix with entries zero and four. Its residual was a computable failure of an eigenvalue equation. The spectral separation let us turn the unwanted part of that failure into an angular statement. Scaling this argument to a whole subspace gave the Sylvester equation and the principal-angle language. The numerical model reminded us that computing the residual also involves arithmetic assumptions. William Kahan's contribution to this story is the combination of quantitative subspace perturbation with a wider insistence on analyzing computation. The outcome of a numerical calculation becomes trustworthy when we can state what was approximated, how error was measured, and which mathematical conditions make the resulting error bound valid.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Residual-to-angle mechanism

For an isometric trial frame `E`, a self-adjoint matrix `A`, the Ritz
compression `H=E^*AE`, and residual `R=AE-EH`, Galerkin orthogonality gives

\[
E^*R=E^*AE-H=0.
\]

If `Q` projects onto the exact target invariant subspace and
`X=(I-Q)E`, then the restricted unwanted operator `A_\perp` satisfies

\[
A_\perp X-XH=(I-Q)R.
\]

A suitable **specified** separation hypothesis bounds the inverse
Sylvester map and hence the principal-angle sines associated with `X`.

For `A=diag(0,4)` and `v=(\cos t,\sin t)`,

\[
\lambda=v^*Av=4\sin^2 t,
\quad r=Av-\lambda v
=4\sin t\cos t\,(-\sin t,\cos t),
\quad \|r\|=4|\sin t\cos t|.
\]

The unwanted coordinate residual is `4\sin t\cos^2 t`, and the spectral
denominator is `4-\lambda=4\cos^2 t`; their ratio is `\sin t`.
This is exact when `\cos t\ne0` and the unwanted spectral set is correctly
identified. The small-residual example for a matrix with eigenvalues
`0,10^{-6}` demonstrates the need for a gap.

### Numerical context

A computed residual has its own floating-point error; using it in a sharp
subspace theorem may require independent validation of both the residual
and the gap. Kahan's IEEE 754 work and compensated summation are related
through their emphasis on analyzable numerical behavior, not because they
are consequences of the Davis–Kahan theorem.
