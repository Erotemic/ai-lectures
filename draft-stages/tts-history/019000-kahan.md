# William Kahan: What makes a numerical eigenspace trustworthy?

A numerical algorithm produces an approximate eigenvector and an approximate eigenvalue. The numbers may look plausible. But what evidence shows that the direction it found is close to an exact spectral subspace?

William Kahan is widely known for his work on numerical computation, including floating-point arithmetic and its reliability. His joint 1970 paper with Chandler Davis, The Rotation of Eigenvectors by a Perturbation, part three, places that concern in the language of self-adjoint operators, spectral separation, and angular error.

Begin with a trial unit vector. Apply the true matrix to it, subtract the trial vector multiplied by its estimated eigenvalue, and measure what remains. This is the residual. If the residual is zero, the vector satisfies the eigenvalue equation exactly. If it is small, the equation is nearly satisfied, but that alone does not identify which exact eigendirection the trial vector belongs near.

Imagine a symmetric matrix whose two eigenvalues are almost equal. A trial direction can have a tiny residual even though it is a poor approximation to the particular eigenvector you had in mind. Spectral separation supplies the missing condition. The residual measures failure of invariance; the gap determines how much angular movement that failure can produce.

The Davis–Kahan sine theorem formalizes this relationship. Project the residual into the unwanted spectral subspace. The unknown component of the trial vector in that region satisfies a Sylvester equation. Dividing by the spectral separation, in the appropriate operator or ideal norm, yields an angular bound.

Different theorem versions have different gap and norm hypotheses. An ordered separation supports estimates that need not hold with the same constant for interlaced spectra. A bound for the largest principal angle is different from a bound for the whole singular-value list. Numerical reliability depends on recognizing the exact hypothesis one has actually established.

Kahan's role in this story is the demand that computational answers come with quantitative control. The 1970 collaboration supplies a family of methods for connecting an observed residual and spectral information to a verifiable statement about subspace geometry. The result is a way to judge a numerical approximation instead of simply trusting its displayed digits.
