# 071000 - Adrien-Marie Legendre: A mathematical life through revolution

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Enlightenment Paris, military mathematics and geodesy, the French Revolution, the metric system, textbooks, disputed credit for least squares, relations with Gauss, Abel and Jacobi, and late financial insecurity. **Background:** ordinary algebra, geometry, and historical curiosity.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

For many years books about the history of mathematics showed readers a portrait of Adrien-Marie Legendre. A man in late eighteenth-century dress looked out from the page, apparently offering a face for the name attached to least squares, special functions and number theory. Then historians established that the familiar image was not the mathematician at all. It was a portrait of Louis Legendre, a politician of the French Revolution. The two men shared a surname and lived in the same broad historical era, but they were different people. A caricature by the artist Boilly is the only known surviving likeness of the mathematician. This is a good way into Adrien-Marie Legendre's life. His works are everywhere in mathematics, but his private person can be difficult to see. Even his face was supplied incorrectly by later generations.

Legendre was born in September seventeen fifty-two. Many biographical accounts place his birth in Paris; some historical material points instead toward Toulouse, followed by the family's move to Paris. It is a small uncertainty worth keeping rather than inventing a perfectly settled childhood. He grew up with access to a strong education and studied at the Collège Mazarin, where mathematics and physics could be pursued seriously. His family had resources that allowed him to concentrate on scholarship for years without immediately needing an income from a profession. Compared with a later figure like Abel, working on problems while worrying how to pay for meals, Legendre began with a different kind of freedom.

As a young man he studied during the last decades of the ancien régime, the political order of royal government and inherited privileges that preceded the Revolution. Science in France could be supported through academies, military schools, royal projects and wealthy private networks. There were not yet the modern departments and grant agencies that a university mathematician might expect. To understand Legendre's career, one must follow how he moved among institutions as those institutions were repeatedly remade by revolution, empire and restoration.

He taught at the École Militaire in Paris in the seventeen seventies, a school where questions about motion, artillery and geometry were not merely theoretical. His appointment was encouraged by d'Alembert, a prominent mathematical physicist and Encyclopédie editor, and for part of this period he worked in the same institutional world as Laplace. In seventeen eighty-two he entered a Berlin Academy prize competition concerning the motion of projectiles through resisting air. A cannonball is a useful mathematical problem because gravity pulls it downward while resistance makes its path depart from the clean parabola taught in an elementary classroom. Legendre won the prize. It gave his research an audience outside France and helped establish his standing with influential mathematicians including Lagrange.

The Academy of Sciences in Paris elected him to a position in seventeen eighty-three. He studied the attraction of ellipsoids, shapes resembling stretched or flattened spheres, and the gravitational problems associated with the figure of the Earth and planets. This work contributed to the development of what are now called Legendre polynomials. The name can sound like a specialized exercise from an advanced course. In fact the functions arose from efforts to represent how an object that is not perfectly spherical creates a gravitational field. Mathematics was being asked to describe an observed world full of irregularities, and the calculations required new families of functions.

In seventeen eighty-seven Legendre joined geodetic work linking measurements around the observatories in Paris and Greenwich. Surveyors could measure angles between distant stations, but they also had to reconcile those measurements with the curved surface of the Earth. Small corrections mattered when a chain of triangles crossed large distances. Legendre was elected to the Royal Society of London and published results about spherical triangles. Such surveys joined countries through scientific measurement even while governments competed politically. They also connected high theory with instruments, weather, labor and error in observations.

Then France entered a revolutionary decade. In seventeen ninety-one Legendre served on a committee associated with the creation of standardized weights and measures. Defining the meter was not merely agreeing on a convenient length for merchants. It required choosing a standard with a claim to scientific universality, measuring parts of the Earth and confronting the errors and costs of large surveys. Astronomers, geodesists, mathematicians and the state all had roles. Legendre contributed to the mathematical organization of this work. He was also involved with Gaspard de Prony and others in an enormous project to create new tables of logarithms and trigonometric functions in the decimal system. The undertaking employed many human calculators. Long before electronic computation, mathematical infrastructure depended on organized teams and careful arithmetic.

The Revolution also destroyed the assumptions on which his earlier life had rested. The old Academy was suppressed in seventeen ninety-three, and Legendre suffered financial losses as the political and economic order changed. The stable private resources that had let him devote himself to research disappeared. In later correspondence with Jacobi he recalled marrying after the revolution had damaged his small fortune and described his wife's help in rebuilding a manageable life. Her work and support matter to the story even though the documentary record tells us less about her than it tells us about his publications. The name of a theorem rarely records who made it possible for a scholar to keep writing through a household crisis.

In seventeen ninety-four he published *Éléments de géométrie*, a textbook that became widely used in Europe and beyond. A book of elementary geometry can be a major historical achievement. It decides what students encounter first, which arguments are presented as foundational and how one generation prepares the next. Legendre reorganized much of Euclid into a form designed for contemporary instruction. He wanted geometry to be accessible as a coherent subject rather than only as a revered ancient text. His textbook achieved enormous circulation across changing governments and languages, a different kind of influence from winning a prize for a difficult research memoir.

The same book also displayed the limits of what even a highly accomplished geometer could prove. Legendre returned repeatedly to Euclid's parallel postulate. Can one demonstrate, from the other axioms alone, that parallel lines behave as Euclidean geometry says they do? He tried over decades, as generations before him had done. Later mathematics showed that consistent alternatives to Euclidean geometry could be developed. The repeated failure does not make his attempt foolish. It shows the strength of a conviction that particular features of space must follow inevitably from what seemed evident. When Bolyai's work on non-Euclidean geometry appeared, Legendre remained committed to the Euclidean position. His life crosses the point at which a venerable certainty was becoming a research question.

An additional aspect of his geometrical work brings the textbook back into its political setting. A nation trying to unify education needed relatively inexpensive, reproducible texts that students and teachers could use across towns. Legendre's book was suited to that need. It arranged geometry in a sequence that could be taught, examined and translated. More than one edition appeared, and its influence reached schools beyond France. This was not merely a book which happened to be written in the same year as a major political revolution. It participated in the educational changes of an era seeking new public standards while still drawing on inherited classical knowledge.

Legendre had also served a mathematical culture in which academy papers, prize essays and working numerical tables had very different audiences. One piece of research might impress a committee of leading analysts; another might help a surveyor or a teacher. He wrote for both kinds of readers. His public reputation rested on an ability to turn difficult analysis into things that others could use—models of planetary attraction, tables of integrals, and a textbook of geometry—not only on original proof of an isolated theorem.

His theory of numbers was similarly fertile and incomplete. In seventeen eighty-five he examined quadratic residues and proposed important statements about primes in arithmetic progressions. He recognized the quadratic reciprocity law, a symmetry describing when one prime can be a square residue modulo another. Earlier forms appeared in Euler, and Gauss would later prove the law rigorously. Legendre's attempts were not complete proofs by modern standards. He also stated a theorem about infinitely many primes in suitable arithmetic progressions without proving it; that proof was later supplied by Dirichlet. His contribution was neither identical to the later proofs nor nothing at all. He saw structures worth investigating and brought them into the mathematical conversation.

In seventeen ninety-eight his *Essai sur la théorie des nombres* brought together important work on arithmetic, and later editions continued to develop it. This publication belongs to a time when number theory was becoming a recognizably modern research field. Legendre and Gauss did not always agree about credit. Gauss was younger by more than twenty years, extraordinarily gifted, and sometimes unsparing in criticism of inadequate arguments. Legendre was prepared to use Gauss's improved proof of quadratic reciprocity while also resenting what he perceived as dismissive treatment of his earlier work. The conflict is intelligible without declaring one of them an entirely virtuous protagonist. Mathematical rigor and fairness in attribution are related but distinct questions.

The clearest example is the method of least squares. Astronomers and surveyors did not receive perfectly consistent observations. Measure the same object several times and the reported values differ. A calculation must use those imperfect observations without pretending that any one reading is exact. In his eighteen oh five *Nouvelles méthodes pour la détermination des orbites des comètes*, Legendre published a way to choose unknown quantities so that the sum of the squares of the errors is as small as possible. The arithmetic of squares had practical advantages, and the rule could be applied to fit observations or estimate orbital parameters.

Gauss later claimed to have used the method earlier, although he published his treatment in eighteen oh nine. This is a genuine priority dispute, not a reason to erase either man's contribution. Legendre has the clear claim to first publication of the method in eighteen oh five; Gauss made his own mathematical contributions to its development and justification. Their disagreement has remained useful to historians because it illustrates the difference between private discovery, documented use, publication and explanatory development. A theorem's place in the literature depends on evidence, not simply on which famous man later said he knew it first.

The calculation itself is worth hearing. Imagine fitting a straight line through measured points. For each point, compare the measured height with the height predicted by a chosen line. Those differences are residuals. If one chooses the line with the smallest sum of squared residuals, large disagreements count more heavily than small ones, and positive and negative residuals cannot simply cancel. With appropriate data this leads to a solvable pair of linear equations for the slope and intercept. The modern matrix formulation is far more general, but Legendre's astronomy gives the method its human setting: someone had to turn observations into a trustworthy orbit.

The argument over least squares is one of the reasons mathematical historians insist on precise wording. Publication is a public act that can be dated and inspected; a claim of private use may be genuine without being independently verifiable in the same way. Gauss's later argument about probability and observational errors supplied a different kind of justification from Legendre's practical rule for minimizing squares. Both mattered for the eventual method, and Laplace's work on error and probability also belongs in the story. It would be misleading to collapse a century of development into a duel between two men over a single modern formula.

Even the phrase 'best fitting' needs interpretation. Least squares is optimal for the objective it chooses, the sum of squared residuals. Whether that objective gives a scientifically reliable estimate depends on the observations, the model and the error structure. Legendre's astronomers worked under practical constraints; they needed a defensible procedure, not a universal guarantee that any fitted curve describes nature. The continuing strength of the method comes from the fact that its assumptions and limitations can be made precise.

Another preoccupation lasted much longer. Certain integrals that arise in geometry, mechanics and astronomy could not be expressed using elementary functions. Legendre spent decades classifying elliptic integrals, assembling transformations and tables, and trying to systematize their use. His multivolume treatises in the eighteen tens and eighteen twenties were remarkable compilations. Then Abel and Jacobi, both much younger, found the far-reaching advantage of investigating inverse functions and complex periodicity. These discoveries transformed the subject in a way Legendre had not anticipated, even as they built upon the territory he had mapped.

Here his response was generous. He corresponded with Jacobi and recognized the importance of Abel's and Jacobi's innovations. He could be combative about Gauss and still welcome the achievements of two other young mathematicians. These are not contradictory facts; they are parts of a particular human personality working in a competitive world. For Abel, who struggled with recognition and money, encouragement from the most established authority on elliptic integrals carried real weight. For Jacobi it was important enough to help his early academic rise. Legendre remained engaged with mathematics that had moved beyond his own original framework.

The elliptic-integral story also places Legendre among several generations of mathematicians with very different personal circumstances. Abel lived with financial difficulties and died young; Jacobi had a productive professorship and a growing family; Gauss was established in Göttingen. Legendre was already elderly when the two young innovators opened the new theory of elliptic functions. He had supplied classifications and tables valuable to their investigations, but their tools reorganized his field. It would have been easy for him to treat the new work as a threat. Instead, his correspondence offered encouragement and helped communicate the discoveries to a wider audience. A career can be influential through the work it inspires even when the next advance is not one's own.

The political conditions around him changed yet again under Napoleon and the restored Bourbon monarchy. The institutions of science continued, reorganized and renamed. In eighteen twenty-four he refused to support the government's preferred candidate in an institutional vote, and his pension was suspended. The financial consequences were serious for a man whose earlier fortune had already been damaged. Legendre died in Paris in January eighteen thirty-three. In the century of his birth, he had seen the French monarchy, Revolution, republican experiment and empire; in his later life he saw the restoration and another revolutionary change in eighteen thirty. His mathematics belonged to all these regimes, but the material conditions of his work were never isolated from them.

Legendre's biography is therefore a useful test of how we define intellectual importance. He did not always discover the final version of an idea. He offered the first publication of least squares while Gauss worked toward related methods; he sought a general theory of elliptic integrals before Abel and Jacobi changed the terms of the problem; he published a widely used geometry textbook while remaining unable to settle the parallel postulate as he hoped. His successes and failures became part of other people's research. Even the mistaken portrait teaches something: later readers can recognize a name and still misunderstand the person. To understand Legendre, one must see the schools, academies, family support, state surveys and political reversals that stood behind a remarkably durable body of mathematics.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1752–1787:** Birth in 1752 (Paris usually given, Toulouse suggested in some sources), Collège Mazarin education, École Militaire teaching, Berlin prize of 1782, Academy election in 1783, planetary work and Paris–Greenwich geodesy.
- **1791–1806:** Metric-system and table-making work; revolutionary financial disruption and marriage; *Éléments de géométrie* (1794); number theory books (1798 onward); first printed least-squares exposition (1805).
- **1811–1833:** Major elliptic-integral treatises, correspondence with Abel and Jacobi, post-1824 pension dispute, death in 1833.

**Portrait caution:** A widely reproduced likeness is of the politician Louis Legendre, *not* the mathematician; the Boilly caricature is the only known surviving authentic likeness. **Priority cautions:** Legendre first published least squares in 1805; Gauss's claims of earlier private work and his 1809 publication must be kept separate. Statements about quadratic reciprocity and primes in progressions preceded successful later proofs by Gauss and Dirichlet. The lecture does not attribute the full modern theory of special functions to Legendre.

### Mathematical display companion
- Least-squares fitting: choose \(\beta\) to minimize \(\|A\beta-y\|_2^2\); if \(A\) has full column rank the normal equations \(A^TA\beta=A^Ty\) yield the unique minimizer. This is modern notation, and numerical algorithms often avoid forming \(A^TA\).
- Legendre polynomials: \(P_0(x)=1\), \(P_1(x)=x\), \(P_2(x)=\tfrac12(3x^2-1)\); they arise in expansions of potential fields with spherical symmetry broken by the distribution of mass.
- Euler's and Legendre's earlier observations on quadratic reciprocity should not be equated with Gauss's completed proof; the quadratic-residue symbol and modern formulation come from a history of refinements.
