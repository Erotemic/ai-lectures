# Paul Halmos: What is the geometry hidden inside a pair of projections?

Imagine that two scientific teams examine the same collection of measurements, but retain different aspects of the data. One team keeps the directions that explain one phenomenon. The other keeps the directions that explain another. Each team has selected a subspace, and each selection can be represented by an orthogonal projection. A projection takes any vector and keeps its component in the chosen subspace, discarding the perpendicular component.

Either projection is easy to describe on its own. It acts as the identity on one space and as zero on the perpendicular space. Put two projections together, however, and their geometry becomes much more interesting. Some directions might be kept by both. Others might be kept by one and rejected by the other. The remaining directions can meet at a range of angles. What is the simplest exact description of all these possibilities?

This is the problem at the heart of a paper that Paul Halmos published in nineteen sixty-nine, with the remarkably plain title, *Two Subspaces*. It is nine pages long. Its main ideas connect geometry in the plane, the algebra of operators, and the structure of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. They also belong to a longer history: the principal-angle work of the nineteenth century, the earlier analysis of pairs of subspaces by Jacques Dixmier, and the development of modern spectral perturbation theory.

Halmos's contribution was to make the geometry of a pair of subspaces into a normal form that one can calculate with. A few universal pieces are separated first. The part that remains can be expressed through two commuting positive operators, which behave like cosine and sine. Then questions about pairs of projections can be attacked as familiar trigonometry, even when there is no finite list of angles.

To understand why he arrived at that construction, it helps to know the mathematical life behind it. Halmos was a probabilist, a scholar of measure and dynamics, a logician, an operator theorist, and one of the twentieth century's influential writers of mathematics. His career crossed several subjects, and his habit of finding the right way to explain a problem is visible in the architecture of *Two Subspaces*.

## From Budapest to American mathematics

Paul Richard Halmos was born on March third, nineteen sixteen, in Budapest, then in the Austro-Hungarian Empire, which would dissolve two years later. His mother died while he was an infant. His father, a physician, emigrated to the United States in the nineteen twenties, and Paul eventually joined him in Chicago in nineteen twenty-nine. At thirteen he found himself entering an American school system whose language he did not yet speak.

He had learned Hungarian and German, and his recollections of the move include the practical difficulty of arriving in a classroom without English. He acquired the language rapidly. He also advanced through school at a pace that led him to the University of Illinois while still in his teens. The story is worth remembering because later descriptions of Halmos can make his mathematical trajectory seem inevitable. It was not. His first university subject was chemical engineering. He shifted toward mathematics and philosophy, and for a time he pursued philosophy with more enthusiasm than mathematics.

He finished his undergraduate degree in nineteen thirty-four and continued graduate work at Illinois. His intellectual direction changed again when he began to work in analysis. In nineteen thirty-eight he completed a doctorate under Joseph Doob, who was helping transform probability into a rigorous measure-theoretic discipline. Halmos's dissertation concerned stochastic transformations and gambling systems. It belonged to a subject where the intuitive idea of chance was being rebuilt with precise definitions of measurable sets, transformations, and probability.

That beginning is relevant to the operator theorist he later became. A probability space contains random events and measurable functions. An operator can describe how those functions change when the underlying system advances in time. Orthogonal projections can describe conditional expectations, while Hilbert spaces of square-integrable functions supply a geometry for probability. The connection between measure and operators was part of Halmos's mathematical education, rather than an incidental change of specialty in middle age.

After the doctorate, finding an academic position proved difficult. Halmos later described writing an enormous number of applications and receiving almost no favorable replies. He briefly accepted a job at Reed College, but decided to pursue a place at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. His friend Warren Ambrose had obtained support there; Halmos sought an invitation without a fellowship, borrowed money from his father, and went anyway.

At Princeton he encountered John von Neumann. Halmos eventually became von Neumann's assistant. He attended lectures, worked through mathematical arguments, and helped turn ideas into written papers. Von Neumann could move with remarkable speed between a conceptual insight and an abstract formulation. Halmos also saw how demanding the writing process could be. In his later account of a joint paper, drafts traveled back and forth repeatedly, changing both their length and their presentation. The work was a lesson in mathematical precision and in the discipline of exposition.

A course by von Neumann inspired Halmos to write *Finite Dimensional Vector Spaces*, published in nineteen forty-two. The book introduced linear algebra through abstract vector spaces and linear maps, rather than by starting with arrays of numbers and leaving the geometry implicit. The approach was especially useful for readers who would later work with infinite-dimensional spaces: finite-dimensional ideas were presented in forms that would survive generalization.

## How a writer of mathematics became an operator theorist

During the nineteen forties Halmos taught at Syracuse University, and in nineteen forty-five he married Virginia Pritchett, who had studied logic and foundations. He moved to the University of Chicago in nineteen forty-six, entering a lively department where analysis, probability, algebra, and logic were being developed alongside one another. Over the following years he taught, wrote books, and investigated several fields.

His nineteen fifty *Measure Theory* provided a systematic treatment of integration and measurable spaces. *Introduction to Hilbert Space and the Theory of Spectral Multiplicity*, published in nineteen fifty-one, developed the geometry and classification of operators. *Lectures on Ergodic Theory* followed in nineteen fifty-six; *Naive Set Theory* in nineteen sixty; and *Algebraic Logic* in nineteen sixty-two. He also wrote *A Hilbert Space Problem Book* in nineteen sixty-seven. The titles reveal a productive pattern. He liked to find the essential language of a subject, to organize it economically, and to invite a student to work through its ideas.

Halmos's writings did more than explain theorems already known. His own research helped shape the questions other mathematicians would investigate. In ergodic theory he worked on measure-preserving transformations, the mathematical models of systems that evolve over time without changing their overall measure. In a nineteen forty-four paper, he proved that weak mixing is generic in a suitable topological sense among invertible measure-preserving transformations. That does not mean every such transformation mixes, or that a randomly selected physical system necessarily does. It means the transformations with the specified property form a dense, countable intersection of open sets in an appropriate topology. That is a precise and powerful way of saying that a behavior is topologically prevalent.

Here is an example to separate the main ideas. Place a marker on a circle and rotate it by an irrational multiple of a full turn at each step. The marker eventually visits every arc as closely as we wish. In the measure-theoretic sense, this rotation is ergodic: the only invariant measurable sets have either full or zero measure. But irrational rotation is not mixing. Knowledge of an initial arc continues to have structured correlations with its future positions. Ergodicity, weak mixing, and strong mixing ask successively different questions about how a system distributes information over time. Halmos helped clarify the landscape of such questions.

In logic he explored how operations that manipulate variables can be represented algebraically. The aim was to bring the techniques of algebra to systems of logical formulas. This was another instance of a recurring intellectual move: take a collection of rules, represent it by concrete operations on a structured space, and ask what those operations reveal.

By the nineteen sixties, operator theory had become a central focus. In this field the objects being studied are themselves transformations. One asks how they compose, what they preserve, what subspaces remain invariant, and when two transformations should be considered the same up to a change of coordinates. Halmos's growing interest in invariant subspaces helped produce the questions behind *Two Subspaces*.

An invariant subspace for an operator is a collection of vectors that stays inside itself when the operator acts. For a self-adjoint operator, invariance has a particularly strong consequence: the perpendicular complement is invariant too. Such a subspace is called reducing. Orthogonal projections are self-adjoint, so finding a common invariant subspace for two of them amounts to finding a common reducing subspace. That makes the geometry of two projections a natural testing ground for questions about operator structure.

## Start with one line and a tilted line

Return to two perpendicular coordinate directions in the plane. Let the first subspace be the horizontal line. Let the second be the line obtained by rotating it counterclockwise through thirty degrees. The orthogonal projection onto the first line simply takes a point, keeps its horizontal coordinate, and sets its vertical coordinate to zero.

To project onto the tilted line, first take the component of the point along a unit vector pointing thirty degrees above the horizontal, then multiply that unit vector by the component. The coordinate formula involves the square of the cosine, the square of the sine, and their product. The full array is in the mathematical companion. For listening, the important fact is that each entry comes directly from the geometry of a single tilted direction.

Apply the horizontal projection to a horizontal unit vector and nothing changes. Apply the tilted projection to the same vector and its image moves onto the tilted line and becomes shorter. At thirty degrees, the length of that image is the cosine of thirty degrees, about eighty-seven hundredths. The component that the tilted projection discards has length one half. That one half is the sine of thirty degrees.

Now subtract the two projections and measure the largest amount their actions can differ on a unit vector. In this two-dimensional example the answer is exactly one half. More generally, if the angle between the lines is theta, the operator norm of the difference of their orthogonal projections is the sine of theta. The two eigenvalues of that difference are opposite numbers, plus and minus that sine. The sign records the direction of change; the norm keeps its magnitude.

This is the first reason an angle is a useful invariant of a pair of subspaces. The geometry of the lines gives a number, and the difference of their projections gives an operator whose norm recovers that number. We can discuss angles without choosing which unit vector on either line should be positive, and without relying on a particular coordinate system.

But a pair of subspaces in three or four dimensions need not have one angle. Suppose the first subspace is a plane and the second is another plane. The planes might meet in a line, with their remaining directions tilted. Or they might meet at no nonzero vector at all, yet still have two distinct principal angles. A single tilt cannot describe every part of their relationship.

That is the point at which Halmos's decomposition begins.

## Four special pieces and the fifth part

Call the two closed subspaces M and N. Their orthogonal complements contain the vectors perpendicular to them. There are four evident kinds of vectors. The first kind belongs to both M and N. The second belongs to M but is perpendicular to N. The third belongs to N but is perpendicular to M. The fourth is perpendicular to both.

Each kind forms a closed subspace. More importantly, the four pieces are mutually perpendicular. A vector shared by M and N is perpendicular to a vector in M and perpendicular to N, and the analogous orthogonality checks handle the other pairs. We may therefore separate all four pieces without one interfering with another.

On the first piece, both projections act as the identity. On the second, the projection onto M acts as the identity and the projection onto N acts as zero. The third reverses those roles. On the fourth, both act as zero. These are the easy pieces, and they are easy in every dimension.

What remains after removing them is a fifth, orthogonal component. Halmos called its pair of subspaces a pair in generic position. In this remaining space, none of the four special intersections contains a nonzero vector. No direction is shared exactly, no direction lies entirely in one space and perpendicular to the other, and no direction is perpendicular to both.

The phrase generic position is a technical definition here. It does not claim that every pair of subspaces found in an application is random or that the four exceptional pieces are statistically negligible. It tells us which exact intersections have been separated so that a uniform description of the rest becomes possible.

A concrete example makes the five-part structure visible. Take a four-dimensional space with perpendicular unit directions called e one through e four. Let M be spanned by the first and third directions. Let N be spanned by the first direction and by a new direction tilted thirty degrees from the third toward the fourth. Then the first coordinate belongs to both subspaces. The second coordinate belongs to neither. In the plane of the third and fourth coordinates, neither selected line agrees with the other and neither is perpendicular to the other. That final plane is the generic part.

On the shared first coordinate, both projections are one. On the second coordinate, both are zero. In the final two-coordinate plane they look exactly like our earlier thirty-degree pair. We have converted a four-dimensional comparison into two trivial scalar actions and one ordinary plane calculation.

We could add a direction belonging only to M and perpendicular to N, or one belonging only to N and perpendicular to M, without changing the logic. The general theorem just arranges all those cases at once. Once the four intersections are isolated, the serious geometry takes place in the generic component.

## Why the generic part comes in pairs

Here is Halmos's first deeper observation. On the generic component, the part belonging to M and the part perpendicular to M have the same Hilbert-space dimension. It is possible to identify the generic component with two copies of one Hilbert space, usually called K. In this identification, M becomes the first coordinate axis: pairs whose second entry is zero.

Why should two copies appear? Look again at the plane. One direction describes the axis retained by the first projection; a perpendicular direction describes what it rejects. Changing the subspace couples these two directions. A block describing the relationship needs room for each member of the pair. Halmos proved that the analogous pairing is available even when the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional.

One way to see the pairing is to project vectors of N onto M. Generic position says that this projection is injective: if a vector in N projects to zero, then it lies in N and perpendicular to M, one of the intersections we already removed. It must therefore be zero. Generic position also says that the projected vectors are dense in M. If a vector of M were perpendicular to all of them, it would belong to M and perpendicular to N, another forbidden intersection.

So the projection from N into M has no kernel and dense range. In Hilbert-space language, those conditions force the relevant dimensions to match. Symmetry gives corresponding matches among M, its perpendicular complement, N, and the perpendicular complement of N. Once we choose an isometric identification of M with its complement, the whole generic component becomes K plus K.

This pairing is what permits a normal form. The first projection now keeps the first coordinate and erases the second. The second projection can be written as a two-by-two block matrix, except that each entry is an operator on K rather than a single real number.

A block matrix may sound like a complicated object. Its job is straightforward. Feed the pair two vectors, one from each copy of K. The top row computes the first output component, and the bottom row computes the second. The diagonal blocks tell us how much of each input coordinate remains in the same coordinate. The off-diagonal blocks tell us how the two coordinates interact.

To make that interaction as simple as possible, Halmos uses the freedom in choosing the identification between the two copies of K. With a suitable choice, the off-diagonal blocks become positive and equal. Then the algebra of an orthogonal projection forces a trigonometric form. Instead of four arbitrary blocks, everything is controlled by two commuting positive operators called C and S.

## Cosine and sine become operators

The first projection has the block form: identity in the upper left, zero everywhere else. The second has four blocks. The upper left is C squared. The lower right is S squared. Both off-diagonal blocks are the product C times S. The two operators commute, each is positive, and their squares add up to the identity.

If K were one-dimensional, C and S would simply be two nonnegative numbers. We would recognize them as cosine theta and sine theta. Their squared sum equals one, and the matrix is precisely the projection onto a line tilted by theta. Halmos's theorem says that the same pattern survives when K is an entire Hilbert space.

What does an operator-valued cosine mean? Think first of several independent angle planes in a finite-dimensional space. Perhaps one plane has an angle of fifteen degrees and another has an angle of sixty degrees. Put the two cosine values on the diagonal of one operator. Put the two sine values on the diagonal of another. Both operators act on the space of angle labels. On the first label they return the first angle's cosine or sine; on the second they return the second values.

The resulting two-by-two block matrices actually describe four ordinary dimensions: two coupled planes, each with its own angle. The operators C and S allow both angles to be handled in a single expression. In higher finite dimensions they can carry a whole list of angles. In general Hilbert space, they can represent angular information that is not captured by any finite or countable collection of eigenvectors.

The mathematical tool behind the construction is the functional calculus for self-adjoint operators. A positive contraction C has its spectrum between zero and one. We may define S as the positive square root of the identity minus C squared. These functions of C commute automatically. We can also introduce an angle operator, call it Theta, for which C is cosine Theta and S is sine Theta. The spectrum of Theta lies between zero and ninety degrees.

There is an important qualification. On the generic component, C and S have no nonzero vector in their kernels. That excludes exact angles of zero or ninety degrees as eigen-directions of the generic piece. But it does not exclude zero and ninety degrees as limits of angles. In infinite-dimensional analysis, a number can belong to the spectrum without being an eigenvalue. That distinction will become important when we examine a second example.

At this stage we have answered the structural question. All four exact-intersection pieces have been removed. The remaining pair of projections has the universal sine-and-cosine block representation. Halmos's nineteen sixty-nine paper proves this for arbitrary Hilbert spaces, not merely for matrices whose eigenvectors we can list by hand.

## What a pair of angles can tell us

Let us work a four-dimensional example without getting trapped in the full matrix. Take two independent perpendicular planes. In the first, the two selected lines meet at thirty degrees. In the second, they meet at sixty degrees. Let M contain the first line from each plane, and N contain the tilted line from each plane. The pair is in generic position, and its two principal angles are thirty and sixty degrees.

The operator C has two diagonal values: about eighty-seven hundredths and one half. The operator S has values one half and about eighty-seven hundredths. We immediately know the size of the difference between the two projections. It is the largest sine among the two planes, about eighty-seven hundredths. The sixty-degree plane controls the maximal difference.

Now ask another question. In what sense do the two projections fail to commute? Applying P and then Q need not give the same answer as applying Q and then P. In a single angle plane, the commutator has size sine theta times cosine theta. For thirty degrees, that number is about forty-three hundredths. For sixty degrees, it has exactly the same size. In our four-dimensional example the norm of the commutator is therefore about forty-three hundredths.

This gives a revealing contrast. The distance between the projections is largest when their lines approach perpendicularity. But their failure to commute is not largest there. At a right angle, projecting onto one line and then onto the other gives zero in either order, so the projections commute. At zero angle they also commute, because they are the same projection. The commutator reaches its maximum in a single plane at forty-five degrees, halfway between those extremes.

The distinction helps explain why the geometry of two subspaces is richer than any one operator norm. The difference of projections measures angular displacement. The commutator measures a different aspect of their incompatibility. The two quantities are related through sine and cosine, but they answer different questions.

There is another useful operator, the sum of the two projections. In a single generic angle plane, its two eigenvalues are one plus the cosine and one minus the cosine of the angle. For thirty degrees, one eigenvalue is near one point eight seven and the other near zero point one three. For sixty degrees, they are one point five and one half.

Why is the sum informative? A vector counted strongly by both projections produces a large value of their sum. A direction poorly retained by both produces a small one. The paired eigenvalues sit symmetrically around one, revealing the same cosine that controls the mutual angle. This is connected to a theorem of Jacques Dixmier, published before Halmos's paper, on classifying pairs of subspaces up to unitary equivalence. Halmos used his normal form to offer a transparent new route to that classification in generic position.

So the block representation does more than look tidy. It turns geometric questions into explicit operator formulas. In a finite collection of angle planes, the formulas reduce to scalar computations. In general, they still work through the spectral calculus, even if no finite angular list is available.

## Slopes, graphs, and a hidden difficulty

Halmos's paper develops another model for the generic part, based on the graph of an operator. In ordinary coordinate geometry, a line through the origin can be described by its slope. The horizontal axis consists of pairs with second coordinate zero. A tilted line consists of pairs whose second coordinate is the slope times the first.

Now replace the scalar slope by an operator T on K. Its graph consists of all pairs formed from a vector and its image under T. The horizontal axis is one subspace of K plus K; the graph is the other. This transforms a question about two subspaces into a question about one operator.

In the simple angle plane, the slope is the tangent of the angle. That suggests the operator formula: tangent Theta equals S times the inverse of C. But a warning appears as soon as the angle can approach ninety degrees. Cosine becomes small. Dividing by it can produce an arbitrarily large tangent. In an infinite-dimensional space, C might be injective without having a bounded inverse.

Halmos dealt with this properly. In the full theorem the graph operator may be unbounded, yet closed and densely defined. It is not an arbitrary partial rule. Its domain is chosen so that it represents a closed subspace of the full Hilbert space. After a polar-decomposition adjustment, the graph operator can be taken positive and self-adjoint, and its unitary-equivalence class contains the remaining angular information.

Consider an explicit infinite-dimensional construction. Let K be the Hilbert space of square-summable sequences. For the n-th coordinate choose an angle that gets closer and closer to ninety degrees, but never reaches it. For example, choose its cosine to be one divided by n plus one. Every cosine is positive. So C has no kernel. Every sine is also positive, so S has no kernel. The pair of subspaces is in generic position.

Yet the tangent of the n-th angle grows without bound. The graph operator T multiplies the n-th coordinate by that tangent. On sequences with too much mass in high coordinates, the result need not be square-summable. T cannot be defined as a bounded map on all of K. It has a dense domain consisting of the sequences for which multiplication by the tangent still produces a square-summable sequence.

The difference between those statements is central. Each coordinate plane has a perfectly ordinary, finite tilt. Every individual angle is below ninety degrees. Nevertheless, no single finite bound controls all their slopes. Infinite dimensionality allows limiting behavior that is absent from any fixed finite matrix.

There is a complementary example. Choose angles that approach zero but never equal it. Then the subspaces are still in generic position, since they have no shared direction exactly. Yet there are vectors in the first subspace that can be approximated extremely closely by vectors in the second. No positive lower bound on their nonzero angles exists. Even their algebraic sum can fail to be closed. For the graph of a diagonal slope that tends to zero, the vertical components lie in the range of a compact injective operator whose range is dense but not closed.

This pair of examples explains why the four intersection tests do not tell the entire story. The intersections detect exact coincidences and exact perpendicularities. The operator C encodes the limiting geometry between them. Halmos's five-part decomposition keeps the two roles distinct.

## A second way to draw the picture

The graph model has a symmetry that is easy to overlook. If one subspace is the horizontal axis and the other is a tilted line, rotate the entire drawing backward through half their angle. The two lines now sit at equal and opposite inclinations. One is the graph of a positive slope, and the other the graph of its negative.

Halmos turned this elementary picture into a third theorem in his paper. Every pair of closed subspaces in generic position can be represented by the graphs of T zero and minus T zero, where T zero is a positive bounded contraction with the appropriate kernel conditions. The half-angle slope is bounded by one, even when the original full-angle tangent was unbounded.

That is a striking advantage of centering the two spaces symmetrically. Angles in the generic part lie between zero and ninety degrees. Half of such an angle lies between zero and forty-five degrees, and the tangent of a half-angle cannot exceed one. A model that required an unbounded graph operator in the axis-based description acquires a bounded graph operator after the two subspaces share the tilt.

Take a line at eighty degrees from the horizontal. Its ordinary slope is about five point seven, already quite steep. The symmetric model rotates the reference frame by forty degrees. The two resulting slopes have magnitude the tangent of forty degrees, about eighty-four hundredths, one positive and one negative. The geometry is the same; the representation is better conditioned.

For an infinite collection of angles approaching ninety degrees, the original tangents become unbounded, but their half-angle tangents remain at most one. Functional calculus replaces the numerical half-angle identity by an operator expression involving the positive square root of the ratio of identity minus C to identity plus C. The companion writes this formula explicitly.

This is a characteristic mathematical move: rather than struggling with an inconvenient representation, find a coordinate system adapted to the symmetry of the problem. The theorem does not imply that the two subspaces themselves are closer after the change. A unitary change of coordinates preserves their geometry. It does make that geometry easier to express.

## From angle blocks to invariant subspaces

Why had Halmos been studying these configurations? In the introduction to *Two Subspaces*, he explained that the results arose from questions about invariant subspace lattices of operators. The normal form exposes a direct consequence about common invariant subspaces for pairs of orthogonal projections.

Return to the generic sine-and-cosine blocks. Suppose an operator X commutes with both projections. First, commuting with the projection onto the first coordinate axis forces X to preserve the first and second copies of K separately. Its block matrix must therefore have zero off-diagonal blocks. Let the two diagonal actions be A and B.

Now demand that X also commute with the second projection. Its cosine-squared block forces the relevant diagonal actions to commute with C. Its off-diagonal block, the product C times S, ties the actions A and B together. Because that product has no kernel in generic position, the two actions must agree. The common commutant consists of the operators that act in the same way on each copy of K and commute with C.

A commutant is the collection of transformations compatible with a given family of operators. It tells us which other operations can be performed without disturbing that family. Finding the commutant of a pair of projections is therefore a way of determining which further symmetries the pair possesses.

There is a simple consequence. On a complex Hilbert space of dimension greater than two, two orthogonal projections always admit a nontrivial common reducing subspace. The four exceptional parts already supply such spaces when they are present. Otherwise, on a generic component of dimension greater than two, the angle-operator representation gives the required structure. In a two-dimensional generic plane, by contrast, two noncommuting rank-one projections can act irreducibly: no common nonzero proper invariant line exists.

This theorem has a clear boundary. It concerns a pair of orthogonal projections, which are self-adjoint idempotents. It does not say that any two arbitrary operators have a common invariant subspace. That broader question is far more complicated. Halmos's analysis succeeds because a projection contains so much geometric structure: it splits the space orthogonally into its range and its kernel.

The lesson is that special algebraic objects deserve special coordinates. If we ignore their defining identities and treat two projections as arbitrary matrices, we miss the relationships that force their behavior. The trigonometric block form is powerful because it preserves those relationships throughout the calculation.

## A projection comparison becomes a perturbation estimate

We can now connect Halmos's geometry to a wider mathematical question. Suppose a symmetric matrix represents a system with distinct modes. Its eigenvectors form orthogonal directions, and a chosen group of eigenvectors spans a spectral subspace. What happens to that subspace when the matrix changes?

A spectral subspace is represented by an orthogonal projection. If the original matrix has a selected eigenvalue cluster, call its spectral projection P. If the perturbed matrix has the corresponding cluster, call its projection Q. The problem of comparing the two spectral subspaces is exactly a problem about two projections.

Halmos's theorem tells us the possible geometry of P and Q. It does not, by itself, tell us how far the perturbed eigenvalues move or whether a spectral cluster remains isolated. Those facts require hypotheses on the matrix perturbation and its separation from the rest of the spectrum. Theorems in the Davis–Kahan tradition provide estimates that connect those hypotheses to subspace angles. The five-part decomposition and sine-and-cosine blocks then show what the geometric conclusion means.

For example, imagine a symmetric matrix with one important eigenvector and an eigenvalue separated by a large gap from all others. A small perturbation typically tilts the associated one-dimensional subspace by a small angle. The norm of the difference between the old and new spectral projections is the sine of that angle. An inequality controlling that norm therefore controls the geometric displacement directly.

For a higher-dimensional cluster, there may be several principal angles. The operator S in Halmos's normal form records their sines, including the limiting behavior of the infinite-dimensional setting. Its operator norm captures the largest angular deviation. Other operator norms can aggregate information from several angles rather than keeping only the worst one.

The historical roles must be separated. Angles between higher-dimensional subspaces had been studied long before nineteen sixty-nine. Dixmier investigated the relative position of closed subspaces in nineteen forty-eight. Szőkefalvi-Nagy had earlier constructed unitary maps between sufficiently close projections. Halmos supplied a clean, broadly useful structural picture of two arbitrary closed subspaces. Spectral perturbation estimates, in the forms associated with Davis and Kahan and other researchers, answer additional quantitative questions.

It is the combination that proves useful in applications: identify the spectral subspaces, establish the gap and perturbation hypotheses, estimate the angles, and then interpret those angles through the geometry of projections. A normal form cannot replace the analytical estimate. It gives the estimate a precise geometric language.

## The human work of mathematical exposition

We have spent much of this portrait on a paper about two subspaces, but Halmos's influence cannot be confined to it. His books helped generations of readers learn mathematics by learning how to pose questions. His problem book, in particular, asks students to solve a sequence of carefully selected problems instead of following a long chain of ready-made proofs. That approach reflects a view of mathematics as an activity in which understanding grows through argument and calculation.

He was also interested in the craft of writing itself. He published advice about how to write mathematics, how to speak it, and how to organize a paper for an audience. He championed clear definitions and efficient exposition, and was associated with the now-familiar small square used to mark the end of a proof. A reader who has seen that symbol thousands of times may never think about the historical human decisions that shaped mathematical typography.

His interest in mathematical people took a literal photographic form. Halmos made thousands of photographs of fellow mathematicians. The resulting collection preserves a kind of history that theorems cannot: what the community looked like, which people gathered together, how researchers appeared outside formal portraits, and the social texture of a working discipline. He published a collection under the title *I Have a Photographic Memory*.

He moved from Chicago to Michigan, spent time at the University of Hawaii, and joined Indiana University in nineteen sixty-nine. There he helped build an active network of functional analysts in the Midwest. Later he taught at Santa Clara University. Virginia Halmos remained an important participant in the intellectual and social life around him. Together they supported mathematical education and expository prizes, extending their concern for mathematical communication beyond their own books and lectures.

The American Mathematical Society awarded Halmos its Steele Prize for exposition in nineteen eighty-three. The recognition was for his sustained influence as a mathematical writer. He continued to teach, edit, and write into later life. His autobiography, *I Want to Be a Mathematician*, appeared in nineteen eighty-five. Its subtitle, *An Automathography*, gives some sense of how he understood his life through mathematical work. He died in California in October two thousand six, aged ninety.

There is a temptation to turn an excellent expositor into a caricature of pure simplicity. But Halmos's mathematical work also confronted genuine technical difficulties: infinite-dimensional domains, unbounded operators, noncommuting projections, spectral invariants, and topological properties of transformations. His explanations succeeded when they brought those difficulties into focus and chose a form in which each could be handled explicitly. The readable surface was the result of serious mathematical organization.

## What remains after the coordinates disappear

Let us return one final time to our two teams selecting different directions from the same data. The four kinds of exact agreement or disagreement can be separated: retained by both, retained only by the first, retained only by the second, and retained by neither. After those pieces are removed, the surviving geometry consists of coupled directions. In a small matrix, each pair has a principal angle. In a general Hilbert space, the pair is governed by an operator-valued cosine and sine.

From those objects we can recover the largest angular displacement, understand why the projections fail to commute, express their sum, describe common symmetries, and construct graph models. When the full-angle graph slope becomes unbounded, the half-angle construction supplies a bounded symmetric alternative. When angles accumulate at zero or ninety degrees, the functional calculus records their limits without inventing nonexistent eigenvectors.

The choice of representation is the achievement. Halmos did not discover that two lines have an angle; nor did his normal form settle every perturbation problem. He showed how to carry the elementary trigonometry of two lines into the language of general operators while accounting for intersections, complements, and the behavior specific to infinite dimensions.

That result captures something about his broader career. In probability, dynamics, logic, linear algebra, and operator theory, Halmos helped make a problem accessible by finding operations and representations that expose its structure. His historical portrait combines mathematical research with the sustained work of teaching other people to see what an argument is doing.

Two projections can seem like the simplest objects in operator theory. Their ranges and kernels are defined in a sentence. Yet the relationship between them can contain a whole angular spectrum. Halmos's *Two Subspaces* gives that geometry a form one can calculate, explain, and use.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. The four intersection components and the generic summand

For closed subspaces \(M,N\subset H\), with orthogonal projections \(P,Q\), define

\[
H_{11}=M\cap N,\qquad H_{10}=M\cap N^\perp,\qquad
H_{01}=M^\perp\cap N,\qquad H_{00}=M^\perp\cap N^\perp.
\]

These four subspaces are pairwise orthogonal. If \(H_g=(H_{11}\oplus H_{10}\oplus H_{01}\oplus H_{00})^\perp\), then \(H_g\) reduces both \(P\) and \(Q\). Their respective restrictions are

\[
(P,Q)|_{H_{11}}=(I,I),\quad (P,Q)|_{H_{10}}=(I,0),\quad
(P,Q)|_{H_{01}}=(0,I),\quad(P,Q)|_{H_{00}}=(0,0).
\]

The reduced subspaces \(M\cap H_g\), \(N\cap H_g\) are in *generic position*: all four intersections between these subspaces and their respective orthogonal complements in \(H_g\) equal \(\{0\}\).

### 2. The Halmos cosine–sine block (1969, Theorem 2)

For the generic part, there is a Hilbert space \(K\) and a unitary \(W:H_g\to K\oplus K\) such that

\[
WPW^*=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
WQW^*=\begin{pmatrix}C^2&CS\\CS&S^2\end{pmatrix},\quad
C,S\ge0,\quad C^2+S^2=I,\quad CS=SC,\quad
\ker C=\ker S=\{0\}.
\]

The operators \(C,S\) are positive contractions. One may set
\(\Theta=\arccos C\), with \(C=\cos\Theta\), \(S=\sin\Theta\) and
\(\sigma(\Theta)\subseteq[0,\pi/2]\). Both endpoints may lie in the
continuous spectrum even though neither is an eigenvalue on \(H_g\).

For two rank-one projections in \(\mathbb R^2\) with angle \(\theta\),
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
Q=\begin{pmatrix}c^2&cs\\cs&s^2\end{pmatrix},\quad
c=\cos\theta,\ s=\sin\theta.
\]
Then
\[
(P-Q)^2=s^2I,\quad \operatorname{spec}(P-Q)=\{-s,+s\},\quad
\|P-Q\|=s,\quad \|PQ-QP\|=cs,
\]
\[
\operatorname{spec}(P+Q)=\{1-c,1+c\}.
\]
On the generic block,
\[
(P-Q)^2=\begin{pmatrix}S^2&0\\0&S^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
PQ-QP=\begin{pmatrix}0&CS\\-CS&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
so \(\|P-Q\|_{H_g}=\|S\|\) and \(\|PQ-QP\|_{H_g}=\|CS\|\le\frac12\).
On \(H_{10}\oplus H_{01}\), the projection difference has norm 1 if that summand is nonzero. On \(H_{11}\oplus H_{00}\), it is zero.

### 3. Explicit four-dimensional example

Take \(H=\mathbb R^4\), \(M=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_3\}\),
\(N=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\cos(\pi/6)e_3+\sin(\pi/6)e_4\}\).
Then \(H_{11}=\operatorname{span}(e_1)\), \(H_{00}=\operatorname{span}(e_2)\),
\(H_{10}=H_{01}=\{0\}\), and \(H_g=\operatorname{span}(e_3,e_4)\).
The generic projection matrices are the thirty-degree matrices above;
\(\|P-Q\|=1/2\).

For a second example, let \(K=\mathbb R^2\) and
\(\Theta=\operatorname{diag}(\pi/6,\pi/3)\) on \(K\). Its generic pair
on \(K\oplus K\) has two independent angle planes, with
\(\|P-Q\|=\sqrt3/2\),
\(\|PQ-QP\|=\sqrt3/4\), and eigenvalues of \(P+Q\)
\(1\pm\sqrt3/2,\,1\pm1/2\).

### 4. Axis–graph normal form (1969, Theorem 1)

For a generic pair, let \(M=K\oplus\{0\}\). The other subspace can be
identified with \(\operatorname{graph}(T)\), where \(T\) is closed,
densely defined, injective, and has dense range. It can be taken positive
self-adjoint by polar decomposition. In the cosine–sine model,
\[
T=SC^{-1},\quad \operatorname{dom}T=\operatorname{ran}C,
\quad C=(I+T^2)^{-1/2},\quad S=T(I+T^2)^{-1/2}.
\]
The inverse \(C^{-1}\) need not be bounded, so \(T\) need not be bounded.

Infinite-dimensional illustration: on \(K=\ell^2(\mathbb N)\), set
\(C e_n=(n+1)^{-1}e_n\),
\(S e_n=\sqrt{1-(n+1)^{-2}}\,e_n\). Both are injective,
so the pair is generic. Yet
\(T e_n=\sqrt{(n+1)^2-1}\,e_n\) is an unbounded positive self-adjoint
operator with the natural weighted square-summability domain.

For small-angle accumulation, set \(\theta_n=1/(n+1)\),
\(C e_n=\cos\theta_n\,e_n\), \(S e_n=\sin\theta_n\,e_n\).
The pair remains generic, but \(S\) is compact, injective, and has
nonclosed dense range. Here \(M+N=K\oplus\operatorname{ran}S\), hence
\(M+N\) is not closed.

### 5. Reflected graphs and the half-angle (1969, Theorem 3)

The generic pair is unitarily equivalent to
\(\operatorname{graph}(T_0)\) and
\(\operatorname{graph}(-T_0)\), with
\[
T_0=\bigl((I-C)(I+C)^{-1}\bigr)^{1/2}
=\tan(\Theta/2),\qquad 0\le T_0\le I,
\]
\(\ker T_0=\ker(I-T_0)=\{0\}\). This is bounded even when the
axis–graph slope \(T=\tan\Theta\) is unbounded. Define
\(C_0=((I+C)/2)^{1/2}\), \(S_0=((I-C)/2)^{1/2}\); these are the
operator-valued cosine and sine of \(\Theta/2\). A suitable block rotation
using \(C_0,S_0\) transports the axis/graph representation to the
symmetric ±graph representation.

### 6. Commutants, invariant subspaces, and Dixmier's theorem

For the generic pair, \(X\) commutes with \(P\) iff it has block form
\(\operatorname{diag}(A,B)\). Commutation with \(Q\) and injectivity of
\(CS\) then force \(A=B\) and \(AC=CA\). Thus
\[
\{P,Q\}'=\{\operatorname{diag}(A,A):AC=CA\}
\quad\text{on }H_g.
\]
Halmos observes that two orthogonal projections on a complex Hilbert
space of dimension greater than 2 always possess a nontrivial common
reducing subspace. The two-dimensional generic rank-one case shows the
dimension hypothesis cannot simply be dropped.

For pairs in generic position, the unitary-equivalence class of \(P+Q\)
classifies the pair, as proved earlier by Jacques Dixmier (1948). Halmos
recovers this in the corollary to his Theorem 3; the 1969 paper explicitly
attributes the classification result to Dixmier.

### 7. Historical chronology and qualifications

- **1916:** Born in Budapest, 3 March.
- **1929–1938:** Joins family in Chicago; studies at Illinois; Ph.D. under
  Joseph Doob in probability and stochastic transformations (1938).
- **1939–1942:** Institute for Advanced Study; assistant to von Neumann;
  *Finite Dimensional Vector Spaces* (1942).
- **1944:** Research on generic weak mixing; *The Foundations of Probability*
  essay, later awarded the Chauvenet Prize.
- **1946–1960:** University of Chicago; *Measure Theory* (1950),
  *Introduction to Hilbert Space and the Theory of Spectral Multiplicity*
  (1951), *Lectures on Ergodic Theory* (1956).
- **1960–1967:** *Naive Set Theory* (1960), *Algebraic Logic* (1962),
  *A Hilbert Space Problem Book* (1967); teaching at Michigan.
- **1969:** *Two Subspaces*, *Transactions of the American Mathematical
  Society* 144, 381–389, DOI 10.1090/S0002-9947-1969-0251519-5.
- **1969–1985:** Indiana University, with other visiting appointments;
  later Santa Clara University.
- **1983:** AMS Steele Prize for expository writing.
- **1985–2006:** Autobiography, *I Want to Be a Mathematician* (1985);
  died 2 October 2006.

The older geometric study of principal angles (including Camille Jordan)
and the 1948 classification by Dixmier precede Halmos's 1969 paper. The
paper develops the axis–graph, cosine–sine, and reflected-graph forms as
Theorems 1, 2, and 3, and derives Dixmier's classification as a corollary.
