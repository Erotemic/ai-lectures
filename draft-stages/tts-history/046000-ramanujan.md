# 046000 — Srinivasa Ramanujan: From Madras notebooks to Cambridge

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** partitions, infinite series, mathematical collaboration, and the conditions of discovery. **Background:** arithmetic, fractions, elementary algebra, and the meaning of a limit.

In the early nineteen tens, a clerk at the Madras Port Trust was filling notebooks with mathematical results that few people around him could evaluate. Srinivasa Ramanujan had lost the ordinary route through college examinations. He relied on a small circle of Indian mathematical enthusiasts, on paid work, and eventually on correspondence with people abroad. The story that followed would carry him from South India to Cambridge during the First World War. The people and circumstances that allowed his notebooks to be read are as important to this portrait as the formulas they contained.

Srinivasa Ramanujan devoted much of his short life to questions in which surprisingly precise formulas emerge from the apparent chaos of whole numbers. He was born in eighteen eighty-seven in Erode, in the region now called Tamil Nadu, and grew up largely in Kumbakonam in South India. His father worked as a clerk in a cloth merchant's business. His family life and religious community were central to his upbringing. Those details matter because his career took shape outside the European university structures that dominated the publication of research mathematics at the time.

As a schoolboy he showed a strong fascination with mathematics and encountered G. S. Carr's *Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure Mathematics*. This book gathered many mathematical statements in a compact format, often with little explanation. It gave Ramanujan access to a large landscape of formulas, but also provided a model for writing mathematics in which results appeared much more often than full demonstrations. Later commentators sometimes describe his notebooks as miraculous expressions of intuition. A less romantic description is both more accurate and more interesting: he worked with the books available to him, developed powerful methods of calculation, recorded many results with extraordinary economy, and did not always have the same conventions of proof and citation as research mathematicians elsewhere.

The limits of his educational situation were practical as well as intellectual. He won a college scholarship but lost it after concentrating on mathematics at the expense of other subjects. He had difficulty passing broader examinations and did not obtain the ordinary university qualification that might have led to an academic position. His mathematical work continued through stretches of financial insecurity and illness. In nineteen hundred nine his mother arranged his marriage to Janaki Ammal, who was then a child, as was possible under the customs of that time; the couple did not begin living together immediately. We should neither erase this part of his family history nor project a modern romantic narrative onto an arrangement whose circumstances were different.

By the early nineteen tens Ramanujan was submitting problems and papers to the *Journal of the Indian Mathematical Society*. This local mathematical community mattered. His work was not produced in complete isolation, and he was not simply discovered by a European mathematician who alone conferred legitimacy on it. Indian supporters, including Ramachandra Rao and colleagues associated with the mathematical society and Madras Port Trust, helped him obtain time and resources for research. His notebooks were often the form in which a prospective patron first encountered his ideas.

One of the most revealing recorded episodes comes from Ramachandra Rao's account of a visit. Ramanujan arrived carrying a worn notebook and began explaining his results. Rao was impressed but did not immediately know how to assess them. The scene shows a problem facing original researchers: how do you persuade someone that an unfamiliar calculation is meaningful when your work cannot be judged by conventional credentials? A notebook full of striking formulas can look like nonsense to a reader without the relevant background. The difficulty is not solved merely by enthusiasm. It takes independent checking, explanation, and shared standards.

Ramanujan obtained a position as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust. In January nineteen thirteen he wrote to G. H. Hardy at Cambridge, enclosing a selection of his mathematical results. His request was not for fame in the abstract. Correspondence from the period records his need for support so he could continue working. Hardy was struck by parts of the letter, though he and J. E. Littlewood also found mistakes and unsupported assertions. That mixed reaction is important. Ramanujan possessed extraordinary insights, and some of his claims were wrong. Both facts belong in the same biography.

Hardy helped arrange support from the University of Madras and, later, for Ramanujan to travel to England. With assistance from E. H. Neville and others, Ramanujan sailed in March nineteen fourteen and came to Cambridge, just before the First World War transformed European life. It was a profound change in working conditions, climate, food, and social environment. Ramanujan was an observant Hindu and vegetarian, and his dietary needs became more difficult to meet in wartime Cambridge. He also experienced serious illness. Biographers and medical historians have proposed different explanations for the disease that eventually killed him, but the surviving evidence does not justify presenting a single retrospective diagnosis as certain.

At Cambridge he collaborated with Hardy and encountered a mathematical culture that demanded explicit arguments of a particular kind. Hardy sometimes found himself attempting to teach methods that Ramanujan then connected to new ideas almost immediately. Littlewood also participated. This encounter can easily be turned into a cliché in which intuitive Indian genius meets rigorous British reason. The historical reality is more complex. Ramanujan could devise demanding calculations; Hardy could recognize and develop an unfamiliar result; both benefited from the exchange; and mathematical rigor is not the property of one country. The partnership worked because distinctive strengths met a shared problem.

Ramanujan's journey also separated him from his wife, Janaki, whom he had married in nineteen oh nine. She remained in India during his years in England. A strict vegetarian, he struggled to obtain familiar food in Cambridge, especially after wartime shortages began. His health declined seriously, but it would be too simple to claim that a single dietary circumstance explains his illnesses. The personal cost of the journey belonged alongside the mathematical opportunity. He was pursuing recognition in a foreign academic system while living far from his household and the everyday practices he knew.

The most famous personal anecdote comes from Hardy's later account of visiting Ramanujan while he was ill. Hardy mentioned a taxi numbered seventeen twenty-nine and said it seemed an uninteresting number. Ramanujan replied that it was interesting because it is the smallest positive integer expressible as a sum of two positive cubes in two different ways. One cube plus twelve cubed gives seventeen twenty-nine; nine cubed plus ten cubed gives the same number. We should present this as Hardy's recollection, not as a recorded conversation whose every word is guaranteed. Its appeal is mathematical as well as biographical: an ordinary number offered an immediate opportunity to notice an unexpected structure.

The taxi story can also mislead. It suggests that a mathematician's work is a stream of instant tricks performed for visitors. Most of Ramanujan's serious contributions required extended calculations, reflection, and communication with others. The asymptotic theory of partitions is not a party puzzle, and the theory of modular relations is not explained by having a gift for mental arithmetic. The story is a doorway into a style of attention, not a substitute for the arguments.

Ramanujan was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in nineteen eighteen and later that year became a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge. These honors recognized achievements that had become impossible for the international mathematical community to ignore. They arrived while his health was severely compromised. In nineteen nineteen he returned to India. He continued doing mathematics, and in early nineteen twenty sent Hardy work concerning what he called mock theta functions. The mathematical understanding of those functions would grow enormously long after his death, showing once again how a compact group of formulas could outlive the original context in which they were produced.

Ramanujan died in April nineteen twenty at the age of thirty-two. His surviving notebooks and loose manuscripts were studied for decades, producing results and new research questions. This posthumous history should not be made into a legend of all-knowing intuition. Some of his entries required correction, and others were already known in related forms. What made the notebooks extraordinary was their density of fertile ideas and the amount of work later researchers found necessary to understand them properly.

His life also raises questions about opportunity. What becomes of a remarkable student's work when the educational system offers little room for someone who fails conventional examinations? Who supplies enough money, social trust, or professional introductions for original research to continue? The answers in Ramanujan's case include his own sustained effort, family and local supporters, the Indian Mathematical Society, his employers and patrons in Madras, and later Hardy, Littlewood, Neville, and other Cambridge colleagues. A history that names only the final famous partnership forgets too much of the human network that made it possible.

A life cannot be reconstructed from a collection of brilliant formulas. Still, the formulas explain why his letters and notebooks stirred such excitement. We can now study one of those mathematical themes—partitions of whole numbers—and follow an accessible part of the thinking he developed with Hardy.

How many different ways can we write the number five as a sum of positive whole numbers, if the order of the pieces does not matter? We can leave it alone as five. We can write four plus one; three plus two; three plus one plus one; two plus two plus one; two plus one plus one plus one; and one plus one plus one plus one plus one. That gives seven ways. Mathematicians call them partitions of five, and they write the number of such partitions as p of five.

The problem may sound like a small recreational puzzle. Yet partitions multiply rapidly in number as the integer grows, and there is no immediately obvious formula for counting them. A hundred is small enough to say aloud but has an astronomical number of different partitions compared with five. How can anyone discover a usable pattern in this explosion of possibilities? And how can a pattern that seems true for many examples become a theorem with a proof?

Return to partitions. There is a way to encode every partition count in one mathematical expression. Imagine a formal variable called q. For every positive integer k, create a geometric series that counts how many copies of k we might use in a partition: zero copies, one copy, two copies, and so on. The terms are one, q to the k, q to twice k, and so forth. Multiply these series for every positive k. When you collect the coefficient of q to the n, you count precisely the partitions of n. Each way to choose how many ones, how many twos, how many threes, and so on corresponds to one partition.

Why is that a useful change? Because multiplying a list of simple series can reveal information about all partition counts at once. The resulting product is called a generating function. It is a bridge between arithmetic and analysis: a counting problem about integers becomes a question about the coefficients of a function. The same function can be studied near its singularities, and those analytic features tell us about how fast the coefficients grow.

Let us check a small case before talking about infinity. We want partitions of four. From the factor corresponding to ones, choose four ones. Or choose two ones and one two. Or two twos. Or one one and one three. Or one four. Those are five possibilities, so p of four is five. The generating function bookkeeping gives exactly that coefficient. We have not magically solved the whole problem. We have encoded it in a way that brings other mathematical methods within reach.

Hardy and Ramanujan developed a powerful asymptotic formula for p of n. Asymptotic means that, as n becomes large, the formula predicts the scale of the answer with a relative error that tends to zero. Its leading term grows roughly like an exponential whose exponent is proportional to the square root of n, divided by a factor proportional to n. That is neither ordinary polynomial growth nor a simple exponential in n. It is a distinctive middle behavior that the elementary list of partitions gives little reason to expect.

What would such an approximation buy us? Suppose we want to know the number of partitions of ten. We could enumerate them and discover the exact answer, forty-two. The leading Hardy–Ramanujan approximation gives a number somewhat larger than forty-two. At ten, it is not yet spectacularly accurate, but that is not a defect in the meaning of an asymptotic theorem. The claim concerns what happens as the input grows. The ratio between the approximation and the true count tends toward one for very large inputs. An asymptotic formula is a guide to large-scale structure, not a promise of a perfectly rounded answer for every small example.

The shape of the formula is worth understanding in words. Its dominant exponential grows like the exponential of a constant times the square root of n. This grows faster than any fixed power of n. But it grows more slowly than an exponential of a positive constant times n. The partition function sits between two familiar classes of growth. That makes it a natural target for a method that combines combinatorial counting with the behavior of analytic functions near singular points.

There is another lesson in the circle method's use of complex numbers. The count p of n is a nonnegative whole number. The generating function that encodes it can be evaluated at complex inputs, meaning numbers with both real and imaginary components. Why go from the integers into the complex plane only to come back again? Because information that is entangled in a coefficient sequence may become more accessible when represented as the behavior of one analytic object. A contour integral can isolate a coefficient, and a careful estimate can show which parts of the contour matter most. Complex analysis is not a decorative trick added to arithmetic. It is an alternative language in which certain counting questions can be attacked systematically.

The mathematical technique they developed is associated with the circle method. The basic picture is to treat a generating function as a complex function and recover its coefficients by integrating around a circle in the complex plane. Different parts of the contour contribute in different ways. A sharp estimate requires understanding where the generating function becomes large and how the contributions near those locations interact. The full proof is difficult, but its conceptual logic is accessible: move from a list of counts to a function, study that function near places where it has pronounced structure, then turn the structure back into information about the counts.

This was not simply an isolated collaboration between two exceptionally talented people. Their circle-method work drew on earlier developments in analytic number theory and inspired later refinements by many mathematicians. For some partition formulas, the asymptotic description can be sharpened into exact convergent series. The historical lineage matters because the mathematical method became a community resource, useful for questions far beyond the original partition problem.

Ramanujan also discovered remarkable congruence patterns among partition numbers. A congruence says that two integers have the same remainder when divided by a given modulus. For example, p of four is five, so its remainder after division by five is zero. Ramanujan found that the partition number of every integer of the form five times n plus four is divisible by five. The pattern does not stop at four; it holds at nine, fourteen, nineteen, and indefinitely onward.

We can test it. The number nine has thirty partitions. Thirty is divisible by five. This does not prove the rule. We might test the first million cases without resolving what happens afterward. The theorem needs an argument showing why the relationship holds for all nonnegative integers n. Related congruences assert that p of seven n plus five is divisible by seven, and p of eleven n plus six is divisible by eleven. These patterns became central objects in the modern theory of modular forms and q-series. The underlying mathematics reveals that the strange arithmetic regularities are connected to transformations of analytic functions, not merely lucky coincidences in a table.

There is a human lesson in the contrast between these examples and the published papers. A mathematician can be excellent at spotting structure and still need help proving a general assertion, or even identifying what a valid general assertion should be. Conversely, a polished formal proof is not a record of every false start and unexpected guess that made discovery possible. Ramanujan's notebooks preserve some of that distance between finding a pattern and placing it inside an established mathematical framework. They contain truths, errors, and statements whose exact intended interpretation needs care.

Return to the seven partitions of five. A small arithmetic count led to a generating function; a generating function led to complex analysis; complex analysis led to an asymptotic formula; and experiments with the coefficients revealed congruences that demanded new explanations. Each step makes a different kind of mathematical claim. An example shows a possibility. A pattern suggests a conjecture. A precise theorem states the conditions. A proof explains why it holds for every case covered by the statement. Ramanujan's life stands near the center of this movement from observation to explanation. Its tragedy was the brevity of his working years. Its enduring achievement was the mathematics that continued to give other people new problems, new techniques, and new reasons to look more closely at numbers.

The partitions and their formulas survived Ramanujan's death. So did his notebooks and the collaborations, institutions and arguments that had made it possible to read them. He was not only a natural talent arriving in Cambridge; he was a working person from colonial India who sought help, learned new methods, experienced loss, and left a record that colleagues continued to study long after his short life ended.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Partition generating function

For \(|q|<1\),
\[
\sum_{n\ge0}p(n)q^n=\prod_{k\ge1}\frac{1}{1-q^k}.
\]
The product encodes multiplicities of each positive summand. Examples: \(p(4)=5\), \(p(5)=7\), \(p(9)=30\), \(p(12)=77\), and \(p(17)=297\).

### Hardy–Ramanujan leading asymptotic

\[
p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt 3}\exp\!\left(\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}\right)\quad(n\to\infty).
\]
The symbol \(\sim\) means the ratio of the two sides tends to one. The full circle-method argument and later exact improvements require much more than this leading term.

### Partition congruences

For all \(n\ge0\),
\[
p(5n+4)\equiv0\pmod 5,\quad
p(7n+5)\equiv0\pmod 7,\quad
p(11n+6)\equiv0\pmod{11}.
\]
These statements concern specific residue classes and cannot be generalized indiscriminately to every modulus.

### The 1729 recollection

\(1729=1^3+12^3=9^3+10^3\). The conversation is remembered by Hardy in later writing and should not be treated as a contemporaneous stenographic transcript. The sums are of **positive** cubes; allowing zero or negative cubes changes the scope of any minimality claim.

### Historical caveats

Do not conflate a lack of conventional academic credentials with a lack of predecessors, friends, or mathematical community. Ramanujan's health history permits several retrospective hypotheses; no confident diagnosis is given in the narration. Source materials include the 1913 letters as published by the AMS and Trinity's historical account, alongside MacTutor's biography.
