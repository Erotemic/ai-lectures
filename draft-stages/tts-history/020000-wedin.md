# Per-Åke Wedin: How do singular subspaces change when a rectangular matrix changes?

Imagine trying to describe how a human knee moves. Several small markers have been attached to a rigid segment, and cameras have recorded their positions at two moments. The measurements have errors. You would like to recover one rotation and one translation that explain the movement as closely as possible. If the markers are nearly arranged along a single straight line, some rotations become hard to distinguish. If the markers surround the segment in a well-spread configuration, the same measurement error produces a much more reliable estimate.

What makes the difference? It is a question about geometry hidden inside a rectangular matrix. A set of observed points determines directions in which the measurements are sensitive, and other directions in which they provide little information. The numbers measuring that sensitivity are called singular values. The associated directions form singular subspaces. When the observations change, those subspaces can turn, and the turn can be much larger than the change in the original data.

Per-Åke Wedin is most frequently encountered through a theorem about that rotation. His nineteen seventy-two paper gave perturbation bounds for singular subspaces of rectangular matrices. But the mathematical path that led him to that result, and the paths he followed afterward, reach far beyond that one theorem. He worked on pseudoinverses and nearly rank-deficient least squares, on algorithms that separate linear from nonlinear unknowns, on constrained optimization, and eventually on the geometry of markers used to reconstruct skeletal motion. That last application brings us back to the question with which we began.

We will follow the mathematics through a single recurring problem. Suppose a matrix represents a measurement process. We know its important directions, then the matrix changes slightly. Which conclusions survive? The answer depends on the size of the change, the separation of the singular values, and precisely which subspace we are trying to follow.

## A rectangular transformation has two sets of directions

Begin with a matrix that has two input coordinates and three output coordinates. The first input direction produces an output of length four along the first output axis. The second produces an output of length one along the second axis. The third output coordinate remains zero. If we send in a vector with two components, the first is multiplied by four and placed on the first output axis; the second is left at its original size and placed on the second output axis.

This matrix treats the two input directions very differently. One is amplified fourfold. The other passes through with unit gain. The singular value decomposition records this behavior with three ingredients. There are right singular vectors, describing the preferred input directions. There are nonnegative singular values, describing their amplification factors. And there are left singular vectors, describing where the amplified inputs land in the output space.

In our example the two right singular vectors are the ordinary horizontal and vertical coordinate directions in the two-dimensional input. Their singular values are four and one. The corresponding left singular vectors are the first and second coordinate directions in the three-dimensional output. The matrix sends each right vector to its paired left vector times its singular value.

Notice the asymmetry between the two sides. The right vectors live in a plane; the left vectors live in three-dimensional space. We could rotate the output coordinate system without changing the input directions at all. We could also rotate the input coordinates while leaving the output axes fixed. This is why a rectangular matrix has two geometries that must be tracked together.

A square symmetric matrix has a special relationship between its input and output coordinates. Its spectral theorem uses one orthonormal family of eigenvectors. For a general rectangular matrix, the left and right families must be distinguished. Even for a square nonsymmetric matrix they need not agree.

If the matrix depends on observations, we need to know which singular directions remain meaningful when those observations are noisy. Wedin's nineteen seventy-two result was designed for precisely this kind of question.

## The direction of a line is different from a particular arrow

Before discussing perturbations, we need a way to measure changes in a direction. Take two unit vectors in a plane. Suppose one lies along the horizontal axis and the other is tilted thirty degrees upward. The angle between their one-dimensional spans is thirty degrees. Whether we point either vector forward or backward does not affect that line. A singular vector is also determined only up to a sign when its singular value is simple, so comparing raw vector coordinates can give a misleading impression of change.

For two lines, we take the acute angle between them. For two subspaces of higher dimension, there can be several angles. These are principal angles. One way to find them is to choose unit directions from the two subspaces that have the largest possible inner product, then continue under orthogonality constraints. Another way is to collect orthonormal bases for each subspace and compute the singular values of the matrix of inner products between their columns. Those singular values are the cosines of the principal angles.

Why use the sine of an angle in perturbation theory? Return to our line tilted by thirty degrees. A unit vector along the tilted line has a component perpendicular to the original horizontal line. That perpendicular component has length one half. It is the sine of thirty degrees. Thus the sine measures precisely how much a unit direction leaks outside the original subspace.

The same construction works in many dimensions. Take a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for a proposed subspace, and project those columns onto the perpendicular complement of another subspace. The singular values of the resulting matrix are the sines of the principal angles. If they are all zero, the subspaces agree. If one of them is one, a direction in one subspace is perpendicular to the other.

For a collection of angles, we can combine their squared sines and take a square root. That is the Frobenius measure of subspace rotation. It pays attention to the movement of every independent direction. A separate operator-norm version records the worst angle. These different measures answer different numerical questions, and a theorem must specify which one it controls.

## A small change can rotate a direction a long way

Consider a two-by-two diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are both one. Every input direction is amplified by exactly one. There is no unique dominant singular line. Any orthonormal pair can serve as the singular basis. Now add a small positive number to the first diagonal entry. The horizontal direction becomes the strongest one. Instead add that same small number to the second diagonal entry. The vertical direction becomes the strongest one.

The two changed matrices can be as close as we wish. Nevertheless their leading singular lines are perpendicular. The difficulty comes from the original tie between the singular values, not from any dramatic change in matrix entries. When singular values coincide, their associated individual directions need not have a unique identity.

Suppose instead that the original matrix amplifies the first direction by four and the second by one. The two singular values are separated by three. A perturbation much smaller than three can change the dominant direction, but it cannot so easily exchange it with the weak one. A genuine gap makes a singular subspace followable.

A group of tied singular values can still define a stable larger space. Imagine three dominant singular values packed close together and a fourth much smaller. Small perturbations may exchange vectors within the dominant triple, while leaving the three-dimensional span relatively stable. The geometric object to compare is then that entire span. A bound for three individual vectors would ask for information the data do not determine.

Here is the central distinction. Singular values tell us about amplification. Singular vectors give a convenient coordinate system. Singular subspaces represent the pieces of that coordinate system that can remain identifiable as a group. This last object is the one perturbation theory can often protect.

## Numerical analysis in Sweden

Wedin's professional history belongs to the development of Swedish numerical analysis as an identifiable research field. His full name appears in Swedish university and government records as Nils Per-Åke Wedin. The official university directory from nineteen seventy-eight places him at Umeå University, in information processing with a specialization in numerical analysis. A later university record identifies him as a docent and, later still, the university's computing-science directory lists him as professor emeritus.

The chronology of his papers tells us something more specific about his research environment. In nineteen sixty-nine, a technical report on pseudoinverses of perturbed matrices appeared from the Department of Computer Science at Lund University. His nineteen seventy-two paper on singular-subspace perturbations was published in BIT, the Nordic numerical mathematics journal. In that same period he circulated a report from Lund on the geometry of nonlinear least squares. By the latter nineteen-seventies, his work on least-squares algorithms was associated with Umeå. These dates place his early research in the era when solving a matrix problem meant designing a practical algorithm and asking how trustworthy its output would be on an actual computer.

At the time, large numerical problems were increasingly being treated by general-purpose linear algebra routines. Algorithms based on orthogonal transformations could compute least-squares solutions while controlling the growth of rounding error. The singular value decomposition was a particularly revealing factorization: it could expose weak directions and rank deficiency. But calculating an accurate decomposition and interpreting it correctly were separate tasks. A program could return an extremely accurate answer to a matrix whose weak directions were themselves determined poorly by the original measurements.

In nineteen seventy, Chandler Davis and William Kahan published a major treatment of the rotation of eigenspaces under perturbation. Their work concerned self-adjoint operators and the separation of spectral groups. Wedin's paper, published two years later, addressed the paired left and right subspaces needed for singular value decompositions. The abstract of his nineteen seventy-two paper explicitly identifies the Davis–Kahan sine-theta theorem as a special case of the more general bounds he was developing.

This is a productive way to understand the historical relationship. A perturbation of a square self-adjoint matrix moves spectral subspaces. A perturbation of a rectangular measurement matrix moves two linked families of singular subspaces. Wedin's analysis gave the latter situation its own working estimates, closely connected to least squares and the pseudoinverse.

## Watching the left and right sides move differently

Return to a two-by-two diagonal matrix with singular values four and one. Now insert a small positive entry above the second diagonal position, in the first row and second column. The new matrix sends the first input direction to four times the first output direction. It sends the second input direction mostly to the second output axis, but with a small added component along the first.

That extra component changes both singular bases. But it does not change them by the same angle. The left singular basis is determined by multiplying the matrix by its transpose. The right singular basis comes from multiplying the transpose by the matrix. These two symmetric products are related, but their off-diagonal entries and diagonal gaps can be different.

Set the added matrix entry to zero point six. The leading left singular direction moves by about two point two degrees. The leading right singular direction moves by about nine point one degrees. So the right direction has turned about four times as far in this example. The original matrix change had a norm of zero point six, and the original gap between the two singular values was three.

This example is worth keeping in mind. If a signal-processing application cares about the input combinations encoded by right singular vectors, a theorem that discusses only left singular vectors does not answer its question. If an application cares about the image of a measurement process, left singular vectors may be the priority. Wedin's central estimate binds the two rotations together while respecting their separate spaces.

There is a deeper point in the calculation. The singular values themselves have barely changed: the largest is just over four, and the smaller is just under one. Looking only at the singular values would conceal the rotation of the coordinate directions. To describe stability we need information about both the spectrum and the subspaces.

We can even hear why the two angles differ. To find the left singular vectors, form the matrix times its transpose. In this example, the off-diagonal entry of that symmetric product is zero point six. To find the right singular vectors, form the transpose times the matrix. Its off-diagonal entry is four times zero point six, or two point four. The two products have almost the same separation between their diagonal entries, but one has much stronger coupling between its coordinate axes. The stronger coupling makes the leading right eigenvector rotate farther. Wedin's result is useful because it handles both of these rotations without pretending they must be equal.

## The residual is what the proposed directions fail to explain

How can we measure the error of a proposed singular subspace without knowing the correct one? Suppose a numerical algorithm gives a collection of approximate right singular vectors, a collection of approximate left singular vectors, and a list of proposed singular values. We can test how closely the matrix sends each proposed right vector to the corresponding proposed left vector times its proposed singular value.

Subtract the predicted output from the actual output. What remains is a residual. Call it the right-to-left residual. Then reverse the roles: transpose the matrix, apply it to the proposed left vectors, and subtract the proposed right vectors times the same singular values. The result is the left-to-right residual.

Why are two residuals necessary? Because a singular pair satisfies two equations. The matrix sends a right singular vector to the left one, scaled by the singular value. Its transpose sends that left vector back to the right one, with the same scale. Satisfying only one equation leaves room for error in the other half of the geometry.

Take the original matrix to be A and the changed matrix to be A plus E. Suppose the proposed singular vectors are exact for the changed matrix. If we test them against A, both residuals are generated entirely by the perturbation E. One has the form of E applied to a proposed right vector, with an overall minus sign. The other has E transposed applied to a proposed left vector, also with a minus sign. As a result, each residual is bounded by the size of E.

A perturbation bound can therefore be expressed in terms of residuals when those are known, or in terms of E when all we know is the difference between two matrices. The residual form has a practical advantage. Some perturbations are large in directions irrelevant to our selected singular subspace. Their total norm can exaggerate the error in the directions we care about. Residuals let the estimate see the part of the change that actually acts on the chosen vectors.

Now we need a denominator. A small residual alone cannot guarantee a small rotation: our example with tied singular values already showed why. The missing ingredient is separation from the other singular values.

## Wedin's sine-theta bound

Imagine that the proposed left and right singular vectors belong to a selected group of singular values of the changed matrix. The original matrix has another group, representing the directions outside the part we are trying to follow. Assume every selected proposed singular value is separated by at least a positive amount, call it delta, from every original singular value in that unwanted group.

For a rectangular matrix with more rows than columns, we also need to keep those selected singular values away from zero. That second requirement will become especially important in a moment. With these separation conditions, a Frobenius version of Wedin's result says that the combined squared sine errors of the left and right subspaces are bounded by the combined squared residual sizes, divided by delta squared. Taking square roots, the combined rotation is no greater than the combined residual size divided by delta.

In plain language, error is controlled by defect over separation. The numerator asks how badly the approximate singular equations are satisfied. The denominator asks how easily the selected singular directions could be confused with directions outside the selected group. A small numerator and a large denominator yield a useful certificate.

Here is how the proof works at its simplest. Pick one proposed singular pair and one original unwanted singular pair. Measure how much the proposed left vector points along the unwanted left vector. Do the same for the right vectors. These are the two unknown overlap numbers. Project the two residual equations onto the unwanted singular vectors. The result is a little system of two linear equations in those two overlaps.

In that system the selected singular value is paired with the unwanted singular value. Solving it introduces denominators equal to their difference and their sum. Since singular values are nonnegative, the sum causes no greater difficulty than the difference. If the difference stays above delta, the combined overlap cannot exceed the combined projected residual divided by delta. Summing the squares across all selected and unwanted directions produces the Frobenius bound.

We have just crossed the main conceptual bridge. The theorem turns a geometric rotation question into many two-by-two linear systems, one for each interaction between selected and unwanted singular directions. The spectral separation ensures each small system can be solved stably. The residuals supply the forcing terms. The principal-angle sines collect the resulting overlaps.

The square-root-of-sums form of the estimate deserves attention. Suppose the left subspace turns a little and the right one turns a little. The theorem controls their combined geometric displacement. One can then bound either side individually, but the combined result describes the structure of the proof more accurately.

## The zero directions that a rectangular matrix hides

A three-by-two matrix has an output dimension that the two input directions cannot fill. Even when its two singular values are positive, there remains a nonzero output direction perpendicular to its entire range. That perpendicular direction belongs to the nullspace of the transpose. In the square block-matrix picture, it behaves as an additional zero eigenvalue. George Stewart later used the memorable term ghost singular values for these extra zeros.

This is more than a bookkeeping concern. Return to the three-by-two example, but make its weak singular value equal to a small positive number, epsilon. The first input direction produces an output of length epsilon along the first output axis. The second input direction produces an output of length one along the second axis. The third output coordinate is zero.

Now add epsilon to the third output coordinate of the first column. The matrix has changed by norm epsilon, which can be arbitrarily small. Yet the weak left singular vector has changed from the first output axis to the diagonal halfway between the first and third axes. That is a rotation of forty-five degrees. The corresponding weak right singular vector has not moved at all: it is still the first input coordinate direction.

What happened? The weak output vector had length epsilon. Adding a perpendicular component of the same length was enough to turn its direction halfway toward a previously unused output axis. The weak singular value changed only from epsilon to the square root of two times epsilon. Its separation from the strong singular value, near one, remained large. But its separation from zero was only of order epsilon. The neglected zero direction made the large rotation possible.

This example appears in Stewart's later survey of singular-value perturbations, where it illustrates the importance of the zero-separation condition in Wedin's theorem. It also sharpens our interpretation of a singular-value gap. Merely comparing positive singular values can miss an essential geometric competitor. When the range of a rectangular matrix is involved, zero can be the dangerous neighbor.

There is a useful asymmetry here. The left vector rotates dramatically; the right vector stays fixed. In some problems we can obtain a stronger estimate for one side by examining a complementary singular subspace. In others, the joint bound is the appropriate object. The geometry of the input and output spaces determines which estimate answers the practical question.

## Least squares and the cost of a weak direction

Consider an overdetermined system. We have more measured equations than unknown parameters, and no parameter choice makes every equation exactly true. The usual least-squares solution chooses the parameters that minimize the sum of the squared residuals. The singular value decomposition explains how that answer is assembled.

Project the measured output onto a left singular direction. Divide that coefficient by the corresponding singular value. Place the result along its paired right singular direction. Do this for each nonzero singular value and add the contributions. The resulting rule is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.

If a singular value is four, dividing by it shrinks error in that direction by a factor of four. If a singular value is one ten-thousandth, dividing by it magnifies a measurement error by ten thousand. A tiny residual in the data can become a large change in the estimated parameters. That is the numerical danger of a weak singular direction.

A common response is to truncate the singular value decomposition. Decide that some singular values are too small to trust, discard their associated components, and solve using the remaining ones. But once we truncate, we are no longer following individual weak vectors. We are selecting a subspace of retained directions. Its stability under perturbation becomes exactly the sort of question Wedin had studied.

His original nineteen seventy-two abstract names overdetermined linear systems and rank-deficient approximations as applications. His nineteen seventy-three papers continued directly into perturbation theory for pseudoinverses and the nearly rank-deficient least-squares problem. These were sustained research interests, not a later mathematical interpretation imposed on a theorem with a different original purpose.

Try a simple numerical thought experiment. Suppose two singular values are ten and one hundredth. Measurement error of size one thousandth may be small relative to ten, but is a tenth of the weak singular value. If you invert that weak direction, the resulting coefficient error can be comparable with a substantial fraction of the parameter itself. If you discard it, you lose information about that parameter direction. Choosing a numerical rank is therefore a decision about the balance between fitting the observations and amplifying their errors.

The cutoff also requires geometric care. When a set of singular values lies close to the cutoff, a small matrix perturbation can alter which singular vectors are retained. A gap around the cutoff gives us a stable selected subspace. Without one, the numerical model has an identifiable ambiguity, and a precise perturbation estimate should reveal it.

There is also a difference between solving the least-squares problem and choosing a safe formulation of the computation. One familiar derivation multiplies an overdetermined system by the transpose of its matrix, producing the so-called normal equations. Those equations are mathematically correct when the columns are independent. But the singular values of the resulting square matrix are the squares of the original singular values. A ratio of ten to one in the original data becomes a ratio of one hundred to one in the normal equations. Squaring the condition number can make a weak direction harder to resolve numerically.

Orthogonal factorizations such as QR and the singular value decomposition provide alternative routes. A QR factorization transforms the observations while preserving lengths, then solves a triangular system. The SVD goes further by exposing individual strong and weak directions. These methods may all minimize the same mathematical residual, yet their numerical behavior and the diagnostic information they provide can differ. The question of which computation to trust was a central concern throughout Wedin's work.

In work with Mårten Gulliksson, this concern extended to constrained and weighted systems. Some observations carry larger weights because they are regarded as more precise. Some equations may be enforced as exact constraints. A numerical method should respect those distinctions rather than treat all equations as interchangeable. Their modified QR approach was designed to handle weighted structure directly, and the related work examined rounding error and iterative refinement. The geometric theme is still visible: choose transformations that respect the structure of the problem, then analyze what errors they can amplify.

## From linear equations to nonlinear fitting

Wedin's research did not stop with linear least squares. Many scientific models depend nonlinearly on some parameters while remaining linear in others. Imagine fitting an exponential decay curve to observations. The decay rate enters inside an exponential, so its effect on the model is nonlinear. The overall amplitude multiplies that exponential and enters linearly.

If we try to optimize the amplitude and decay rate as two equal unknowns, a generic nonlinear optimizer must search in both directions. But for any chosen decay rate, the best amplitude can be found by an ordinary one-variable least-squares calculation. That suggests a more economical procedure: solve the easy linear part exactly at each trial value of the nonlinear parameter, then adjust the nonlinear parameter.

We can write the idea without a page of equations. Suppose the predicted observations are an amplitude times an exponential shape. Choose a candidate decay rate. Calculate the exponential shape at every observation time. The best amplitude is the inner product of that shape with the data, divided by the squared length of the shape. Substitute the resulting amplitude back into the residual. Now the remaining problem has only the decay rate as its free variable.

This is an example of separable nonlinear least squares. Gene Golub and Victor Pereyra developed influential variable-projection methods for problems of this sort. In nineteen eighty, Axel Ruhe and Wedin published *Algorithms for Separable Nonlinear Least Squares Problems* in SIAM Review. They analyzed Gauss–Newton-type methods that separate variable groups, including a version for the linear-nonlinear case. Their paper compared convergence properties and computational costs and gave an algorithm designed to exploit that structure.

Here again Wedin's interests were tied to the numerical consequences of geometry. The columns of the model's design matrix may become nearly dependent for certain parameter values. Then estimating the linear coefficients can be ill-conditioned. The pseudoinverse and singular subspaces enter the nonlinear problem through the linear subproblem. A method that discards this conditioning information can perform poorly even when its outer iteration is mathematically plausible.

Wedin also worked with Per Lindström on a nineteen eighty-four line-search algorithm for nonlinear least squares. Later collaborations at Umeå explored weighted and constrained least squares, modifications of QR decompositions, regularization, and parameter estimation in systems of differential equations. These papers show a long research program: use the structure of the problem to choose the numerical method, and analyze which features of the solution are sensitive to perturbation.

## A rotation reconstructed from markers

Let us now return to the skeletal-motion problem. Imagine three or more markers attached rigidly to a segment. We measure their positions before and after the movement. Ideally there is one rotation and one translation taking every first position exactly to its second position. Measurement noise spoils that exact correspondence. We seek the rigid motion giving the smallest total squared discrepancy.

The translation is easier to remove than the rotation. Find the average position of the markers before the movement and the average position after it. Subtract the appropriate average from each measured point. Now both point clouds have centroid zero, and the translation has disappeared from the rotation calculation.

Next assemble a small matrix from products of the centered coordinates in the two point sets. This matrix expresses how the two configurations are correlated. Compute its singular value decomposition. The orthogonal factors give a candidate rotation aligning the clouds. If a proper rotation, rather than a reflection, is required, we adjust the sign of the final axis as needed. The translation can then be recovered from the two centroids.

So far this sounds like a straightforward application of an established algorithm. The real question is how the placement of the markers affects reliability. If all markers lie almost on one line, twisting the segment about that line changes their positions very little. The measurement data cannot reliably determine that twist. If the markers are placed in a wider spatial configuration, the same twist moves points more visibly, making it easier to distinguish from noise.

In nineteen ninety-three Inge Söderkvist and Wedin published *Determining the Movements of the Skeleton Using Well-Configured Markers* in the Journal of Biomechanics. They formulated the rigid-motion fit using singular value decomposition and developed a geometrically interpretable condition number that depends on the marker configuration. Their analysis addressed the practical question of where markers should be placed if the inferred skeletal movement is to be trustworthy.

This is a direct meeting of abstract matrix geometry and physical experiment. Singular values can diagnose directions in which a measurement arrangement contains little information. Condition numbers quantify how errors in the observed coordinates affect the recovered motion. A theorem about the stability of numerical directions helps explain why an apparently small observational error may have a large effect on a reconstructed movement.

Consider the marker geometry one step further. Suppose every marker lies exactly on a rod-shaped axis. Rotate the rod about that axis. Every marker remains where it was. There is no measurement, however accurate, that can determine this twist from those marker positions alone. Now move one marker off the axis. Twisting the rod moves that marker around a circle, giving the measurement a way to detect the rotation. Move it farther away, and the same angular twist produces a larger displacement. The location of the markers has changed how well conditioned the inference is.

The marker-placement problem also explains the distinction between accuracy and conditioning. Even a perfect computer algorithm cannot recover a rotation that the markers fail to identify. Conversely, an informative marker configuration can still be undermined by a poor numerical method. Wedin and Söderkvist's analysis provided a way to judge the information geometry and the resulting sensitivity, using the algebra of singular values.

The later work carried forward the concern that animated the earlier perturbation papers: distinguish the strength of measured information from the reliability of the directions reconstructed from it.

## An academic life seen through collaborations

The record of Wedin's career becomes particularly concrete in his later collaborations. With Ruhe he worked on separable optimization. With Lindström he addressed line searches. With Inge Söderkvist he studied rigid-body motion from measured landmarks. With Mårten Gulliksson and other colleagues he published on weighted and constrained least squares and numerical conditioning. With Lennart Edsberg he worked on numerical parameter estimation for differential-equation systems, where solving a model repeatedly is part of fitting its parameters.

University documents from Umeå identify him as a professor in computing science and later as professor emeritus. In November two thousand five, Umeå University hosted a conference on least squares and optimization in his honor. Its title is revealing. The occasion recognized a much broader body of work than one perturbation inequality: least squares, optimization, and the connections between them.

In nineteen ninety-three Mårten Gulliksson completed a doctoral dissertation at Umeå titled *Algorithms for Overdetermined Systems of Equations*. The dissertation assembled work on weighted QR factorizations, constrained nonlinear least squares, and iterative refinement. It identified Wedin as Gulliksson's adviser and thanked him for ideas, encouragement, and support. The thesis also documented their shared publications and the wider group of colleagues, including Inge Söderkvist, working on related numerical questions. The record gives a view of Wedin as a research adviser whose mathematical program continued through the work of students and collaborators.

We should also be careful with historical credit. The singular value decomposition has a long history, with substantial contributions made before Wedin's work. Principal angles between subspaces have their own older development. Davis and Kahan had already established self-adjoint subspace perturbation results. Wedin's nineteen seventy-two contribution lay in deriving perturbation estimates suited to the coordinated left and right singular subspaces of a rectangular matrix, with attention to least-squares applications and rank deficiency. Later authors, including G. W. Stewart, refined the exposition and showed how to use the results in numerical practice.

The phrase Wedin's theorem has endured because it names a useful tool. The surrounding papers reveal the mathematical career that made the tool possible.

## What the theorem lets us conclude

We can now answer the opening question about noisy measurements. A matrix may encode a signal, a least-squares model, or the geometry of markers attached to a body. Its singular vectors describe input and output directions associated with different strengths of response. If a group of singular values is separated from its competitors, then small residual errors constrain the rotation of the corresponding left and right singular subspaces. The principal-angle sines quantify that rotation.

The estimate also tells us when confidence is not justified. If singular values cluster across the boundary of the selected group, the denominator becomes small. If a rectangular matrix contains a weak output direction near the unused zero subspace, that direction can rotate greatly under a tiny perturbation. If a least-squares solution divides by a tiny singular value, small measurement error can become a large parameter error. These are distinct manifestations of insufficient separation.

Wedin's lasting contribution is a way to turn those concerns into mathematics. We can test how well proposed singular vectors satisfy their paired equations. We can identify the relevant spectral gap, including zero when the geometry requires it. We can then bound the rotation of the subspaces that the computation is supposed to recover. The same way of thinking carries from a perturbation theorem in nineteen seventy-two to inverse problems, optimization algorithms, and the reconstruction of motion from imperfect observations.

A reliable numerical conclusion is one whose geometry survives the error present in its inputs. Wedin gave a precise framework for judging when that happens.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Spaces, singular pairs, and residuals

Let \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), \(m\ge n\), and \(\widehat A=A+E\). Select \(r\) singular pairs of \(\widehat A\):
\[
\widehat A\widehat V_1=\widehat U_1\widehat\Sigma_1,\qquad
\widehat A^{\mathsf T}\widehat U_1=\widehat V_1\widehat\Sigma_1,
\qquad \widehat U_1^{\mathsf T}\widehat U_1=\widehat V_1^{\mathsf T}\widehat V_1=I_r.
\]
Test these pairs against the original matrix \(A\):
\[
R=A\widehat V_1-\widehat U_1\widehat\Sigma_1=-E\widehat V_1,
\qquad
S=A^{\mathsf T}\widehat U_1-\widehat V_1\widehat\Sigma_1=-E^{\mathsf T}\widehat U_1.
\]
The minus signs have no effect on any norm. For an approximate singular triple that is **not exact** for \(\widehat A\), use these residual definitions directly; \(R=-E\widehat V_1\) and \(S=-E^{\mathsf T}\widehat U_1\) no longer hold automatically.

Partition an SVD of \(A\) by selected and unwanted directions:
\[
A=[U_1\ U_2\ U_0]
  \begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_1&0\\0&\Sigma_2\\0&0\end{bmatrix}
  [V_1\ V_2]^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Here \(U_0\) spans the additional left nullspace when \(m>n\). Zero must be included among the unwanted spectral competitors when estimating angles of the selected **left** subspace.

### 2. A Frobenius form of Wedin's sin-theta bound

Define the separation
\[
\delta:=\min\left\{
  \min_{i,j}\left|\widehat\sigma_i-\sigma_j\right|,
  \min_i\widehat\sigma_i\right\}>0,
\quad \sigma_j\in\sigma(\Sigma_2).
\]
If \(\Sigma_2\) is empty, omit the first minimum. For fixed equal-dimensional selected left and right subspaces, let \(\Phi\) and \(\Theta\) be their principal-angle matrices. A standard Wedin bound is
\[
\boxed{\left(\|\sin\Phi\|_F^2+\|\sin\Theta\|_F^2\right)^{1/2}
\le\frac{\left(\|R\|_F^2+\|S\|_F^2\right)^{1/2}}{\delta}.}
\]
The notation is the residual-based Frobenius version of the 1972 result (see Stewart, 1990, §7). For the exact perturbed singular pairs, \(\|R\|_F,\|S\|_F\le\|E\|_F\). The bound is deliberately **not** claimed with the gap between positive singular values alone; the lower bound on selected singular values protects against additional zero directions.

To see the mechanism, let \((u_j,v_j,\sigma_j)\) be an unwanted singular pair of \(A\) and \((\widehat u_i,\widehat v_i,\widehat\sigma_i)\) a selected approximate pair. Write
\[
x=u_j^{\mathsf T}\widehat u_i,\quad y=v_j^{\mathsf T}\widehat v_i,
\quad r=u_j^{\mathsf T}R_i,\quad s=v_j^{\mathsf T}S_i.
\]
Project the two residual equations to obtain
\[
\sigma_j y-\widehat\sigma_i x=r,\qquad
\sigma_j x-\widehat\sigma_i y=s.
\]
Their coefficient matrix has eigenvalues \(-\widehat\sigma_i\pm\sigma_j\); its smallest absolute eigenvalue is \(|\widehat\sigma_i-\sigma_j|\). Therefore \(x^2+y^2\le(r^2+s^2)/\delta^2\). For a left nullspace vector \(u_0\), \(u_0^{\mathsf T}A=0\), hence \(-\widehat\sigma_i u_0^{\mathsf T}\widehat u_i=u_0^{\mathsf T}R_i\). Squaring and summing all orthogonal components gives the stated bound.

For equal-dimensional subspaces, \(\|\sin\Phi\|_F=\|U_{\perp}^{\mathsf T}\widehat U_1\|_F\), with analogous expression for the right side. These are geometrical leakage measures, invariant under a change of orthonormal bases.

### 3. Explicit two-by-two asymmetry (editorial calculation)

\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\widehat A=\begin{bmatrix}4&t\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad t=0.6.
\]
Then
\[
\widehat A\widehat A^{\mathsf T}=
\begin{bmatrix}16+t^2&t\\t&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\widehat A^{\mathsf T}\widehat A=
\begin{bmatrix}16&4t\\4t&1+t^2\end{bmatrix}.
\]
For the principal top eigenvectors, \(\tan 2\alpha=2t/(15+t^2)\) on the left and \(\tan 2\beta=8t/(15-t^2)\) on the right. Numerically,
\[
\widehat\sigma\approx(4.04764152,\ 0.98822981),\quad
\alpha\approx 0.03898332\ \mathrm{rad}=2.2335^{\circ},\quad
\beta\approx 0.15841224\ \mathrm{rad}=9.0764^{\circ}.
\]
Thus \(\sin\alpha\approx0.03897344\), \(\sin\beta\approx0.15775053\); the combined sine is approximately \(0.16249357\). The matrix change has operator and Frobenius norm \(0.6\). This example is about left/right asymmetry and is *not* a sharpness claim for Wedin's theorem.

### 4. Small singular value versus a rectangular zero direction

Stewart (1990, §7) gives, for \(0<\varepsilon\ll1\),
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\widehat A=\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon&0\\0&1\\\varepsilon&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\|\widehat A-A\|_2=\varepsilon.
\]
The weak right singular vector remains \(e_1\in\mathbb R^2\). The left weak singular vector turns from \(e_1\in\mathbb R^3\) to \((e_1+e_3)/\sqrt2\), an angle of \(\pi/4\). The corresponding singular value changes from \(\varepsilon\) to \(\sqrt2\varepsilon\), while the other singular value stays at one. Separation from the zero direction is therefore decisive for the weak left singular vector.

### 5. From pseudoinverse to nonlinear elimination

For a full-column-rank \(A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\),
\[
A^+=V\Sigma^{-1}U^{\mathsf T},\qquad
\|A^+\|_2=1/\sigma_{\min}(A).
\]
For a rank-deficient matrix, \(\Sigma^+\) reciprocates the **positive** singular values and leaves zeros at zero. Truncated SVD replaces reciprocals below a chosen cutoff by zero, trading bias against noise amplification. Stability of the retained subspace depends on separation across that cutoff.

For separable nonlinear least squares, a simple exponential model is \(y_i\approx a\exp(-\lambda t_i)\). Set \(f_i(\lambda)=\exp(-\lambda t_i)\). Then
\[
a_*(\lambda)=\frac{f(\lambda)^{\mathsf T}y}{f(\lambda)^{\mathsf T}f(\lambda)},\qquad
\min_{a,\lambda}\|af(\lambda)-y\|_2^2
=\min_\lambda\|a_*(\lambda)f(\lambda)-y\|_2^2.
\]
This illustrates the parameter separation discussed by Ruhe and Wedin (1980); it is an elementary example, not a verbatim algorithm from that paper.

### 6. Rigid marker configurations and Procrustes rotation

Given matched points \(x_i,y_i\in\mathbb R^3\), center them to obtain \(X=[x_i-\bar x]\) and \(Y=[y_i-\bar y]\). The least-squares proper rigid motion minimizes \(\|RX-Y\|_F^2\) over \(R\in SO(3)\), with translation \(t=\bar y-R\bar x\). If \(YX^{\mathsf T}=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}\), one minimizer is
\[
R=U\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\det(UV^{\mathsf T}))V^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Degenerate or nearly collinear marker configurations leave rotations poorly determined; the sensitivity to perturbation of the landmark locations is controlled by the configuration geometry. The 1993 Söderkvist–Wedin paper studies marker placement through a condition number for this recovery problem. Distinguish conditioning of the physical marker geometry from numerical stability of an SVD algorithm.
