# Per-Åke Wedin: How do singular subspaces change when a rectangular matrix changes?

Suppose a rectangular matrix maps many input coordinates to fewer output measurements. Its most important singular directions describe the combinations of inputs and outputs that are strongly coupled. A small perturbation to the matrix raises two questions: how much do the left singular directions move, and how much do the right singular directions move?

Per-Åke Wedin's 1972 paper Perturbation Bounds in Connection with Singular Value Decomposition addresses this setting. It belongs to the development of matrix perturbation theory for data that are naturally rectangular. The result is commonly called Wedin's sine-theta theorem, and it parallels aspects of the Davis–Kahan theory for invariant subspaces of Hermitian matrices.

A singular value decomposition gives two coordinated families of directions. Right singular vectors live in the input space. Left singular vectors live in the output space. The matrix sends a right singular vector to its corresponding left singular vector scaled by the singular value. The spaces and their dimensions need not be the same.

Take a matrix with two columns. Suppose the first column points mostly to the first output coordinate and has a much larger length than the second column. The dominant right singular direction is the input combination that receives the largest amplification; the dominant left singular direction is where that amplified vector lands. If the dominant and next singular values are well separated, a small perturbation constrains how much the corresponding singular subspaces can rotate.

The spectral gap is essential. If two singular values nearly coincide, a small change to the matrix can substantially exchange their singular vectors, even if the two-dimensional span of the pair remains stable. As with eigenvectors, an individual direction can be more fragile than a cluster subspace.

One way to connect this with self-adjoint theory is to build a larger symmetric block matrix with the rectangular matrix in an off-diagonal corner and its transpose in the other. The positive and negative eigenvalues of that block matrix are the singular values with signs. But extracting the desired left and right subspace bounds still requires careful accounting of their geometry.

Wedin's place in the story is a change of setting. Spectral perturbation is not limited to square symmetric matrices. In data analysis, inverse problems, and numerical linear algebra, the relevant stable objects are often the left and right singular subspaces of a rectangular transformation.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), define
\(\mathcal H(A)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&A\\A^*&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
Its nonzero eigenvalues are the signed singular values of \(A\).
Wedin (1972), *Perturbation Bounds in Connection with Singular Value
Decomposition*, BIT 12, 99–111, DOI 10.1007/BF01932678.
