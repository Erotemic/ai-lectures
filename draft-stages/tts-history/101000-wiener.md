# 101000 - Norbert Wiener: Probability, feedback, and the responsibility of a mathematician

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** the burdens of early celebrity, a complicated father-son education, European mathematical apprenticeships, MIT, Brownian motion and harmonic analysis, wartime prediction, feedback and cybernetics, collaboration, and the social responsibilities of technology. **Background:** probability, signals and feedback are introduced through spoken examples.

In the middle of the twentieth century, Norbert Wiener came to think that a machine and a living creature could sometimes be understood through the same question: how does either one respond when the world changes? A thermostat measures temperature and adjusts a furnace. A person watches a ball approach and moves a hand to catch it. An aircraft-control device measures an approaching target and must decide what to do before the target reaches the predicted position. These systems differ enormously in purpose and complexity, but all involve information, prediction, and correction. Wiener called the broader study of such relationships cybernetics. That word eventually traveled much farther than its original mathematical setting. To understand where it came from, we need to begin not with a mechanical brain, but with a child whose own education had become a public experiment.

Norbert Wiener was born in Columbia, Missouri, in November eighteen ninety-four. His father, Leo Wiener, was a scholar of languages who had emigrated from the Russian Empire and eventually taught Slavic languages at Harvard. His mother, Bertha Kahn Wiener, came from a family with its own educational and cultural expectations. Their household valued books, languages, and relentless study. Leo was not a passive spectator of his son's education. He directed it with energy that admirers called remarkable and that the son would later describe as emotionally demanding. Norbert learned quickly, but the speed of his progress should not erase how much of his childhood was structured by adults who regarded him as an exceptional pupil.

His family lived in Massachusetts, and after home schooling he attended high school in Ayer. He entered Tufts College when he was eleven and graduated in nineteen oh nine, still a teenager. Newspapers liked the story of the prodigy. A degree earned so early seemed to offer proof that academic ability could be accelerated almost without limit. But there is a difference between knowing enough to pass examinations and being ready to live as an independent scholar. Wiener's later memoirs suggest a young man whose intellectual range had expanded faster than his confidence about people and institutions. Reading his life only as a sequence of precocious triumphs misses the insecurities and the work of finding a subject he could make his own.

He experimented with several fields after Tufts. Biology did not hold him. Philosophy, logic, and mathematics began to seem more fertile. He studied at Harvard and Cornell and returned to Harvard for a doctorate in mathematical logic. He completed it in nineteen thirteen, at the age of eighteen. The dissertation concerned relations and ideas associated with Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead's logical foundations of mathematics. That is a very different starting point from the field later named after him. It was an education in precision: what can we define, what follows from which assumptions, and how does a formal expression represent a claim about the world?

The next stage of education was international. Wiener crossed the Atlantic to Cambridge in England, where Russell advised him to strengthen his mathematics and he encountered G. H. Hardy. At Göttingen he attended lectures associated with David Hilbert and Edmund Landau. The moves were interrupted by the approach and outbreak of the First World War. To study with the best teachers was to join a circulating community, but the war showed that the same routes between universities depended on political conditions that could disappear in days. Wiener learned that mathematics was not only solitary labor. Ideas were sharpened through conversations, arguments, and unexpected connections between fields.

Getting a secure job proved harder than collecting degrees. In the years around the war he taught philosophy, did industrial work, and tried journalism and editorial employment. He worked for a time on calculations connected to ballistics at the Aberdeen Proving Ground. None of these activities made him a public authority on machines. They did, however, expose him to practical systems in which measurement was imperfect and where a result had to arrive in time to be useful. In nineteen nineteen he joined the mathematics faculty at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The institution was still establishing the balance between theoretical science and technical instruction that would help define its later reputation. Wiener found a professional home there, though neither his research nor his temperament fitted neatly inside a single department's conventional boundaries.

His first great research achievement at MIT concerned what mathematicians call Brownian motion. Watch a tiny particle suspended in fluid under a microscope. Its path appears to jitter unpredictably. The visible disorder arises from countless molecular collisions too small and numerous to track individually. Physicists, including Einstein, had shown how statistical laws might describe the movement. Wiener attacked the mathematical problem of describing entire random paths rigorously. If each position is uncertain, how can we speak about probabilities of whole trajectories? How can we distinguish a typical irregular path from a special one, and how can infinite collections of possibilities be handled consistently?

Wiener's construction did not assert that a physical particle follows the mathematical model in every microscopic detail. Instead it created a mathematical space of continuous paths, equipped with a probability law that captures the random-walk limit. A path can be continuous while still being so jagged that it has no ordinary velocity at any instant. That difference between continuous motion and differentiable motion is conceptually powerful. A person looking at a smooth line on paper tends to imagine a tangent. Brownian motion teaches that continuity by itself does not guarantee one. Later probability theory gave this structure a central place; the probability measure on such paths still bears Wiener's name. Its technical legacy stretches well beyond the mechanical imagery sometimes attached to cybernetics.

The construction of a measure on paths required a new kind of patience about infinite-dimensional questions. A random variable may be described by a single number, but a trajectory is an entire function of time. If we define probability only for finitely many observations, we have not yet explained what it means to talk about a whole path. Wiener gave a rigorous setting in which events concerning paths could be assigned probabilities. The questions inspired later work by Kolmogorov and many others on stochastic processes. A common classroom story begins with Einstein's account of a pollen grain and ends with a modern formula. Wiener's actual achievement lies in the difficult middle, in making an intuitive description into a legitimate mathematical object that can be used in further arguments.

Why does this matter beyond probability theory? Modern mathematical finance, diffusion models, stochastic differential equations and the study of noisy dynamical systems all rely on concepts that treat random paths as objects. The later applications cannot be credited to Wiener in advance, and some use substantially different models. But his work helped establish that the apparently irregular path of a moving particle was a subject on which exact theorems could be proved, not merely a curve that a laboratory happened to record.

His work on random processes was part of a wider investigation of waves and signals. Classical Fourier analysis expresses a signal in terms of oscillations at different frequencies. It is a natural tool for a pure tone, but a radio receiver or a noisy measurement does not receive a perfect mathematical note. Signals may extend over long intervals, fluctuate, and contain components whose precise shape cannot be known in advance. Wiener developed generalized harmonic analysis to understand such phenomena. He also worked on Tauberian theorems, results that allow conclusions about underlying quantities to be inferred from their averaged or transformed behavior when suitable conditions hold. The names sound technical, but the common theme is clear: a signal can conceal its structure, and careful mathematics can tell us what can and cannot be recovered from what is observed.

There is a telling difference between a deterministic prediction and a statistical prediction. If we know an ideal pendulum's state and a perfect model, we can calculate a future position. If measurements have noise, the first question is whether a calculation can distinguish relevant motion from error. Suppose a microphone picks up a voice mixed with static. A filter tries to estimate the voice, not by pretending the static vanished, but by using what is known about the statistics of both sources. Wiener helped develop the mathematics of optimal linear prediction and filtering in such conditions. His work shared deep connections with Andrey Kolmogorov's independent studies of stationary processes. It is better to tell this as the convergence of mathematical research programs than as a solitary invention by one man.

The great Depression and the growing threat of another European war changed the social setting of the laboratory. Scientific institutions grew more connected to states, industrial companies and military contracts. Wiener married Margaret Engemann in nineteen twenty-six; they raised two daughters. The demands of research, family, and a career that increasingly involved public scrutiny did not always sit easily together. His autobiographical accounts make no secret of his anxieties, and they are worth reading as personal testimony rather than as a diagnostic explanation for mathematical creativity. Wiener could be generous, difficult, amusing, and combative. Those traits affected collaborations, but no single character label explains the papers.

When the Second World War came, the problem of predicting motion became urgent. Aircraft could travel quickly enough that aiming at their present positions was of little use. A gun had to be directed toward a future position inferred from recent observations, with errors in tracking and delays in the mechanism. Wiener worked on prediction in this context and collaborated with engineers including Julian Bigelow. One important mathematical account, written during the war and circulated under restrictions, treated extrapolation, interpolation, and smoothing of stationary time series. It used statistical features of past observations to construct a best estimate under stated assumptions. The calculations were not a magical device that could foresee an arbitrary adversary; they depended on how motion and noise were modeled.

Another difficulty was the person operating the equipment. If an operator observes an error, moves a control, watches the result, and adjusts again, then the person is part of a loop. The behavior of the complete system depends not only on the gun, aircraft, or sensor, but also on how quickly and accurately corrections return to the operator. Feedback can stabilize a process; too much delay or overcorrection can make it oscillate. This was not a concept discovered from nothing by Wiener. Engineers had long studied governors, servomechanisms, and electrical feedback, while physiologists studied regulation in organisms. Wiener helped create a common language connecting several established streams of work.

He talked with researchers who were themselves crossing disciplinary borders, notably the physiologist Arturo Rosenblueth. Walter Cannon's work on homeostasis had already offered examples of physiological regulation. Bigelow brought engineering knowledge. In the early nineteen forties discussions of purpose, behavior, and regulation led Wiener, Rosenblueth, and Bigelow to examine how goal-directed activity might be described without making a machine into a conscious organism. The point was not that people and machines are the same thing. It was that both can sometimes be analyzed as systems receiving information and adjusting actions. That idea would prove fruitful, and it would also invite overstatement by enthusiasts who forgot the word sometimes.

The connection between communication and control invites a useful conceptual separation. Information theory asks, among other things, how many bits a channel can reliably carry under specified noise constraints. Control theory asks how an actuator should respond to observations when there are delays, disturbances and objectives. A mathematical model of an organism may include both questions, but it must also include biology. The word feedback appears in each discussion without forcing their theorems to coincide. Wiener was a mathematically sophisticated bridge builder; the bridges did not dissolve every disciplinary boundary.

When Wiener wrote about a system's purpose, he did not have a universal formula for human intention. A mechanical controller may be described by the signal it tries to keep near a target. A person changes goals, remembers the past, negotiates social meaning and can refuse to act. Putting both cases under a shared language can illuminate a relationship, but only if we retain the differences. Those limitations are part of the history of cybernetics, not a modern objection added after its original advocates disappeared.

Cybernetics, published in nineteen forty-eight, assembled mathematical research, engineering experience, physiology, and philosophical speculation into one ambitious book. Its title came from a Greek word associated with steering. The book drew readers far outside mathematics, helped frame new conversations about automation and communication, and sometimes seemed to promise a single science of almost everything. In nineteen fifty Wiener published The Human Use of Human Beings, a book aimed at a much wider audience and more explicitly concerned with social consequences. By then Claude Shannon's mathematical theory of communication had appeared, and the two programs shared terms such as information and noise while pursuing different aims. Shannon constructed a precise theory for communication through channels. Wiener was also asking how signals help organisms and machines act on the world, and what such systems might mean for societies.

Consider a factory that installs an automatic controller. At first the engineering question might be how quickly the machine responds and whether the process stays stable. Then come other questions: who chooses the objective; who loses work; who owns the information produced; who is responsible if an automatic decision harms someone? The mathematical description of feedback does not answer those questions. Wiener thought this separation mattered. After wartime research he became increasingly concerned about the military uses of mathematical ideas, about automated systems built without understanding their consequences, and about a researcher's responsibility to choose where and for whom to work.

In a widely discussed postwar essay he explained why he would not supply some kinds of military mathematical assistance. He had contributed to the war effort, and his later position cannot be reduced to someone who had never faced the same practical pressures. His concern sharpened after seeing the scale of industrialized warfare and the possibility of applying new control techniques to systems designed for destruction. It is possible to debate his judgments without dismissing the underlying ethical problem. Advanced mathematics enters the world through institutions; institutions may use it for purposes quite different from those a theorem's author imagined.

Wiener was not the sole architect of a complete modern theory of artificial intelligence, and neither he nor his collaborators built every device later labeled cybernetic. The history is more distributed. Control theory has long roots in engineering. Computer science grew through several independent and connected programs. Mathematical statistics, logic, information theory, and neuroscience each supplied methods. A word that links them can be productive, but it can also obscure their differences. Wiener's influence is better understood as helping researchers recognize recurring structural questions, then provoking arguments over how far the analogies should go.

His later work returned repeatedly to the relationship between prediction and action. A feedback controller changes the system it observes, so an apparently simple forecast may be altered by the response to that forecast. A society operating with automatic decisions raises a related difficulty: people may change their behavior when they know they are being measured or controlled. An engineer can define a cost function and calculate an optimum, but the choice of that cost function expresses values that are not derived from the calculation. Wiener did not resolve all these questions. He made them difficult to ignore in a period when it was tempting to treat increasing technical power as an adequate definition of progress.

His intellectual life remained international. He traveled, lectured, wrote memoirs, and maintained connections with mathematicians, engineers, and physicians. His scientific publications continued alongside books for general readers. These varied audiences responded to different parts of his work: a probabilist might remember a rigorous construction of random paths; an electrical engineer, a framework for filtering and prediction; a philosopher or social critic, the insistence that communication and control had consequences for power. It is a mistake to infer from the later celebrity of the word cybernetics that he stopped doing mathematics after inventing it.

As a public intellectual he also confronted an uncomfortable tension. Mathematical researchers often say that their job is to find correct results and that the uses of those results belong to others. Wiener had lived through a war in which mathematical prediction could be translated into military machinery, and he was unwilling to treat the separation as complete. He understood that refusal by a single scientist might not stop a government project, while public criticism might put him at odds with colleagues who had different views about security and duty. The issue was not settled by his own choices. What made those choices consequential was that a prominent mathematician placed ethical responsibility beside technical competence in the account he gave of his profession.

His concern about automation included the distribution of benefits. A device that makes a process more efficient can reduce drudgery; it can also give the owners of a process more control over workers or concentrate power over decisions. Which outcome occurs depends on laws, institutions, employment conditions and design choices. Mathematics may quantify a system's performance without determining whether that performance improves human lives. That distinction anticipated many later debates, even when Wiener's specific predictions proved imperfect.

He received major honors, including the United States National Medal of Science in nineteen sixty-three. He died the following year, in nineteen sixty-four, in Stockholm while traveling. The years between his adolescent graduation and his final scientific books were not a straight march toward a predetermined destination. They included unsuccessful jobs, demanding education, friendships, institutional conflict, mathematics so abstract it could barely be drawn, war work, and public disagreements about the moral use of research. All those experiences changed the questions he was prepared to ask.

We can finish with the problem of a signal that is both meaningful and noisy. Wiener spent years developing tools that make it possible to infer something stable from uncertain observations. He also knew that a technically correct estimate can serve a dangerous goal. The distinction between being able to predict and being justified in acting is not a theorem about random processes. It belongs to the people who build systems and live with their results. That is why his career matters in two histories at once: the history of rigorous probability and harmonic analysis, and the history of scientists learning to ask what their mathematics does after it leaves the page.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1894–1913:** Columbia, Missouri birth; education guided by Leo and Bertha Wiener; Tufts graduation in 1909; Harvard doctorate in mathematical logic in 1913.
- **1913–1919:** Cambridge and Göttingen study; wartime interruptions and temporary work in teaching, engineering, journalism and ballistics; MIT appointment.
- **1920s–1930s:** work on Brownian motion, generalized harmonic analysis, Tauberian theorems and prediction; marriage to Margaret Engemann in 1926.
- **1940s–1950s:** wartime prediction research with engineering collaborators; work with Rosenblueth and Bigelow; *Cybernetics* (1948), *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), postwar ethical debates.
- **1963–1964:** National Medal of Science in 1963; death in Stockholm in 1964.

### Mathematical companion

**Wiener measure.** Brownian motion is commonly modeled by a continuous stochastic process with stationary independent Gaussian increments. In one standard normalization, the increment over an interval of length \(t\) is normal with mean zero and variance \(t\); almost every path is continuous but nowhere differentiable. Mathematical Brownian motion is an idealized stochastic model, not the exact microscopic trajectory of a fluid particle.

**Wiener filtering.** A linear minimum-mean-squared-error estimator of one stationary signal from another can be characterized in terms of covariance functions and power spectra, provided assumptions making the inversions meaningful. Kolmogorov developed closely related prediction theory independently; avoid assigning the entire subject to Wiener.

**Feedback.** In a closed-loop controller, measured output changes the next input. Depending on delay, gain, dynamics and nonlinearities, this can stabilize or destabilize a system. Feedback was an established engineering and physiological idea before *Cybernetics*.

**Historical cautions:** Cybernetics linked existing work in mathematics, engineering and physiology. Treat Wiener as a leading integrator rather than the inventor of feedback, computers, or all later artificial intelligence. His memoirs provide first-person recollections, not an objective psychological diagnosis. Wartime mathematical prediction did not amount to a functioning universal autonomous weapon. Keep Shannon's communication theory, Kolmogorov's probability work and Bigelow and Rosenblueth's contributions visible.
