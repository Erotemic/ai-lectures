# 085000 - Mary Cartwright: Oxford, Girton, and mathematics in wartime

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** family and schooling, Oxford after the First World War, teachers Hardy and Titchmarsh, Girton, collaboration with Littlewood, radar-related equations, university administration, and mathematical institutions. **Background:** school mathematics; later complex functions and nonlinear oscillators explained intuitively.

In the late nineteen thirties, British researchers were trying to understand circuits whose behavior changed as the signal grew strong. Their equations looked manageable when the signal was weak and increasingly treacherous when it became large. An official research board asked whether pure mathematicians could help. Mary Cartwright, whose work had been in the theory of complex functions, recognized something worth investigating. She brought the problem to John Edensor Littlewood, and their collaboration revealed complicated patterns of oscillation that later generations would recognize as forerunners of chaos theory. Cartwright did not enter mathematics because she was trained for wartime electronics. She reached those equations after years of working in a university culture where being a woman sharply limited the ordinary routes to authority.

Mary Lucy Cartwright was born in December nineteen hundred at Aynho, a Northamptonshire village where her father, William Degby Cartwright, served as vicar. Her mother was Lucy Harriette Maud Cartwright. The household contained several children and belonged to a clerical and professional social world with traditions of education and public service. These circumstances did not make a university career inevitable. For girls in Britain, educational opportunities had expanded, but prestigious universities and learned institutions still granted degrees and professional recognition on unequal terms. Cartwright's story belongs to that long transition, rather than to a simple tale in which institutions suddenly saw merit and became fair.

She attended boarding schools, including the Godolphin School at Salisbury. History originally attracted her most. Mathematics won her over partly because it allowed her to discover a solution by reasoning rather than spend so much time memorizing lists of facts. That recollection is revealing but should not be made into a universal contrast between the subjects; serious history also demands arguments and evidence. It tells us how one pupil encountered two school subjects. Cartwright retained an interest in history and later wrote historical reflections and biographical accounts of other mathematicians. She did not abandon the humanities so much as find that mathematical problems suited the way she wanted to work.

The First World War entered her family before she began university. Two older brothers died in military service. When she reached Oxford in nineteen nineteen, Britain had just emerged from a devastating war, and the lecture rooms were crowded with men resuming interrupted education. Cartwright studied at St Hugh's College. She was among only a handful of women reading mathematics there. The university was changing its formal treatment of women: Oxford began awarding degrees to women in nineteen twenty. Those rules mattered to the substance of scholarly lives. You could master difficult material and still depend on changing institutional permission to receive the qualifications accorded to a male colleague.

The overcrowding also had a direct effect on learning. She sometimes had to obtain notes because she could not get into lectures. After her early examinations, she received a lower classification than she had hoped and considered leaving mathematics. It is tempting to describe this as a brief test of confidence before an inevitable triumph. At the time she did not know the outcome. A student can feel that an examination has settled the question of ability, even though it partly measures the conditions of instruction and the format of the test. She chose to continue, aided by advice from a fellow student, V. C. Morton, who urged her to read an advanced analysis text and attend G. H. Hardy's discussions.

Hardy's sessions were especially significant. They showed mathematics not just as completed exercises for an examination but as questions people were actively studying. Cartwright was introduced to a culture in which researchers spoke with one another about methods that did not yet have textbook explanations. She graduated with first-class honors in nineteen twenty-three. Hardy's role mattered, but this was not a case in which a great man simply bestowed knowledge on a promising woman. Cartwright had made her own decision to persist, undertaken the study, and found a form of mathematical life she wanted to join.

Her family finances played a part in the next decision. Rather than continue immediately to research, she taught mathematics in schools for several years, first at the Alice Ottley School in Worcester and then at Wycombe Abbey. School teaching meant real responsibility and an income, but administrative duties and strict directions about curriculum left her limited room to explore subjects as she wished. The experience made clear both the value of teaching and the difference between following a prescribed lesson and asking an open research question. In nineteen twenty-eight she returned to Oxford for doctoral study, under Hardy's supervision.

When Hardy spent a year in Princeton, Edward Charles Titchmarsh helped oversee her work. This is a reminder that doctoral education is often a network rather than a single pair of names. Her thesis concerned zeros of entire functions, functions of a complex variable that have a particularly strong kind of regularity throughout the complex plane. A zero is a point where a function takes the value zero. One can ask how many zeros lie in a region and how the function's growth constrains them. Such questions are not merely about computing roots. They connect patterns in the complex plane to the structure of an analytic function as a whole.

Littlewood was an external examiner for her doctorate. Their first formal encounter was therefore a mathematical examination, not the beginning of an easy research partnership. Cartwright earned her doctorate in nineteen thirty and won a Yarrow Research Fellowship at Girton College, Cambridge. Girton was one of the institutions created to provide advanced education for women when much of the older university structure resisted their full participation. Cambridge did not grant women full university membership and degrees on equal terms until nineteen forty-eight. By then Cartwright had been producing research there for years. The distinction between doing academic work and enjoying the institutional status attached to it is central to her life.

At Cambridge she returned to problems in complex analysis. Littlewood posed a question about the possible size of an analytic function under conditions limiting how often it could take a particular value. Cartwright solved it, drawing on methods related to conformal mapping. The resulting theorem made her known to analysts beyond Girton. This achievement deserves attention because the later wartime dynamics work can otherwise make her earlier career seem like a preliminary. It was not. Complex analysis had its own intellectual landscape of growth, zeros, boundary behavior, and difficult counterexamples. Cartwright developed methods for that landscape and continued to contribute to it throughout her life.

A general problem helps convey the idea. Imagine drawing a family of points in the complex plane and asking how quickly a function can grow as you move outward. A polynomial grows in a way controlled by its degree. An entire function such as the exponential grows more rapidly and with a geometry that changes along different directions. If extra restrictions are known about the values it assumes, one may be able to bound its maximum size. Cartwright's research sought rigorous conclusions of this kind. The precise theorems are technical; the historical point is that she learned to reason about complicated global behavior from conditions expressed locally or through the distribution of values.

She became director of studies in mathematics at Girton in the mid-nineteen thirties, combining individual research with teaching and administration. Then the Radio Research Board of the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research circulated its memorandum in nineteen thirty-eight. Some amplifying circuits could not safely be treated as linear systems. In a linear model, doubling the input doubles the output, and different waves can be analyzed separately before being added. Real amplifiers may saturate; feedback can make an oscillation grow or diminish depending on its amplitude. That changes the mathematics. The equations can have several regimes, sudden transitions, or complicated behavior not visible in a weak-signal approximation.

Cartwright became interested in the differential equations being used to model these effects and turned to Littlewood for help with the dynamical side. Their sustained collaboration explored nonlinear oscillators, including forced versions of the van der Pol equation. The model has a built-in mechanism that can amplify weak oscillations and damp strong ones, producing sustained rhythms. Now add an external periodic force and vary its strength or frequency. A single smooth equation can generate solutions that switch their qualitative behavior. Instead of a regular response at one clean frequency, there may be complicated combinations of cycles, multiple possible long-term states, and strong sensitivity near transitions.

It would be anachronistic to say that Cartwright and Littlewood wrote the modern theory of chaos, complete with the vocabulary and computational tools used today. They did, however, obtain rigorous results about intricate behavior in nonlinear systems at a time when the modern theory was not yet organized. Later historians of mathematics and dynamical systems have emphasized the importance of this work. The research also gives the lie to a comfortable distinction between pure and applied mathematics. A public request motivated by radio engineering reached a scholar trained in complex analysis, and her methods helped expose mathematical structure that engineers and analysts had not anticipated.

Their division of labor was not a matter of Littlewood providing the serious mathematics while Cartwright supplied an application. She had brought the problem to their attention, contributed analytical methods, and was a full research collaborator. Littlewood later described their surprise at the richness of what they found. The publication record and historical studies of their joint work support recognition of both mathematicians. So do the later uses of that work in nonlinear dynamics. When a story concentrates on one more famous male collaborator, it can erase the route by which the mathematical problem entered the partnership in the first place.

The Second World War made radio research urgent, but the research partnership should not be reduced to a single military objective. The full application environment mixed civilian research, electronic technology, and wartime pressures. Mathematical theorems do not come labeled as exclusively peaceful or exclusively military; they can arise in one context and later belong to another. Cartwright's interests also continued beyond the original technical prompt. She studied cluster sets and other questions in the theory of functions and took part in shaping the institutions that would educate a later generation of mathematicians.

In nineteen forty-seven Cartwright was elected to the Royal Society. She was the first woman mathematician elected to its fellowship, not the first woman of any discipline: that more general distinction belonged to earlier women scientists. She became Mistress of Girton College in nineteen forty-nine, a position requiring decisions about students, teachers, funding, and the future of women's education at Cambridge. The college's role was changing as the university slowly opened more of its structures to women. Her responsibilities involved more than ceremonial authority. Decisions about admission, working conditions, and academic standards could determine which younger scholars had a path forward.

Cartwright served as president of the London Mathematical Society in the early nineteen sixties, again the first woman to hold that office. The Royal Society awarded her the Sylvester Medal in nineteen sixty-four, and the London Mathematical Society the De Morgan Medal in nineteen sixty-eight. She was appointed a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire in nineteen sixty-nine. These recognitions came after decades of research and leadership, not at the beginning of her career. They also mark institutional change: a profession that had offered women restricted credentials now placed a woman mathematician in several of its most visible offices. The change was significant, though it did not mean every structural inequality had vanished.

Her interests were not confined to college offices and equations. Colleagues recalled her enthusiasm for travel, art, music, and history, and an often dry sense of humor. After leaving the Girton headship she traveled and worked at institutions in Britain, Europe, and the United States. She lived until April nineteen ninety-eight, dying in Cambridge at ninety-seven. Her life stretched from an England in which women were only beginning to secure university degrees to a scientific world in which her early work had become part of the history of nonlinear dynamics.

The unity of Cartwright's career is not that she passed from pure theory to useful application and then to public service, as though these were separate and mutually exclusive phases. She worked throughout with the institutions that make intellectual labor possible. She had to find a place to study, to teach, and to research. She moved from the study of complex functions to equations arising from amplifiers because she recognized mathematics she wanted to understand. She shared discoveries with collaborators and helped run a college that made learning possible for others. The history of the discipline changes when we remember not only the results that survived, but also the rooms, restrictions, and opportunities through which the work was done.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Historical chronology

- **1900–1923:** born at Aynho in 1900; family lost two older sons in the First World War; Godolphin School; St Hugh's, Oxford, from 1919; first-class final honors in 1923.
- **1923–1934:** school teaching; doctoral study at Oxford with Hardy and Titchmarsh; D.Phil. in 1930 with Littlewood an external examiner; Yarrow fellowship and early complex-analysis results; Girton teaching posts.
- **1938–1947:** Radio Research Board's nonlinear equation memorandum, sustained Cartwright–Littlewood investigations, Royal Society election in 1947.
- **1949–1998:** Mistress of Girton, leadership of learned societies, Sylvester and De Morgan medals, later mathematical work and travel; death in 1998.

### Mathematical companion

The forced van der Pol oscillator is commonly displayed as

\[x''-\mu(1-x^2)x'+x=A\cos(\omega t).\]

For \(\mu>0\) and no forcing, the damping term changes sign with amplitude, yielding a self-sustained oscillation. Adding periodic forcing can produce complicated dynamics; not all parameter choices are chaotic. Cartwright and Littlewood's rigorous analyses were historically important precursors of later nonlinear dynamics rather than a fully developed modern chaos theory.

An *entire function* is complex differentiable at every point of the complex plane. Cartwright's prewar work studied value distribution, growth, and related function-theoretic bounds. Do not replace that work with a retrospective story focused only on nonlinear oscillators.

**Historical caution:** She was the first *woman mathematician* elected FRS, not the first woman ever elected. Cambridge and Oxford had different rules and dates for women's degrees.
