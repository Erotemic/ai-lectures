# 076000 - Nikolai Lobachevsky: Building a university and remaking geometry in Kazan

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** provincial Russian education, Kazan University, Martin Bartels, university reforms and reaction, rectorship, family and financial difficulties, non-Euclidean geometry, contemporary criticism and posthumous acceptance. **Background:** plane geometry and the idea of parallel lines.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

When Nikolai Lobachevsky challenged a foundational assumption of Euclid's geometry, he was not working in one of Europe's celebrated mathematical capitals. He was teaching, administering and rebuilding a university in Kazan, hundreds of miles east of Moscow. His name now describes a geometry in which the familiar rules of triangles and parallels change. But he was also a rector, a librarian, an organizer of observatories and laboratories, a teacher of young people whose scientific opportunities had to be created as well as used. His mathematical originality and institutional labor belonged to the same crowded working life.

Lobachevsky was born in December seventeen ninety-two at Nizhny Novgorod in the Russian Empire. His father worked in an office associated with land surveying. After his father's death, his mother, Praskovia, moved with three sons to Kazan. Money was limited. Government scholarships made schooling possible. These facts matter because they place the later geometer within a widening imperial project of education. A boy did not need an aristocratic fortune to study at a new provincial institution, but he did need the institution to exist and someone to fund his place in it.

Kazan lay near the Volga and was a center of trade, languages and cultures. The imperial government founded the university there in eighteen oh four, and it opened shortly afterward. Lobachevsky entered in eighteen oh seven, initially considering medicine before turning toward physics and mathematics. It was a young institution still assembling a faculty, library and scientific apparatus. Unlike an old university living off centuries of custom, Kazan had to decide what subjects to teach and how to train students rapidly enough to meet growing demand.

A decisive teacher was Martin Bartels, a German mathematician who had known Gauss in earlier years. Bartels offered strong mathematical instruction and courses on the history of the subject. One historical account he used discussed Euclid and the problem of parallels. We should distinguish the plausible educational influence from a tempting but unsupported tale. The fact that Bartels and Gauss knew one another does not show that Gauss secretly passed a non-Euclidean discovery through Bartels to Lobachevsky. Later investigation of their correspondence has undermined that claim. Lobachevsky's achievement needs no borrowed secret to explain it.

The young scholar advanced quickly. He completed a master's qualification in eighteen eleven, taught as a lecturer, and became a full professor by eighteen twenty-two. Early nineteenth-century Russian universities, however, could be subject to powerful administrative and ideological interference. Under the curator Mikhail Magnitskii, Kazan experienced suspicion toward modern philosophy and some sciences, internal disputes, dismissals and departures. The atmosphere reflected wider conservative currents in Alexander the First's later reign, when university instruction could be judged not just for accuracy but for supposed religious or political danger.

Lobachevsky did not simply retreat into private calculation. He served as dean, became university librarian, obtained equipment for teaching physics and helped build the institutional resources available to colleagues. He ordered books, attended to an observatory and took responsibilities for new university buildings. The mathematician who would eventually ask whether a geometrical axiom could be changed was also confronting a daily list of very practical questions: where instruments should go, which books students needed, who could teach a course and how laboratories could function.

Kazan's geographical position made these choices consequential. A university on the Volga could study astronomy and medicine, but it could also study the languages, history and societies of regions that European academic centers often understood poorly. Building instruction in Oriental studies was one of the institution's major ambitions. Lobachevsky's educational policy supported this breadth alongside mathematics and physical science. The surviving rector's reports occupy many pages and bear witness to administrative effort of the sort that rarely enters the history of a theorem. They record a man trying to make a university work: hiring and teaching, supervising facilities, writing to authorities, and meeting emergencies while continuing original research.

An administrative change in eighteen twenty-six improved the university's prospects. Magnitskii was removed and a new curator, Musin-Pushkin, worked with Lobachevsky to reconstruct and expand the institution. In eighteen twenty-seven Lobachevsky became rector, a position he held for nineteen years. That length of service deserves attention. A rector was not simply a ceremonial professor presiding over examinations. His decisions concerned finances, discipline, construction, teaching appointments, educational policy and relations with authorities far away from Kazan.

Under his leadership the university developed an observatory, library and laboratories, alongside medical facilities and new buildings. Its work in Oriental studies expanded, reflecting Kazan's location and the interests of scholars working across several languages and historical traditions. Lobachevsky promoted scientific research while arguing for the importance of education more broadly. In a speech on educational aims he connected learning to the development of a person's capacities and to public life. It is a mistake to imagine that his only serious interest was the parallel postulate. The sources show an educator who believed that an institution ought to cultivate knowledge in more than one discipline.

Two crises tested that administrative commitment. Cholera struck in eighteen thirty, and a major fire damaged Kazan in eighteen forty-two. The contemporary and later accounts of his rectorship describe effective responses intended to protect the university and its students. One should avoid converting such reports into a theatrical story of a single man personally saving every building. They do, however, establish that he carried responsibility during emergencies. For long periods these obligations competed with research and teaching for the same hours.

Amid this work Lobachevsky addressed a problem that had resisted mathematicians since antiquity. Euclid's *Elements* developed geometry from definitions, postulates and propositions. Most of the postulates seemed simple: one could draw a straight line from one point to another, or extend a line further. The famous fifth postulate, concerning the behavior of lines and angles, had a more complicated form. Later textbooks often express an equivalent condition as follows: through a point outside a given straight line there is exactly one line parallel to it, with parallel meaning a line that does not meet the original line in the plane.

For centuries mathematicians tried to prove this condition using the other principles of geometry. It seemed less self-evident than the rest. Perhaps, some hoped, Euclid had included an unnecessary assumption and a sufficiently clever argument would turn it into a theorem. Attempts often hid an equivalent assumption inside a different definition. Important precursors, including Saccheri and Lambert, explored the consequences of modifying the postulate without fully establishing a new accepted geometry.

Lobachevsky took a different route. Instead of trying yet again to force the fifth postulate from the others, he studied what happens when the geometry permits more than one line through a point that does not meet a given line. This was not a permission to abandon proof. It was a demand to reason carefully from a different set of assumptions. The resulting geometry can look unfamiliar to someone trained exclusively with flat paper, yet it has its own internal structure. In a plane of negative curvature, lines understood as intrinsic shortest paths behave differently from ordinary straight lines drawn in Euclidean coordinates.

A simple way to feel the difference is to consider a triangle. On the usual flat plane, the interior angles of a triangle add to one hundred eighty degrees. In hyperbolic geometry, the total is less than one hundred eighty degrees. The shortfall is not a measurement error or a poorly drawn triangle. For a surface of constant negative curvature, the angle deficit is related to the triangle's area. Larger triangles can reveal larger differences. The shift changes trigonometry, distance and the geometry of parallel lines together. It does not mean Euclid's familiar formulas cease to work for ordinary flat drawings; it means those formulas describe a particular geometrical setting rather than every possible one.

In February eighteen twenty-six, at Kazan, Lobachevsky presented or submitted an outline of his new approach for consideration by colleagues. The original text of that presentation has not survived, which limits exactly what we can assert about its wording. His first printed exposition appeared in eighteen twenty-nine in a local Russian university publication. Its relative obscurity mattered. The manuscript did not arrive in a form easily read by all the scholars who might assess it. A later submission to the St Petersburg Academy met rejection. Publications in French and German during the following decade improved access, yet wider acceptance was slow.

Local publication made the problem of recognition especially sharp. A paper could be mathematically important and still fail to circulate among the people whose acceptance would give it international standing. Scientific exchange did not happen instantaneously. Language, journal access, reputation, travel and the authority of academies all affected whether someone even saw the argument. The relative isolation of Kazan was not an absolute barrier—its teachers and libraries were linked to European mathematics—but it lengthened the path between discovery and criticism. Lobachevsky repeatedly sought broader publication rather than assuming that one local manuscript would settle the matter forever.

The history is intertwined with the independent work of János Bolyai, whose account of a non-Euclidean geometry appeared as an appendix to a work by his father in eighteen thirty-two. Gauss had also considered non-Euclidean possibilities privately and discussed them in correspondence. Those three lines of research cannot be collapsed into a single event or arranged as a simple race with one winner. Lobachevsky's eighteen twenty-nine publication has an important priority as the first printed account of this kind of geometry. Bolyai's work was independent. Gauss's private investigations were real, but did not constitute the same public scientific contribution at the same time.

Legends about these relationships accumulated. One says that Gauss passed the new geometry to Lobachevsky through Bartels; another that he learned Russian only to read Lobachevsky's papers; another that he corresponded directly with Lobachevsky about the subject. Specialist historical work has challenged these claims. Gauss admired the work and supported Lobachevsky's election as a corresponding member of a Göttingen scholarly society, but private admiration and institutional recognition are not the same as public advocacy or direct mathematical collaboration. Keeping these distinctions is essential to a history that respects evidence.

In eighteen thirty-seven Lobachevsky wrote about his imaginary geometry in French, and in eighteen forty he issued a clearer German exposition of parallels. The word imaginary at that time could mark a mathematical departure from familiar space without implying mere fantasy. His subject was no less systematic than ordinary geometry. One can see its rigor by observing the new consequences it forces: revised trigonometric identities and relationships among distances, angles and areas. The mathematics challenges a presumed necessity, then replaces it with another controlled deductive system.

The wider career was not limited to geometry. He taught mechanics, hydrodynamics, differential equations and astronomy. He also developed a method for finding approximate roots of polynomial equations; related techniques were discovered independently by others and are now usually associated with other names. The discrepancy in naming is itself historically informative. A mathematical idea may be discovered in more than one place, yet the name that survives can depend on which printed tradition later researchers inherited. The same is true of the fame of non-Euclidean geometry, which grew slowly and unevenly across national scientific communities.

In eighteen thirty-two he married Varvara Moisieva. They had a large family, and household responsibilities accompanied an already heavy public career. By the eighteen forties, administrative work, family expenses and health weighed on him. His time as rector ended in eighteen forty-six under circumstances often described as dismissal rather than voluntary retirement. He later experienced the death of an eldest son and deteriorating eyesight, eventually blindness. There were also financial pressures. But biographers have sometimes exaggerated the story into one of total isolation and incompetent estate management; later archival examination disputes aspects of those inherited narratives. Suffering in his final years is documented without every colorful allegation being true.

Lobachevsky died in Kazan in February eighteen fifty-six. His mathematical contemporaries had not given his new geometry a reception commensurate with its later significance. The subsequent transformation of its reputation did not happen in one act of enlightened recognition. French translations helped transmit the work. In the eighteen sixties Eugenio Beltrami constructed models demonstrating that hyperbolic geometry could be realized within accepted mathematics, an essential step in clarifying its consistency relative to familiar geometric assumptions. Klein and Poincaré later developed further models and interpretations. By then a new generation could study the geometry without needing to accept its founder's word against every prevailing intuition.

Modern mathematics uses non-Euclidean geometry in topology, geometric group theory and theoretical physics. But the familiar equation between mathematical truth and the shape of physical space also requires care. Constructing a logically coherent geometry does not by itself prove that the universe has that geometry. Mathematics identifies possible structures; physics tests which structures help describe observed phenomena. Lobachevsky's work created conceptual room in which later physical theories could be formulated. It was not itself an early measurement of spacetime curvature.

The university rector of Kazan did two kinds of institution-building. With colleagues and staff he made a physical and educational institution capable of training students and supporting scientific work. With a different set of postulates, he helped build a conceptual institution—a geometry with its own consequences, methods and standards of proof. Both achievements required persistence through skepticism, practical difficulty and political change. The second transformed mathematics; the first explains much about the working life in which that transformation became possible.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology and evidence cautions
- **1792–1816:** Born Nizhny Novgorod; moved with his mother to Kazan after his father's death; gymnasium scholarship, Kazan University, Bartels, mathematics professorial appointments.
- **1819–1827:** Conservative curator Magnitskii interfered with university work; Lobachevsky managed teaching and library duties; submitted his geometry lecture in February 1826; became rector in 1827.
- **1829–1846:** First print account of non-Euclidean geometry in 1829; 1832 marriage; German and French expositions; university expansion, cholera response and fire; rectorship ended in 1846.
- **1846–1856:** Illness, family bereavement and decreasing vision; death in Kazan. Posthumous translation and models significantly improved understanding and acceptance.

**Attribution cautions:** The 1826 presentation is not preserved verbatim; do not quote a reconstructed original as an extant text. Gauss, Bolyai and Lobachevsky reached related insights independently with different publication histories. Claims that Gauss sent Lobachevsky the breakthrough through Bartels are not supported by the surviving correspondence. Financial and estate-management stories vary in reliability. Hyperbolic geometry is not synonymous with every form of non-Euclidean geometry, nor does its mathematical consistency settle the empirical structure of spacetime.

### Mathematical display companion
- Hyperbolic triangle area for constant Gaussian curvature $K=-1/R^2$: $A=R^2(\pi-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma))$, with angles in radians.
- For constant curvature $-1/R^2$, a hyperbolic circle of radius $r$ has circumference $C=2\pi R\sinh(r/R)$, versus Euclidean $2\pi r$.
- The Poincaré disk model writes $ds^2=4R^2(dx^2+dy^2)/(1-x^2-y^2)^2$ for $x^2+y^2<1$. This is a later model, not Lobachevsky's original presentation.
- The equivalent modern parallel axiom must be stated relative to the correct underlying geometric axioms; changing one assumption while leaving other hidden equivalents in place is not a valid independence argument.
