# Åke Björck: How can principal angles be computed reliably from matrices?

Two research groups independently fit low-dimensional models to the same collection of measurements. Each believes its model captures the important directions in the data. Their coordinate lists look different. One team uses columns measured in meters, another has rescaled a variable into millimeters. One has chosen a basis whose vectors are nearly parallel. The other has chosen mutually perpendicular vectors. Are the two models actually different? And if they are different, by how much?

The question sounds geometric. The answer seems to require an angle. But computers receive arrays of numbers, and those numbers do not come with a guarantee that they describe their geometry reliably. A matrix can represent exactly the right subspace on paper while concealing its direction in rounding errors. The amount of error may depend on the procedure we use to calculate the angle. Two mathematically equivalent formulas can give sharply different numerical answers.

Åke Björck spent much of his working life studying this boundary between mathematical meaning and numerical computation. His name is closely associated with a nineteen seventy-three paper, written with Gene Golub, on computing the angles between linear subspaces. Yet that collaboration came from a much wider body of work: solving least-squares problems, constructing orthogonal bases, correcting computed answers, and understanding why apparently reasonable matrix algorithms can lose information. Björck also helped shape an international numerical-analysis community through books, collaborations, editorial work, and a career at Linköping University in Sweden.

## A plane does not care how we name its directions

Start with a sheet of paper resting flat on a table. We could describe the sheet using one horizontal arrow and one vertical arrow, or using two slanted arrows. If the slanted arrows are independent and remain in the sheet, they span exactly the same plane. If one arrow is twice as long as the other, the sheet has not changed. If we switch the order of the arrows, the sheet has not changed. The plane is a geometric object; the arrows are its coordinates.

A matrix can serve as such a list of arrows. Its columns are vectors, and its column space contains every linear combination of those vectors. When the columns are independent, they provide a basis. But a basis may be well chosen or badly chosen. For example, take one arrow along the first coordinate axis. Take a second arrow that points almost in the same direction, deviating from it by only a tiny amount along the second axis. So long as that deviation is nonzero in exact arithmetic, the two arrows still span the entire first-and-second-coordinate plane.

Now imagine making the tiny deviation smaller and smaller. The plane is unchanged until the deviation reaches exactly zero, when the two arrows collapse onto one line. Numerically this is treacherous. A computer does not have infinitely many digits. At some scale, it becomes difficult to tell a very small independent component from rounding error. The formula for the plane remains correct, but the ability to discover the plane from its columns is deteriorating.

This gives us two separate questions. What is the angle between two exact subspaces? And how reliably can that angle be recovered from the particular matrices used to describe them? The second question has no satisfactory answer until we discuss the conditioning of the input and the stability of the algorithm. A poor answer may arise because the physical data never determined the subspace well, because the calculation lost information that was available, or because both happened.

To see the difference, imagine the same plane represented by two perpendicular unit arrows. Their lengths are one, and their inner product is zero. Small changes to their coordinates generally produce small changes to the plane. In this representation the geometry is clearly exposed. An orthonormal basis is a basis whose arrows are all of length one and mutually perpendicular. Producing such bases is one of numerical linear algebra's most useful skills.

The computational methods that produce orthonormal columns are not mere preparatory conveniences. Their reliability controls what we can say about the geometry afterward. That observation is the road into Björck's work.

## A Swedish generation learns to trust calculations carefully

Åke Björck was born in nineteen thirty-four and was trained in a period when scientific computing was becoming an academic discipline. In Sweden, a particularly important figure was Germund Dahlquist. Dahlquist combined theoretical numerical analysis with attention to actual calculating machines, error behavior, and the practical demands of scientific work. His work on differential equations became internationally influential, and he helped establish a research culture in which mathematical proofs and computational experiments could inform one another.

Björck's association with that environment was more than an incidental connection. His education is recorded at the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, and Dahlquist is identified as his doctoral adviser. During the nineteen sixties Björck was a lecturer at the Royal Institute of Technology. By nineteen sixty-nine he had moved to Linköping, where he would spend the central decades of his professorial career. He later held emeritus status there. That institutional move belongs to a broader expansion of Swedish universities and research in numerical mathematics.

Björck and Dahlquist also wrote a numerical-methods textbook together. Their Swedish-language *Numeriska metoder* appeared in nineteen sixty-nine; a revised and enlarged English-language *Numerical Methods* followed in nineteen seventy-four. The English book moved through subjects ranging from numerical linear algebra to differential equations, interpolation, and optimization. It was intended to teach how a problem becomes a calculation and how to judge whether the calculation deserves confidence.

A detail in the history of that book is revealing. George Forsythe, a major force in the development of computer science at Stanford, encouraged the expanded English edition. Sweden's mathematical-computing community and the American one were in conversation. Researchers traveled, compared algorithms, tested the limits of floating-point arithmetic, and traded ways of explaining computational mathematics to a growing generation of students.

To understand the problems that drew Björck's attention, consider the very common task of fitting a line to measurements. Suppose we have observed three values at three times. There are more measurements than unknown coefficients, and no single line may pass exactly through all three points. The least-squares method finds the line that makes the sum of the squared deviations as small as possible. Geometrically it projects the observation vector onto a subspace of all vectors that can be represented by the model.

In exact arithmetic, that projection has an elegant characterization. The residual, which is the observed vector minus the fitted vector, is perpendicular to every column of the model matrix. In a computer, however, finding the coefficients of the projection is a further numerical problem. If the model columns point in nearly the same direction, many different coefficient pairs can produce nearly the same fitted vector. The model prediction may be reasonably determined while the individual coefficients are sensitive. A solver must distinguish these facts.

## The trap in the easiest least-squares formula

There is a celebrated shortcut for least squares. Multiply the equations by the transpose of the model matrix, and solve the resulting square system. These are the normal equations. On paper they turn the problem into something familiar. They are also a perfect example of a mathematically valid procedure that can be numerically hazardous.

Take two vectors with three components. Let the first be one along the first axis and zero elsewhere. Let the second be almost the same: one along the first axis, plus a tiny component along the second. Use these as the columns of a matrix. In exact arithmetic, the two columns are independent. They span the ordinary plane of the first two coordinates, regardless of how small the second component is, so long as it remains nonzero.

The normal equations require us to calculate every inner product of the columns. The squared length of the first column is one. Their mutual inner product is also one. The squared length of the second column is one plus the square of that tiny component. If the component is a hundred-millionth, its square is about one part in ten quadrillion. In ordinary double-precision arithmetic, adding such a small correction to one can round back to one. The computed matrix of inner products may therefore have two identical rows. It looks rank deficient, even though the original columns were independent.

The damage is not mysterious. The normal-equation construction squares the relevant condition number. A moderately difficult problem can become a very difficult one. In our example, the squared tiny component is trying to preserve precisely the information that makes the two columns independent. Once rounding has erased that information, solving the normal equations more carefully cannot recover it.

A different method avoids forming that squared quantity. We can systematically turn the original columns into perpendicular directions. The first direction is the unit vector along the first axis. From the second column, subtract its component along the first. What remains is the tiny vector along the second axis. Divide by its length, and we obtain a unit vector pointing along the second axis. The two resulting vectors are perpendicular. We have found the same plane, but without first taking a tiny number and squaring it.

This is the organizing idea of a QR factorization. The letter Q denotes columns that are orthonormal. The letter R denotes an upper-triangular matrix of coefficients explaining how the original columns are assembled from those perpendicular directions. One may solve least squares by first rotating or reflecting the data into these orthogonal coordinates, then solving a triangular system. This route often preserves substantially more information than the normal equations.

## Gram-Schmidt, Householder, and a paper from nineteen sixty-seven

There are several ways to obtain those perpendicular directions. The Gram-Schmidt process constructs them one column at a time, removing components in directions already found. The classical form is an old mathematical construction. Its numerical behavior depends on exactly how those subtractions are organized. Modified Gram-Schmidt performs them in an order that often improves the behavior of the computed basis.

An alternative uses Householder reflections. A reflection is an orthogonal transformation: it changes a vector's coordinates without changing its length. Carefully chosen reflections can reduce a column to a single nonzero coordinate and then do the same to the remaining columns. A product of such transformations yields a QR factorization. These methods can require different numbers of operations, different amounts of storage, and different care when very small components are present.

In nineteen sixty-seven Björck published *Solving Linear Least Squares Problems by Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization* in the journal BIT. The title reflects a broad computational concern: how a classical geometric process behaves when used to solve a numerical problem. That same year he published work on iterative refinement, a way to improve a computed least-squares result by checking and correcting its error. With Golub he also studied refinement based on Householder transformations. The story was already about algorithms as carefully engineered chains of decisions.

Modified Gram-Schmidt reduces some opportunities for this loss, yet it does not make finite precision disappear. Reorthogonalization means taking another pass: after constructing a new direction, subtract its remaining components along the preceding basis vectors again. If the first pass left a small overlap, the second can reduce it. But the additional cost must be justified, and highly ill-conditioned data can still require a rank decision.

The practical question is not simply which method has the prettier formula. We want to know which one preserves the properties needed downstream. For least squares, a high-quality fitted solution may be enough. For principal vectors, we may also require a collection of computed directions to remain mutually orthogonal. An algorithm adequate for one output can be inadequate for another.

Björck would return to the loss and recovery of orthogonality decades later with Christopher Paige. This long continuity included people as well as methods. Their later paper was dedicated to their friend and mentor Gene Golub on his sixtieth birthday; it credited an observation relayed by Golub as the starting point of the analysis. The exchange is a reminder that work on numerical algorithms often starts with somebody noticing a surprising computational equivalence, discussing it with colleagues, and asking them to explain it. To understand the earlier principal-angle paper, we need to meet the collaborator with whom Björck developed that question.

## Stanford and a meeting with Gene Golub

During the academic year nineteen sixty-eight to nineteen sixty-nine, Björck was a postdoctoral visitor at Stanford University and the University of California, Berkeley. This put him near Gene Golub, whose work on matrix algorithms was helping establish the computational foundations of modern scientific computing. Golub had played a central role in developing practical methods for the singular value decomposition. Stanford's computer science department was also a gathering place for researchers concerned with the new problems created by increasingly capable computers.

Many years later, Björck wrote a personal reminiscence for a collection celebrating Golub's contributions. He described being able to see Golub regularly during that year and occasionally meeting George Forsythe. He recalled Golub sitting down with a sheet of paper and rapidly sketching an elegant outline of what would become their article. Björck made clear that he did not remember the exact moment their collaboration began, so his account is a remembered scene rather than a dated transcript. What he did remember was the mathematical starting point: the resemblance between the variational description of singular values and the variational description of principal angles.

That is a lovely piece of mathematical history because the insight can be reconstructed. A singular value tells us how much a matrix can stretch a unit vector, subject to orthogonality conditions for subsequent values. A principal angle tells us how closely we can align one unit vector from each of two subspaces, again adding orthogonality conditions for subsequent pairs. The two problems are already phrased in similar language. All that is needed is to find the matrix whose singular values encode the desired alignments.

The collaboration first circulated as a Stanford technical report in nineteen seventy-one. The journal paper, *Numerical Methods for Computing Angles Between Linear Subspaces*, appeared in *Mathematics of Computation* in nineteen seventy-three, on pages five hundred seventy-nine through five hundred ninety-four. That interval matters historically: the same work existed in research circulation before the journal publication. It also means that the numerical context of the paper belongs to a time when stable computation of singular values was itself a relatively fresh practical achievement.

Björck wrote that he was excited by Golub's newly developed stable singular-value algorithm and by the opportunity to put it to use. This is a very different story from the discovery of an isolated geometric identity. Mathematicians had studied angles between subspaces for many decades. Their question was how to calculate those angles and the associated principal vectors accurately from real input matrices, especially when those inputs were badly scaled or nearly dependent.

## Two planes and two different angles

Return to the sheet of paper on the table. Place one plane along the first two coordinate axes in three-dimensional space. Place a second plane so that it shares the first coordinate direction with the first plane, but its other direction tilts sixty degrees upward out of the first plane. The first plane is spanned by the first and second coordinate axes. The second is spanned by the first axis and by an arrow that mixes the second and third axes, with a cosine of sixty degrees in the second direction and a sine of sixty degrees in the third.

What is the angle between these two planes? There is a familiar single angle between their normals. That angle is sixty degrees. But the richer idea of principal angles gives two answers, one for each independent direction being compared. The planes share a line, so their first principal angle is zero. Within the direction perpendicular to that common line, they differ by sixty degrees, so the second principal angle is sixty degrees.

These are the best possible pairwise alignments, selected in order. First find the unit vectors, one in each plane, whose inner product is as large as possible. Because the planes share the first axis, choose that direction on both sides. Their inner product is one, corresponding to zero angle. Then restrict attention to directions orthogonal to that first pair. The remaining choices have inner product one half, corresponding to sixty degrees.

Now construct a matrix of pairwise inner products between orthonormal bases for the planes. In the simple coordinate choice just described, that matrix has a one along the first diagonal position, a one half in the second diagonal position, and zero off the diagonal. Its singular values are one and one half. The principal angles therefore have cosines one and one half. We recover zero and sixty degrees.

Suppose we choose bases in which the common line is no longer the first column. The overlap matrix may become full of nonzero entries. Its individual entries change, but its singular values remain one and one half. They encode the geometric information that survives the changes of coordinates. This is the central algorithmic bridge of the Björck–Golub paper.

One extra piece of information comes from the singular vectors. They tell us which combinations of the columns of our orthonormal bases produce the actual principal directions in the original ambient space. Thus we can compute both how strongly two spaces align and which directions achieve that alignment. In statistical applications, those directions become particularly meaningful.

## Why singular values reveal principal angles

Let's unpack the argument rather than trusting a recipe. A subspace can be described by an orthonormal matrix Q. Multiplying Q by a coefficient vector constructs a vector in the subspace. Because its columns are orthonormal, Q preserves the coefficient vector's length. If the coefficients have unit length, so does the vector in the subspace. And every vector in the subspace has such a representation.

Call the two orthonormal basis matrices Q for the first space and P for the second. Take a unit coefficient vector for the first and another for the second. Their corresponding vectors in the ambient space are Q times the first coefficient vector and P times the second. Their inner product is the product of the first coefficient vector, the transpose of Q, P, and the second coefficient vector. The middle matrix is the small overlap matrix between the orthonormal frames.

So maximizing the alignment of two unit vectors, one from each space, is equivalent to maximizing an expression involving this overlap matrix and two unit coefficient vectors. That is exactly the variational characterization of its largest singular value. Once the first pair of maximizing directions has been found, the orthogonality constraints defining the next principal directions translate into orthogonality constraints on the coefficient vectors. The second singular value gives the cosine of the second principal angle, and the argument continues.

There is an important difference between an eigenvalue and a singular value here. The overlap matrix may be rectangular, because the two spaces need not have the same dimension. It may not be symmetric. Singular values still exist and are nonnegative. This makes them the natural quantities for comparing different-dimensional subspaces. If one space has five dimensions and the other has three, there are three principal angles. The shared geometry only has three directions in which to pair the spaces.

Now consider a tempting mistake. Why bother constructing orthonormal bases? Why not take the transpose of the original matrix of spanning columns, multiply it by the second spanning matrix, and calculate singular values directly? The problem is that raw columns are not guaranteed to have unit length, nor to be perpendicular. Scaling one column by a thousand would scale parts of that raw overlap matrix. The subspace would remain unchanged while the calculated quantities could change dramatically.

QR factorization removes that arbitrary coordinate scaling. It exposes an orthonormal frame before asking about angles. It is the computational realization of the geometric rule that angles depend on directions and their inner products, not on arbitrary lengths assigned to basis arrows. Once the bases are constructed, the remaining singular-value decomposition is comparatively small and has a direct interpretation.

But there is a catch. An accurate calculation of the cosine of a very small angle may be an inaccurate calculation of the angle itself. That leads to another idea from the same paper.

## The problem with an angle close to zero

Imagine two lines that differ by an extremely small tilt. Suppose the angle is about one hundred-millionth of a radian. Its cosine is extremely close to one. In fact the cosine differs from one by approximately half the square of the angle. The difference is around five parts in a hundred quadrillion. That is on the scale at which standard double-precision arithmetic may round the computed cosine all the way to one.

If we then apply the inverse cosine function, we may obtain an angle of zero. The geometry had a small but definite difference, yet the calculation could not distinguish it from exact coincidence. This failure is not a weakness of the definition of principal angles. It is a weakness of recovering a tiny angle from a number extraordinarily close to one.

In the plane, there is a more direct measurement. Take a unit vector along the tilted line and measure its component perpendicular to the reference line. That perpendicular component has length equal to the sine of the angle. For a tiny angle, the sine is almost equal to the angle itself. Instead of finding a difference between two almost equal numbers, we seek the small displacement directly.

The same idea works for subspaces. We first take an orthonormal basis for the second space. Then we project its columns onto the orthogonal complement of the first space. The singular values of that projected matrix are the sines of the principal angles, including zeros for shared directions. The calculation measures the part of the second space that actually lies outside the first.

In our example of two planes meeting at sixty degrees, one singular value of the overlap matrix is one and the other is one half. The perpendicular-component matrix has corresponding singular values zero and the sine of sixty degrees, about zero point eight six six. The first direction stays in the original plane. The other has a substantial component outside it. Both calculations describe the same geometry from complementary viewpoints.

For a very small angle, the complementary measurement becomes crucial. Taking the sine can preserve information that is lost when the cosine rounds to one. The nineteen seventy-three paper explicitly derived formulas for both cosines and sines and investigated their numerical behavior. It also examined how choices of orthogonalization affect the result. The algebraic equality of two formulas is only the beginning; the actual sequence of floating-point operations has to be considered.

There is a further subtlety. A formula saying take the identity matrix, subtract a projector, and multiply by a basis looks straightforward. If implemented naively, subtracting nearly equal vectors may itself lose accuracy. The paper compared ways to construct the perpendicular component and found that some variants behaved markedly better in numerical tests. Thus the lesson is not to chant a particular symbolic formula. It is to preserve the perpendicular information while performing the computation.

## A tiny input change with a large geometric consequence

We have talked about a method preserving information, but no algorithm can guarantee that the subspace itself is insensitive to small data changes. Here is an example that separates those issues cleanly.

Take two columns in three-dimensional space. The first points along the first axis. The second has a component of one along the first axis and a tiny component along the second. The columns span the first-and-second-coordinate plane. Now modify only the second column, adding another tiny component of the same magnitude along the third axis. The new columns span a different plane. Their common first direction is unchanged. Their remaining direction points equally along the second and third axes, so the second principal angle between the old and new planes is forty-five degrees.

The numerical change to the matrix can be as small as we please, simply by choosing the tiny component smaller. Yet the orientation of the plane changes by forty-five degrees. We have perturbed the matrix by a vanishingly small absolute amount and produced a macroscopic rotation of its column space. What happened? The original columns were almost dependent. Their weak independent part had size equal to that tiny component, so changing another component of the same size redirected the whole second basis direction.

The example exposes a fundamental limit. To recover a subspace from nearly dependent spanning columns, a small error in the columns can cause a large error in the subspace. An algorithm can be stable relative to the given data and still return a substantially different subspace if the data themselves have changed. This is conditioning. The algorithm may be doing everything correctly for the perturbed input.

Björck and Golub performed a perturbation analysis that connected the sensitivity of the computed angles to the condition numbers of the matrices describing the spaces. A condition number measures, roughly, the potential amplification of a small input change into a larger output change. When a spanning matrix has a very small singular value relative to its largest, the smallest independent direction is fragile. It takes little noise to redirect or even obscure it.

The right response is to ask for the data's scale and accuracy. If the tiny component has strong physical meaning and is accurately measured, the plane may still be recovered. If it is smaller than the measurement noise, claiming a precise orientation is not warranted. A numerical method must also recognize exact or effective rank deficiency. Choosing how many dimensions the subspace has is part of the problem; it cannot always be postponed until after the angles have been computed.

In linear algebra, singular values give a quantitative warning about this loss of information. A tiny singular value means some combination of input coordinates has almost no effect in the output. Distinguishing true rank from numerical rank requires information about the precision and reliability of the data, not merely a universal threshold announced by a computer program.

## Why statisticians also need these angles

The nineteen seventy-three paper explicitly connected principal angles to canonical correlation analysis in statistics. That link is easy to miss if we think of subspaces only as planes in a drawing. But a table of measurements defines vectors in a high-dimensional space: every variable is a column, and every observation contributes a coordinate. A collection of variables spans a subspace of possible linear combinations.

Suppose one collection of variables describes economic conditions and another describes some measured outcomes. A statistician may ask which linear combination of the first collection is most strongly correlated with which linear combination of the second. After appropriate centering and normalization, this is a search for pairs of directions whose inner product is largest. The search is followed by a second pair, constrained to be orthogonal in the appropriate standardized geometry to the first, and so on.

That is exactly the structure of principal angles. The maximal canonical correlations are the cosines of the principal angles between suitably standardized column spaces. The corresponding canonical variables are the paired principal directions. This does not mean that a correlation automatically proves causation, nor that the geometry resolves every question of statistical interpretation. It means that the optimization at the center of canonical correlation has a clean subspace description.

This interpretation is computationally useful because statistical data frequently have variables of different scales and closely related predictors. Naively using raw inner products can confuse scaling with geometric alignment. Forming normal-equation-like products can compound conditioning problems. Using orthonormal bases and singular values can expose the invariant structure and help diagnose the data's numerical rank.

The same mathematics applies when comparing reduced-order models. A simulation may approximate a large state space using a small collection of modes. Another simulation, or another run with different parameters, may identify a second collection. The vectors can be rotated among themselves without changing the subspaces they span. Principal angles provide a basis-independent comparison. A small largest principal angle means every direction in one of two equal-dimensional spaces remains close to the other; a large one indicates a direction that escapes.

Björck's contribution was to turn these relationships into computational methods and to analyze when those methods could be trusted. The work connects statistical data analysis, eigenvalue computations, subspace perturbations, and numerical linear algebra without requiring those fields to adopt the same surface notation.

## Refinement is a calculation about the error you have actually made

A distinctive strand in Björck's earlier and later work concerns iterative refinement. The principle is easy to describe. First compute an approximate solution to a least-squares problem. Then use that candidate to calculate what error remains. From the residual, compute a correction, add it to the current solution, and repeat if necessary.

For a square linear system, iterative refinement has a straightforward interpretation: the residual tells us how far the current vector is from satisfying the equations. Least squares adds a complication. When the system is overdetermined and inconsistent, even the best solution has a nonzero residual. We cannot demand that every equation be satisfied. The correct optimality condition is that the residual be orthogonal to the model's column space. A refinement scheme therefore has to distinguish the unavoidable residual from the error in the computed minimizer.

Björck's nineteen sixty-seven work analyzed procedures for reducing rounding error in least-squares solutions, including methods that calculated residual information more accurately than the initial factorization. The idea was to use the computed structure of the problem to make targeted corrections. Improved residual precision can provide information that was lost in the first solve, although the process still faces conditioning limits and depends on how the corrections are computed.

Imagine fitting a line to noisy observations. The first calculation gives a slope and an intercept. When you evaluate the fitted line, you do not expect the observed values and predictions to match exactly. But you can check whether the remaining deviations have a component in the direction of the constant column or in the direction of the time column. If they do, the coefficients can still be adjusted to reduce the squared error. An accurate least-squares solution makes these directional residual correlations very small.

Refinement also forces a distinction between an answer that is accurate for a nearby problem and one that is accurate for the exact intended problem. A method can be backward stable, meaning that its output is the exact answer to a slightly perturbed input, while the requested output is still sensitive to that perturbation. Near rank deficiency, the coefficients can change greatly even if the fit changes little. Both statements can be true at once.

This concern with error, rather than with formulas alone, gives unity to decades of Björck's work. It explains why the least-squares problem and the principal-angle problem belong in the same portrait. Both are stories about extracting a geometric invariant from matrix data while keeping track of how arithmetic error interacts with the problem's conditioning.

## The return to orthogonality with Christopher Paige

By the nineteen nineties, orthogonalization had become a central component of scientific computing. Gram-Schmidt, Householder QR, and their variants were used in eigenvalue algorithms, iterative methods, and least-squares solvers. Yet the old issue remained: a theoretically orthogonal set of vectors can gradually lose orthogonality when computed one vector at a time with rounding error.

In nineteen ninety-two Björck and Christopher Paige published *Loss and Recapture of Orthogonality in the Modified Gram-Schmidt Algorithm*. Their choice of language is instructive. Orthogonality is a property the computation can lose and, under suitable circumstances and with a suitable method, recover. The paper investigated numerical behavior at a deeper level than a statement that modified Gram-Schmidt is generally better than the classical implementation.

Think of a sequence of nearly dependent vectors. Each new vector should be stripped of components along the directions already accepted. If those components are calculated inaccurately, a small remnant stays behind. Subsequent steps may amplify the accumulated failure. Reorthogonalization provides one means of correcting it. Another useful perspective is to understand certain Gram-Schmidt procedures in relation to Householder transformations and their stability properties.

There is a practical tension. Householder methods are often strong choices when we need a reliably orthogonal set of computed vectors. Modified Gram-Schmidt can be economical and convenient in algorithms that build a basis incrementally. The best selection depends on the objective: speed, memory, the need to update a factorization, the expected condition number, and the desired orthogonality of the final basis.

The nineteen seventy-three principal-angle paper compared Householder transformations with modified Gram-Schmidt in precisely this spirit. Its numerical experiments indicated that modified Gram-Schmidt could compute the angles with comparable precision at lower arithmetic cost in the problems examined, while Householder transformations delivered principal vectors that were orthogonal to working accuracy more reliably. That is a careful empirical and mathematical comparison, rather than a timeless ranking of every implementation under every input condition.

One of the important lessons is that accuracy is a property of a requested output. Two procedures can agree on the angles and disagree on how orthogonal their recovered principal vectors are. If we only need a diagnostic scalar, the distinction may have little practical cost. If we will use the principal vectors as a new basis for another computation, the orthogonality failure can affect the next stage of the calculation.

## Books, students, colleagues, and a research community

Björck's academic life can also be followed through the students, publications, and colleagues around him. An older university report preserves a routine scene from Linköping. In January nineteen seventy-eight, Björck examined Lars Eldén's dissertation on regularization and constrained least squares, while Per-Åke Wedin came from Umeå to serve as the faculty opponent. The department's annual report also lists Björck teaching a course in advanced numerical methods. The subject of the defense connects with his own work: how can information be extracted from ill-conditioned data without treating numerical noise as a meaningful signal? The dissertation and its examination were part of the way those questions passed to another generation.

He traveled between Swedish and American research communities over a long career, including visiting appointments at Stanford and the University of Southern California. From nineteen ninety-three through two thousand three, Björck served as managing editor of the journal BIT Numerical Mathematics. Editorial work meant reading and judging other mathematicians' approaches, asking whether a numerical experiment supported a claim and how a method compared with established algorithms. The journal was also where he had published some of his earliest results. After his term, Axel Ruhe took over the journal's leadership, continuing a professional association spanning several Swedish universities.

In January nineteen ninety-five, Linköping hosted a conference marking his sixtieth birthday. The announced theme was *Least Squares Methods: Theory, Algorithms and Applications*. Among the speakers were Gene Golub, Christopher Paige, G. W. Stewart, Charles Van Loan, and other numerical analysts who had helped develop the subject with him. A birthday in academic life can become a meeting of people who have spent decades disagreeing constructively about methods, exchanging ideas, and teaching students who will carry the field forward. Here, least squares was the common topic of that gathering.

In nineteen ninety-six Björck published *Numerical Methods for Least Squares Problems*, a detailed treatment of theory, algorithms, conditioning, and extensions of least squares. The book made the subject available to readers who could learn not only how to run a procedure but also how to examine its weaknesses. Five years after the nineteen ninety-five gathering, another event at Linköping celebrated his sixty-fifth birthday and contributions to the field. Its theme was *Thirty Years of Numerical Linear Algebra in Sweden*. The speakers included Germund Dahlquist, Axel Ruhe, Per-Åke Wedin, Lars Eldén, and others. A contemporary message by Eldén said Björck was likely to continue with research and editorial work after the usual retirement age. His colleagues associated him with persistence in both.

In twenty fourteen, the Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics named Björck a Fellow, recognizing his contributions to numerical linear algebra, particularly least-squares methods. Another major volume, *Numerical Methods in Matrix Computations*, appeared in twenty fifteen. It treated linear systems, least squares, eigenvalue problems, and iterative methods as parts of a connected discipline, with principal-angle calculations appearing within a much wider account of matrix computation.

That year Björck also appeared in print in a different role. When Ruhe died in two thousand fifteen, Björck and Lars Eldén wrote a remembrance of their colleague. They recalled his animated lectures and handwritten overhead transparencies. They remembered a conference in which visitors went skiing on the frozen Gulf of Bothnia, as well as Ruhe's cultural and civic interests. The piece has the texture of a colleague recalling years of shared working life. Björck was helping to preserve the life of another mathematician, including the things that had happened outside seminar rooms. Those remembrances complement the papers and books through which numerical analysis usually records its history.

A second edition of *Numerical Methods for Least Squares Problems* was published much later, in twenty twenty-four. Across those decades, Björck kept returning to how mathematical structure, numerical error, and effective algorithms should be taught together. The work is linked by a network of advisers, coauthors, students, journal editors, and friends. The principal-angle algorithm remains one important result in a much longer intellectual life.

## A final comparison between geometry and computation

We began with two research groups presenting models through different matrices. We can now say what an informed comparison requires. First, we must decide what subspace each matrix actually represents, including whether its numerical rank is established by the data. Then we construct orthonormal bases using methods appropriate for the input's conditioning. We form the smaller overlap matrix and calculate its singular values. Those give the cosines of principal angles. If the spaces are nearly aligned and the small differences matter, we may need a more direct calculation of the sines using perpendicular components.

The calculation yields a geometric comparison, but its meaning also depends on the reliability of the starting data. If a tiny singular value controls a supposedly independent direction, that direction may be sensitive to measurement error. A mathematically stable algorithm cannot repair a data set that fails to determine the requested geometry. It can, however, avoid adding unnecessary error of its own and can help expose the sensitive directions rather than conceal them.

Björck's career repeatedly returned to this partnership between invariance and computation. Least squares asks for the best approximation within a subspace. QR factorization constructs an orthogonal description of that subspace. Principal angles compare two such descriptions without depending on their arbitrary coordinate choices. Perturbation analysis tells us when the subspaces themselves are sensitive. Iterative refinement and reorthogonalization help recover numerical accuracy when the computation has failed to preserve all the structure available in the input.

There is a satisfying human continuity in the story. A Swedish school of numerical analysis developed around the insistence that error be studied rather than ignored. A visiting researcher at Stanford found a collaborator with a powerful singular-value algorithm. Their combined insight connected a classical geometric question to a practical computation. Later books, papers, and editorial work made that insight part of a larger discipline in which proofs, data, algorithms, and students continually challenge one another.

The result is a principle worth remembering whenever geometry enters a computer: the object we want to measure and the coordinates we happen to possess are different things. A good numerical method respects the distinction. It gives us the geometric answer whenever the available data support one, and it helps us recognize the limits when they do not.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Principal angles and the Björck–Golub reduction

For subspaces \(F,G\subseteq\mathbb C^m\) of dimensions \(p\ge q\), choose matrices \(Q_F\in\mathbb C^{m\times p}\), \(Q_G\in\mathbb C^{m\times q}\) with orthonormal columns. Form

\[
M=Q_F^*Q_G=Y\,\operatorname{diag}(c_1,\ldots,c_q)\,Z^*,\qquad
1\ge c_1\ge\cdots\ge c_q\ge0.
\]

The principal angles obey \(\cos\theta_k=c_k\), with paired principal vectors \(u_k=Q_Fy_k\) and \(v_k=Q_Gz_k\). Proof: for unit coefficient vectors \(x,z\), \(\langle Q_Fx,Q_Gz\rangle=x^*Mz\); unit norms and orthogonality constraints are preserved by the two isometries. Björck–Golub (1973), Theorem 1, pp. 582–583.

### 2. Sines without inverse-cosine cancellation

Let \(P_F=Q_FQ_F^*\). Then

\[
[(I-P_F)Q_G]^*[(I-P_F)Q_G]
=I-Q_G^*P_FQ_G=I-M^*M,
\]

so the singular values of \((I-P_F)Q_G\) are \(\sin\theta_1,\dots,\sin\theta_q\) (in reverse magnitude order). In the example \(F=\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)\), \(G=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\tfrac12 e_2+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}e_3)\),

\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix},\quad
\cos\Theta=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/2),\quad
\sin\Theta=\operatorname{diag}(0,\sqrt3/2).
\]

For \(\theta=10^{-8}\), \(1-\cos\theta\approx\theta^2/2=5\cdot10^{-17}\); rounding near unity can erase this difference in binary64. Computing a well-conditioned perpendicular component can preserve the displacement. The paper investigates both direct cosine and sine computation and different implementations of the perpendicular-component calculation; the literal expression \((I-P_F)Q_G\) need not be implemented by a naive cancellation-prone subtraction.

### 3. The rank-sensitive plane counterexample

For \(\varepsilon>0\), let

\[
A_\varepsilon=[e_1,\ e_1+\varepsilon e_2],\qquad
B_\varepsilon=[e_1,\ e_1+\varepsilon e_2+\varepsilon e_3].
\]

Then \(\|A_\varepsilon-B_\varepsilon\|_2=\varepsilon\), while \(\operatorname{range}(A_\varepsilon)=\operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2)\) and \(\operatorname{range}(B_\varepsilon)=\operatorname{span}(e_1,(e_2+e_3)/\sqrt2)\). Their principal angles are \(0,\pi/4\), independent of \(\varepsilon\). The sensitivity reflects \(\sigma_{\min}(A_\varepsilon)=O(\varepsilon)\).

The normal-equation Gram matrix for \(A_\varepsilon\) is

\[
A_\varepsilon^*A_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1+\varepsilon^2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For \(\varepsilon=10^{-8}\), binary64 arithmetic typically rounds \(1+\varepsilon^2\) to \(1\). QR can avoid explicitly squaring the critical small component.

### 4. Householder, MGS, and the 1973 comparison

The paper considers obtaining \(Q_F,Q_G\) by Householder transformations or modified Gram–Schmidt. The experiments report comparable principal-angle precision for the examined test problems, with MGS cheaper in arithmetic operations, but Householder yielding more accurately orthogonal principal vectors. This is a finding about the tested formulations, not a blanket ranking of every implementation. See paper abstract and Sections 5–7.

### 5. Canonical correlations and model spaces

Canonical correlations between two variable collections can be represented as cosines of principal angles between standardized column spaces, when the statistical inner product and centering conventions are properly handled. The finite-dimensional geometry above does not, by itself, encode inferential uncertainty or causation. The 1973 paper discusses canonical correlations in Section 2.

### 6. Conditionally reliable geometry

The central distinction is between (a) backward stability or other algorithmic accuracy relative to the input and (b) conditioning of a subspace determined by that input. Near a loss of rank, tiny perturbations can substantially change the column space. With strong spectral separation, principal angles of estimated subspaces can be meaningfully bounded by residuals or input perturbations. The lecture's \(\varepsilon\)-plane example demonstrates the first issue; Wedin and Davis–Kahan type perturbation results address the second under additional hypotheses.

### 8. Author recollection versus independent chronology

Björck's contribution to a collected volume honoring Golub recalls the outline of their collaboration and Golub's habit of producing an elegant scheme rapidly; it expressly says he cannot reconstruct the exact origin of their collaboration. Treat this as a participant's recollection, while the 1971 report and 1973 article provide independently dated publications.
