# Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy: How can an imperfect transformation be understood through exact geometry?

Imagine a perfectly rigid motion. A rotation of a room changes where everything points, yet preserves every distance and every angle. We can reverse it without losing information. Mathematics calls the corresponding transformation on a complex Hilbert space a unitary operator.

Now imagine a process that does lose information. It compresses a signal, attenuates a vibration, or sends several possible inputs toward the same output. Its mathematical description is a contraction: an operator that never increases the length of a vector. Such an operator need not be invertible, and it need not preserve any length beyond zero.

Can a lossy transformation be explained using a perfectly reversible one? Perhaps we have been looking at only part of a larger system. Perhaps the information that seems to disappear has traveled into directions we were not measuring.

This question leads to one of the central achievements of Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy, the Hungarian mathematician whose name is often abbreviated in mathematical writing to Béla Sz.-Nagy. In nineteen fifty-three, he proved that every contraction on a Hilbert space can be represented through the powers of a unitary operator on a suitably larger Hilbert space. The original process can be recovered by looking at that reversible motion and projecting back to the original space.

There is another question, seemingly quite different, that belongs to his earlier work. Suppose an operator changes a little, and its spectral subspace changes with it. Can we transport the old subspace into the new one by an actual unitary motion of the surrounding Hilbert space? In his nineteen forty-seven work on self-adjoint perturbations, Szőkefalvi-Nagy made a major contribution to this problem. He showed how sufficiently close orthogonal projections can be connected by a controlled unitary transformation.

We will follow both questions. The first asks how to carry geometry from one nearby subspace to another. The second asks how to find exact geometry behind an operator that appears to dissipate it. They belong to distinct theorems and distinct periods of his career, but both reveal an operator theorist's habit of making difficult transformations more understandable by placing them in the right geometric setting.

## A mathematical life centered on Szeged

Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy was born on July twenty-ninth, nineteen thirteen, in Kolozsvár, in Transylvania. The city is now called Cluj-Napoca and is in Romania. Political borders in that region changed dramatically after the First World War. The family moved to Szeged, in Hungary, in nineteen twenty-nine, when Béla was a teenager. His father, Gyula Szőkefalvi-Nagy, was also a mathematician and teacher.

His father's career gives a more specific reason for the family's move. Gyula had taught in Kolozsvár, but in nineteen twenty-nine he lost his position and moved to a teaching post in Szeged. Béla therefore entered the city as a teenager whose family's educational life had been altered by the political geography of Central Europe. His father would continue as a mathematician, and for a time the two generations worked within the same city's institutions. The son was not inheriting an abstract academic tradition: he was growing up within a family's practical struggle to sustain one.

Szeged offered a remarkable place to study mathematics. Frigyes Riesz had helped make the city a major center of functional analysis. Alfréd Haar, known for his work on orthogonal systems and the measure later named after him, was another formative presence. The mathematical life of the university revolved around a growing theory of functions, transformations, and infinite-dimensional spaces. Problems that had once appeared to concern entirely different subjects were becoming versions of the same questions about operators.

In nineteen thirty-six Szőkefalvi-Nagy completed his qualification to teach mathematics and physics. He earned his doctorate in nineteen thirty-seven and studied in Leipzig during nineteen thirty-seven and thirty-eight. By nineteen thirty-nine he had a teaching position in Szeged. He would remain deeply associated with that city's mathematical institutions for the rest of his career.

It is tempting to tell the history of operator theory as a string of discoveries made by isolated geniuses. The Szeged story is also a history of courses, seminars, editorial work, textbooks, and sustained collaboration. A mathematical school has to transmit its tools. New students must learn what a Hilbert space is, how a bounded operator behaves, why a spectrum can contain more than ordinary eigenvalues, and how an abstract theorem becomes a calculation they can use.

Szőkefalvi-Nagy's early book, published in nineteen forty-two, concerned the spectral representation of linear transformations on Hilbert space. That subject gave him a language for making the geometry of an operator precise. An operator may be described by its action on vectors, by its spectral behavior, or by how it sits inside a larger family of operators. Each description can illuminate something that the others hide.

In nineteen forty-five he became a corresponding member of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences. In nineteen forty-eight he became a professor and departmental leader at the University of Szeged, a position he held until his retirement in nineteen eighty-three. He later served as dean of the faculty of science during two periods. His research ranged across approximation theory, Fourier series, orthogonal function systems, and operator theory.

The scale of that career deserves emphasis. The names attached to his theorems may suggest a narrow specialty. In fact he worked in the broad analytical setting from which modern Hilbert-space operator theory grew. His books helped turn this setting into a shared mathematical language.

The university's own history describes him as severe and disciplined, a respected figure who nevertheless remained approachable. That combination gives the editorial and teaching work a more human scale. Maintaining a research journal, developing its technical standards, and helping build a library are demanding, mostly invisible tasks. A colleague who upheld exact standards in such work was supporting the same culture of precise argument that his operator theorems required.

## What is a subspace without a chosen basis?

Consider a plane with a horizontal line through the origin. We can describe that line by choosing a unit vector pointing to the right. But the line is still the same line if we reverse the arrow. We could also select a different coordinate system for the whole plane. The subspace is an object in its own right, independent of those choices.

An orthogonal projection gives a way to encode that object. Take any vector in the plane. Drop a perpendicular to the horizontal line and keep the component that lands on the line. The rule that performs this operation is the orthogonal projection onto the line. Call it P.

P has two important properties. Applying it twice has the same effect as applying it once, because a vector already on the line stays there. And it is self-adjoint, meaning that its action is symmetric with respect to the inner product. These two properties characterize orthogonal projections on a Hilbert space.

Now tilt the line upward through an angle of thirty degrees. Let Q project orthogonally onto the tilted line. We want to compare the original line with the new one. One possibility is to compare chosen unit vectors. But that comparison depends on which way we pointed the arrows. Comparing P and Q avoids the ambiguity.

For our two lines, the operator norm of their difference is exactly the sine of the angle between them. At thirty degrees that is one half. At sixty degrees it is the square root of three divided by two. At ninety degrees it reaches one.

The operator norm asks how much a transformation can move a unit vector at most. Thus the norm of P minus Q measures the largest possible difference between the projections of the same input. It is a distance between subspaces expressed entirely in the geometry of the ambient space.

In higher dimensions we can have several angles between two subspaces, called principal angles. One direction in the old subspace might have a nearly identical direction in the new one, while another could have moved considerably. When the dimensions match and the pair is in a suitable finite-dimensional setting, the norm of the projection difference is governed by the largest principal angle. This is why a single operator norm can measure the worst loss of alignment even when many directions are involved.

There is a limitation worth seeing early. A difference of projections cannot have norm greater than one. Yet reaching one is a serious boundary: the two subspaces may have a right-angle obstruction, or they may even have different dimensions. Small differences support a canonical kind of transport. At the boundary the geometry can change abruptly.

## From close projections to a real motion

Suppose two projections are close. Measuring their difference tells us how far apart the subspaces are. But what if we want to carry a vector in the old subspace into the new one? We need an actual transformation, preferably one that preserves all inner products. That is the unitary transport problem.

A naive attempt is to apply Q to vectors that lie in the range of P. If the two lines are close, Q sends a horizontal unit vector to a shorter vector along the tilted line. It points in the desired direction, but its length has changed. A mere projection is not a rotation.

For a two-dimensional example we could normalize that result. The trouble in a general Hilbert space is that the transport must work consistently for every vector at once. It must also do something sensible to the vectors orthogonal to the original subspace. A unitary transformation acts on the entire Hilbert space, including the complement of the part that interests us.

Szőkefalvi-Nagy's construction begins with a strikingly simple operator. Given P and Q, combine two pieces: first project into the old subspace and then into the new one; second project into the old orthogonal complement and then into the new orthogonal complement. Add those two operations. Call their sum S.

Why include the second piece? Think about the two perpendicular directions in the plane. If we rotate the horizontal line into the tilted line, the vertical direction must rotate along with it. The complement term arranges exactly that correspondence. On an input in the original subspace, S aims toward the new subspace. On an input in the original complement, S aims toward the new complement.

The operator S has the right destination for every piece of the space. But it can still shorten vectors. The solution is to separate its directional action from its stretching action. This is the polar decomposition: an operator is factored into a length-changing positive part and a length-preserving unitary part, when the relevant invertibility conditions hold.

For this particular S, the calculation is especially clean. Its squared length operator, S adjoint times S, is the identity minus the square of P minus Q. Consequently, if the norm of P minus Q is strictly smaller than one, S is invertible. The positive factor in its polar decomposition is also invertible. Removing that factor leaves a unitary operator U.

That unitary carries the range of P onto the range of Q. It carries the orthogonal complement of the first range onto the orthogonal complement of the second. In operator language, U intertwines the two projections. Apply P before U, or apply U and then Q, and you obtain the same result.

Return to the tilted line. With an angle of thirty degrees, this construction gives the familiar rotation through thirty degrees. A horizontal unit vector rotates to a unit vector on the tilted line; a vertical unit vector rotates to the perpendicular direction. Every length and angle is preserved. The construction does for abstract projections what a plane rotation does for two lines.

This is a geometric theorem with a precise threshold. The condition is that the norm of P minus Q is less than one. If the norm equals one, the proposed polar construction may cease to be invertible. We should avoid drawing the stronger conclusion that no unitary can ever connect the spaces. Two perpendicular lines in the plane can certainly be related by a ninety-degree rotation. What fails at the boundary is the guarantee of this particular near-identity transport; in other cases the ranges may have different dimensions and no unitary intertwiner can exist.

The power of the strict inequality is that it gives a uniform answer. We do not have to select and track a basis vector by vector. We do not have to decide which eigenvector has which sign. We can build the unitary from the two projections as operators. That makes the construction suitable for infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces as well as ordinary matrices.

## Why spectral perturbation leads to projections

A spectral subspace appears whenever we select a group of eigenvalues and collect their eigenvectors. In a symmetric matrix, for example, the largest eigenvalue might represent a strongest vibration mode or a direction of maximal energy. Several close eigenvalues might define a cluster whose combined subspace is more stable than any individually labeled eigenvector.

Suppose the matrix changes slightly. If the selected eigenvalues remain separated from the others, we can compare the original spectral subspace with the new one. The comparison becomes a comparison between two orthogonal projections.

For a simple illustration, take a two-by-two diagonal matrix with entries zero and three. Its coordinate axes are the spectral subspaces. Add a small off-diagonal perturbation. The eigenvectors tilt, and the spectral projections tilt with them. As long as the separation between the two eigenvalues remains substantial compared with the perturbation, the tilt is controlled. The exact angle can be calculated in this example, but the general problem asks for estimates that hold without explicitly solving a characteristic polynomial.

One of the major techniques in perturbation theory is to describe a spectral projection through the resolvent. Instead of selecting eigenvectors, consider the inverse of the operator minus a complex number. Integrate that inverse around a contour enclosing the desired part of the spectrum. The resulting operator is the spectral projection. When the operator changes and the contour stays away from the spectrum, the resolvent changes continuously under appropriate hypotheses. The spectral projection changes with it.

That procedure gives a route from a spectral gap to closeness of projections, and then from closeness of projections to a unitary transport. The steps have different purposes. The contour argument identifies which portion of the spectrum is being followed. The projection estimate controls how far the associated subspace has moved. The unitary construction turns the comparison into a motion of the whole ambient Hilbert space.

Szőkefalvi-Nagy's nineteen forty-seven paper, published in Commentarii Mathematici Helvetici, belongs to this development. The historical record also includes an earlier Hungarian version of part of the work. Its treatment of close orthogonal projections and analytically varying spectral subspaces contributed a mechanism that later perturbation theory could build upon.

One must keep the historical claims proportionate. Rellich had already developed analytic perturbation methods, and Riesz's resolvent techniques supplied essential tools. Szőkefalvi-Nagy's achievement in this line was a specific advance in implementing spectral-subspace transport. Later researchers, notably Tosio Kato, developed a broad modern perturbation theory. The nearby-projection result is one important part of that history rather than the origin of all spectral stability estimates.

## A useful warning about eigenvectors

Here is a reason that transporting a subspace can be more reliable than trying to transport each eigenvector individually. Imagine a symmetric matrix whose two largest eigenvalues are very close to one another, but both are far from the rest. A tiny perturbation can rotate the individual eigenvectors dramatically inside their shared two-dimensional subspace. It may even interchange which one is called the first eigenvector and which the second.

The two-dimensional subspace itself can remain stable. A projection onto the whole cluster sees the object that survives the perturbation. The labels within the cluster may be fragile, but the space spanned by those vectors can be well controlled.

This distinction has consequences in computation. An algorithm may return a different orthonormal basis for nearly the same subspace on two runs. Comparing its columns one by one could suggest a dramatic change when the geometric answer is almost unchanged. Comparing the orthogonal projections asks the invariant question. A unitary intertwiner then gives a way to identify the two spaces without pretending their chosen bases are uniquely determined.

There is a practical condition here: the selected spectral cluster must stay separated from its complement. Without an appropriate separation, the spectral projection can change discontinuously. And even with separation, the numerical size of the change depends on the perturbation and the gap. The transport theorem begins once the projections are known to be close; it does not generate an eigenvalue gap by itself.

We have seen one form of Szőkefalvi-Nagy's geometric thinking. Start with a transformation that may obscure the desired object, turn the object into a projection, and build a controlled map that preserves the relevant structure. His later work on contractions asks for a different enlargement of our geometric picture.


## What does it mean for an operator to lose length?

Consider a machine that takes a number and returns half that number. Apply it to ten and get five. Apply it again and get two and a half. Every time we repeat the process, its output has half the previous magnitude. This is the simplest possible example of a contraction.

A contraction is any bounded linear operator T on a Hilbert space whose operator norm is at most one. Equivalently, it never makes a vector longer. Rotations are contractions, because they preserve every length. Orthogonal projections are contractions, because they discard the perpendicular part. Strict shrinkings are contractions. More complicated operators can move and reshape vectors while remaining nonexpansive.

The word contraction sometimes suggests that every nonzero vector must get strictly shorter. The mathematical definition is broader. The identity operator is a contraction. A shift that moves every coordinate one place while preserving the total squared length is also a contraction. The condition places an upper bound on amplification, and leaves several kinds of behavior possible.

Imagine we have a unitary transformation U. It preserves all lengths and has an inverse given by its adjoint. If T strictly shrinks some vector, T itself cannot be unitary on the same space. Yet it might be the visible part of a unitary acting on a larger space. When the visible coordinates lose energy, the missing energy may have gone into invisible coordinates.

That explanation would be easy if we cared about only one application of T. We could take an input vector, add some extra output coordinates, and arrange to preserve the length. The problem is that the original machine can be run repeatedly. We want to recover the result after two steps, three steps, or a hundred steps. A proposed dilation must reproduce every power of T with the same single unitary motion.

This is the strength of Szőkefalvi-Nagy's unitary dilation theorem of nineteen fifty-three. Given a contraction T on a Hilbert space H, there is a larger Hilbert space K containing H and a unitary U on K such that, for every nonnegative integer n, T to the power n is obtained by applying U to the power n and then projecting back from K onto H.

We need the words 'for every power' in that statement. If they disappear, the theorem becomes far weaker. A single-step extension can be built with a small block matrix. Making all the steps agree is the mathematical challenge that turned dilation into a fundamental tool.

## A one-step reversible machine can give the wrong answer

Take the machine that multiplies a real number by three fifths. We can place the original number along the horizontal axis of a plane. Now rotate the plane through an angle whose cosine is three fifths and whose sine is four fifths. After the rotation, look only at the horizontal coordinate.

The visible output is three fifths of the original number. Perfect: a rigid motion has reproduced our contraction in one step. The missing four fifths in the perpendicular coordinate carries the rest of the squared length, so the full two-dimensional motion really is length-preserving.

But what happens after the second rotation? The horizontal coordinate is the cosine of twice that angle, which equals twice the square of three fifths, minus one. That works out to negative seven twenty-fifths. Repeating the original contraction twice would give positive nine twenty-fifths. The two answers are different, even in sign.

The failure is instructive. During the second rotation, the hidden coordinate is allowed to move back into the visible coordinate. Its influence changes the observed dynamics. The two-dimensional rotation was designed to imitate one step, and it has no reason to imitate the entire sequence of powers.

A successful dilation arranges the larger system so that the visible observations follow the original dynamics at every nonnegative time. Information may flow into additional degrees of freedom, but it must not return in a way that changes what the original contraction would have done. In general this requires a larger space with many more directions. For a strict scalar shrinking, an exact unitary dilation covering every power cannot be achieved in any fixed finite dimension.

This is one of those moments when infinite-dimensional geometry solves a problem that finite-dimensional geometry cannot solve in the same form. The extra dimensions are doing precise work. They allow the unitary process to carry a record of what has left the visible subsystem without forcing it to cycle back at a finite collection of frequencies.

## The shift: how zero can be the shadow of perfect motion

Let's build a complete dilation of the simplest possible contraction: the operator that sends every input to zero. At first glance zero seems maximally distant from a reversible transformation. Apply it once, and the entire input vanishes. Apply it again, and it remains vanished.

Picture an infinite row of numbered slots. There is a slot zero, slots one, two, three, and so on to the right, and slots minus one, minus two, minus three, and so on to the left. A state is a square-summable list of complex amplitudes, one at each integer. The total squared magnitude of all those amplitudes is its squared length.

Define U to shift every amplitude one slot to the right. A shift changes none of their values. It simply relocates them. Its inverse shifts everything left. Therefore U is unitary.

Now declare that the only part of this infinite row we can observe is slot zero. Our original Hilbert space is one-dimensional, represented by states supported at slot zero. Start with an amplitude in that slot. After one application of U, the amplitude has moved to slot one. Project back onto slot zero, and the result is zero. After two steps the amplitude is in slot two, so the projection is still zero. The same argument works after any positive number of steps.

Thus the zero operator, raised to every positive power, is exactly what we see by compressing powers of a completely reversible shift. No amplitude was destroyed in the full space. It simply left the observed coordinate and continued moving away.

There is something beautifully concrete about this example. The entire dilation theorem says that every contraction admits some version of this larger reversible perspective. The details become more complicated when different input directions attenuate differently, or when the operator also mixes those directions. The basic principle remains visible in the shift.

A related example is the unilateral shift. Start with a square-summable sequence indexed by zero, one, two, and so on, and shift everything right, inserting a zero in the first position. This operation preserves length but is not onto: a sequence with a nonzero first coordinate has no preimage. By extending the sequence to include all negative indices, we obtain the bilateral shift, which is unitary. The unilateral shift becomes the forward restriction of that larger motion. This illustrates how a missing inverse can sometimes be supplied by a larger past.

The zero contraction and the unilateral shift display two different defects: loss of visible amplitude, and failure to reach every output. A complete dilation theory has to accommodate both.

## Where does the missing length go?

For a general contraction T, consider the difference between the squared length of a vector and the squared length after applying T. Because T is a contraction, the difference is nonnegative. The operator identity behind that fact is that the identity minus T adjoint times T is positive.

Positive operators have positive square roots. Take the positive square root of the identity minus T adjoint times T. This is called the defect operator of T. Applied to a vector, it measures the part of the length budget that the contraction failed to preserve.

The central identity can be heard without symbols. The squared length of the original vector equals the squared length of its image under T plus the squared length of its image under the defect operator. The two numbers add up exactly.

That identity suggests a way to restore lost information. Send the input vector to a pair of outputs. Put T of the vector in the visible output, and put the defect-operator output in a new hidden channel. The pair has the same total length as the input. We have turned a contraction into an isometric map by allowing an additional channel.

But an isometry need not be onto. We may need still more channels, and eventually an infinite sequence of them, to make the full map unitary and make all powers work. The one-step defect identity shows how to begin. The dilation theorem gives the architecture in which that beginning can be continued consistently through every future step.

There is also a second defect operator, built from the identity minus T times T adjoint. The distinction between the two is important for a general operator, because the failure to preserve length and the failure to fill the target space need not be the same. They coincide for a simple scalar shrinking, but a shift shows why the two viewpoints differ.

In the block constructions associated with this theory, the two defect operators appear together. They allow a reversible transformation to exchange information between the visible and hidden channels while preserving the Hilbert-space inner product. A two-by-two block matrix combines these operations, and its unitarity can be checked by multiplying it by its adjoint.

The characteristic idea is that a contraction has an internal geometry of defect spaces. These spaces are not bookkeeping invented by the analyst. They measure concrete failures of isometry and coisometry, and they carry the information required to build a dilation.

## A scalar shrinking and the circle

There is a second example that shows why the word harmonic belongs in this story. Let T multiply a complex number by a fixed real number r, where r lies strictly between zero and one. Then applying T n times multiplies the input by r to the power n. The observed magnitude decays exponentially with the number of steps.

The zero-operator shift was easy because every positive-time observation was zero. Here we want a prescribed nonzero value at every time: r, then r squared, then r cubed, and so on. Where can we find a unitary motion whose successive observations produce that exact sequence?

A unitary multiplication operator on a space of functions on the circle provides the answer. Imagine a complex-valued wave defined around a circle. At each step multiply that wave by its position on the unit circle, regarded as a complex number of magnitude one. Multiplication by a number of magnitude one preserves the squared magnitude of the wave at every position. The full operation is unitary.

The choice of how we average around the circle determines what we observe. There is a family of probability weights called Poisson kernels. For a weight corresponding to r, the average of the n-th power of the circle coordinate is exactly r to the power n for every nonnegative integer n.

Represent our original one-dimensional state by a constant function. Apply the unitary multiplication operator n times. Now project back onto the constant functions, meaning take the weighted average around the circle. The result is the original constant multiplied by r to the power n. Every power agrees.

No individual point of the circle is shrinking. Each point carries a phase that rotates without loss. Yet the weighted average of all those phases becomes smaller and smaller as their contributions increasingly cancel. The decay of the observed scalar is an interference effect within a larger reversible system.

This example makes a bridge to Fourier analysis. The successive powers of a unitary operator behave like oscillatory functions, and the observed contraction is encoded in their moments. It also shows why a collection of infinitely many phases can produce decay that a finite list of phases cannot reproduce exactly for all time.

The shift example and the circle example describe the same general idea from different angles. One uses positions on an infinite chain; the other uses frequencies on a circle. Shifts and Fourier multipliers are tightly related. Szőkefalvi-Nagy's dilation theorem made this kind of connection systematic for arbitrary Hilbert-space contractions.

## The mathematical benefit of dilating an operator

Why devote so much effort to replacing a contraction with a unitary on a larger space? Because unitary operators are especially well understood. Their spectra lie on the unit circle in the complex plane. They admit a spectral representation, and continuous functions of the operator can be analyzed using the familiar language of functions on the circle.

Suppose we want to estimate a polynomial expression in T. We might take T squared, add three times T, subtract the identity, or choose a more complicated polynomial. If every power of T is the compression of the corresponding power of U, the same is true of the polynomial. The polynomial of T is the projection of the polynomial of U back to the original space.

Projection cannot increase operator norm. Therefore the norm of the polynomial of T is at most the norm of the polynomial of U. The spectral theorem then says that the latter is controlled by the largest absolute value of the polynomial on the unit circle. By the maximum modulus principle, this is at most the largest absolute value on the closed unit disk.

We arrive at an inequality associated historically with John von Neumann: for a contraction T, the norm of any complex polynomial evaluated at T is bounded by the largest magnitude of that polynomial on the unit disk. The inequality has its own history. Unitary dilation provides an illuminating mechanism for proving it.

For a concrete case, let the polynomial be one plus z. The inequality says that the operator norm of the identity plus T is at most two. We can see that particular estimate from the triangle inequality. The power of the general result is that it holds for every polynomial simultaneously and exploits the complex-analytic structure of the disk.

Another example uses the polynomial one minus z squared. For a contraction, the bound is at most two because the polynomial has magnitude no greater than two on the closed disk. The method extends far beyond these simple cases, and its value grows when the polynomial has cancellation or when direct estimates on powers would be awkward.

A spectral theorem for a unitary can also reveal the structure of a contraction through Fourier-analytic methods. This is a different kind of leverage from estimating a nearby subspace. In one story, we transform a projection comparison into a unitary transport. In the other, we transform non-unitary dynamics into observations of a unitary evolution. Both are ways to trade a difficult operator problem for one with a more rigid geometry.

## Collaboration with Ciprian Foiaș

Szőkefalvi-Nagy's nineteen fifty-three paper was the beginning of a much larger development. Over the following years, the theory of contractions evolved from a powerful existence theorem into a framework for describing operators by their internal structure. An important collaborator was the Romanian mathematician Ciprian Foiaș, who joined this line of research in the late nineteen fifties.

Their joint work led to a sequence of papers and to a major book published in French in nineteen sixty-seven, later known in English as Harmonic Analysis of Operators on Hilbert Space. An English edition appeared in nineteen seventy. Later editions and extensions brought further contributors into the development of the theory.

The title reflects the central strategy. Unitary dilations make Fourier analysis and function theory available to questions about general contractions. An operator can be understood through how it sits inside a larger unitary system, through the defect spaces attached to it, and through analytic functions that encode its action.

One of the important objects in the Szőkefalvi-Nagy–Foiaș theory is the characteristic function of a contraction. It is an operator-valued analytic function of a complex variable. Roughly speaking, it records how the visible dynamics interacts with the two defect spaces as the complex parameter changes. For suitable classes of completely non-unitary contractions, it provides a powerful invariant for classification up to unitary equivalence.

We should be careful with that description. An arbitrary contraction can have a reducing subspace on which it is already unitary. The characteristic-function model is most informative after separating the unitary part from the completely non-unitary part. It is also a sophisticated development, with hypotheses and distinctions beyond what our examples alone establish.

The gain was a language that linked operator theory with complex function theory. Questions about invariant subspaces, factorization, and functional calculus could be approached using analytic functions and the geometry of defect spaces. The methods later became relevant in interpolation theory and in mathematical systems and control theory, where one also studies how hidden internal states produce observable input-output behavior.

The collaboration is important to the historical portrait. The fundamental dilation theorem came from Szőkefalvi-Nagy's earlier work; the richer harmonic-analysis and model-theoretic framework was built through sustained research by Szőkefalvi-Nagy, Foiaș, and other mathematicians. A book bearing two names can mark the creation of a research tradition, not merely the consolidation of one isolated proof.

## A textbook and a school of analysis

Another collaboration reveals a different side of Szőkefalvi-Nagy's work. In nineteen fifty-two, he and Frigyes Riesz published Leçons d'analyse fonctionnelle, a large text on functional analysis. Their own preface explains that it grew out of courses taught over many years. Riesz was principally responsible for the first part, on real functions and integration; Szőkefalvi-Nagy was principally responsible for the second, on integral equations, linear functionals, and operators. The parts were designed to form a coherent whole.

That organization tells us something about what functional analysis had become. One begins by learning to handle functions and limits with enough care to make infinite processes legitimate. Then one treats entire functions as points in linear spaces. Integral equations become operator equations. Spectral questions become questions about transformations of these spaces.

The book was translated and republished across languages and generations. Its longevity suggests how valuable that synthesis was to mathematicians learning a field that was still taking its modern form. A theorem might show that a particular transformation exists; a good textbook teaches readers how to recognize the many situations in which the theorem's viewpoint applies.

Szőkefalvi-Nagy also held institutional responsibilities in Szeged and within the Hungarian Academy of Sciences. He was a corresponding member of the academy from nineteen forty-five and a full member from nineteen fifty-six. His research continued through several decades in which mathematical communication crossed political and national boundaries under difficult conditions. He maintained a substantial scientific career rooted in Szeged while participating in an international conversation about operators and analysis.

Beginning in nineteen forty-seven he spent decades helping edit Acta Scientiarum Mathematicarum and later helped establish another journal, Analysis Mathematica. This labor was inseparable from the survival of Szeged as an international mathematical center: research moves through manuscripts, libraries, correspondence, and the sometimes exhausting discipline of revision. The man best known for exact geometric constructions also helped sustain the channels through which those constructions could be checked and taught.

He received several major honors, including the Lomonosov Gold Medal in nineteen seventy-nine. Recognition came for more than the single theorem about projections or the single theorem about dilations. It reflected a body of work and a sustained role in the mathematical community.

When he died in Szeged on December twenty-first, nineteen ninety-eight, he left several overlapping legacies: a perturbation result still used to understand close spectral subspaces, a dilation theorem that reshaped operator theory, a theory of contractions developed with collaborators, and textbooks and institutions that made those tools available to others.

## Two geometries, and a boundary to each

Let us return to our two original problems. In the first, we knew a pair of nearby subspaces. The orthogonal projections P and Q described those subspaces without choosing bases. When the norm of their difference was strictly below one, a construction using both the ranges and their complements produced a unitary that transported the first projection to the second.

The idea was to find a geometric motion that faithfully carries the object being perturbed. It works locally, under a strict closeness condition. At the norm-one boundary the natural formula can become singular, and separate information is required to decide whether any suitable unitary exists.

In the second problem, we knew a contraction T, which could lose visible length or fail to have an inverse. The nineteen fifty-three dilation theorem gave a larger Hilbert space and a unitary U whose every nonnegative power, when projected back, agreed with the corresponding power of T. The added dimensions could retain information that the observed dynamics seemed to discard.

The first unitary lives on the original Hilbert space and transports between two projections. The second unitary usually lives on a larger Hilbert space and explains the dynamics of one contraction. Confusing these two theorems would obscure their different hypotheses and uses. Seeing both together reveals a repeated geometric method: use exact, reversible transformations to understand objects that are changing, compressed, or only partially observed.

There is a final lesson in the mathematics. Projections, contractions, and unitaries are related, but they are not interchangeable. Projecting loses components. Contracting limits amplification. Acting unitarily preserves all inner products. Szőkefalvi-Nagy learned how to connect these three operations with enough precision to get substantial theorems out of the connections.

A subspace can rotate while keeping its dimension and internal geometry. A contraction can appear to dissipate information while arising as the observation of a larger reversible motion. And a mathematical school can preserve insights over decades by turning them into arguments, courses, books, and new research problems. Those are three aspects of the life and work of Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

The spoken lecture is deliberately basis-independent and equation-light. These notes
state the mathematics precisely, distinguish two different unitary constructions,
and record the source trail.

### 1. Nearby orthogonal projections: the Sz.-Nagy intertwiner

Let \(P,Q\in\mathcal B(H)\) be orthogonal projections on the same real or
complex Hilbert space. Put \(d=\|P-Q\|\) and define

\[
S=QP+(I-Q)(I-P)=I-P-Q+2QP.
\]

Direct multiplication and \(P^2=P=P^*\), \(Q^2=Q=Q^*\) yield

\[
SP=QP=QS,\qquad
S^*S=SS^*=I-(P-Q)^2.
\]

The positive operator \(I-(P-Q)^2\) commutes with both \(P\) and \(Q\).
If \(d<1\), then

\[
S^*S\succeq(1-d^2)I>0,
\quad
U=S\big(I-(P-Q)^2\big)^{-1/2}
\]

is unitary and \(UP=QU\), equivalently \(UPU^*=Q\).
Here \(\|S^{-1}\|\le(1-d^2)^{-1/2}\). The unitarity follows since
\(S\) is normal and invertible; intertwining follows from the displayed
relations and commutation of the polar correction with \(P\).

For two lines of angle \(\theta\in[0,\pi/2)\), write

\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q=\begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\theta&\sin\theta\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\cos\theta&\sin^2\theta\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then \(\|P-Q\|=\sin\theta\), \(S=\cos\theta R_\theta\) where
\(R_\theta\) is the standard plane rotation matrix, and \(U=R_\theta\).
The construction is canonical for \(d<1\), but \(d=1\) does not prevent
all possible unitary equivalences. In the plane, perpendicular lines are
unitarily equivalent although \(S=0\). On \(\mathbb C^2\), \(P=I\) and
\(Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)\) have \(d=1\) and cannot be unitarily
conjugate because their ranks differ.

The attribution of the close-projection analytic intertwiner to Sz.-Nagy's
1946/47 work is specifically corroborated in Barry Simon's 2018 history of
Kato's perturbation theory. A nineteenth-century-to-modern spectral-gap
story should not assign Rellich's earlier analytic perturbation work or later
Kato–Davis–Kahan results retroactively to Sz.-Nagy.

### 2. Two-dimensional spectral rotation

Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(0,3)\) and
\(\widehat A=\begin{pmatrix}0&\varepsilon\\\varepsilon&3\end{pmatrix}\).
For the eigenvectors associated with the separated spectral branches,
\(\tan(2\theta)=2\varepsilon/3\) with the appropriate branch convention.
For \(|\varepsilon|\ll3\), \(|\theta|\approx |\varepsilon|/3\). Thus
\(\|P-\widehat P\|=|\sin\theta|\approx |\varepsilon|/3\). This
example illustrates the role of separation without asserting an identical
estimate for arbitrary unbounded self-adjoint operators.

For a bounded operator \(A\) and a contour \(\Gamma\) avoiding its
spectrum, the Riesz spectral projection associated with the enclosed
spectrum is

\[
P_\Gamma(A)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\Gamma(zI-A)^{-1}\,dz.
\]

For self-adjoint \(A\) and an isolated spectral cluster, this is an
orthogonal projection. Its stability under perturbation can be studied
using the resolvent identity

\[
(zI-\widehat A)^{-1}-(zI-A)^{-1}
=(zI-\widehat A)^{-1}(\widehat A-A)(zI-A)^{-1}.
\]

Analytic continuation and the projection intertwiner are related but
distinct steps. An existence theorem for \(U\) based on \(d<1\) does
not by itself furnish the best possible \(d\)-versus-gap inequality.

### 3. Power dilation, rather than a one-step block extension

A contraction \(T\in\mathcal B(H)\) satisfies \(\|T\|\le1\). The
Sz.-Nagy dilation theorem gives a Hilbert space \(K\supseteq H\) and a
unitary \(U\in\mathcal B(K)\) for which

\[
T^n=P_HU^n\big|_H,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
\]

The original 1953 article states this as Theorem I on page 88, and also
presents \((T^*)^n=P_HU^{-n}|_H\). The displayed \(T^n\) condition
for every \(n\) is stronger than \(T=P_HU|_H\). It is a *power*
dilation, not just a one-step compression.

Set \(T=r\) on \(H=\mathbb C\), with \(r=3/5\). The real plane
rotation \(R=\begin{pmatrix}r&-\sqrt{1-r^2}\\
\sqrt{1-r^2}&r\end{pmatrix}\) satisfies \(P_HR|_H=T\), whereas

\[
P_HR^2|_H=2r^2-1=-7/25\ne 9/25=T^2.
\]

This exposes exactly why a one-step dilation is inadequate.

### 4. Defect spaces and a unitary block

Define the two defect operators

\[
D_T=(I-T^*T)^{1/2},\qquad D_{T^*}=(I-TT^*)^{1/2}.
\]

For every \(x\in H\),

\[
\|Tx\|^2+\|D_Tx\|^2=\|x\|^2.
\]

The Julia/Halmos block operator on \(H\oplus H\),

\[
J_T=\begin{pmatrix}T&D_{T^*}\\D_T&-T^*\end{pmatrix},
\]

is unitary. Its off-diagonal cancellation uses
\(T D_T=D_{T^*}T\), which follows by continuous functional calculus.
The top-left compression of \(J_T\) is \(T\), but \(J_T\) is **not in
general a power dilation**. The bilateral extension and additional
channels of the full theorem are needed to enforce every power identity.

### 5. Complete dilation of the zero contraction

Take \(K=\ell^2(\mathbb Z)\) and let \(e_j\), \(j\in\mathbb Z\), be
its standard basis. Put \(Ue_j=e_{j+1}\). This bilateral shift is
unitary, and \(H=\operatorname{span}\{e_0\}\) satisfies

\[
P_HU^0|_H=I_H,
\qquad P_HU^n|_H=0\quad(n\ge1).
\]

Thus the compression of the powers of one unitary exactly realizes all
powers of the zero operator on \(H\). By contrast, the unilateral shift
on \(\ell^2(\mathbb N_0)\) is isometric but not surjective; its natural
unitary extension is the bilateral shift.

### 6. Scalar contraction as Fourier moments of a circle measure

For \(0<r<1\), introduce the Poisson probability measure

\[
d\mu_r(t)=\frac{1-r^2}{1-2r\cos t+r^2}\frac{dt}{2\pi},
\qquad t\in[0,2\pi).
\]

On \(K=L^2([0,2\pi),\mu_r)\), let \(U f(t)=e^{it}f(t)\) and let
\(H=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf1\}\). Then \(U\) is unitary and

\[
P_HU^n\mathbf1
=\left(\int_0^{2\pi} e^{int}\,d\mu_r(t)\right)\mathbf1
=r^n\mathbf1\qquad(n\ge0).
\]

The Poisson kernel's Fourier coefficients are \(r^{|n|}\), so this
makes an explicit unitary power dilation of scalar multiplication by
\(r\). No finite-dimensional unitary provides these exact moments for
all \(n\ge0\) if \(0<r<1\): if
\(\langle U^n e,e\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^m w_je^{in\theta_j}\) with
\(w_j>0\), \(\sum w_j=1\), the Cesàro means of the squared magnitudes
have a positive limit \(\sum w_j^2\) after grouping equal eigenvalues,
whereas \(r^{2n}\to0\).

### 7. Dilation implies von Neumann's polynomial inequality

For any complex polynomial \(p\), linearity and the simultaneous power
identities imply \(p(T)=P_Hp(U)|_H\). Consequently

\[
\|p(T)\|\le\|p(U)\|
=\max_{z\in\sigma(U)}|p(z)|
\le\max_{|z|=1}|p(z)|
=\max_{|z|\le1}|p(z)|.
\]

This is von Neumann's inequality for a single Hilbert-space contraction;
its historical development predates Sz.-Nagy's power-dilation proof.
It does **not** claim an identical estimate for arbitrary noncommuting
*tuples* of contractions. The 1953 paper explicitly identifies
von Neumann's earlier result as an application of Theorem I.

### 8. Distinct contributions and chronology

- **1913:** Born July 29 in Kolozsvár, now Cluj-Napoca, Romania.
- **1929:** Family moves to Szeged. He studies in the school shaped by
  Frigyes Riesz and Alfréd Haar; his father Gyula is also a mathematician.
- **1936–1939:** Mathematics/physics teaching qualification (1936), PhD
  (1937), Leipzig study (1937–38), teaching career at Szeged beginning 1939.
- **1942:** *Spektraldarstellung linearer Transformationen des Hilbertschen
  Raumes*, a monograph on spectral representation.
- **1945–1948:** Corresponding member of the Hungarian Academy (1945);
  university professorship and department head in Szeged (1948).
- **1946/47:** *Perturbations des transformations autoadjointes dans
  l'espace de Hilbert*, *Commentarii Mathematici Helvetici* **19**,
  347–366. Close-projection unitary intertwining in the Hilbert setting
  appears in this line of work; Barry Simon records an earlier 1942
  Hungarian version of the self-adjoint perturbation work.
- **1952:** Riesz–Sz.-Nagy, *Leçons d'analyse fonctionnelle*, a two-part
  textbook based on long-established courses. The preface explains the
  authors' principal division of writing responsibility.
- **1953:** *Sur les contractions de l'espace de Hilbert*, *Acta Sci. Math.*
  **15**, 87–92, with Theorem I giving all-power unitary dilation. Some
  digitized repositories label the journal volume **1954**; the article
  self-references and standard mathematical histories cite the paper as
  **1953**. The bibliography should keep the volume and page numbers in
  addition to the historically standard year.
- **1958–1970:** Research series with Ciprian Foiaș, including functional
  models and analytic characteristic functions; 1967 French book
  *Analyse harmonique des opérateurs de l'espace de Hilbert*, revised
  English edition in 1970.
- **1956–1983:** Full member of the Hungarian Academy (1956); long-term
  professor and departmental leader at Szeged through retirement (1983).
- **1979:** Lomonosov Gold Medal.
- **1998:** Died December 21 in Szeged.

**Attribution boundary:** Sz.-Nagy's 1947 nearby-projection construction and
1953 dilation are different results. The analytic perturbation groundwork
includes Rellich and resolvent methods associated with Riesz. Later direct
rotation and subspace-angle bounds have further authors. The Sz.-Nagy–Foiaș
characteristic-function and model theory is a joint and subsequent research
program. Von Neumann's polynomial inequality is a distinct earlier theorem,
which unitary dilation supplies a direct proof of.
