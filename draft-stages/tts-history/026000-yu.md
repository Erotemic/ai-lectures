# Yi Yu: Can a statistical eigenspace bound use a population gap alone?

Suppose a hospital records fifty measurements for every patient. The investigators suspect that much of the variation can be summarized by a few combinations of those measurements. They estimate a covariance matrix from their sample and compute its leading eigenvectors. Then a harder question begins. How closely do those computed directions represent the directions that would emerge if they had data on the entire population?

There are two matrices in that question. One is the population covariance, which is fixed in the mathematical model but unknown to the investigator. The other is the estimate computed from a finite sample. The entries differ because observations are random. Even if the entries are close, the leading eigenvectors need not be close when the population eigenvalues are crowded together.

An attractive solution would use two quantities: the size of the estimation error, and a gap between eigenvalues of the population covariance. Both are natural objects for a statistical proof. Yet the version of a classical perturbation theorem routinely used in statistics contained a more awkward ingredient: a separation involving the observed eigenvalues as well as the population ones. To invoke it, an author often had to prove a second result before applying the first.

In a 2015 paper in Biometrika, Yi Yu, Tengyao Wang and Richard Samworth developed a variant of the Davis–Kahan theorem that puts the denominator entirely in terms of the population spectrum. Its importance lies partly in the inequality and partly in how well the inequality fits the structure of statistical reasoning. The paper also addressed rectangular matrices, where singular vectors replace the eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix.

Yi Yu's research career provides a particularly good way to understand that change of emphasis. It begins with high-dimensional variable selection, moves through spectral methods and the geometry of estimation, and extends to detecting changes in data streams, analyzing networks, and deciding what reliable inference means when privacy or contamination limits the information available. Across those subjects, a recurring task is to turn an exact mathematical description into a guarantee for something we can only estimate.

## Fudan, Cambridge, and the questions of high dimension

Yi Yu studied mathematics at Fudan University in Shanghai, earning a bachelor's degree in 2009 and a doctorate in mathematical statistics in 2013. Her doctoral supervisor was Zhiliang Ying, and her thesis concerned contributions to high-dimensional variable selection. Her training placed her at the center of an emerging challenge in statistics: how to reason about datasets containing more candidate explanatory variables than observations.

Why was variable selection an important problem in the early twenty-first century? In classical regression, one often imagines that there are substantially more observations than explanatory variables. With sufficient data and an invertible design matrix, least squares can estimate a coefficient for every variable. But large biomedical, genetic and economic datasets may record thousands of candidate predictors, sometimes more than the number of observations. In that setting, there are many different coefficient vectors that fit the same data. Identifying a useful prediction rule requires additional structure.

A popular assumption is sparsity. Perhaps only a small fraction of recorded variables carry a substantial signal, and the others have zero or negligible coefficients. The lasso method searches for a vector that balances goodness of fit against the sum of the absolute values of its coefficients. The penalty favors zeros and may produce a model that is easier to estimate and interpret. Yet this creates new mathematical questions. How strong must the signal be? What relationships among predictors make a sparse solution identifiable? Does the method select the right variables, and how does its error behave when the number of variables grows?

Yu's early publications, including work with Jian Huang, Tingni Sun, Zhiliang Ying and Cun-Hui Zhang on oracle inequalities for the lasso in the Cox survival model, belong to this research setting. An oracle inequality compares an estimator with a hypothetical benchmark that knows something valuable, such as a good sparse approximation, ahead of time. It offers a quantitative way to say that a feasible data-driven method performs nearly as well as a well-informed benchmark, under stated assumptions.

The Cox model is itself a reminder that data do not all look like ordinary regression tables. Survival data may include censored observations: a participant leaves a study before an event occurs, so the exact event time is unknown. Inference has to accommodate what was and was not observed. When the number of candidate variables is large, ordinary low-dimensional formulas may require modification. Yu and her coauthors developed results in which sparsity and the partial-likelihood structure enter the mathematics together.

Her work with Yang Feng on computational paths for penalized likelihood and on modified cross-validation also addressed the practical side of selecting models. A sequence of penalty values generates a sequence of fitted models. Computing every model from scratch wastes structure; choosing the penalty using held-out data is itself a statistical procedure whose behavior has to be studied. Already, the career involved two directions at once: proving that a method behaves well, and designing a method a scientist can actually run.

## A collaboration in statistical operator theory

After her doctorate, Yu worked as a postdoctoral researcher with Richard Samworth at Cambridge, beginning in 2013. Tengyao Wang was undertaking doctoral research in the same statistical environment. Their collaboration produced the 2015 article A Useful Variant of the Davis–Kahan Theorem for Statisticians, first circulated as a preprint in 2014.

To see the issue, consider a population covariance matrix with eigenvalues ten and four. The leading eigendirection is the direction along which population variation is largest. The second eigendirection is perpendicular to it. The gap is six. Suppose the estimated covariance differs from the population covariance by a small symmetric error. We want to know how much the leading direction can rotate.

In two dimensions the calculation is transparent. Let the population covariance have ten in its first diagonal position and four in its second. Add an off-diagonal error of one. The estimated matrix has ten and four on its diagonal, and one in both off-diagonal positions. Its leading eigenvector turns through about nine point two degrees from the original horizontal direction. The population eigengap of six resists the rotation. A perturbation with the same off-diagonal size, but a population gap of only one, can cause a much larger turn.

Why is the gap so important? An eigenvector is an extremizer of a quadratic form. When the best direction has a much larger value than competing directions, moving away from it costs substantial variance. When two directions have nearly equal variance, many mixtures of the two are almost equally good. A small estimation error can change which mixture wins. The data may be quite accurate entry by entry while the reported direction is unstable.

The classical Davis–Kahan theory, developed in 1970 by Chandler Davis and William Kahan, gave precise ways to control angles between invariant subspaces of self-adjoint operators. It was more general than finite covariance matrices. One widely used statistical form involved a denominator constructed from population eigenvalues in a selected block and sample eigenvalues outside it. A probabilistic proof could then become entangled with accidental crossings in the estimated spectrum.

Yu, Wang and Samworth focused on the consecutive population eigenvalue block one wishes to estimate. Their result uses the gaps between that block and the eigenvalues immediately above and below it, whenever those neighbors exist. Those are population numbers. Inside the block, eigenvalues are allowed to coincide. We must estimate a subspace, rather than insist that individual directions within a tied block are uniquely determined.

That distinction is foundational. If the second and third population eigenvalues are equal, the second and third eigenvectors are not individually identifiable: any orthonormal basis of their two-dimensional eigenspace is equally valid. Yet the two-dimensional space itself may be sharply separated from the first and fourth eigendirections. A theorem about the angle between spaces respects this geometry, while a theorem purporting to identify each basis vector may ask an impossible question.

## What an angle between spaces measures

For two lines through the origin, the acute angle is easy to imagine. The lines agree at zero degrees and are perpendicular at ninety. For two subspaces of dimension greater than one, there is a list of principal angles. The smallest measures their closest alignment; the largest measures their most divergent direction. One can calculate the cosines of these angles using singular values of a matrix made from orthonormal bases for the two spaces.

Let the population subspace have an orthonormal basis matrix V, and let the estimated subspace have an orthonormal basis matrix V hat. Their columns may differ even when the spaces agree. For example, rotating the two basis columns within the same plane changes the basis but not the plane. Therefore a useful error measure should ignore those harmless internal rotations.

The sines of the principal angles do exactly that. If all the angles are zero, the subspaces coincide. If a direction is nearly perpendicular to its counterpart, its sine is near one. To summarize all the angles, one may take the square root of the sum of their squared sines. This is the Frobenius norm of the sine-angle matrix. It measures the discrepancy of the entire estimated subspace.

There is also a related distance between particular basis matrices: rotate the columns of V hat by the best possible small orthogonal matrix, and then compare the aligned columns with V. This is useful when an application reports several component loadings. The freedom to align them is essential; otherwise an arbitrary sign flip or rotation inside a subspace can appear to be a large estimation error.

For two unit vectors representing lines, an elementary identity explains the comparison. Orient the vectors so their inner product is nonnegative. The squared distance between the vectors is two minus twice the cosine of their angle. The squared sine is one minus the squared cosine. Over angles from zero to ninety degrees, the vector distance is at most the square root of two times the sine. The multi-dimensional version uses a singular value decomposition to choose the best alignment.

The 2015 theorem gives both a principal-angle bound and a corresponding aligned-basis bound. The first is often the more fundamental statistical statement. The second is a convenient consequence when a proof must compare individual coordinates after the arbitrariness of basis choice has been handled.

## A denominator the statistician can reason about

Here is the structure of the population-gap bound. Let the target eigenvectors occupy positions r through s in decreasing eigenvalue order. Suppose there is a strictly positive gap between the selected block and the adjacent population eigenvalues outside it. Write d for the number of selected directions. Then the total sine-angle error is at most twice the smaller of two perturbation measures, divided by that population gap.

One perturbation measure is the Frobenius norm of the whole matrix error. The other is the operator norm multiplied by the square root of d. That second choice is important when the ambient matrix is enormous but the target subspace has only a few dimensions. The Frobenius norm sums error over all entries; the operator norm measures the largest effect along any unit direction. A matrix can have moderate operator norm and a very large Frobenius norm when errors extend over many coordinates.

Consider a diagonal perturbation of a hundred-dimensional covariance matrix. Suppose the error has size point one in every diagonal entry. Its operator norm is point one, because no unit vector can be enlarged by more than point one. Its Frobenius norm is one, since one hundred squared errors of point zero one sum to one. For a single target direction, the smaller numerator is point one, ten times less than the Frobenius alternative. For a block of four directions, the operator-norm alternative is point two. The size of the ambient matrix does not automatically appear as a square-root penalty.

The theorem does not promise that every estimate will be accurate. If the population eigengap is close to zero, the upper bound may be large. If the matrix estimation error is substantial, the same happens. Indeed, the bound can exceed the maximum possible sine-angle error and thus become mathematically true but uninformative. The point is that the assumptions and denominator now fit a statistical model whose population covariance is the reference object.

This also clarifies the difference between an algebraic inequality and a statistical confidence statement. The angle theorem holds deterministically for any pair of symmetric matrices satisfying its conditions. It says nothing by itself about how likely the estimated covariance is to be near the population covariance. That probability must come from a separate concentration theorem, sampling model, or argument about the estimator.

The composition is powerful. First control the matrix-estimation error with high probability. Then divide that error by a population gap to control the subspace angle on the same event. If one uses an estimator designed for structured covariance matrices, such as a banded or tapered estimator, its specific error bounds can be inserted. The matrix geometry and the probability calculation have separate roles. This is one reason a convenient deterministic formulation can simplify an entire literature of statistical proofs.


## Why the proof can use an external population gap

The proof idea is accessible even if the complete block calculation takes several pages. Decompose the ambient space into the population target subspace and its perpendicular complement. Inside the target, the population covariance acts with the selected eigenvalues. Inside the complement, it acts with the unselected eigenvalues. The assumed population gap says that every selected eigenvalue is separated from every eigenvalue in the relevant complementary spectral block.

The estimated eigenvectors satisfy an eigenvalue equation for the estimated matrix. Replace the estimated matrix by the population matrix plus the error. Then project this equation into the population complement. The part of an estimated eigenvector that lies in that complement is precisely its failure to remain in the target space. We want to bound that projected part.

After collecting terms, the calculation resembles a Sylvester matrix equation. On one side acts the population covariance restricted to the complement. On the other side acts a diagonal matrix of estimated eigenvalues. At first this appears to reintroduce a mixed-spectrum gap. The crucial additional step is to rewrite the same residual using a population eigenvalue block and a difference between estimated and population eigenvalues. Weyl's inequality controls those eigenvalue differences by the matrix perturbation norm.

The resulting estimates combine into a bound with the population gap in the denominator and a factor of two in the numerator. A more careful Frobenius-norm argument keeps the estimate sensitive to the number of desired directions rather than all ambient dimensions. This outline follows the logic of the 2015 proof; the companion records the exact theorem with its constants.

There is a subtle accounting point. If the estimated eigenvalue ordering differs from the population ordering, we still compare the selected positions in decreasing order. The theorem promises a bound that may be large when the perturbation has been strong enough to swap directions across a gap. It does not require a preliminary declaration that such swaps never occurred. That is exactly why a condition involving only population eigenvalues can be more convenient in applications.

The payoff becomes clearer in a probability argument. Suppose a covariance estimator is known to satisfy an operator-norm error bound with ninety-five percent probability. On each sample outcome where that bound holds, the deterministic theorem supplies a subspace-angle estimate using the fixed population gap. Therefore the same probability statement carries to the geometric error, after substituting the numerical error threshold. One does not need a second probability event just to certify that an estimated outside eigenvalue stayed away from a population eigenvalue. This is a small change in the organization of a proof, but it can remove an entire auxiliary argument from a statistical analysis.

## An instructive failure of the older formulation

In their paper, Yu, Wang and Samworth gave an example where the conventional mixed-spectrum gap vanishes even though the target eigenspace is completely unambiguous. The matrices are diagonal. The population eigenvalues, from largest to smallest, are fifty, forty, thirty, twenty and ten. The estimated eigenvalues are fifty-four, thirty-seven, thirty-two, twenty-three and twenty-one.

Choose the population block corresponding to the second, third and fourth largest eigenvalues: forty, thirty and twenty. Its eigenvectors span the three middle coordinate axes. Because the estimated matrix is also diagonal and has the same ordering of eigenvectors, the estimated block spans exactly the same three-dimensional subspace. The true principal-angle error is zero.

Yet the older gap definition looks at the estimated eigenvalues outside the chosen block. The last estimated eigenvalue is twenty-one, which lies inside the interval from twenty to forty. The distance between the two sets of spectral values is zero. Therefore that particular formulation cannot provide a finite useful bound, despite the two subspaces being identical.

Notice what has happened. We have not discovered an instability. We have discovered a mismatch between the quantity a statistician wants to bound and the separation condition chosen for that bound. The population-only separation is positive: the block is ten units away from the population eigenvalue above it and ten from the one below it. The revised inequality applies and produces a finite, though here nonzero and loose, upper bound.

This example is worth remembering because it guards against a common interpretation error. A theorem's hypotheses can fail without its desired conclusion being false. The failure may indicate that the theorem was designed for a different kind of comparison. Yu and her collaborators changed the statement so it was better aligned with how statistical applications identify and estimate their target spaces.

They also compared the two formulations in a high-dimensional spiked-covariance simulation. In that model a small number of population directions have elevated variance against a large background of weaker directions. The new bound could be substantially tighter, and in some sampled cases the conventional mixed-spectrum version supplied no bound at all. The important historical result is the demonstrated improvement of a reusable analytical tool; the exact simulation values are secondary to understanding the mechanism.

## From spectral estimates to scientific meaning

A spectral method can produce an answer without making that answer scientifically stable. Consider network clustering. We start with a graph and form an adjacency or Laplacian matrix. A few leading eigenvectors can reveal patterns of connections among vertices. When the network is observed with noise, some edges are missing or spurious, and the computed matrix differs from an underlying idealization. The leading eigenspace is used to embed the vertices into a low-dimensional geometric picture; the hope is that clusters visible in the idealized picture remain recognizable after perturbation.

The population-gap bound is one part of that argument. It controls how the geometric representation changes when the matrix is estimated. But it cannot alone prove that a clustering algorithm assigns every vertex correctly. That requires further reasoning about how the rows of the spectral embedding relate to groups, the separation of the groups, and the distribution of observational noise.

Yu's subsequent work with collaborators on statistical networks and change-point detection repeatedly encountered this kind of multi-stage reasoning. Her work with Ivor Cribben on estimating whole-brain dynamics using spectral clustering, published in 2017, is an example of spectral tools moving into the interpretation of changing connectivity. Network measurements can provide a large collection of dependent variables, yet the questions a researcher asks may concern patterns and transitions that the raw matrix does not display directly.

This is a career with a clear movement from estimation of static structures toward detecting how those structures evolve. In 2017 Yu became a lecturer at the University of Bristol; in 2019 she joined the University of Warwick as an associate professor. She was promoted to Reader in 2021 and professor in 2023. Her published profile lists her as Deputy Head of Department for Research. The chronology follows an expanding research program rather than a sudden break with her early statistical work.

## When does a signal change?

Imagine monitoring the average temperature of an industrial process every hour. For a while, readings fluctuate around twenty. At some unknown time a component fails and the readings fluctuate around twenty-three. The observations contain random noise, so no single reading proves a change has occurred. The task is to estimate the change time from the whole sequence.

A simple method compares the average before a candidate split with the average after it. If the two averages are sharply different, the candidate may be plausible. But the uncertainty depends on how many observations lie on either side. A change near the beginning has little data on one side; a change near the middle may be easier to detect. A standardized cumulative-sum statistic, often called CUSUM, takes that sample-size difference into account.

Suppose there are six noiseless observations: zero, zero, zero, two, two, two. At a split after the third observation, the mean on the left is zero and the mean on the right is two. Their difference is two. If we split after the second observation instead, the left mean is zero and the right mean is one point five. The contrast is weaker. With independent noise added, the optimal split does not always win, but the calculation explains what information a change-point method tries to accumulate.

In their 2020 paper on univariate mean change-point detection, Yu, Daren Wang and Alessandro Rinaldo analyzed the roles of the jump size, the minimal spacing between changes, and the noise level. A large jump helps. A long interval before the next jump gives more evidence. Higher noise makes changes harder to distinguish. Those three features form a statistical signal-to-noise ratio that determines when reliable localization is possible.

The paper develops matching kinds of upper and lower results: methods that work above an appropriate signal threshold, and impossibility results below it. It also studies penalized least squares and segmentation methods. A penalty discourages inserting a change every time a noisy observation deviates from its neighbors. Without such a penalty, a model that places a separate mean at every time point can fit the data perfectly while explaining nothing.

The relationship to the eigenvector question is structural. In both cases, an estimator's reliability depends on separation. For eigenspaces it is the gap between spectral blocks. For changes in a time series it is the amount of evidence distinguishing two regimes. A numerical output can exist even when the statistical target is not distinguishable at the available sample size.

## High-dimensional changes and networks

Now imagine one thousand measurements recorded at every time point. A failure affects only four sensors. If we average all one thousand signals, the effect may be diluted. If we inspect each sensor separately, we face a large multiple-testing problem and may overlook weak but coordinated evidence. An effective procedure has to make use of structure without assuming in advance which four sensors changed.

Yu and her coauthors studied several versions of this challenge: high-dimensional covariance changes, sparse dynamic networks, nonparametric mean changes, and time-varying regression coefficients. The questions are related but require different observations and different notions of difficulty. In a dynamic network, a structural change may alter connection probabilities among groups of vertices. In a regression model, the relationship between predictors and response may shift while the distribution of the predictors remains similar.

Some algorithms first compress data to a statistic that is sensitive to change, then search over candidate locations. Others combine penalized estimation with segmentation. Theorems must track both false alarms and missed changes, and sometimes the more demanding problem of locating a change precisely. Detection asks whether any change occurred. Localization asks when. The distinction has a practical consequence: proving that an interval contains a change is easier than showing that an estimate lies within a few time steps of the truth.

The mathematical literature also has to distinguish offline from online questions. An offline procedure may examine the whole sequence after collection is complete. An online procedure must decide while new observations arrive. Waiting for more data improves confidence but increases detection delay. A good sequential guarantee balances the chance of a false alarm against the speed of detecting a real change.

A 2023 note by Yu and collaborators studied this sequential problem, using cumulative-sum methods and guarantees about false alarms and delay. Her research program also includes graph-based change-point localization and models where observations are dependent, missing or contaminated. No single algorithm solves all these versions. The scientific problem determines which signal structure and risk measure the method should respect.

## Privacy and robustness change the information itself

Statistical methods often begin with the assumption that the investigator can inspect every observation. In privacy-sensitive settings that assumption fails by design. Under local differential privacy, each participant randomizes the information they send. The analyst sees a deliberately distorted message rather than the raw measurement. More privacy means the message leaks less about the individual, but usually carries less information about the population.

A simple thought experiment illustrates the cost. Imagine trying to estimate the proportion of people who answer yes to a question. Suppose each participant uses a randomized response rule: with some probability they report their true answer, and otherwise they report a random answer. Given a known randomization probability, the analyst can correct the average reported response to estimate the underlying proportion. But the correction magnifies noise. As the responses become more private, the estimate becomes less precise.

Another difficulty is contamination. A proportion of observations may be corrupted, erroneous, or even adversarial. A robust estimator aims to retain a reliable guarantee without trusting every data point. Privacy and contamination are distinct, but both make information less accessible. They impose losses that can sometimes interact in surprising ways.

Yu, Mengchu Li and Thomas Berrett investigated that interaction in a 2023 Annals of Statistics paper on robustness and local differential privacy. They studied fundamental minimax lower bounds and particular problems including testing, mean estimation, density estimation and medians. A minimax result concerns performance under the hardest distribution in a specified class; it can reveal a limit that no allowed estimator can beat. The task is then to construct feasible methods that approach those limits.

This research shifts the framing of a perturbation bound. Rather than merely asking how accurately a matrix can be estimated from ordinary measurements, one must ask how accurately any quantity can be estimated from restricted or unreliable information. Only after answering that statistical question does it make sense to propagate error into a geometric object such as an eigenvector or subspace.

Yu's later work also treats functional data, differential privacy in distributed settings, nonparametric learning and fairness-related statistical problems. These topics share a concern about what information and assumptions a mathematical answer consumes. More data does not automatically eliminate bias, invalid sampling, or privacy constraints. The problem is to describe those constraints in a form that admits both algorithms and honest error guarantees.

## Recognition, collaborators, and the shape of a research program

In 2023 Yi Yu received a Philip Leverhulme Prize. Her academic record includes editorial work for journals such as Biometrika and Bernoulli and collaborations across statistical inference, networks, survival analysis and machine learning. The range is considerable, but attribution should remain precise. The 2015 population-gap theorem was authored jointly with Wang and Samworth. The later change-point and privacy results likewise belong to the named coauthor groups; an individual career portrait is not a claim of sole invention.

Her Fudan training in mathematical statistics helps explain an early interest in high-dimensional variable selection and survival models. Her Cambridge collaboration brought spectral perturbation theory into view. Subsequent work at Bristol and Warwick broadened the mathematical questions to nonstationary, dependent and protected data. This is an intellectual chronology established by degrees, appointments, papers and collaborations, rather than a speculative account of private intentions.

There is a useful lesson in how the 2015 result fits this career. A mathematician can prove an exact inequality that is valid for every matrix in its domain. A statistician needs that inequality inside a chain of claims about data drawn from a model. Each link must refer to quantities that can be controlled using the available assumptions. A denominator involving an observed eigenvalue crossing may be valid but cumbersome. A population gap is aligned with the target that the statistical model defines.

Return to the covariance example. We estimate a matrix, and we want a reliable space of directions. The first question is how large the estimation error can be with high probability. The second is whether the population eigenspace is isolated from its neighbors. Given both answers, a principal-angle theorem converts an entrywise or operator-norm estimation guarantee into a geometric one. If either answer fails, we should diagnose the reason rather than trust a crisp-looking eigenvector produced by software.

The same discipline carries beyond covariance matrices. A change point requires evidence that two regimes differ. A network cluster requires enough signal to stand apart from sampling noise. A private estimator must account for the information the privacy mechanism suppresses. Reliable statistics begins by specifying what is identifiable, what is observed and how uncertainty travels through the computation.

Yu, Wang and Samworth's population-gap theorem is one particularly clear expression of that way of thinking. It made a classical geometric fact easier to use under statistical assumptions, and it taught a durable lesson: a bound becomes most useful when its hypotheses correspond to the quantities the application can actually justify.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Statistical eigenspace setup and notation

For symmetric real population \(\Sigma\) and estimate \(\widehat\Sigma\), order eigenvalues decreasingly. Fix consecutive indices \(1\le r\le s\le p\), \(d=s-r+1\), and orthonormal basis matrices \(V,\widehat V\in\mathbb R^{p\times d}\). Let \(E=\widehat\Sigma-\Sigma\). Let \(\lambda_0=+\infty\), \(\lambda_{p+1}=-\infty\), and assume the **population external gap**
\[
\gamma=\min\{\lambda_{r-1}-\lambda_r,\ \lambda_s-\lambda_{s+1}\}>0.
\]
A tie *inside* the block does not violate this assumption. The principal angles are determined by singular values of \(V^T\widehat V\); the Frobenius sine distance is
\[
\|\sin\Theta(\widehat V,V)\|_F=\|(I-VV^T)\widehat V\|_F.
\]

### 2. The 2015 Yu–Wang–Samworth population-gap statement

Theorem 2 of Yu, Wang and Samworth (2015) gives
\[
\|\sin\Theta(\widehat V,V)\|_F \le
\frac{2\min\{\sqrt d\,\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|E\|_F\}}{\gamma}.
\]
It also yields, for some orthogonal \(O\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\),
\[
\|\widehat V O -V\|_F\le
\frac{2^{3/2}\min\{\sqrt d\,\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}},\|E\|_F\}}{\gamma}.
\]
Both are deterministic. A statistical result additionally requires a probabilistic control for \(E\). The authors' Theorem 3 covers rectangular matrices using squared singular-value gaps; see the companion for `027000` for its form.

### 3. Why the alignment bound follows

Let the singular values of \(V^T\widehat V\) be \(\cos\theta_j\). An SVD supplies the optimal orthogonal alignment \(O\), for which
\[
\|\widehat V O-V\|_F^2=2\sum_{j=1}^d(1-\cos\theta_j)
\le2\sum_{j=1}^d(1-\cos^2\theta_j)
=2\|\sin\Theta\|_F^2.
\]
This distinguishes coordinate-wise basis error from subspace error.

### 4. Worked symmetric perturbation

Set \(\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(10,4)\) and \(E=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]\). For the leading eigenvector of \(\widehat\Sigma\),
\[
\tan(2\theta)=\frac{2}{10-4}=\frac13,
\quad \theta=\tfrac12\arctan(1/3)\approx9.217474^\circ.
\]
Thus \(\sin\theta\approx0.16018\), while the population-gap theorem gives the conservative bound \(2\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}/6=1/3\). If the gap shrinks to 1 with the same perturbation, \(\theta=\tfrac12\arctan 2\approx31.7175^\circ\).

### 5. Mixed-gap failure without angle error

For \(\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(50,40,30,20,10)\) and \(\widehat\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(54,37,32,23,21)\), take \(r=2,s=4\). Both middle-three eigenspaces equal \(\mathrm{span}\{e_2,e_3,e_4\}\), so every principal angle is zero. Yet the sample eigenvalue \(21\) in the complement belongs to the interval \([20,40]\), making the conventional mixed-spectrum separation \(\delta=0\). The population external gap is \(\min(50-40,20-10)=10\). This example is adapted from the 2015 paper.

### 6. Operator norm versus Frobenius norm

For \(E=0.1 I_{100}\), \(\|E\|_{\rm op}=0.1\), \(\|E\|_F=1\). For target dimension \(d=1\), the theorem's minimum equals \(0.1\); for \(d=4\), \(0.2\). The example illustrates a norm comparison; diagonal shifts of all eigenvalues cause no actual eigenspace rotation, so the angle bound is loose.

### 7. CUSUM signal-to-noise construction

For data \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) and candidate change time \(t\), define
\[
C_t=\sqrt{\frac{t(n-t)}{n}}\left(\frac1t\sum_{i\le t}x_i-\frac1{n-t}\sum_{i>t}x_i\right).
\]
In the noiseless sample \((0,0,0,2,2,2)\), the split \(t=3\) has raw mean contrast \(2\) and \(|C_3|=\sqrt6\). A split at \(t=2\) gives contrast \(1.5\), with \(|C_2|=\sqrt3\). In the independent sub-Gaussian setting of Wang, Yu and Rinaldo (2020), detection/localization conditions scale with \(\kappa\sqrt\Delta/\sigma\), where \(\kappa\) is jump magnitude, \(\Delta\) minimal spacing and \(\sigma\) noise scale. Precise minimax rates require the paper's assumptions and logarithmic factors.
