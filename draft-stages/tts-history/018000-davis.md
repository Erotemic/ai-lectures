# Chandler Davis: How should we measure the rotation of an entire eigenspace?

If a matrix changes slightly, one of its eigenvectors can change drastically when its eigenvalue nearly collides with another. But the span of a separated group of eigenvectors may remain stable. How should that geometric movement be measured?

Chandler Davis worked in operator theory and linear algebra and developed a sustained investigation of subspace geometry. His 1958 paper Separation of Two Linear Subspaces and the papers called The Rotation of Eigenvectors by a Perturbation, parts one and two, preceded the joint third paper with William Kahan in 1970. The numbering in that series reflects a genuine mathematical development, not three lectures written to fit a syllabus.

The key step is to regard a spectral subspace as the object of interest. Choose an orthonormal frame for the approximate subspace and project it onto the orthogonal complement of the target. The result measures leakage into unwanted directions. Its singular values are the directed sines of the principal angles between the spaces.

In a two-dimensional example, take a diagonal matrix with eigenvalues zero and four, and add a small off-diagonal coupling. The lower eigenvector turns away from the horizontal axis. The angle is related to the coupling divided by the spectral separation. For a larger cluster, the same idea uses operators rather than a single scalar angle.

Davis was also interested in what a canonical rotation between subspaces can optimize. This introduces a different question from angle estimation. Given many ambient orthogonal maps carrying one subspace to another, which map minimizes displacement under a specified norm? The answer depends on whether we measure the source subspace alone or all ambient directions, and on how the singular values of displacement are combined.

The 1970 paper contains a broad unsquared ambient optimality claim that a four-dimensional real example contradicts. That mathematical correction does not negate the paper's surviving angle theorems or the valid restricted and squared-displacement results. The distinctions make the geometry more precise.

Davis's historical role is in both the geometric language of separated subspaces and the quantitative development of perturbation theory. His work shows why eigenvector stability is naturally a theory of subspaces, angles, and the spectra of the operators acting on them.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

For equal-rank finite subspaces with projections \(P,Q\), the nonzero
singular values of \((I-Q)P\) are sines of principal angles.
If the largest principal angle is \(\theta_{\max}\), then
\(\|(I-Q)P\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sin\theta_{\max}\).
Davis's 1958 and 1963/65 results precede the jointly authored 1970 paper.
