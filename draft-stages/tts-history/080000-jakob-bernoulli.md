# 080000 - Jakob Bernoulli: Basel, family conflict, and the art of uncertainty

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** seventeenth-century Basel and family migration, theological training, scientific travels, early calculus, the relationship with Johann, the mathematical culture of journals, chance and decision, and the long publication of *Ars Conjectandi*. **Background:** elementary algebra and the idea of probability.

In modern mathematics textbooks, the Bernoulli name seems to be everywhere. There are Bernoulli numbers, differential equations, probability trials, families of curves, and principles of fluid motion. The temptation is to imagine a single prolific mathematician. In fact the name belonged to a family whose members studied, taught, argued, and competed across generations. Jakob Bernoulli was the first of the great mathematicians in that family, and the work for which he is now most famous was published only after he died. His life belongs to the story of a Swiss city, a trading family, the spread of calculus, and a new ambition to reason mathematically about uncertainty.

Jakob was born in Basel in the middle of the seventeenth century. Some historical sources record the date as December twenty-seventh, sixteen fifty-four; the equivalent date in the Gregorian reckoning often used now is January sixth, sixteen fifty-five. This is a calendar issue, not evidence for two different births. Basel stood at a European crossroads, connected by trade, the Rhine, Protestant religious networks, and intellectual exchange. The Bernoulli family's earlier ancestors had migrated from the Low Countries amid religious persecution and later established commercial and civic standing in Basel. The family trade involved spices and other merchandise. Jakob's father, Nicolaus, was active in city government; his mother came from another locally prominent family.

A prosperous household could provide schooling, books, and the expectation that a son would join an established profession. It did not necessarily provide freedom to choose the profession he preferred. Jakob was directed toward philosophy and theology. He completed a master's degree in philosophy in sixteen seventy-one and his theology qualification in sixteen seventy-six. He studied languages and religious subjects seriously, while pursuing mathematics and astronomy outside the course that his parents had chosen. This contrast can be narrated without treating his family as enemies of learning. They valued education, but they wanted an education leading to recognized religious or civic work. Mathematics was not yet an obvious or secure career.

After the theology degree he traveled. He worked as a tutor in Geneva, studied in France among readers of Descartes, went to the Netherlands, and visited England. The network included people associated with Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke. There is a difference between meeting famous natural philosophers and becoming their disciple. These travels supplied access to books, conversations, and intellectual practices, rather than a single decisive revelation. Jakob was fascinated by astronomy and made claims about cometary motion that did not survive later science. His early curiosity was not a sequence of uninterrupted victories; it was a young investigator testing what mathematics might explain.

Back in Basel, he lectured on mechanics in the early sixteen eighties. In sixteen eighty-four he married Judith Stupanus. They had a son and a daughter, neither of whom became one of the family's prominent mathematicians. The household now had ordinary demands while Jakob developed an increasingly public scientific life. He wrote for the *Acta Eruditorum*, a learned journal founded in Leipzig, and became professor of mathematics at the University of Basel in sixteen eighty-seven. The appointment placed his mathematical work within an institution, but the republic of letters in which he worked crossed political and linguistic borders. A problem posed in one country might provoke a published response in another.

He was entering the subject at a remarkable moment. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz had published a short account of differential calculus in sixteen eighty-four. It offered symbols and procedures for finding tangents and extrema, but it was far from the beginner-friendly calculus book familiar today. Even capable mathematicians struggled to understand the new methods. Jakob and his younger brother Johann were among the early readers who learned to use the differential techniques, tested their scope, and applied them to curves and mechanical problems. Their work helped turn a compressed announcement into a living mathematical practice. Calculus was not merely received by students; it was reconstructed and extended by the people willing to work through its difficulties.

Johann's entry into this work transformed the family. He was twelve years younger than Jakob and had been directed toward medicine. Johann asked his older brother for mathematical instruction, and they initially shared discoveries and problems. Each had skill, ambition, and an increasingly strong public reputation. They also had overlapping professional interests in a small European world where priority could establish a career. Cooperation gave way to bitter competition. The brothers attacked one another in print and disputed whose solution or method deserved credit. Later portraits sometimes make Johann solely responsible or Jakob solely responsible for the rupture. The surviving polemics show that both men contributed to the feud, and that the feud lasted long enough to affect the handling of Jakob's unpublished papers after his death.

The mathematical disputes were not made up to manufacture a drama. Consider the curve made by a flexible chain hanging under its own weight between two supports. It resembles a parabola, but it is not a parabola. The catenary became a challenge through which the new calculus could demonstrate its power. Jakob, Johann, and other contemporaries including Huygens and Leibniz worked on versions of the problem. A correct solution must balance gravity against tension along the whole chain, rather than merely fit a visually plausible curve. The answer belongs to the family of hyperbolic cosine curves. It was a lesson in how mathematics can distinguish between two shapes that the eye finds similar.

The brothers also explored curves involving growth, winding, and motion. Jakob was particularly attached to the logarithmic spiral, a curve that keeps a characteristic form under changes of scale. A logarithmic spiral winds around a center but never reaches it in finite turns. Jakob studied its geometrical properties and the way it appears again after certain transformations. He reportedly wished a spiral to appear on his tomb with a motto about rising again after change. The monument survives, but the engraved curve is not a faithful logarithmic spiral. Even here, the object that survives from a mathematician's life is not identical to the mathematical object the person imagined. The story is useful as a visible artifact, not proof of whatever personal philosophy we might choose to assign to him.

Jakob's lasting work on uncertainty grew from the mathematics of games. Gambling problems had already attracted Pascal, Fermat, Huygens, and others. They showed that when the possible outcomes and their chances are specified, one can calculate fair prices and expected gains. But everyday decisions seldom arrive with a complete list of known chances. Merchants, judges, physicians, and public officials confront incomplete information. Can mathematics help us make rational choices in circumstances that are neither absolutely certain nor entirely random? This question occupied Jakob for years. It connected the commercial and civic world of his family to the abstract study of probability, though we should not claim that a specific transaction in his father's shop inspired the theorem.

He called his emerging discipline an art of conjecturing. His great manuscript, *Ars Conjectandi*, did not simply make the probability calculations in games more elaborate. It tried to establish why repeated observation could tell us something about an unknown underlying chance. That is a much harder problem than calculating the probability of a known fair die landing on six. In the known-die problem, the model supplies the probability and we calculate consequences. In the unknown-chance problem, repeated results are the evidence from which we try to infer what probability governs them.

Jakob developed the central result over many years, with work on probability concentrated particularly in the late sixteen eighties. He discussed progress with correspondents, including his brother before the relationship broke down. He did not complete a polished book before his death in Basel in August seventeen oh five. The work survived in manuscript, held within a family divided by distrust. *Ars Conjectandi* finally appeared in seventeen thirteen, eight years after Jakob's death. It matters to say the date plainly, because the book belongs intellectually to decades of seventeenth-century research but entered print in the eighteenth century, after other writers had already extended the field.

The publication history is more complicated than a neat sentence saying “his nephew edited the book.” Jakob's widow Judith and his son Nicolaus controlled access to the manuscript. Family disagreement delayed publication. The nephew Nicolaus Bernoulli, himself a mathematician, contributed a preface and help correcting errors, but modern historical scholarship cautions against assigning him sole editorial responsibility for preparing the text. Two other individuals were involved in bringing the manuscript into print. Several people in different parts of the family shared the same first name. The distinction between son and nephew is therefore not pedantry: it changes the story of who had custody of the work and who actually handled it.

The published *Ars Conjectandi* had several parts. It engaged with Christiaan Huygens's work on games of chance, developed combinatorial tools, and solved gaming problems. Its final section addressed applications to civic, ethical, and economic judgment. It also circulated material on series and the numbers later named for Bernoulli. The book did not fully solve the broad social problems it proposed to mathematize. What it achieved was a foundation for reasoning from repeated events to estimates of probability, along with a program that later writers would pursue in new ways.

Here is the essential mathematical idea, expressed in language closer to the modern classroom than to Bernoulli's original Latin. Imagine an experiment that has one of two outcomes: success or failure. Suppose a success has a fixed probability, say three fifths, and suppose successive trials are independent. Over many trials, the fraction of successes usually gets close to three fifths. The word “usually” needs precision. The fraction can be far off in a short run, and a long run can still contain surprises. But for any fixed margin of error, the probability that the observed fraction lies outside that margin becomes arbitrarily small as the number of trials increases. This is the weak law of large numbers.

There is a useful distinction between what the theorem says and what the listener might wish it said. It does not claim that every infinite-looking streak must alternate fairly. It does not say that ten heads in a row obliges a fair coin to land tails next. It does not guarantee that the next thousand trials will exactly match the underlying chance. It says that, under specified assumptions, the average becomes increasingly concentrated near the expected value. A contemporary proof may use variance or a concentration inequality; Jakob's original proof used the mathematical tools of his own time. We should not attribute a twentieth-century presentation to him while explaining the result in a modern way.

The insight is also conditional. We cannot learn the true probability of a civic event merely by collecting an arbitrarily large table and assuming the world remains unchanged. In Bernoulli's own ambitions, extending probability beyond artificial games raised the problem of what counts as the same kind of trial. Do independent observations really sample a stable chance? Are social decisions and legal evidence as repeatable as dice? Later probability theory developed many tools for handling dependence, changing distributions, and measurement. Jakob's theorem opened an important route without resolving all these difficulties. It offered a mathematical reason that repeated observations could be informative, not a declaration that counting always produces truth.

The Bernoulli numbers belong to a different line of work. Suppose you want to add the first hundred squares, or the first hundred cubes, or a higher power across a long sequence of integers. Individually adding every term is possible but inefficient. Bernoulli and his predecessors sought general formulas whose structure could be reused. The sequence of coefficients now called Bernoulli numbers organizes such formulas. They later appeared in series expansions, numerical quadrature, and number theory. These numbers were not invented for casino games, and it is misleading to fuse their history automatically with the law of large numbers simply because both appeared in the same posthumous volume. Modern research has also compared the development of power-sum methods in Basel and in Japan, reminding us that European family stories are not the only history of a mathematical technique.

Jakob lived before modern scientific disciplines were rigidly separated. A university teacher trained in theology could investigate curves and mechanics, correspond about astronomy, and make probability into an inquiry about practical judgment. His struggles with Johann also reveal that mathematics was a human profession before it was a collection of clean historical theorems. Family loyalty, institutional positions, prestige, and print publication shaped what got recognized and when. His great probability book was not the polished endpoint of a career. It was an incomplete manuscript, inherited and debated by people who had their own complicated relationships to its author.

What makes Bernoulli important is not the fantasy that he made uncertainty disappear. He saw that uncertain knowledge might itself be investigated rigorously. The move from computing chances in a game to using repeated observations as evidence changed the intellectual possibilities of statistics. Much later, Laplace, Chebyshev, Markov, and Kolmogorov would build further layers of theory. Their work did not simply repeat Bernoulli's insight; it clarified, extended, and sometimes challenged what his program could claim. In Basel, centuries before modern data science, Jakob Bernoulli had proposed a question that would remain central: when does repeated experience justify confidence about what we have not yet seen?

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Dates and relationships

- **Birth-date convention:** 27 December 1654 in the older calendar is commonly converted to **6 January 1655**; scholarship sometimes indexes the birth under different years. These refer to one birth.
- **Basel household:** merchant and civic family, parents Nicolaus Bernoulli and Margarethe Schönauer; university philosophy and theology before a mathematical vocation. The family name covers many distinct authors.
- **1683–1687:** mechanics lectures, marriage to Judith Stupanus (1684), professorship in mathematics (1687), and early published use of Leibnizian calculus. The brothers Jakob and Johann initially worked together before public rivalry.
- **1705–1713:** death with *Ars Conjectandi* unfinished; family custody and publication delay. The **son Nicolaus** is distinct from the **nephew Nicolaus I**. The nephew supplied a preface and errata assistance; do not designate him sole editor on the strength of older capsule biographies.
- The logarithmic spiral tomb should be discussed as memorial art rather than a literal mathematical construction.

### Mathematical note

For independent Bernoulli random variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) with constant success probability \(p\), let \(\bar X_n=n^{-1}\sum_i X_i\). A modern weak-law statement is

\[\text{for every }\epsilon>0,\qquad \Pr\bigl(|\bar X_n-p|>\epsilon\bigr)\longrightarrow 0\quad(n\to\infty).\]

One modern route uses \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=p(1-p)/n\) and Chebyshev's inequality. **This is a modern demonstration, not Jakob Bernoulli's original proof.** It assumes independent identically distributed trials; the scope of historical “civil, moral, and economic” inference was and remains a separate question. The Bernoulli numbers in power-sum formulas belong to a different mathematical strand.
