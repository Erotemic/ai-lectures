# 116000 — John Horton Conway: Games, groups, and the mathematics of play

**Track:** History — People and ideas. **Focus:** Liverpool and Cambridge, the Leech lattice and finite groups, Game of Life, surreal numbers, major collaborators and the *Atlas*, Princeton's mathematical commons, and the interaction between public puzzles and rigorous research. **Background:** no higher mathematics presumed; groups, games, and iterative rules are explained in speech.

Consider a board covered in squares. Every square is either alive or dead. There are no players making moves. The board changes because each square looks at its eight neighbors and follows a very short rule. An empty square becomes alive if exactly three neighbors are alive. A living square survives if it has two or three living neighbors. Otherwise it dies. From this, one can get still patterns, repeating patterns, shapes that travel across the board, and arrangements that carry out computations. It sounds like a pastime. It is also a useful introduction to John Horton Conway, whose life repeatedly dissolved the boundary between play and serious mathematics.

Conway was born in Liverpool in 1937. He grew up during and after the Second World War, in a Britain where universities and schools were changing but where a place at Cambridge still opened an important social and intellectual door. Accounts from his family and colleagues describe an early appetite for numbers, patterns, and puzzles. He became interested in finding the day of the week for a date and in fast mental calculation. These recollections are not proof that he was destined to become a celebrated mathematician; many children enjoy puzzles. Their relevance is that the habit persisted into a scholarly career, becoming a way to invite other people into difficult questions.

In 1956 he entered Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge. He earned his first degree in 1959 and completed a doctorate in 1964 under Harold Davenport. Cambridge offered access to established number theorists, an intense culture of problem solving, and people who took elegant arguments seriously. Conway joined the academic staff and remained in the city for roughly three decades. He was not confined to one established mathematical specialty. A theorem in number theory could interest him in the same week as a geometrical construction, an unfamiliar game, or an algebraic curiosity encountered in conversation.

His doctoral work and early career included number theory. One problem concerned writing integers as sums of fifth powers; Conway settled an upper-bound question related to Waring's problem. To understand its attraction, imagine that instead of writing every number as a sum of ones, you may use only perfect fifth powers: one, thirty-two, two hundred forty-three, and so forth. You ask for a single fixed limit on how many such terms are needed to express any sufficiently large positive integer, or perhaps all positive integers depending on the precise theorem. Problems of this kind connect an elementary rule to remarkably difficult global estimates. Conway was capable of technical, sustained work long before the public knew his name through a board of cells.

Cambridge mathematical life gave Conway a setting in which an apparently recreational question could be shown to someone with the expertise to make it precise. The way he circulated among problems was itself a professional skill. A difficult combinatorial puzzle can often be rephrased as the search for an invariant: a property that cannot change under the legal moves. If every legal move preserves some quantity but the desired final configuration has a different quantity, the goal is impossible. This style of reasoning helps explain why games were not a withdrawal from mathematics for him. The pattern of available moves invites the same structural questions as algebra or geometry.

The discovery that put him at the center of a new algebraic research program involved the Leech lattice. John Leech had constructed a highly symmetric arrangement in twenty-four-dimensional space. One cannot picture twenty-four dimensions in the ordinary sense, but the idea of a lattice is not alien: a square grid is a simple lattice in two dimensions, made by integer combinations of two basis directions. In higher dimensions, one can search for especially efficient and symmetric arrangements. The Leech lattice has extraordinary properties relevant to sphere packing, where the goal is to arrange equal spheres with as little wasted space as possible.

Conway studied the symmetries of this lattice in the late nineteen-sixties. A symmetry is a transformation that preserves an object. For a square, turning the board through a right angle gives one symmetry; reflecting it gives another. Under composition these operations form a group. The Leech lattice's symmetries are enormously more complicated. Studying them led Conway to three sporadic simple groups, now bearing his name. A simple group is an algebraic building block in a precise structural sense, not necessarily a small or easy group. The word sporadic signals that these groups lie outside the main infinite families that appear in the classification of finite simple groups.

This work was not a solitary creation of the Leech lattice. Leech had constructed the lattice, many mathematicians were investigating finite groups, and group theory already had a sophisticated classification effort. Conway's contribution was to reveal previously unknown symmetries and reorganize the possibilities. It transformed his standing among professional algebraists. One of the crucial points of his career is that this deep, technical achievement and the later Game of Life belonged to the same mind, but neither can be reduced to the other.

In 1970 Martin Gardner introduced Conway's Game of Life to readers of *Scientific American*. The name suggests a simulation of biology, but Conway's purpose was mathematical: he wanted a simple cellular rule with behavior rich enough to resist immediate classification. The rules had to balance stability with growth. If every occupied cell stayed occupied, the system would freeze into a large mass. If births were too easy, the board would explode. If survival were too hard, everything would vanish. Conway and collaborators experimented to find a striking balance. The precise three-neighbor birth rule and two-or-three-neighbor survival rule became a laboratory for emergence from local constraints.

Let us follow one small pattern rather than simply admire the phrase emergence. Put three living cells in a straight horizontal row. On the next turn, the end cells have too few neighbors, while the empty square above and below the center each sees exactly three. The row becomes a vertical column of three. After another turn the same reasoning gives the original horizontal row. This little object is called a blinker. It does not move across the board, but it oscillates every two generations. A square block of four living cells, by contrast, remains unchanged. More complex shapes, such as gliders, reappear displaced after several generations and therefore travel.

Once you have stable objects, oscillators, and mobile signals, further possibilities appear. People found constructions capable of implementing logic, storing information, and supporting universal computation. That development involved a large community of experimenters and programmers. A glider makes the distinction between local rules and long-distance effect concrete. After four synchronous updates, a familiar five-cell pattern returns to the same shape shifted diagonally by one square. No cell has an instruction to travel northeast; all cells merely obey birth and survival rules based on their neighbors. The motion is a collective pattern rather than a little organism literally moving between squares. If you arrange gliders to collide, the collisions can change other patterns, and that makes signal-like behavior possible. This is why the game eventually became relevant to computation: one can construct mechanisms whose large-scale purpose is not written into the rule followed by any individual cell.

The Game of Life became important to computer culture partly because its rules were simple enough to implement on early machines and its behavior was difficult enough to keep observers curious. Conway did not individually design every later Life discovery, and the broader field of cellular automata long predates his game, including research by John von Neumann and others.

The popular reception also depended on the availability of computers and a community willing to share discoveries. The tiny written rule could spread through a magazine, be implemented on machines with different architectures, and create new puzzles faster than one person could catalog them. A reader might discover an oscillator and share it with another reader. That open-ended character brought Conway recognition he could not fully direct. A mathematical invention that allows other people to participate has an afterlife different from a theorem whose proof is mainly read by specialists. Both can be serious intellectual contributions, but they create very different public portraits of the author.

Conway had complicated feelings about this fame. Colleagues report that he feared the popular game would overshadow his more difficult mathematical achievements. There was a real tension: a broad audience recognized the name Conway from Life, while specialists might recognize it from finite groups or number theory. Yet the public attention also helped carry mathematics into new spaces. A reader who watched gliders move might begin asking which patterns can reproduce themselves, what counts as a computation, or why simple rules generate patterns that no one can easily predict. For a good mathematical communicator, the entry point need not contain the deepest result.

Another of his great inventions grew from analyzing two-player games. Consider a game with positions, legal moves, and a winner determined by who can move or reach a target. If one knows all the possible moves from a position, one can reason recursively about what the position is worth. A winning advantage can sometimes be assigned a numerical value, but only after deciding what comparisons of games mean. Conway, together with colleagues in combinatorial game theory, developed a system in which surprisingly familiar-looking numbers emerged from this recursion.

Begin with a position where neither player has a move. Treat it as zero. A position offering the left player a move to zero but no move for the right can be represented by the number one. Reverse the sides and get negative one. A position whose left option is zero and right option is one can represent one half. The recipe continues to give integers, fractions, and eventually quantities beyond the ordinary real numbers. This is the foundation of Conway's surreal numbers. Their construction produces a proper class, not merely another list of large decimal numbers, and it contains infinite and infinitesimal values as well as the familiar real numbers.

One reason the idea is surprising is that numbers are normally taught first and games later. Conway reversed that direction. He asked what structure emerges when you compare positions according to available moves. With careful definitions, arithmetic appears inside a theory of strategic options. The analogy with Dedekind cuts is suggestive: both use order relationships to specify numbers, though the precise constructions are different. The surreal numbers were developed in the early nineteen-seventies and systematically presented in *On Numbers and Games*, published in 1976. Donald Knuth also popularized them in a mathematical fiction, a separate literary reception of the mathematical discovery.

The early surreal-number construction also gives a caution against trusting every informal move. A pair of left and right options is not automatically a well-defined number; the options must satisfy an ordering condition, and the recursive universe needs a careful foundation. In combinatorial game theory some positions are numbers, while others behave like games that cannot be assigned an ordinary numerical advantage. A beginner's example such as one half is illuminating, but it is not the whole subject. Conway's originality lay in developing the definitions and operations sufficiently that these intuitive comparisons became theorems. The playful entry point was followed by hard formal work.

Conway's ideas moved rapidly through collaboration. With Elwyn Berlekamp and Richard Guy he produced *Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays*, a substantial treatment of strategy and numerical game values. With Simon Norton he contributed to what became known as monstrous moonshine, a set of surprising relationships between the largest sporadic simple group—the Monster—and functions arising in number theory. John McKay's observation about the near-equality of an important number and a representation dimension was one early clue; work by John Thompson, Norton, Conway, and others developed the conjectures. Richard Borcherds later proved a central part of this program, receiving a Fields Medal. It would miss the collaborative history to give the entire theory to its most publicly visible coauthor.

The term moonshine expresses how implausible the connections first appeared. How could the symmetries of an enormous finite group be related to coefficients in an analytic function? In most everyday problems, group theory and complex analysis look like different subjects. The conjectures revealed deep structural relations. Conway helped make such a question intelligible to specialists by treating numerical coincidences as possible evidence of hidden algebraic organization. His habit of taking unlikely examples seriously could be mathematically productive precisely because he also had the expertise to test them against rigorous requirements.

He was also one of the principal authors of the *Atlas of Finite Groups*, published in 1986. This immense reference work was a collaboration involving Robert Curtis, Simon Norton, Richard Parker, and Robert Wilson as well as Conway. An atlas has a different intellectual role from a single theorem. It gathers generators, representations, and structural data so that researchers can work effectively with a vast and difficult subject. Its preparation took years and required attention to detail that the playful public persona could obscure. The reference volume helped make the classification of finite simple groups usable in practice even for mathematicians who had not participated in the long proof effort.

During the nineteen-eighties Conway moved to Princeton, where he held the John von Neumann professorship. Accounts differ in minor dating between his appointment and arrival, but he left Cambridge in the middle of that decade and made Princeton his home. There he continued teaching and giving talks, and he developed a recognizable place in the department's social life. Colleagues remember him spending time in the common room rather than remaining secluded in an office. Paper models, puzzles, arguments at blackboards, and conversations with visitors were not interruptions to his mathematics. For him, such exchanges could be sources of mathematical problems.

A graduate student visiting Princeton might find the normal barriers between an expert and a novice somewhat lower in Conway's presence. This was not because he never cared about correctness. Colleagues remember him challenging claims and delighting in counterexamples. But a puzzle can be checked immediately, and a proposed simple rule gives both sides something concrete to manipulate. That makes disagreement less dependent on formal seniority. Stories about such conversations survive mainly in recollections by colleagues and family; they should be treated as testimony about his social style rather than a verbatim record of all his interactions.

His style was generous with access. Princeton recollections describe conversations with undergraduates, local enthusiasts, people arriving with an idea, and distinguished researchers. A hobbyist could sometimes ask a question that Conway treated as worth thinking about. This did not imply that every speculation was right. It reflected an attitude that mathematical insight could begin with an example accessible to someone outside the professional hierarchy. The same person could move from a toy game to finite groups because he took the structure of the question seriously rather than judging it solely by its appearance.

Conway collaborated with Neil Sloane on the mathematics of sphere packing and lattices. Their book *Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups* became an important synthesis. Again, the visible name is only part of the research lineage. The subject involved John Leech, earlier geometric packing results, numerical classification, and later advances. Conway's contributions to sphere packing and group theory were connected through the common theme of symmetry: when an arrangement is extraordinarily regular, understanding its symmetry can reveal what makes it exceptional.

He continued to devise smaller problems that became mathematical landmarks. His Doomsday algorithm lets a person calculate weekdays in a chosen year using memorable anchor dates; it is a good example of making a computation available to mental arithmetic. He developed the fifteen theorem with William Schneeberger, relating representation of integers by certain positive quadratic forms to a finite list of test integers. A later, stronger 290 theorem was proved by Manjul Bhargava and Jonathan Hanke. These results illustrate the long arc from playful conjecture to tight mathematical classification, with successive authors credited for successive improvements.

In his later years Conway worked with Simon Kochen on the free will theorem, a result in mathematical physics whose premises and interpretation have attracted substantial discussion. Its technical content concerns quantum measurements and assumptions about experimenters' choices; it does not prove human freedom in the everyday philosophical sense. That caution matters because Conway was particularly good at giving memorable names to difficult ideas. A memorable name can invite inquiry, but the theorem still has to be stated under its actual assumptions rather than under the widest possible interpretation.

Personal accounts of Conway often emphasize his humor and conversational enthusiasm. They also describe a life with marriages, children, and difficult periods. It would be inappropriate to turn those recollections into an invented psychological key to his discoveries. The evidence is strong enough to say that friendship, family, teaching, and public attention mattered to his life; it is not a license to speculate about an internal state in every decade. In 2001 he married Diana Conway, who later described the variety of people and ideas visiting their home. His work generated correspondence and personal connections extending well beyond his department.

He died in April 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic. Students, colleagues, mathematicians, and recreational puzzle enthusiasts remembered different aspects of him. Some remembered the man at a blackboard explaining the Monster group. Others remembered a card trick, a problem in a summer program, or a handful of cells changing shape across a grid. Those memories are not competing verdicts on whether he was serious. They are evidence of how many kinds of mathematical conversation he helped sustain.

Conway's life challenges the idea that there must be a sharp wall separating recreational and professional mathematics. The Game of Life became a practical model for computation and complex behavior. The surreal numbers began in games but led to a vast algebraic structure. The Leech lattice led to newly discovered finite groups. The same instincts for asking what happens under a simple rule and looking for a surprising symmetry recur in all three cases, although the methods and proofs are very different.

Return to the three living cells in a row. On the next turn they become a column; one turn later they become a row again. It would be easy to dismiss this as a trick. Conway's contribution was to see that humble rules can support genuinely deep questions, then to do the disciplined mathematics required to answer some of them. The full biography is not simply a story of playfulness. It is a story of patient research, difficult collaboration, university life, and a lifelong willingness to ask whether a small example might conceal a larger world.

## Historical companion — not spoken

### Chronology and source considerations

- **1937:** Born in Liverpool; **1956–1964:** student, then doctoral researcher at Gonville and Caius, Cambridge; Harold Davenport supervised the doctorate.
- **1968–1969:** Identification of new sporadic simple groups through the symmetries of the Leech lattice; John Leech's prior discovery is essential.
- **1970:** Martin Gardner's *Scientific American* account popularizes the Game of Life.
- **1970s:** Surreal numbers and *On Numbers and Games* (1976); collaboration on combinatorial game theory and monstrous moonshine.
- **1982:** *Winning Ways* with Elwyn Berlekamp and Richard Guy; **1986:** five-author *Atlas of Finite Groups*.
- **1986–1987:** Transition from Cambridge to Princeton; institutional biographies give different appointment and arrival dates.
- **1988:** *Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups* with Neil Sloane; subsequent work includes the fifteen theorem and the free will theorem with Simon Kochen.
- **2020:** Died of complications associated with COVID-19.

### Mathematical companion

- **Life rule B3/S23:** A dead cell is born when exactly three of its eight neighbors are alive. A living cell survives with two or three neighbors. Synchronous update matters. A three-cell row is a period-two oscillator.
- **Group:** A set of symmetry operations closed under composition and inverses; the Conway groups are sporadic simple groups tied to the Leech lattice.
- **Surreal numbers:** In recursive notation \(\{L\mid R\}\), elements of \(L\) must be less than elements of \(R\). \(0=\{\mid\}\), \(1=\{0\mid\}\), \(1/2=\{0\mid1\}\). These constructions require the recursive formalism and proper-class care; games and numbers are related but not identical categories.
- **Moonshine:** Attributing its origins solely to Conway would omit McKay, Thompson, Norton and later Borcherds, among others.
- **Free will theorem:** Relies on specific mathematical assumptions in quantum physics; avoid philosophical overinterpretation.
