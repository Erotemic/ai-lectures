# 008000 — David Hilbert: Why do infinitely many coordinates still have geometry?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** Hilbert spaces and operator theory. **Background:** introductory linear algebra; the lecture defines its local objects.

David Hilbert's name appears in many parts of modern mathematics, but the Hilbert space that carries his name grew from a particular need. Mathematicians were solving equations whose unknowns were functions, not just numbers or finite vectors. They wanted to approximate a function using a long sequence of simpler ones, take limits, project onto convenient families, and speak about geometry even when there were infinitely many directions. The central question is simple enough to begin with: what must be true of an infinite collection of coordinates for ordinary Euclidean reasoning to remain valid? The answer brings together inner products, completeness, orthogonal projection, and the spectral theory of operators.

Hilbert was born in 1862 in Königsberg and became one of the major mathematicians associated with Göttingen. His work ranged through invariant theory, the foundations of geometry, number theory, analysis, mathematical physics, and logic. The famous list of twenty-three problems that he presented in 1900 helped shape mathematical research, but the geometry of infinite-dimensional spaces arose through another part of his work: integral equations and the variational methods surrounding them. The modern definition of a Hilbert space was assembled and clarified through contributions from several mathematicians, including Hilbert, Schmidt, Riesz, and von Neumann. Attaching one name to a mathematical structure should not erase that collective development.

His childhood gives little warning of the way he would transform mathematical research. His father Otto was a judge who prized regularity, while his mother Maria was interested in philosophy, astronomy, and prime numbers. At a classical school that rewarded memorization, David did not excel. He later recalled himself as a poor pupil there. A transfer to a school with better mathematical teaching changed his fortunes. In Königsberg he then found people who could share the pleasure of following a hard idea for hours: Adolf Hurwitz and the younger Hermann Minkowski became important friends. This was more than an intellectual convenience. Mathematical conversation, sustained by trust and argument, became one of the enduring features of Hilbert's working life.

Minkowski and Hilbert spent years exchanging ideas before either became a universally recognized name. Minkowski's wit and geometric imagination complemented Hilbert's desire for general principles. When Hilbert later established himself in Göttingen, he used a Berlin job offer as leverage to secure a chair for Minkowski there. He had married Käthe Jerosch in 1892, and they had a son, Franz, the following year. Göttingen was therefore both a family home and a scientific undertaking. The personal and institutional threads belong together because Hilbert's great mathematical school was built in a community of colleagues, students, visitors, and households.

Begin with vectors in two-dimensional space. The squared length of a vector is the sum of the squares of its coordinates. The inner product of two vectors is the sum of the products of corresponding coordinates. These operations reveal angles, perpendicularity, and the shortest distance to a line or plane. Now imagine an infinite sequence of real numbers. We might attempt to use the same formulas by summing forever. Not every sequence has finite squared length. The sequence consisting entirely of ones would have an infinite squared sum and cannot be treated as a finite-length vector. The natural candidate space consists of sequences whose squares have a finite total sum. Mathematicians call this square-summable sequence space, or ell two.

To understand the new space, take the sequence whose nth term is one divided by n. Its squared length is the sum of one over n squared, which converges, so the sequence belongs to ell two. The sequence whose entries are all one does not. Its squared total diverges. The space therefore admits infinitely many coordinates without admitting every possible infinite list. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality shows that the inner product of two square-summable sequences is well defined: the sum of their pairwise products converges absolutely. We can measure distance between two sequences using the squared length of their difference. We can ask whether they are perpendicular, and project onto subspaces, just as in Euclidean geometry.

The crucial new condition is completeness. A sequence of approximations might be successively closer to one another, but does it converge to an element of the space? An inner product alone does not ensure that the limit stays inside our chosen collection. Completeness says that every Cauchy sequence converges in the norm supplied by the inner product. This is the property that turns an inner-product space into a Hilbert space. Without it, we can run an approximation argument and discover that its proposed limit is no longer admissible. In an infinite-dimensional problem, that failure is not a technical inconvenience. It can invalidate an existence theorem.

Consider all finite sequences: lists with only finitely many nonzero coordinates, followed by zeros forever. This collection has an inner product, and every vector has finite length. But it is not complete. Take the first N coordinates of the sequence one, one half, one third, and so on, with the rest set to zero. Each finite truncation lies in the collection. As N increases, the truncations become Cauchy in squared length because the sum of the omitted reciprocal squares becomes small. Their limit has infinitely many nonzero entries, so it does not belong to the finite-support collection. Passing to its completion adds precisely the kind of limit demanded by the approximation process. That example is the finite-to-infinite transition in a form we can calculate.

Why did mathematicians care about such limits? Think about approximating a vibrating string. Its displacement is a function of position. We can approximate the function by adding sine waves of different frequencies, each multiplied by a coefficient. Truncating after a finite number of frequencies gives a manageable approximation, but the physical model involves the entire waveform. Orthogonality of sine modes lets us compute how much energy lies in each frequency. Completeness tells us when the finite approximations approach an honest function in the relevant norm. The coefficients play the role of coordinates, and the basis functions play the role of perpendicular coordinate axes. Function spaces inherit geometry through their inner products.

At Göttingen, Felix Klein encouraged mathematics to remain in contact with physical problems, and Hilbert's seminars could draw analysts, geometers, and physicists into the same room. Hilbert was willing to speak in very general terms, but the problems he chose often came from concrete questions: how can a boundary-value equation have a solution, or how can an infinite family of coefficients describe a physical state? Richard Courant learned this atmosphere as Hilbert's assistant before developing his own style of mathematical physics. It was a teaching culture that made abstraction seem like a way to get closer to reality, provided one could also establish the missing existence and convergence arguments.

For square-integrable functions on an interval, the inner product is the integral of one function times the other, with complex conjugation in the complex case. Two functions are considered the same vector when they agree almost everywhere. That last clause matters because changing a function at a single point does not change its square integral. The norm measures an average or energy-like size rather than its worst pointwise value. A sequence of functions can converge in this norm without converging uniformly at every point. This is another reason that infinite-dimensional analysis needs to specify what kind of convergence is intended. The ambient Hilbert-space norm is a powerful tool, but it is only one topology among several useful ones.

Orthogonal projection is the next piece of the geometry. In a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, every vector has a nearest point in any subspace. The difference between the vector and that nearest point is perpendicular to the subspace. In a Hilbert space, the same theorem holds for closed linear subspaces. Closedness is essential: if we project toward a dense but nonclosed subspace, the best approximation distance may be zero without any point of that subspace attaining it. The orthogonal projection theorem gives each vector a unique decomposition into its part inside a closed subspace and its perpendicular remainder. That decomposition is the basis for speaking about errors, residuals, and principal angles without committing to a finite coordinate system.

Here is an example using square-summable sequences. Let M consist of sequences whose first coordinate is zero. M is a closed subspace. For a sequence x, its projection onto M simply deletes the first coordinate and leaves every other coordinate unchanged. The discarded part is a scalar multiple of the first coordinate vector. That discarded vector is perpendicular to every vector in M. If the first coordinate of x is three, the distance from x to M is three in absolute value, regardless of what happens in all its later coordinates. This is the infinite-dimensional version of dropping a perpendicular to a plane. Nothing about the calculation requires listing all coordinates explicitly.

A different subspace shows why the closedness assumption cannot be skipped. Let F be the set of finite-support sequences inside ell two. F is a linear subspace, but it is dense and nonclosed. Choose the sequence with nth entry one divided by n. Its distance to F is zero: finite truncations come arbitrarily close. Yet the vector is not itself in F, so there is no nearest point in F with distance zero. The best approximation is only an unattained infimum. Replacing F by its closure, the whole ell-two space, restores the projection theorem. This example lets us hear the difference between 'arbitrarily good approximations exist' and 'a best approximation exists.'

The geometry also supports operators. A bounded linear operator sends vectors to vectors and satisfies a uniform estimate: the output norm is at most a constant times the input norm. A compact operator has a stronger approximation property: the image of the unit ball has compact closure, so in a Hilbert space it can often be approximated well by finite-rank operators. Integral operators with sufficiently regular kernels provide important examples. Hilbert's studies of integral equations helped make such operators central objects. Instead of solving an equation one scalar at a time, one studies an operator and asks about its spectral behavior, inverse, and eigenfunctions.

Think of an integral operator that averages a function against a kernel. The output at position x is the integral over y of the kernel at x and y times the input function at y. If the kernel is symmetric in the real case, the operator is self-adjoint under appropriate assumptions. In a favorable compact setting, the operator has orthogonal eigenfunctions and a discrete sequence of nonzero eigenvalues whose magnitudes can accumulate only at zero. These eigenfunctions play the role of principal axes of an ellipsoid. The infinite-dimensional spectral theorem is an extension of the matrix intuition, but the hypotheses matter: a general bounded self-adjoint operator need not have an orthonormal eigenbasis made entirely of ordinary eigenvectors.

The difference becomes clear with a multiplication operator. Take functions on the interval from zero to one, and multiply each function by the coordinate x. The resulting operator is bounded and self-adjoint on square-integrable functions. But it has no nonzero eigenfunction for a fixed value lambda: an eigenfunction would have to be supported where x equals lambda, which is a set of measure zero. The spectrum is nevertheless the full interval from zero to one. This is continuous spectrum. A basis of eigenvectors cannot describe it. The spectral theorem must instead use a projection-valued measure or an equivalent multiplication representation. The fact that a self-adjoint operator can have spectrum without eigenvectors marks a genuine departure from finite matrices.

How can an operator have spectral information when it lacks ordinary eigenvectors? For the multiplication operator, define the spectral projection for a measurable set B by retaining the part of a function supported where x lies in B and discarding the rest. If B is the interval from zero to one half, the projection keeps the function on that half and sets it to zero elsewhere. These projections behave like a collection of coordinated yes-or-no filters. They add over disjoint measurable regions and reconstruct the identity when the whole spectrum is included. Integrating a function of the spectral parameter against this projection-valued measure constructs functions of the operator. In the multiplication model, it simply replaces multiplication by x with multiplication by the chosen scalar function of x.

It is historically important not to say Hilbert personally wrote every detail of this modern spectral-measure construction. The mature spectral theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators and its projection-measure formulations were developed further by later mathematicians, especially von Neumann and others. Hilbert's work on integral equations, orthogonal expansions, and infinite-dimensional methods helped create the context in which such structures became necessary. The name Hilbert space records a foundational role in a mathematical evolution, not a claim of single-handed authorship of every theorem now proved in that setting.

The difference between a historical program and its later formal completion is also visible in Hilbert's relationships. He did not work as a solitary creator of all these definitions. Ernst Schmidt sharpened approximation methods; Riesz clarified the relationship between coefficients and functions; and von Neumann later gave operator theory a more systematic abstract setting. Hilbert's gift was to create problems and intellectual space in which such developments became urgent. His lectures, questions, and insistence on exact formulation drew other mathematicians into work whose final language none of them initially possessed in full.

The infinite-dimensional setting also forces a distinction between bounded and unbounded operators. On ell two, take the map that multiplies the nth coordinate by n. For a vector with finitely many nonzero entries this is always defined. But if we allowed every square-summable vector as input, the output could fail to be square summable. The correct domain consists of sequences whose n-weighted coordinates are square summable. The operator is unbounded because unit vectors concentrated in high coordinates have outputs with arbitrarily large norm. Yet it is a well-defined closed self-adjoint operator with that domain. Differential operators arise in a similar way: differentiation magnifies high frequencies and requires regularity assumptions on its inputs.

This matters directly for the geometry of perturbation theory. A small bounded change in a matrix is comparatively straightforward to define. For an unbounded operator, changing the differential expression may also change its domain, and smallness has to be measured in a suitable graph norm, form norm, or resolvent topology. Spectral projections can move continuously under some perturbation conditions and jump under others. The Hilbert-space language allows the problem to be stated: we know what the operator is, which vectors lie in its domain, what topology describes convergence, and which spectral subspace is under comparison. Without those specifications, 'a small change of the operator' remains ambiguous.

Hilbert's mathematical biography is broader than operator theory. His work on the foundations of geometry changed the role of axioms. His problems address number theory, geometry, analysis, and other fields. He was central to Göttingen's mathematical culture, where problems in mechanics, physics, and pure mathematics interacted. His later program sought secure foundations for formal mathematics; subsequent work, especially Gödel's incompleteness theorems, changed what could be hoped for from some of those ambitions. Those chapters are not prerequisites for proving the projection theorem. They matter because a historical lecture should place the operator-theoretic contribution within a career that also shaped questions about mathematical reasoning itself.

The same willingness to challenge conventions appeared in his defense of Emmy Noether. When Noether came to Göttingen, obstacles to a woman's official university appointment remained formidable. Hilbert and Klein supported her habilitation. For a time her courses appeared under Hilbert's name, an arrangement that both made it possible for her to teach and exposed the institution's unfairness. Noether was no mere beneficiary of a famous man's favor. Her work on invariant theory and the relation between symmetries and conservation laws transformed the mathematics around them. Hilbert's support is a telling feature of his university life: he could be uncompromising about the intellectual competence of a colleague while the surrounding academic system questioned whether she should be permitted to lecture at all.

A recurring theme of Hilbert's work was to turn a problem about particular formulas into a problem about general structure. In finite Euclidean geometry, the structure is an inner product and perpendicular projection. In sequence and function spaces, it is an inner product plus completeness, which makes approximation and limit arguments legitimate. For suitable operators, spectral analysis then turns complicated transformations into collections of simpler actions along orthogonal directions or measurable spectral regions. Each extension preserves part of the finite-dimensional mental model while requiring new hypotheses. This combination is why the language remains so useful for matrix perturbation, numerical algorithms, quantum mechanics, and partial differential equations.

There is an important theorem that turns the inner product into a universal language for continuous linear measurements. Suppose we have a bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space: a rule that takes a vector and returns a scalar, continuously and linearly. The Riesz representation theorem says there is a unique vector y such that the functional is the inner product with y, with the argument order adjusted to the chosen complex convention. In finite-dimensional Euclidean space this is familiar. Every linear functional is a dot product with some coefficient vector. The theorem says the same remains true in a complete infinite-dimensional inner-product space. The result is due to Frigyes Riesz, and its separate attribution is important even in a lecture on the space named after Hilbert.

Consider ell two and the functional that takes the first coordinate of a sequence. It is represented by the first coordinate unit vector: taking the inner product with that vector extracts exactly the first entry. A more interesting functional multiplies each coordinate by one over n and sums. The representing vector has entries one over n, which belong to ell two. Cauchy–Schwarz guarantees that this sum defines a bounded functional on every square-summable sequence. What if we attempted to represent the sum of all coordinates with no decaying coefficients? The all-ones sequence is not in ell two, and the proposed functional is not bounded on ell two. On vectors with N equal entries of size one over square root N, the input norm is one but the coordinate sum is square root N, which grows without bound. The representation theorem explains this failure precisely.

Orthonormal expansions provide another route from finite to infinite geometry. In a finite-dimensional space, an orthonormal basis makes squared length equal to the sum of squared coordinates. In a Hilbert space with a complete orthonormal family, the analogous Parseval identity says that the squared norm is the sum of squared inner-product coefficients. But completeness of the family must be distinguished from completeness of the space. An orthonormal family can fail to span densely, even in a complete Hilbert space. Then Bessel's inequality gives only that the sum of squared coefficients is at most the squared norm; some component of the vector lies perpendicular to the chosen family. Parseval's equality holds when no such leftover component remains. This distinction is easy to miss when a Fourier series is introduced by analogy with a finite vector expansion.

As a concrete example, choose sine functions on an interval under boundary conditions that make them an orthogonal system. For a function that belongs to the corresponding closed span, its sine coefficients determine its squared norm through Parseval's identity after normalization. Truncating the expansion gives the orthogonal projection onto the first N modes, and it is the best approximation from that N-dimensional span in the squared-integral norm. A different approximation criterion, such as maximum pointwise error, may select a different function and require different techniques. The familiar Fourier partial sum is optimal for a specific Hilbert-space metric, not for every conceivable error measure. This is an early encounter with the same norm sensitivity that later appears in matrix and subspace perturbation problems.

Compact operators show another way finite-dimensional intuition survives. In ordinary Euclidean space, a bounded sequence of vectors has a convergent subsequence. In an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, this fails. Take the standard coordinate unit vectors in ell two. Each has norm one, but any two different unit vectors are separated by square root two, so no subsequence converges in norm. The unit ball is not compact. A compact operator is one that transforms bounded sets into sets with compact closure. In ell two, the diagonal operator whose nth multiplier is one over n is compact: it suppresses high-frequency coordinates, and its finite-coordinate truncations approximate it uniformly in operator norm. The diagonal operator whose multiplier is constantly one is the identity and is not compact. The decay of singular values, not merely boundedness, drives the compactness.

This distinction affects spectral theory. A compact self-adjoint operator has an orthonormal spectral decomposition associated with its nonzero eigenvalues, with possible accumulation only at zero. The identity on infinite-dimensional ell two is bounded and self-adjoint but not compact; it still has an eigenbasis for a different reason. The multiplication-by-x operator on the interval is bounded and self-adjoint yet lacks a nonzero eigenfunction. It is not compact. These three operators show why we should not identify self-adjointness, compactness, and diagonalizability by a countable eigenbasis as if they were one property. The spectral theorem handles the general self-adjoint case through a more flexible measure-theoretic construction.

We can also understand the minimum principle geometrically. In a finite-dimensional inner-product space, minimizing a positive quadratic form over unit vectors produces the smallest eigenvalue. If we restrict the search to a subspace, the minimum may increase because we are searching over fewer vectors. The Courant–Fischer principle characterizes ordered eigenvalues by optimizing over subspaces of specified dimension. Similar variational ideas apply to compact self-adjoint operators, with care about the spectrum and accumulation. The projection theorem ensures best approximations in closed subspaces, while compactness often supplies existence of extremizers for spectral problems. This is the intellectual route from variational analysis to quantitative statements about Ritz values and spectral subspaces.

A short example makes the min–max principle audible. Let A be diagonal with eigenvalues one, four, and nine. For a unit vector with coordinates x, y, and z, its Rayleigh quotient is x squared plus four y squared plus nine z squared. The smallest value is one, achieved on the first coordinate axis. The largest is nine, achieved on the third. Now consider any two-dimensional subspace. It must contain some nonzero vector perpendicular to the third coordinate axis, because a two-plane and the coordinate plane spanned by the first two axes intersect nontrivially in three-space. That vector has Rayleigh quotient at most four. Conversely, the two-plane spanned by the second and third axes has every unit vector's quotient at least four. This dimension argument identifies the middle eigenvalue without solving a characteristic polynomial. It is the same geometry of subspace intersections that reappears in spectral perturbation inequalities.

When working with unbounded operators, the Hilbert-space norm alone may be too weak to control the operator. Return to the diagonal map multiplying coordinate n by n. The vectors supported at index n with amplitude one over n converge to zero in the Hilbert-space norm, yet their images all have norm one. To approximate an element of the domain faithfully, we may require convergence in graph norm, which measures both the input and its image. This reminds us that completeness is relative to a chosen norm. The domain equipped with its graph norm can be a complete space even though it is only a proper dense subspace of the ambient Hilbert space. The two spaces have different purposes and should not be conflated.

One can view this entire development as a sequence of carefully justified extensions. Ordinary coordinates suggest a squared-sum norm. Completing the finite vectors gives ell two. Generalizing the inner product to square-integrable functions gives geometric approximation for functions. Orthogonal projection extends nearest-point reasoning to closed subspaces. Bounded and compact operators then inherit useful analogues of matrices, while unbounded operators require domains and graph norms. Spectral measures handle continuous spectra that a simple eigenvector list cannot describe. These steps grew through the work of multiple generations of mathematicians. Hilbert's central historical place is in the problems and analytic techniques that helped make the infinite-dimensional viewpoint indispensable.

By the middle of the 1920s, the institute Hilbert had helped make famous was facing a more personal kind of fragility. He became gravely ill with pernicious anemia, then often fatal. Friends and colleagues, including Courant, helped arrange treatment with a new liver-based therapy, and Hilbert survived. The story is a reminder that the apparently timeless mathematical program depended on a vulnerable person. He continued to appear in the university's life, but age and illness changed the practical rhythm of his work. A life's mathematics can outlast its author's ability to sustain the daily intellectual performance for which students once knew him.

The geographical and institutional context reinforces that point without replacing the mathematics. Göttingen around the turn of the twentieth century was a major center for work linking analysis, geometry, and mathematical physics. Problems about integral equations, boundary values, and eigenfunction expansions were active subjects, not retrospective exercises invented to introduce abstract spaces. The framework developed in response to those problems, and was refined by Hilbert's collaborators, students, and successors. Later terminology gave the completed structure a concise name. Understanding that history can prevent us from mistaking a polished modern definition for an idea that appeared all at once in one person's work.

The final years of Göttingen's old mathematical community were harsher still. After 1933, the Nazi regime expelled Jewish scholars and political opponents from German universities. Many of the mathematicians who had made Göttingen a center of international research lost their posts and emigrated. Hilbert remained in Germany, now elderly and in declining health, as the community around him was dismantled. That history must be told without turning him into a symbol of perfect resistance or assigning him a role that the record will not support. What disappeared was not only a roster of famous names; it was a network of people, conversations, students, and competing ideas on which the mathematical achievements had depended.

A modern application brings the story back to geometry. Suppose an algorithm estimates a low-dimensional eigenspace of a large discretized differential operator. The discretization lives in a finite-dimensional vector space, but its purpose may be to approximate an operator on an infinite-dimensional function space. Orthogonal projections make the approximate and exact spaces comparable. Compactness or spectral isolation may justify a discrete approximation, and a perturbation theorem may bound the resulting principal angles. But a convergence argument is also needed to connect the discretized model to the original operator and its domain. That chain of reasoning draws on the same inner-product, projection, and completeness ideas that motivated the development of Hilbert-space analysis.

There is also a geometric way to hear why completeness makes infinite sums meaningful. Take mutually perpendicular vectors whose squared lengths form a summable sequence. Their finite partial sums form a Cauchy sequence because the squared length of the tail is just the sum of the remaining squared lengths. In a complete Hilbert space, those partial sums converge to a vector. Without completeness, the same formal coefficients might describe an object outside the space. This is the foundation of orthogonal series, Fourier methods, and the expansion of vectors in principal or singular directions. The abstract condition that Cauchy sequences converge has a concrete job: it guarantees that the infinite orthogonal constructions needed by analysis actually produce vectors in the space under study.

Return to the finite truncations of the reciprocal sequence. We began with ordinary coordinate geometry. We discovered a Cauchy sequence of finite approximations whose limit had infinitely many nonzero coordinates, and so we enlarged the space to include it. Once the space was complete, we could take limits and orthogonally project onto closed subspaces. We could then represent operators and understand their spectra, even when no discrete eigenbasis exists. David Hilbert's historical role lies in the development of the methods and problems that led to this geometric language. For the theory of spectral subspace angles, the payoff is immediate: a vector, a projection, an invariant subspace, and an operator can be discussed in the same rigorous geometry, whether the underlying system has three coordinates or infinitely many.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### Why completion is needed

The space of finitely supported sequences, `c_00`, is an inner-product space but
is not complete in the `l^2` norm. For `x=(1,1/2,1/3,...)` and its truncations
`x_N`,

\[
\|x_N-x\|_2^2=\sum_{n>N}n^{-2}\longrightarrow0,
\qquad x\notin c_{00}, \quad x\in\ell^2.
\]

The orthogonal projection theorem applies to **closed** subspaces `M` of a
Hilbert space: each `x` has a unique decomposition `x=P_M x+(I-P_M)x` with
`(I-P_M)x \perp M`. The dense, nonclosed `c_00` illustrates why closedness
cannot be dropped.

### Riesz representation, Parseval, and spectral models

Every continuous linear functional on a real Hilbert space `H` has the form
`f(x)=\langle x,y\rangle` for one `y\in H` (complex conventions affect
argument order). Bessel's inequality becomes equality for a complete
orthonormal family. For a bounded self-adjoint multiplication operator on
`L^2([0,1])`, `Mf(t)=tf(t)`,

\[
(E(B)f)(t)=\mathbf 1_B(t)f(t),\qquad\sigma(M)=[0,1],
\]

but `M` has no nonzero eigenvectors. This distinguishes the general spectral
measure theorem from a pure eigenbasis expansion. The diagonal operator
`A(x_n)=(n x_n)` is self-adjoint on
`D(A)={x\in\ell^2:(n x_n)\in\ell^2}`, not on all of `\ell^2`.

### Historical context

Hilbert's research on integral equations, variational analysis, and
orthogonal expansions was part of the development of modern Hilbert-space
theory. Schmidt, Riesz, and von Neumann made independent contributions to the
modern formulation. Avoid attributing the full modern spectral-measure
formulation to Hilbert alone.
