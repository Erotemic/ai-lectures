# Martin Wilhelm Kutta: How can four slope measurements predict a changing world?

Picture an engineer trying to calculate the path of a small aircraft. The forces change as it climbs and turns. Its velocity changes, and that in turn changes the forces. To predict the flight, we could write differential equations relating position, velocity, acceleration, and time. But the equations may be coupled and nonlinear. Even if they capture the physics well, we are left with the practical question: how does one compute an actual trajectory?

A second question is hiding in the same aircraft. Why does a wing develop lift? Air flows around it; the flow speeds up or slows down, and its circulation is related to the force on the wing. Once again, the mathematics involves continuous fields, derivatives, and the behavior of systems that resist simple formulas. Martin Wilhelm Kutta made enduring contributions to both problems. His name belongs to one of the world's most widely used families of differential-equation solvers and to a fundamental relation in theoretical aerodynamics.

Kutta lived from eighteen sixty-seven to nineteen forty-four. He was a German mathematician and engineer who loved languages, music, art, history, and the problem of making mathematics useful to the physical sciences. His life is often reduced to the second name in Runge–Kutta. That shorthand obscures two important facts. First, his nineteen hundred dissertation and nineteen oh one publication carried out a systematic study of numerical methods well beyond a single formula. Second, he did independent original work on flight and fluid motion at a time when powered aviation was just emerging.

## From Upper Silesia to Munich

Kutta was born on November third, eighteen sixty-seven, in Pitschen, then in Upper Silesia and now called Byczyna in Poland. His parents, Wilhelm Kutta and Anna Koschinsky, died while he was young. He and his older brother Karl were raised by an uncle in Breslau, now Wrocław. After studying at the Gymnasium there, Kutta attended the University of Breslau and later the University of Munich. Mathematics was his central subject, but contemporary biographical accounts emphasize his sustained interest in literature, music, art, and languages. He was neither a narrow technician nor someone who regarded engineering applications as outside mathematics.

Early orphanhood is one of the few concrete facts we have about his private beginnings. The brothers remained together while their uncle's household became their home in Breslau. Karl went on to scholarly work himself. It is possible to imagine how that change of family would shape a child, but the record does not give us Kutta's own account of his feelings, and the lecture should not invent one. What it does show is a young man whose education continued through considerable disruption and whose interests soon spread well beyond the subject in which he became famous.

He qualified to teach mathematics and physics but pursued university work instead. In Munich he assisted Walther von Dyck with instruction in higher mathematics. He spent the eighteen ninety-eight to eighteen ninety-nine academic year at Cambridge in England, extending his contact with a different mathematical culture. At Munich, where geometry, experimental science, and applied mathematics met, Kutta found a setting for the two projects that would define his reputation: numerical integration and aerodynamics.

Those interests were not a passing distraction. He studied languages, music, and art alongside mathematics, and continued to read deeply in history and literature. In Munich he entered a working environment where teaching higher mathematics, looking at experimental photographs, and asking theoretical questions could overlap. His mathematical range began in a life already full of intellectual pursuits. That helps explain why he could move from numerical integration to the physics of flight without treating the second as an alien subject.

The demands of his teaching and research also made one limitation especially plain. Around nineteen hundred, numerical integration meant working without electronic computers. Differential equations arose in heat, electricity, astronomy, and mechanics, but their approximate solutions required long tables and repeated hand calculation. Each derivative evaluation consumed attention. A high-order method that reduced the number of evaluations could save a researcher days of work. Kutta's dissertation arose in this world of laborious arithmetic, where the beauty of a formula had to be measured against its cost and reliability.

## An equation that resists a convenient formula

Begin with a simple equation: the rate at which y changes equals y itself. Start at time zero with y equal to one. This equation happens to have the exact solution y equals the exponential of time. At time one, the answer is about two point seven one eight two eight. We will use it as a laboratory in which to test approximation methods, because knowing the exact answer lets us measure errors honestly.

Euler's method takes the current slope and moves forward as though the slope remained constant for the entire interval. With one step of length one, it predicts two. There is no error in the derivative evaluation: the slope at the starting point really is one. The error comes from treating a changing derivative as constant. We could use many shorter Euler steps, but those extra steps require extra evaluations. A more intelligent procedure would inspect the derivative at additional points inside the same step.

Carl Runge's eighteen ninety-five work gave a systematic starting point. He showed how to compute trial slopes within one step and combine them to improve on Euler. Suppose we estimate the midpoint using the first slope and then use the slope at that estimated midpoint for the full update. For the growing exponential with a step of one, this gives two point five. It is closer to two point seven one eight than Euler's two, but still short. The new question is whether we can select several carefully positioned slopes and weights so that the error cancels to still higher order.

Notice the word select. If we merely compute four random slopes and average them, we have spent more effort but gained no assurance of accuracy. The trial points and the weights must satisfy equations. Kutta investigated how to choose them systematically. His doctoral thesis, submitted in nineteen hundred and published in nineteen oh one, developed formulas for approximate integration of differential equations, including higher-order methods. Karl Heun's work in nineteen hundred was part of the same early development; it is historically more accurate to speak of Runge, Heun, Kutta, and later contributors than to imagine a single isolated inventor.

## Four slopes and a fourth-order answer

The best-known member of the family today uses four derivative evaluations per step. We can describe it without writing a large array of symbols. The first slope is measured at the beginning of the interval. Use that to make a provisional move halfway across. Measure a second slope at this provisional midpoint. Use the second slope to make a new provisional midpoint, and measure a third slope. Then use the third slope to predict the state at the end and measure a fourth slope there.

The final update is a weighted average of those four slopes. Give the first slope one share, the second two shares, the third two shares, and the final slope one share. Divide by six, multiply by the step length, and add the result to the old state. The weights sum to one, so if every slope were identical, the rule would recover the correct straight-line advance. But matching a constant slope is only the first requirement. The internal time positions and repeated midpoint corrections also make the formula agree with more terms in the exact solution's Taylor expansion.

Let's compute one step for y prime equals y. The initial value is one, and the step length is one. The first slope is one. The first midpoint estimate is one point five, so the second slope is one point five. The second midpoint estimate is one plus half of one point five, which is one point seven five, giving the third slope one point seven five. The endpoint estimate based on that third slope is two point seven five, so the fourth slope is two point seven five. Now form the weighted average. We add one, twice one point five, twice one point seven five, and two point seven five. The total is ten point two five. Divide by six and add to the starting value. The new prediction is about two point seven zero eight three three.

That is much closer to the exact two point seven one eight two eight. Euler gave two. The midpoint method gave two point five. The four-stage formula gives about two point seven zero eight. The error has fallen from more than seven tenths, to about two tenths, to less than one hundredth. We have done extra work, but the improvement is larger than a naive average of slopes would suggest.

It is important to interpret the example correctly. With a step as large as one, we should not expect the formal small-step theory to deliver perfect asymptotic ratios. And the equation y prime equals y is exceptionally smooth. The example shows how the weights function; it does not establish a universal guarantee that four evaluations will always give a decimal answer accurate to two digits. The strength of the method is its mathematical order under appropriate regularity, combined with the ability to choose a sufficiently small step.

## A nonlinear equation changes the trial states

The exponential example has an advantage: every derivative is numerically equal to the current value. But the idea works for more complicated laws. Consider y prime equals y squared, starting at one. This law becomes steeper as y grows, and it has the exact solution one divided by one minus t, at least until t reaches one. A step of one tenth should take the value from one to one divided by zero point nine, or roughly one point one one one one one.

The first slope in a Runge–Kutta step is one. The first provisional midpoint value is one point zero five. Because the derivative is the square of the state, the second slope is the square of one point zero five, or one point one zero two five. The next provisional midpoint uses that updated slope, giving a value slightly larger than one point zero five. The third slope is therefore a little larger still. The fourth slope is calculated at a provisional endpoint, and the final answer uses the same one-two-two-one weighting. All four derivative evaluations use the same original differential law, but each evaluation sees a different trial state.

What changed from the exponential example? The derivative is now a nonlinear function of y. The method does not need a new formula tailored to the square. It only needs to evaluate the function at the stage points. That flexibility is why the general construction became so valuable. The same code can integrate models of motion, chemical reactions, electrical circuits, or population change, provided their right-hand sides can be evaluated and the chosen numerical method is suitable for their time scales.

There is also a warning inside this example. The exact solution becomes unbounded as time approaches one. No finite-step method can make that singularity disappear. A high-order approximation can track the solution well for a while and then become unreliable near the blow-up. When a mathematical model itself has a finite-time singularity, numerical output should be interpreted with that structure in mind. A large computed number may be a sign of physical model breakdown, numerical instability, or a genuine analytic singularity. The algorithm alone cannot decide which explanation is correct.

## Why fourth order means something precise

What does it mean to say the scheme is fourth order? Imagine that we already know the exact value at time t and ask how well one numerical step predicts the exact value at t plus h. Expand the true solution in powers of the step size. There is a term proportional to h, another to h squared, another to h cubed, and another to h to the fourth. The classical four-stage rule is constructed so that its expansion matches all those terms. The first possible difference occurs at order h to the fifth.

This is a statement about the local defect, the error made by one step that starts from the exact state. If the equation is sufficiently smooth and its solutions depend regularly on initial conditions over a finite interval, the errors from approximately one over h steps accumulate into a global error of order h to the fourth. Roughly speaking, when we halve a sufficiently small step, the error should fall by about a factor of sixteen. That is a dramatic improvement over Euler's first-order behavior, where the factor is about two, and over a second-order method, where it is about four.

The qualifying conditions matter. An ordinary differential equation can be stiff: some components decay or change on very fast time scales while the feature of interest evolves slowly. A numerical method might need tiny steps merely to remain stable, even when the true solution is smooth on the scale we care about. Higher formal order does not abolish stability restrictions. Nor does a method automatically conserve energy, preserve positivity, or respect every geometric invariant of the original differential equation. Those properties require their own analysis and, sometimes, different integrators.

The freedom of the Runge–Kutta framework becomes clearer here. Why choose the particular weights one, two, two, one? Because they satisfy a collection of algebraic order conditions together with the internal stage choices. Other choices also satisfy fourth-order conditions. One historically important alternative, the three-eighths rule, uses four stages with a different pattern of internal positions and weights. The modern framework is a family of algorithms, not a single immutable recipe.

## How Kutta turned trial steps into design equations

We can understand the first order conditions without sophisticated machinery. Suppose y prime equals f of time and y. A one-step method combines several stage slopes into an update. If every stage slope is the same constant, we want the change to be that constant multiplied by the step length. The weights must therefore add to one. If the slope changes linearly with time, the weighted sample times must reproduce the average position of the interval, so their weighted average must be its midpoint. That creates the next algebraic condition.

For a nonlinear equation the stages interact. A provisional state is built using earlier slopes, and then the derivative is evaluated at that provisional state. As a result, the conditions are about nested derivatives as well as simple powers of time. Some correspond to branching dependencies: changing the state changes the slope, which changes a later trial state, which changes another slope. Modern order theory represents those dependencies by rooted trees. That theory was developed long after Kutta by John Butcher and others, and it should not be projected backward as if Kutta used the contemporary tree notation.

Kutta's contribution was to extend the finite-stage construction and investigate how to obtain high-order accuracy by matching expansions. His work helped turn a useful numerical trick into a mathematical design problem. Instead of memorizing a special formula, one could ask a sequence of general questions. How many evaluations will be used? At what internal times? Which combinations of earlier slopes define the next trial state? What algebraic constraints force the desired accuracy? What remains when the formula is applied to a particular physical system?

That question about translating mathematics into physical motion was already present in the Munich institute around him. Sebastian Finsterwalder, who worked on geodesy and early flight, brought photographs of aircraft into the conversation. They caught Kutta's attention. The same scholar who wanted a reliable numerical step now asked a different but related question: what mathematical information determines the force on a wing? His interest in flight was therefore a change of problem, grounded in a familiar concern for turning continuous laws into usable predictions.

## Where the order conditions come from

Let us derive one condition in speech, rather than treating fourth-order accuracy as an unexplained label. Suppose an equation gives the derivative as a function of the current time and state. Over a short step, the true change is approximately the initial slope times the step length. That is the first Taylor term. A weighted average of stage slopes must reproduce it, so the weights add to one. If the derivative changes with time, the next Taylor term is half the step squared times the rate of change of that derivative along the solution.

That last phrase, along the solution, is important. The derivative of f with respect to time has two contributions. One comes from changing time directly. The other comes from changing the state y, which itself changes at rate f. In compact mathematical language, the total derivative is f sub t plus f sub y times f. The midpoint method captures that combined effect by moving to a provisional state before measuring the second slope. Simply sampling the time at the midpoint but keeping the old state would miss the second contribution whenever f depends on y.

At third and fourth order, the dependencies multiply. A new trial state can depend on a previously calculated slope, which itself depended on an earlier state estimate. The resulting conditions are not just quadrature rules for a time integral with a known integrand. They are conditions for an integral whose integrand depends on the unknown evolving solution. This is why the subject has a real mathematical theory, and why Kutta's extensions were more than an attractive numerical recipe.

There is still room for choice after satisfying the order conditions. Different coefficient patterns may require similar arithmetic but have different error constants or stability properties. If each evaluation is expensive, minimizing the number of stages may matter. If the model has very rapid decaying modes, the stability region may matter more than a high formal order. If the model has conserved quantities, geometric behavior matters. The development from Kutta's early methods to modern integrators is partly the search for ways to make those priorities explicit in mathematical design.

## The mathematics of a wing

Near the turn of the twentieth century, powered flight was still a difficult and developing enterprise. Engineers and experimenters had discovered much through gliders and wind tests, but an adequate mathematical description of lift was still being constructed. Sebastian Finsterwalder, a geodesist and mathematician working in Munich, was interested in aviation and brought photographs of early aircraft to the institute. Kutta became deeply interested in the mathematical problem of how air flowing around a wing can create an upward force.

To approach that problem, imagine a two-dimensional cross section of a long wing in a steady stream of air. Away from the wing, the air moves at some uniform speed. Around the wing, the flow is deflected, and its velocity varies with position. Under idealized conditions we may model the air as incompressible and inviscid outside thin boundary layers, and describe portions of the flow with a potential function. This simplification does not capture every detail of a real wing. Its purpose is to make the relation between geometry, circulation, and force mathematically tractable.

Circulation measures the accumulated component of fluid velocity along a closed curve. Imagine walking around the wing along an imaginary loop and measuring how strongly the air's motion aligns with your direction of travel at each point. Add these contributions around the loop. With a consistent orientation, the result is called gamma. Circulation is a single quantity associated with the flow around the body, even though the velocity field varies over an entire region of space.

The Kutta–Joukowski theorem relates circulation to lift for a steady two-dimensional ideal flow. In magnitude, the lift force per unit span is the fluid density times the speed of the oncoming stream times the magnitude of the circulation. The force direction depends on the orientation assigned to circulation and the stream direction. The formula is powerful because a complicated field of velocities around a curved body enters a simple relation through a single integral quantity.

Work a small example. Suppose the air density is one point two kilograms per cubic meter, the stream speed is ten meters per second, and the circulation magnitude is two square meters per second. The idealized lift per meter of span is one point two times ten times two, or twenty-four newtons per meter. This does not tell us the lift of every real three-dimensional wing. Finite wing effects, viscous flow, stall, compressibility, and changing flight conditions can all alter the situation. It tells us precisely what the ideal two-dimensional calculation predicts under its assumptions.

## A physical condition at the trailing edge

The phrase Kutta condition appears frequently in aerodynamics and deserves explanation. In ideal potential flow around an airfoil, several mathematical flows may satisfy the same broad far-field conditions while having different circulations. The governing ideal equations alone can leave freedom to choose which circulation occurs. For an airfoil with a sharp trailing edge, the physically relevant steady flow is typically selected by requiring the velocity to remain finite and the fluid to leave the edge smoothly, rather than rushing around a sharp corner with a mathematical singularity.

The condition is named for Kutta because of his role in developing the mathematical theory. Its practical meaning can be understood without solving an entire complex-variable boundary-value problem. When a plane wing moves through air, the real fluid is viscous, even if the outer flow is approximated as inviscid. The development of the starting vortex and boundary layer helps select a circulation consistent with smooth flow at the trailing edge. The condition packages that physical selection into the idealized calculation.

The difference between the Kutta condition and the lift theorem is worth preserving. The condition helps determine which potential flow around the wing is the relevant one. The theorem then relates the resulting circulation to the lift. Knowing the latter formula without any way to determine circulation would leave an important part of the engineering problem unsolved. This is another example of mathematics needing both equations and a meaningful procedure to select their solution.

Kutta's work on these questions led to his nineteen oh two habilitation. Nikolai Zhukovsky, frequently transliterated Joukowski, obtained related lift results independently. Their names remain joined in the Kutta–Joukowski relation. Kutta subsequently published important work on plane circulation flows and conformal mappings, including research in nineteen ten and nineteen eleven. Conformal maps transform regions in the complex plane while preserving infinitesimal angles; in fluid mechanics they can turn a hard boundary geometry into one for which the equations are easier to handle.

## Mapping a circle into the shape of a wing

Complex analysis provides one elegant way to study ideal two-dimensional flow. Suppose we know how a potential flow behaves around a circle. The symmetry of a circle makes the equations easier to manipulate. A conformal map can transform the region outside the circle into a region outside a wing-like profile. The transformation preserves angles locally and carries analytic potential-flow constructions from one domain to another. This does not mean that every detail of the flow is unchanged. Velocity values must be transformed, and the geometry of the new boundary creates important conditions at the trailing edge.

A famous example is a Joukowski transformation, in which a complex point z is mapped to z plus a constant divided by z. Under a suitable choice of circle and parameters, its image develops an airfoil-like shape. The underlying mathematical idea is that one can solve an idealized flow problem in a geometrically simple domain and then transport the result to a more relevant one. The sharp edge of the transformed profile is precisely where the requirement of regular, finite velocity becomes important in selecting the circulation.

This technique is striking because it combines pure complex-variable mathematics with the engineering need to predict forces. It belongs to the wider context of work by Kutta, Zhukovsky, and other researchers on airfoil geometry. It also shows why a numerical integration expert could naturally become an aerodynamic theorist. Both subjects are concerned with constructing a manageable representation of a complicated physical situation. The representation becomes scientifically useful when we know how to interpret it and how its assumptions affect the prediction.

## The broader work of an applied mathematician

It is easy to imagine that a mathematician whose name survives in two famous formulas spent every year pursuing those formulas. Kutta's publication history resists that simplification. He worked on heat transfer in calorimeters, the measurement and mapping of glaciers, and the history of mathematics. Some of his glacier research involved photographs taken in the eastern Alps, combined with geometric methods to reconstruct positions and surfaces. This was photogrammetry: using images and geometry to measure objects and landscapes.

A glacier and a wing seem to belong to different sciences. Yet both present a demand for geometry to turn physical observations into quantitative results. In glacier mapping, photographs are converted into measurements of positions and shapes. In the airfoil problem, conformal mappings and circulation integrals make difficult flow fields tractable. In numerical integration, intermediate slope evaluations convert a differential law into a trajectory. Kutta did not need all these projects to be instances of the same theorem. He was attracted to mathematical techniques that let investigators make responsible use of limited information.

He was also a reader of mathematical history. One of his papers examined John Wallis's seventeenth-century work on integration and the length of an ellipse. Historical scholarship was a serious interest for him, rather than a decorative hobby. The mathematician working to improve numerical integration was curious about the origin of earlier integration techniques and about the route by which abstract methods became usable mathematics.

A colleague, F. Pfeiffer, later wrote that he had rarely met anyone with Kutta's range of interests. The same recollection describes him as a lonely man. Both observations deserve to remain together. His reading, art, languages, mathematics, and instruction were expansive, yet a contemporary did not remember him as socially surrounded in the way some university personalities were. We cannot turn that comment into a diagnosis or invent conversations explaining it. It does, however, give the portrait a human tension: wide intellectual curiosity can coexist with a relatively solitary personal life.

Kutta's teaching career crossed Jena, Aachen, and finally Stuttgart, where the German biographical record places his appointment in nineteen twelve. He remained there until retirement in nineteen thirty-five. His audience was often made up of future engineers, students who needed mathematics to work on structures, machines, and physical processes. Biographical recollections praise the clarity and enthusiasm of his instruction. The numerical methods he studied were well suited to such an audience: they are compact enough to calculate by hand, but deep enough to force questions about approximation, error, and reliability.

His historical interests also make a final numerical calculation fitting. Kutta had studied Wallis's seventeenth-century mathematics; he knew that the history of integration was a chain of methods repeatedly improved, questioned, and sometimes corrected. To understand the limits of the newer methods named for him, we should put the celebrated fourth-order scheme in a situation where its apparent strength is tested.

## A more demanding numerical example

Before the lecture ends, consider why a fourth-order method should not be treated as an oracle. Suppose a model describes a decaying quantity whose derivative is minus fifteen times the quantity itself. The true solution starting from one decreases very rapidly. If we use the classical four-stage method with a step length of one, the numerical amplification factor is one plus z, plus half z squared, plus one sixth z cubed, plus one twenty-fourth z to the fourth, with z equal to minus fifteen. This polynomial is an excellent approximation to the exponential only when z is sufficiently small. At minus fifteen it is enormous and positive. Instead of rapid decay, the numerical step produces a huge number.

Why does this happen? The fourth-order method agrees with the exponential's power series through four powers of z, but the true exponential at a large negative argument depends on cancellation among infinitely many terms. Truncating after four terms destroys that cancellation. The lesson is not that Kutta's formula was badly designed. It is that approximation order and stability region answer different questions. A fourth-order method may be extraordinarily effective for a nonstiff problem and ineffective when a large negative eigenvalue forces a time step far smaller than the scale of the behavior we want to observe.

Modern numerical analysis studies implicit methods, adaptive step sizes, embedded error estimates, geometric integrators, and schemes designed for stiffness. Many belong to the Runge–Kutta family. John Butcher and other twentieth-century researchers developed systematic order theory. Peter Fehlberg and others helped develop embedded schemes that estimate local error. These are later developments, not things Kutta himself claimed. His work supplied a powerful starting point: the idea that a numerical integrator can be designed by placing several evaluations within one time interval and imposing mathematical conditions on their combination.

One can see a parallel with his aerodynamic work. A few measurements of velocity do not automatically determine the entire flow around a wing. A few derivative evaluations do not automatically determine the correct next state of an ODE. In both cases a valid result requires structure: conditions that link the local data to the global quantity being sought. For the integrator, that structure is supplied by order and stability analysis. For ideal flow, it is supplied by conservation laws, boundary conditions, circulation, and the physical assumptions behind the model.

Kutta died on Christmas Day in nineteen forty-four, at Fürstenfeldbruck near Munich, as the Second World War approached its end. His lifetime spanned mathematical tables, early aircraft, two world wars, and the threshold of electronic computation. The machines that would soon repeat a Runge–Kutta step millions of times made his work more widely useful than he could have known. But the need to understand the limitations of those steps remained exactly the kind of mathematical question he had taught students to ask.

Kutta's legacy is therefore larger than the mnemonic of one, two, two, one. He contributed to a discipline in which algorithms are themselves mathematical objects. One can derive them, compare them, identify their failure modes, and adapt them to the needs of physical problems. His work in aerodynamics shows the same conviction that mathematical rigor should reach beyond the blackboard into the interpretation of natural phenomena. The best tribute to a method named for him is to know what it approximates, why its weights work, and when its predictions are entitled to our trust.

## Mathematical and historical companion (display only)

### 1. Classical four-stage fourth-order scheme

For \(y'=f(t,y)\), \(h>0\):
\[
\begin{aligned}
k_1&=f(t_n,y_n),\\
k_2&=f(t_n+h/2,y_n+hk_1/2),\\
k_3&=f(t_n+h/2,y_n+hk_2/2),\\
k_4&=f(t_n+h,y_n+hk_3),\\
y_{n+1}&=y_n+\frac h6(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4).
\end{aligned}
\]
For \(y'=y\), \(y_0=1\), \(h=1\), slopes are \(1,3/2,7/4,11/4\), and \(y_1=65/24\approx2.708333\), versus \(e\approx2.7182818\).

### 2. Order conditions through four

For an explicit RK method with \(s\) stages, coefficients \(A=(a_{ij})\), weights \(b\), nodes \(c=A\mathbf1\), and \(C=\mathrm{diag}(c)\), standard order-four conditions are:
\[
\begin{gathered}
b^T\mathbf1=1,\quad b^Tc=1/2,\quad b^Tc^2=1/3,\quad b^TAc=1/6,\\
b^Tc^3=1/4,\quad b^TCAc=1/8,\quad b^TAc^2=1/12,\quad b^TA^2c=1/24.
\end{gathered}
\]
All powers of \(c\) here are entrywise; the final condition uses matrix multiplication. This compact vector notation and rooted-tree interpretation are **modern** theory and should not be attributed directly to Kutta's 1901 paper.

### 3. Three-eighths alternative

A distinct four-stage fourth-order RK method has \(c=(0,1/3,2/3,1)\), \(b=(1/8,3/8,3/8,1/8)\), and
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\1/3&0&0&0\\-1/3&1&0&0\\1&-1&1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Both the classical and three-eighths tableaux have order four, but their internal stages differ.

### 4. Stability on the scalar test equation

For \(y'=\lambda y\), RK4 produces \(y_{n+1}=R(h\lambda)y_n\), where
\[
R(z)=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}+\frac{z^3}{6}+\frac{z^4}{24}.
\]
At \(z=-15\), \(R(-15)=1-15+112.5-562.5+2109.375=1645.375\), whereas \(e^{-15}\approx3.059\times10^{-7}\). This is an explicit demonstration that high order does not imply stability for an arbitrarily large time step.

### 5. Kutta–Joukowski lift and Kutta condition

In steady, two-dimensional, incompressible, inviscid flow with suitable far-field and regularity assumptions, lift per unit span satisfies \(|L'|=\rho U_\infty |\Gamma|\). Orientation conventions determine the sign. For \(\rho=1.2\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(U_\infty=10\,\mathrm{m/s}\), \(|\Gamma|=2\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\), this gives \(|L'|=24\,\mathrm{N/m}\). The Kutta condition selects a physically relevant circulation for a sharp trailing edge by imposing a regular exit of flow; the lift theorem then relates that circulation to force. Real finite wings and viscous phenomena need additional analysis.
