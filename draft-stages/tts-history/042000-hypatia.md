# 042000 — Hypatia of Alexandria: What survives when a mathematician's books are lost?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** ancient mathematical teaching, conics, astronomy, and historical evidence. **Background:** elementary arithmetic and geometry; ancient and modern methods are carefully distinguished.

Imagine an important mathematician whose own books have disappeared. Her students have left letters, later scholars have preserved references to the works she taught, and one contemporary historian has described the public life and violent death of the teacher. What could we honestly reconstruct about her mathematics? That is the challenge of Hypatia of Alexandria. She was a philosopher, a teacher, and a mathematician in the eastern Roman Empire around the beginning of the fifth century. Her name has become a symbol of intellectual courage and of violence against learning. The symbols are powerful. The historical person deserves something more demanding: a close look at the evidence for what she knew, what she taught, and how the mathematical culture she inhabited survived through texts, instruments, and students.

Hypatia lived in Alexandria, the great Egyptian city founded in the Hellenistic period and long a center of Greek scholarship. It belonged by her time to the Roman Empire and contained intertwined Greek, Egyptian, Jewish, and Christian communities. Its libraries and schools had long histories, but we should not picture a single unbroken ancient university functioning exactly like a modern campus. Scholars taught in a variety of settings, and intellectual authority depended on patrons, pupils, texts, personal reputation, and religious politics. The city had a deep inheritance from Euclid, Apollonius, Ptolemy, and Diophantus. Hypatia belonged to a late stage of that continuing mathematical tradition.

Her date of birth is uncertain, often placed somewhere in the middle or later fourth century. Historians agree much more securely on her death in four hundred fifteen. Her father, Theon of Alexandria, was a mathematician and astronomer associated with commentaries and editions of important Greek works. He wrote on Ptolemy's Almagest and contributed to the textual transmission of Euclid. Hypatia received a mathematical education in this environment and became a respected public teacher of philosophy and mathematical sciences. She attracted students from beyond Alexandria, some of them politically and socially prominent. One, Synesius of Cyrene, left letters that are among our strongest windows into her world.

Before reading those letters, it helps to understand what a late-antique mathematical commentary actually did. Modern readers may imagine research only as discovering a wholly new theorem. Ancient and late-antique mathematicians also sustained their disciplines by copying difficult manuscripts, correcting errors, comparing versions, adding explanatory arguments, arranging problems, and teaching readers how to move through advanced demonstrations. The survival of a text through many centuries could depend on these editorial acts. Commentary was intellectual work. It could preserve proofs that otherwise vanished and help generations of students learn complicated reasoning.

A tenth-century Byzantine encyclopedia, the Suda, credits Hypatia with work on Diophantus's Arithmetica, Apollonius's Conics, and an astronomical canon. These notices are late and cannot tell us exactly what each lost work contained. Scholars debate the extent of her independent authorship and her part in manuscripts transmitted under her father's name. An annotation to Theon's commentary on Book Three of Ptolemy's Almagest refers to the philosopher Hypatia, his daughter, in connection with a revision or checking of the text. The wording has been interpreted in different ways. These traces are real evidence of mathematical activity, but they do not give us a surviving theorem proved uniquely by Hypatia.

We can still explore the mathematics linked to those texts, provided we keep the distinction clear. Start with Diophantus. His Arithmetica collects problems involving unknown numbers, often rational solutions to equations. Imagine looking for two positive numbers whose sum is ten and whose product is twenty-one. We could guess and check. Or we can call the first number x, making the other ten minus x. Their product is x times ten minus x. Setting it equal to twenty-one gives a quadratic equation. Move all terms to one side and it becomes x squared minus ten x plus twenty-one equals zero. Factoring gives x minus three times x minus seven. The numbers are three and seven.

This example is modernized. We use algebraic symbols and negative numbers freely; Diophantus had a different notation and problem-solving style. We also have no evidence that this particular exercise appeared in Hypatia's lost commentary. Its point is to show the intellectual task involved in teaching the Arithmetica. A problem that looks like a search among whole numbers becomes a relation among unknown quantities. The teacher helps the student understand why a manipulation is valid, which numerical solutions are allowed, and how to check the answer. That is substantial mathematics even when we cannot identify the exact classroom pages.

One useful feature of this sort of arithmetic is that a problem can be solved in several different ways. For the sum-and-product example, one approach is to guess pairs adding to ten: one and nine give nine, two and eight give sixteen, three and seven give twenty-one. Another approach rewrites the product equation as a square: the square of the first number minus ten times it plus twenty-five equals four, so the square of x minus five equals four. Therefore x lies two units from five, giving three or seven. A teacher who shows both methods is giving the student more than an answer. The student sees how a search, a factorization, and a geometric distance from a midpoint express the same algebraic structure.

Now change the sum from ten to eleven while keeping the product twenty-one. The factorization no longer has small whole-number roots, but a quadratic formula gives two real solutions. Whether those solutions count depends on what kind of number the problem permits. When we discuss Diophantine algebra, the domain of allowed answers is part of the mathematical question. These distinctions between rational, integer, and real solutions appear throughout the long history of number theory, and a sophisticated commentary would need to attend to them.

There is a further subtlety in Diophantine problems. Some equations have no solution in whole numbers but do have rational solutions. Take two squares whose sum is one. If one number is three-fifths, then the other can be four-fifths because nine twenty-fifths plus sixteen twenty-fifths equals one. If we require both numbers to be positive integers, the problem has no nonzero solution. Changing the permitted number system changes the question. The Arithmetica's interest in rational relations, special substitutions, and explicit examples belongs to an older algebraic practice that would later inspire mathematicians such as Fermat. Hypatia's association with this book situates her in a tradition of sophisticated numerical reasoning.

Now move from number problems to geometry. Apollonius's Conics studied curves obtained by cutting a cone with a plane: ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas. These curves were known long before modern coordinate geometry. Their properties emerged from proportions, constructions, and geometric arguments. If Hypatia wrote or taught a commentary on the Conics, her students would have needed to follow reasoning about areas, lengths, ratios, and changing relationships among points and lines. The diagrams would not simply decorate a text; they were essential parts of demonstrations.

A parabola offers a concrete entry. Picture a curve that looks like a smooth bowl, open to the right. It has a special point called a focus and a special line called a directrix. Every point on the parabola lies equally far from the focus and the directrix. If we take the focus one unit to the right of the origin and the directrix one unit to the left, points on the curve satisfy a simple relation. Their vertical coordinate squared equals four times the horizontal coordinate. At horizontal coordinate one, the vertical positions are two and minus two. At horizontal coordinate four, they are four and minus four. The widening bowl follows a precise geometric rule.

The coordinate equation is a modern illustration. Apollonius did not describe curves with Cartesian x and y coordinates, and we should not assign such notation to Hypatia. But the example translates the kind of geometric relation that conic theory studies. The essential idea is that a collection of points can be characterized by a fixed property. A parabola is more than a picture: it is a set defined by an invariant geometric relationship. That is why conics can later appear in optics, projectiles, and planetary astronomy despite their very different physical settings.

For an ellipse, the corresponding focus property uses two fixed points. Add the distances from any point on the curve to the two foci, and the sum stays constant. Imagine two pegs and a loop of string stretched around them by a pencil. Move the pencil while keeping the string taut, and an ellipse emerges. This familiar construction is a modern classroom model of a property deeply studied in ancient geometry. The proof that the resulting figure has a constant sum of distances is almost audible: the taut string has fixed length, and its two straight segments connect the pencil to the two pegs.

A particularly useful question is why the focus-directrix property produces a parabola rather than some other curve. Choose a point one unit to the right of an origin as the focus, and put the directrix one unit to the left. Take a point with horizontal coordinate x and vertical coordinate y. Its distance to the focus is the square root of the sum of the square of x minus one and the square of y. Its shortest distance to the vertical directrix is the absolute value of x plus one. Set the two distances equal, square them, and cancel matching terms. What remains is y squared equals four x. That is the familiar modern formula.

The important step is the cancellation. The two squared distances contain matching terms in x squared and the constant one. The difference leaves a first-degree term in x and a square in y, exactly the combination that creates a parabola. We can test the condition: at x equal one, y equals two or minus two. Both points are equally far from the focus and the directrix. This checks the algebra through geometry. The ancient method organized such relations through constructions and proportions rather than coordinate equations, but the geometric invariance is the same object of study.

The same exercise can distinguish a genuine property from a mere drawing. A curve may look like a parabola when sketched approximately, but the distance condition decides the question for every point. If we alter the focus or move the directrix, the curve shifts or changes scale while the defining relation remains intact. The exactness of that relation is what makes mathematical geometry transferable to later problems in optics or mechanics. The teacher must move a learner from the picture to the invariant and back again.

Why would conics interest an Alexandrian mathematical teacher? One answer lies in astronomy. Greek mathematical astronomy built geometric models for apparent planetary motion using circles, offsets, and more complicated arrangements. The world does not present the heavens as a stationary diagram. Planets seem to move forward, pause, and sometimes move backward relative to the stars. A successful model must convert these changing appearances into calculable angles and positions. Even when the physical interpretation is later discarded, the mathematics of constructing and comparing geometric models can be exact and demanding.

Ptolemy's Almagest provided a sophisticated mathematical astronomy built from observed angles, geometrical circles, eccentric positions, and epicycles. In an epicycle model, a point moves around a small circle whose center moves around a larger circle. The combination can produce an apparently complicated track. The arithmetic requires tables, angular divisions, and careful interpolation. This is why commentary and correction of astronomical texts could be intellectually important. A copying error in a numerical table, a mistaken diagram, or a poorly explained step can break an entire chain of computation.

Imagine a simpler numerical table. At one recorded time, an angle is thirty degrees. A little later it is thirty-four degrees. If the motion is approximately steady over that short interval, then halfway between the observations an estimate is thirty-two degrees. This is linear interpolation, a simple version of a general technique used whenever exact observations or computations are available only at selected times. Ancient astronomers developed much richer tabular techniques, often in sexagesimal arithmetic. Hypatia's participation in the culture of astronomical commentary links her to that exacting numerical work, though the surviving sources do not show us which entries she calculated herself.

Astronomical calculation also demanded an appreciation of error. Suppose a planetary position is estimated from a numerical table. An angle off by a small fraction of a degree may produce a noticeable positional difference, especially after many steps. The problem is not simply to memorize a geometrical scheme, but to know where an approximation enters and how it affects the prediction. Greek astronomy included tables constructed from geometric models and observations, and astronomical commentators helped maintain their usability. Errors could come from copied numerals, from imperfect parameters, from model assumptions, or from an observer's instrument.

A simple angular calculation illustrates this discipline. If an object appears to move one degree in a day and we estimate its position half a day later, a linear estimate adds half a degree. But if the apparent speed changes during the day, the approximation will be biased. More observations or a geometric model can improve the estimate. Mathematical astronomy thus lives at the intersection of ideal geometry and finite observation. The relation is directly relevant to the role of teachers who explained methods of tabulation and correction in the Alexandrian tradition.

The numbers themselves used a different language. Greek mathematicians inherited a base-sixty system in astronomical practice, where one degree can be divided into sixty minutes, and each minute into sixty seconds. Suppose an angle is thirty degrees and thirty minutes. In decimal notation that is thirty and a half degrees. The conversion is simple, but keeping track of such fractions through long computations takes care. The system persisted because dividing sixty into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, and sixths can be convenient. Students of astronomy needed fluency in this numerical language as much as geometric insight.

The connection between geometry and observation can also be made tangible through a very small celestial calculation. If an observer is in the northern hemisphere, the elevation of the north celestial pole above the horizon equals the observer's latitude, under the ordinary geometrical model of the sky. At latitude thirty degrees north, that pole is thirty degrees above the northern horizon. The stars appear to turn around it. An instrument that can measure altitude angles therefore connects a visible sky position to a geographical coordinate. This is one reason graduated circles and astronomical devices became so useful to travelers and scholars.

Real observations require adjustments. The pole may not be marked by a star exactly on its axis. A sighting has finite precision; refraction near the horizon distorts apparent altitude; and the device may not be perfectly aligned. Geometry provides the ideal relation, while observation demands judgment about error. The problem is an apt example of the mathematical culture surrounding Hypatia's teaching because it joins angular reasoning with the construction and use of instruments. We cannot infer that she personally assigned this particular example to her pupils. We can nevertheless explain why astronomical mathematics required a teacher fluent in both proof and measurement.

Synesius of Cyrene gives us direct evidence of Hypatia as a teacher. He studied with her and later became a Christian bishop. His surviving correspondence speaks of her with intellectual affection and respect. That is important for historical interpretation. Her school and influence cannot be described simply as a battle between pagan learning and every Christian. A Christian bishop counted himself among her pupils and continued to treat her as a trusted intellectual authority. The social and religious realities of Alexandria were complicated, and the correspondence resists slogans that reduce all participants to fixed hostile camps.

One of Synesius's writings discusses an astronomical instrument, an astrolabe, and acknowledges Hypatia's contribution to the learning involved in its design. The astrolabe represents celestial geometry in a portable form, allowing certain astronomical positions and angles to be read through a calibrated arrangement of circles and pointers. It is a physical interface to mathematical astronomy. Synesius's testimony supports her expertise and involvement in instruction concerning such instruments. It does not justify the popular claim that she invented the astrolabe. Related instruments and traditions were much older.

Another letter asks Hypatia to arrange for an instrument often translated as a hydroscope or hydrometer. Synesius describes a floating tube with a scale, intended to distinguish liquids by their density or specific gravity. We need not imagine Hypatia inventing the device to see the significance of the request. Someone who trusts a teacher to oversee the making of a precision instrument expects her to understand how it works, how it should be calibrated, and how artisans might construct it. Mathematics here appears in the contact between theoretical geometry, physical measurement, and skilled manufacture.

Let's work out the elementary principle behind such an instrument. A floating body displaces liquid until the weight of the displaced liquid balances its own weight. If the instrument has fixed weight, then a denser liquid requires less displaced volume to support it, so the instrument floats higher. In a less dense liquid, more of the tube must sink. A marked scale turns the immersion depth into an estimate of density. A device built around this relation is a little lesson in proportions. The instrument's form can change, but the buoyancy principle is stable.

We should distinguish what comes from Synesius and what is a modern explanation of hydrostatics. The letter gives a description and a request. The calculation in terms of density times displaced volume belongs to a modernized teaching reconstruction; it does not reveal a manuscript proof by Hypatia. Still, it shows the mathematical content of the technical culture associated with her circle. Geometry, measurement, and philosophy were not sealed off from one another.

The same instrument also offers an accessible lesson in dimensional reasoning. Density is mass divided by volume. A floating tube of fixed mass responds to density through the volume of water it displaces. To mark its scale correctly, the maker has to connect a change in depth with a change in volume. If the tube has a uniform cross-sectional area, additional immersion by one centimeter displaces an additional volume equal to that area times one centimeter. The calibration can therefore be expressed with elementary geometry. The shape of the lower ballast and the tube's cross section determine how uniform the scale will be.

This illustration reminds us that ancient technical learning did not draw a hard boundary between pure mathematics and craft. Measuring the heavens required metalwork and graduated circles. Measuring density required a carefully balanced vessel. Copying diagrams demanded precision with ruled lines and curved constructions. The mathematician who advised on instruments could also depend on artisans whose skills the surviving elite texts rarely name. Synesius explicitly discussed the work of skilled makers. Their contribution belongs in the history of the resulting knowledge.

Hypatia's philosophical teaching was connected with Platonism, especially the late-antique traditions often called Neoplatonism. Such philosophy sought an ordered understanding of mind, mathematics, and the intelligible world. Mathematical study could train a student to move from particular diagrams and numbers to abstract relations. A geometric proof demonstrates a conclusion about every appropriate figure, not just the one drawn before us. The same practice of reasoning from particulars to general structure could serve broader philosophical aims. We should avoid manufacturing a detailed philosophical doctrine in her own words, since no philosophical treatise by her survives.

It is tempting to imagine her teaching in a vast hall with a modern chalkboard and a group of students waiting for a revolutionary original theorem. The evidence supports a more concrete picture: a respected philosopher and mathematician explaining demanding inherited texts, advising advanced pupils, participating in public affairs, and sustaining a network that carried knowledge beyond Alexandria. She had standing with civic elites and was known for public confidence. A historian writing close to her lifetime emphasizes precisely that combination of intellectual reputation and civic presence.

The city was undergoing intense political and religious conflict. In four hundred twelve, Cyril became bishop of Alexandria, while Orestes served as Roman prefect. Their rivalry intersected with disputes involving Jewish and Christian communities, monastic groups, and the authority of church and state. Hypatia was associated with Orestes and became the object of a rumor that she prevented reconciliation between the prefect and the bishop. That rumor proved lethal. It is essential to describe the conflict rather than simply calling her death an expression of abstract hostility to mathematics.

In March of four hundred fifteen, a Christian mob attacked Hypatia, dragged her away, and killed her brutally. The near-contemporary Christian historian Socrates Scholasticus describes the event and condemns it. Later narratives supply additional details and different emphases, including stories hostile to Hypatia and stories idealizing her. We do not have evidence that Cyril personally issued an order to kill her, though his position and the political atmosphere surrounding the violence are central to historical assessment. There is ample reason to condemn the murder without filling evidential gaps with inventions.

The surviving accounts of her death also reveal how sources carry their authors' purposes. Socrates Scholasticus was a Christian church historian writing within decades of the event. His condemnation of the mob is especially significant because it cannot be dismissed as simply the complaint of a hostile pagan writer. Damascius, writing much later, offers another account that includes stories colored by his philosophical affiliations. John of Nikiu, writing still later, treats Hypatia hostilely and celebrates her destruction. Those differences are themselves evidence about the religious and political arguments surrounding her memory. They do not license us to combine every dramatic detail into one seamless narrative.

Some retellings portray Hypatia as attacked in the library while she was working on a final scientific discovery. The historical accounts do not establish such a scene. Others assume the episode can be explained exclusively by a straightforward war between science and religion. But her Christian pupils and the civic rivalry between bishop and prefect complicate that simple frame. A rigorous lecture should state the murder plainly, acknowledge the political and sectarian violence, and then distinguish firmly attested events from later imaginative reconstruction.

Hypatia's death has sometimes been described as the day classical science ended or the day the great Library of Alexandria was destroyed. Neither claim withstands historical scrutiny. The library's institutional history was complex and had already undergone centuries of transformation. Intellectual life in Alexandria and elsewhere continued after four hundred fifteen. Her death was a terrible event in the political life of her city and a profound loss for her students. Its significance does not depend on a false claim that all mathematics ceased at that moment.

The myth of a single ending can hide a more difficult question: how did ancient mathematics survive across discontinuous centuries? Copies of Euclid traveled through Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Latin, and many other scholarly traditions. Astronomical tables were translated, compared, corrected, and recomputed. The survival of Diophantus and Apollonius depended on chains of readers and teachers whose names are often unknown. Hypatia's known work belongs to that world of transmission. Even when the author of a commentary leaves no surviving manuscript, the act of teaching may have shaped what later students could understand and pass on.

There is a reason an editor's contribution can be mathematically creative. Suppose a text states that a certain geometrical construction is possible, then jumps to a conclusion whose justification is missing. A competent commentator can supply the intermediate argument, draw the necessary figure, and explain which earlier theorem authorizes the step. The resulting edition helps turn a fragile sequence of claims into a coherent body of learning. We do not know which such repairs Hypatia made. But knowing the mathematical character of commentary allows us to appreciate the intellectual labor involved without attributing fictitious discoveries to her.

Consider again our simple Diophantine exercise. We found three and seven from a sum of ten and a product of twenty-one. To teach it well, we should ask what happens when the product changes. If it becomes twenty-five, the two numbers coincide at five. If it becomes twenty-six, there are no real numbers satisfying both requirements, since the square of the difference would have to be negative. This reveals a structural restriction: for two real numbers with fixed sum ten, their product cannot exceed twenty-five. The arithmetic is a doorway to inequality, quadratic discriminants, and optimization. Such connections are exactly what thoughtful mathematical instruction makes visible.

The conic example invites a similar extension. A parabola's focus-directrix description makes it possible to identify a curve from an invariant distance relation. The ellipse adds distances to two foci. A hyperbola uses the absolute difference of such distances. These definitions organize three apparently different pictures through one geometric family. Ancient conic geometry was more extensive and subtle than these quick modern characterizations, but the family resemblance shows why the subject could demand a lifetime of study. Teaching it required a firm command of logically connected constructions.

What was the social role of a teacher in such a community? Ancient philosophical education often took place in small circles of sustained conversation. Students might remain connected to their teacher long after leaving the city. Letters helped preserve those relationships, exchange news and recommendations, ask for texts, and introduce friends or colleagues. Synesius's surviving correspondence repeatedly shows Hypatia in that role. It was a world of personal intellectual networks, not a modern department whose work was recorded in annual publication lists. Such a network could transmit ways of reasoning even when few formal treatises survived.

This helps explain the significance of the mixed religious backgrounds in her circle. Learning did not flow only along lines of confession. Philosophical and mathematical training could create loyalties across political divides, even when those divides later became lethal. Synesius was a Christian bishop, but he continued to praise the teacher who had helped form his intellect. His letters reveal the emotional depth of that association, as well as requests for technical help and social contact. They are fragments of a long educational relationship, not a catalog of theorem statements.

One more aspect of Hypatia's story deserves emphasis: the evidence is itself a mathematical kind of problem. We have a small set of incomplete observations: a historian's account, letters, a scholion, late encyclopedic notices, and later stories shaped by ideology. Different hypotheses about her work must explain those observations without claiming more than they support. A historian performs a sort of constrained inference. Which facts are firmly established? Which conclusions are plausible? Which are merely possible? The method is less exact than an algebraic proof, but its discipline is analogous: distinguish what follows from premises from what one hopes might be true.

Hypatia has been claimed by many modern movements: as a martyr to philosophy, a symbol of women's intellectual achievement, a champion of secular reason, or a representative of a lost world. Some of those interpretations express legitimate values. Yet the historical individual belongs to her own time first. Her identity as a teacher of mathematics and philosophy cannot be reduced to a modern political emblem. We honor her most accurately by investigating the texts, the students, the mathematical traditions, and the civic conflict that actually shaped her life.

Return to the opening problem. A mathematician's writings have vanished; what remains? In Hypatia's case, we still have a network of testimony strong enough to establish her public authority, advanced mathematical education, and participation in the transmission of arithmetic, geometry, and astronomy. We have letters showing a student's respect and technical requests. We have a near-contemporary account of the violence that ended her life. We can reconstruct the mathematical world in which she taught, while acknowledging that its specific theorems cannot simply be reassigned to her.

That is a substantive mathematical legacy. Knowledge survives through proofs, certainly, but also through skilled reading, rigorous teaching, repaired texts, and communities of inquiry. Hypatia's historical portrait asks us to recognize those forms of work. Her mathematics is difficult to recover in detail because the documents are lost. The care we take with that loss is itself a way of respecting the intellectual achievement we can still see.

## Display-only mathematical companion

### Diophantine-style illustrative algebra

A modern *illustrative* problem, not an attributed problem or solution by Hypatia:

\[
x+y=10,\qquad xy=21\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(10-x)=21
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x-3)(x-7)=0.
\]

Thus \((x,y)=(3,7)\) or \((7,3)\). For fixed real sum \(S\), the product obeys \(xy\le S^2/4\), with equality when \(x=y=S/2\), because \((x-y)^2\ge0\).

### Conics, expressed in modern coordinates

For focus \(F=(p,0)\) and directrix \(x=-p\), the parabola locus obeys

\[
\sqrt{(x-p)^2+y^2}=|x+p|\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y^2=4px,\quad p>0.
\]

An ellipse with foci \(F_1,F_2\) consists of points with \(|P-F_1|+|P-F_2|=2a\), with \(2a>|F_1-F_2|\). These Cartesian derivations **postdate** Apollonius and Hypatia; they are teaching aids, not reconstructions of her own commentary.

### Hydrometer proportionality

For a floating instrument of fixed mass \(m\) in a liquid of density \(\rho\), Archimedean balance gives \(\rho gV_{\rm immersed}=mg\). Thus

\[
V_{\rm immersed}=\frac{m}{\rho}.
\]

Doubling density halves immersed volume, under ideal conditions. Calibration from volume to visible depth depends on the vessel's shape. Synesius's Letter 15 describes an instrument and request, **not** an invention by Hypatia.

### Historical evidence tiers

- **Near-contemporary testimony:** Synesius's letters and Socrates Scholasticus, *Ecclesiastical History* VII.15.
- **Textual trace:** the attribution to Hypatia in the Book III material associated with Theon's *Almagest* commentary; scholarly interpretations differ.
- **Later witness:** the *Suda* credits commentaries related to Diophantus and Apollonius. The content of her own works is lost.
- **Modern inventions to avoid:** attributing the invention of the astrolabe or hydrometer to Hypatia; claiming her death destroyed the Library or ended ancient mathematics; presenting modern analytic-geometry formulas as her own.
