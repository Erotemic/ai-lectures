# 168000 — Andrew Wiles: The Problem That Led Somewhere Else

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Background:** No higher mathematics required. This is a biographical account of a twentieth-century number theorist, with a patient explanation of Fermat's Last Theorem, elliptic curves, modular forms, the Frey–Ribet connection, and why the repaired proof mattered.

On June twenty-third, nineteen ninety-three, Andrew Wiles stood before an audience of mathematicians at the Isaac Newton Institute in Cambridge, England. He had been giving a series of three lectures called *Modular Forms, Elliptic Curves, and Galois Representations*. Those titles were perfectly respectable at a specialist number theory meeting, but they did not advertise the announcement toward which the talks were building. The last lecture ended with a conclusion that anyone who had studied mathematics for more than a few weeks might recognize. Fermat's Last Theorem followed from what he had just proved.

The theorem said that a certain simple equation has no solutions in positive whole numbers. It had resisted mathematicians for more than three centuries. People who were present understood the announcement's importance; news soon reached people who knew almost nothing about elliptic curves or modular forms. A mathematics seminar had become a public event.

There is a familiar version of this story in which a boy finds a puzzle, spends his life thinking about it, and finally rises from his desk with the answer. The boy really did find the puzzle. The adult really did solve it. But that version leaves out most of the mathematics, the people whose work made the proof possible, and a year in which the celebrated proof turned out not to be complete. It also misses the striking fact that Wiles did not attack Fermat's equation directly. He proved a theorem about a different family of mathematical objects, on a scale that Fermat could never have imagined.

To understand why, we need to know something about the boy, but also about the kind of mathematics he chose to become good at when he grew up.

### A childhood problem

Andrew John Wiles was born in Cambridge on April eleventh, nineteen fifty-three. His father, Maurice Wiles, was a theologian, eventually a prominent scholar of Christian doctrine. His mother was Patricia Wiles. When Andrew was very young, the family lived for several years in Ibadan, Nigeria, where his father taught. They returned to Cambridge in nineteen fifty-nine. In the account Wiles later gave of his early life, this move across countries is part of his childhood rather than an episode to be treated as an exotic footnote. There were schools, books, family arguments, and the ordinary discovery that some problems interested him more than others.

He also remembered a game he and his father played: trying to prove things that were obviously false. His father's alleged proof that he was the pope depended on somehow showing that two was equal to one. The joke was about the shape of an argument. If you accept a bad step, the conclusion can be almost anything. It is an apt memory to keep in mind for the life ahead, though there is no reason to claim that a childhood game foretold a particular theorem thirty years later.

In Cambridge, Wiles attended King's College School. Around the age of ten he came across Eric Temple Bell's book *The Last Problem* in a public library. The book described a challenge written down in the seventeenth century. Wiles could understand the statement, which made it irresistible. He could not understand why so many great mathematicians had failed to settle it. He began trying anyway.

Here is the equation. Take three positive whole numbers, call them x, y, and z. Can the square of x plus the square of y equal the square of z? Yes. Three squared is nine. Four squared is sixteen. Nine plus sixteen is twenty-five, which is five squared. A right triangle with side lengths three, four, and five is a geometric picture of the same arithmetic fact. There are infinitely many such examples.

Now replace squares by cubes. Can one positive whole-number cube plus another be a third cube? And what if we use fourth powers, or fifth powers, or any whole-number exponent greater than two? Fermat's Last Theorem says no. There are no positive whole numbers x, y, and z satisfying x to the power n, plus y to the power n, equals z to the power n, when n is greater than two.

It is a beautiful question because the beginning asks almost nothing of you. You can try three and four, then seven and eight. You can test numbers until you tire of testing them. But no amount of checking settles a claim about every possible number. An enormous computer search could eliminate an enormous finite collection of cases and still leave infinitely many untouched. Fermat's sentence is not a request to find an example. It asserts there is none, anywhere.

The French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat wrote in his copy of Diophantus that he had found a marvelous demonstration too large to fit in the margin. No surviving general proof by Fermat has been established. In the centuries that followed, mathematicians solved special exponents and developed entire branches of arithmetic while the general assertion remained open. The word *last* reflects its status among the famous unsolved problems associated with him, not proof that it was the last thing Fermat worked on.

There is a small simplification that changes how one should hear the challenge. To establish the theorem for every exponent above two, it is enough to deal with the fourth power and all odd prime exponents. If n has an odd prime divisor, then a supposed solution for n gives a solution for that smaller prime exponent by taking suitable powers of x, y, and z. If n has no odd prime divisor, it is a power of two, so an exponent greater than two is divisible by four. This reduces the list of exponents, but it leaves infinitely many odd primes. The hard part remains.

Long before Wiles was born, Sophie Germain had discovered a powerful method for ruling out whole families of potential solutions under carefully stated conditions. Euler, Kummer, and many others made genuine advances. Their partial victories were not embarrassing preludes to a final hero. They were necessary work on a problem that did not yield to a single neat trick. In particular, Kummer's attempt to use factorization in larger number systems exposed failures of ordinary unique factorization and helped create the theory of ideals. The effort to prove that a simple integer equation cannot hold had already changed what mathematicians meant by an integer, a factor, and a proof.

The ten-year-old Wiles knew very little of that history. He knew enough to be curious. As a teenager he found books by G. H. Hardy, E. M. Wright, and Harold Davenport, and was drawn especially to algebra and number theory. The problem did not become a schoolboy success. It became a reason to learn the mathematics in which it was embedded.

### Leaving the puzzle alone

Wiles entered Merton College, Oxford, in nineteen seventy-one. He studied the broad undergraduate curriculum, but his interests lay particularly in number theory. Mathematics at a university is not a sequence of increasingly difficult versions of the sums one solves at school. The objects multiply. A student who wants to understand the integers soon meets algebraic structures, complex numbers, fields, geometric spaces, and abstract rules about maps between them. The first disappointment may be that the favorite childhood problem has ceased to look approachable. The compensating discovery is that the unfamiliar subjects have questions of their own.

After graduating, Wiles went to Cambridge for doctoral work. There he began working with John Coates, a number theorist whose interests joined elliptic curves, complex analysis, and the new arithmetic techniques of Iwasawa theory. Wiles did not make Fermat's Last Theorem the topic of his doctorate. As he later explained, a research student who commits years to an inaccessible question may finish with nothing to show, and John Coates had a program in which genuine progress was possible. This was not a surrender of the childhood ambition. It was a decision to become a working mathematician.

Coates was himself a gifted and demanding collaborator. Wiles's recent memoir of Coates recalls work they did on elliptic curves with complex multiplication, adapting methods from Iwasawa theory. They proved a significant result connected to the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, one of the central problems of arithmetic geometry. That first research success is more than a credential on the way to Fermat. It introduces nearly all the major characters of the later mathematics: elliptic curves, special analytic functions, primes, algebraic extensions, and a problem about translating among them.

Let us begin with elliptic curves, because the name is misleading. They are not ellipses. Over the rational numbers, an elliptic curve can be described by a nonsingular cubic equation, often written as y squared equals x cubed plus A times x plus B. Nonsingular means, roughly, that the curve has no pinched crossing or sharp algebraic point where the geometry breaks. The equation also has a special point at infinity, needed to make its geometry work correctly.

Consider an example: y squared equals x cubed minus x. There are obvious rational points: zero, zero; one, zero; and negative one, zero. But there is something more interesting than looking for a few solutions. Draw a straight line through two points on a smooth cubic. In the typical case the line meets the curve at a third point. Reflect that third point across the horizontal axis and declare it to be the sum of the original two points. With a tangent line the same construction works when you want to add a point to itself. The special point at infinity acts like zero for this addition.

That construction gives the rational points on an elliptic curve the structure of an abelian group. You can add two rational points and get another rational point. You can repeat a point, take its inverse, and ask whether its repeated multiples eventually return to the starting position. A question that began as finding pairs of rational numbers satisfying a cubic equation has turned into group theory. It is an exact transformation, not a metaphor.

The group can be remarkably complicated. Mordell's theorem, extended by Weil to more general settings, says that the rational points of an elliptic curve form a finitely generated abelian group. Informally, there is a finite collection of points from which every rational point can be built using this addition law. But how many independent points of infinite order are required? That number is the curve's rank. It tells us something about the arithmetic richness of the curve, and it is hard to determine in general.

The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture makes an astonishing proposal. On the other side of number theory, one can build an analytic function associated with an elliptic curve, an L-function. It is assembled from information about how the curve behaves after reducing its equation modulo different primes. The conjecture says that the rank should be recorded by the order to which this analytic function vanishes at a particular point. A question about rational solutions to a polynomial equation becomes a question about the behavior of an analytic function. We need not resolve the conjecture to appreciate its audacity. It promises that arithmetic and analysis know the same secret.

Coates and Wiles's early work established important cases involving elliptic curves with complex multiplication. Complex multiplication is an extra symmetry in the curve's algebra; it makes some structures accessible that remain difficult in the general case. Their result was a substantial achievement, but not a proof of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer in full. Wiles learned to work in the narrow space between what powerful conjectures suggest and what existing methods actually justify.

The next stage of his career took him across the Atlantic. In the late nineteen seventies he held research positions associated with Cambridge and Harvard. At Harvard he developed an important collaboration with Barry Mazur. Their work resolved a main conjecture of Iwasawa theory in a broad setting of cyclotomic number fields. Later, Wiles extended the methods to totally real fields. By nineteen eighty-two he had joined Princeton's mathematics faculty. He was already a leading number theorist, not a previously unknown amateur waiting to be recognized by a famous problem.

Iwasawa theory deserves a moment, because the name will return at the decisive point of the biography. Suppose a single number field is too restrictive to reveal a pattern. Instead of studying it alone, study a tower of related fields, each a controlled extension of the last. Look for laws governing how class groups or other arithmetic invariants grow as you ascend the tower. An arithmetic quantity that looks erratic at one level may be governed by a more orderly algebraic object when the levels are considered together. Iwasawa theory makes that comparison precise, often with p-adic numbers, where closeness is measured by divisibility by a chosen prime rather than by ordinary distance on the number line.

The results Wiles proved with Coates and Mazur taught him to coordinate three difficult languages: questions about integer and rational solutions, analytic functions encoding arithmetic data, and algebraic structures sensitive to the action of prime numbers. This knowledge did not solve Fermat's equation. It made Wiles ready for an unexpected change in what solving Fermat's equation would mean.

### Two kinds of arithmetic object

By the middle of the nineteen eighties, elliptic curves had become a meeting place for researchers who might once have thought of themselves as working in different fields. An elliptic curve is algebraic: its points solve an equation. But there was another kind of object, a modular form, that seemed to carry closely related arithmetic information.

A modular form begins as a function of a complex variable. The variable lives in the upper half of the complex plane, and the function obeys strict symmetry laws when the variable is transformed by certain fractional linear transformations. It is also required to be analytic, meaning it has the complex differentiability that makes a function amenable to powerful analytic arguments. These requirements are so restrictive that one can often write the function as an infinite series whose coefficients are deeply structured.

It is easy to feel that we have changed subjects. We started with whole numbers. Now we are discussing analytic functions with symmetries in the complex plane. What could one possibly have to do with the other?

Remember the elliptic curve y squared equals x cubed minus x. Instead of asking only for rational points, reduce its coefficients and its coordinates modulo a prime. For example, modulo five, the only possible remainders are zero, one, two, three, and four. A solution is a pair of remainders whose squares and cubes satisfy the reduced equation. Counting solutions becomes a finite task.

For x equal to zero, one, or four, the right side is zero modulo five, so y must be zero. That gives one solution for each of those x values. For x equal to two, the right side is one, and y can be one or four. For x equal to three, the right side is four, and y can be two or three. We have counted seven ordinary solutions. Add the designated point at infinity, and the curve has eight points over the field with five elements.

The arithmetic quantity attached to this prime is five plus one minus eight, which equals negative two. Number theorists call this quantity a sub five. Repeating the calculation for other primes of good reduction produces a sequence of numbers. The counts are not random. They are constrained by strong theorems, and the whole collection encodes information about the original curve.

For certain elliptic curves, the corresponding modular form has a series expansion whose coefficients match these prime-counting numbers. In our small example, the coefficient at the fifth power of the series variable is negative two. The same number has appeared once by counting solutions to an algebraic equation modulo five and once by extracting a coefficient from a highly symmetric analytic function. That is a genuine mathematical coincidence demanding an explanation. It is also the sort of coincidence that can eventually become a theorem.

The conjecture that interested Wiles said, in a precise form, that every elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular. One formulation says the elliptic curve is related by a suitable map to a modular curve, a geometric object built from modular forms. Another formulation connects the curve's L-function with the L-function of a modular form of weight two. These are not arbitrary analogies; the correspondence is strong enough to transmit arithmetic information from one language to the other.

In the nineteen fifties, Yutaka Taniyama and Goro Shimura developed the ideas from which this conjecture emerged. André Weil later gave it influential formulations and arguments in its favor. The naming and attribution of the conjecture have their own history, but the mathematical claim is clear: algebraic curves over the rationals should not be isolated from the analytic symmetries of modular forms. The conjecture, if true, would connect large regions of number theory.

What was not clear was how to prove it. One can compute examples and gather extensive evidence. One can know a remarkable amount about modular forms and elliptic curves. But to say *every* elliptic curve of the specified kind is modular is again an infinite statement. The experience with Fermat's equation should make us suspicious of how easily an enormous theorem can be said aloud.

### An equation becomes a curve

The key surprise came not from Wiles but from Gerhard Frey, Jean-Pierre Serre, and Kenneth Ribet. Their work in the nineteen eighties connected the old problem to the newer conjecture. Frey considered what would happen if Fermat's Last Theorem were false. Suppose, contrary to Fermat's claim, that positive integers a, b, and c satisfied a to the power p plus b to the power p equals c to the power p, where p is an odd prime greater than three. We can arrange that the three integers have no common factor, because any common factor can be divided out.

Now use these numbers as the coefficients of an elliptic curve. Frey's construction can be written as y squared equals x times x minus a to the power p, times x plus b to the power p. It is not important to memorize this formula. What matters is that an alleged solution of Fermat's equation would generate an elliptic curve with exceptional arithmetic properties. The curve is sometimes called the Frey curve or the Frey–Hellegouarch curve, recognizing earlier related work by Yves Hellegouarch.

Why is this clever? Normally, to disprove an integer solution, you might try factoring the original equation or bounding its terms. Here the proposed solution is turned into a new object belonging to another branch of mathematics. The question becomes: could a curve with these particular properties exist? If one theory says it must exist and another says it cannot, the assumed integer solution has led to a contradiction.

Serre sharpened the mechanism. Ribet proved the critical result in nineteen eighty-six, sometimes described as the epsilon conjecture or the level-lowering theorem in the relevant setting. The broad message was that a hypothetical Fermat counterexample would create a semistable elliptic curve that could not be modular. Semistable is a technical restriction on how a curve degenerates at different primes. It is not the assertion that the curve is smooth after reduction modulo every prime. The Frey curve has controlled bad reduction, which is precisely what lets the modularity argument engage with it.

We can sketch the contradiction more concretely. A curve's conductor is an integer recording aspects of its bad reduction at the various primes. A modular form associated to a curve has a level, another arithmetic measure of complexity. Ribet's theorem allows certain prime factors in the level to be removed when the modular representation has the properties produced by the Frey construction. Following that argument for a supposed Fermat counterexample leads, ultimately, to the need for a weight-two cusp form at level two. There is no such form. Therefore the Frey curve cannot be modular in the way the modularity conjecture demands.

That last sentence is the hinge. It does not say Fermat's Last Theorem follows from the mere existence of elliptic curves, or from computing a few modular forms. It follows from a chain of deep results. A supposed integer counterexample makes a special curve. The curve is semistable. If semistable elliptic curves are modular, it corresponds to a modular form. Ribet's theorem then forces an impossible modular form. So the original integer counterexample cannot exist.

The extraordinary feature of the argument is that Wiles no longer needed to prove modularity for every possible elliptic curve. He needed the semistable case. That was still a vast and difficult theorem, but it was a clearer target. What had been a childhood demand—prove that no integers solve this equation—had become a professional research program: prove a connection between arithmetic geometry and modular forms for an important class of curves.

In nineteen eighty-six, Wiles learned of Ribet's advance. He later described this as the moment the childhood problem returned to the center of his working life. He had not been spending his entire research career attempting random manipulations of Fermat's equation. He had been working on mathematical structures that now gave him a plausible route to it. Without Frey, Serre, and Ribet, and without the older ideas of Taniyama, Shimura, and Weil, there would have been no such route. It would be equally wrong to describe the eventual result as merely filling in an obvious missing line. A major theorem remained to be invented.

### Seven years

Wiles began the effort in nineteen eighty-six, largely without publicly disclosing the real target. He still held his academic appointments and remained part of the mathematical community. The often-repeated image of a solitary mathematician hiding in an attic captures something about his preference for privacy and concentration, but not the conditions that make modern research possible. He read other people's papers. He used an education and a collection of ideas built by many researchers. He discussed related mathematics in forms that did not reveal the full aim. In the final stages he asked Nicholas Katz, another Princeton mathematician, to help check parts of his developing argument.

The secrecy had reasons. Once a mathematician announces an attack on a problem with the fame of Fermat's, ordinary research conversations can become public speculation. An idea that is still fragile has to survive not only mathematical objections but questions about when it will be finished, who gets credit, and why a supposed genius has not yet delivered. Wiles wanted room to attempt a very difficult project without making his career or his colleagues answer for every failed approach. The choice also had costs. Private research can withhold access to the very criticism and suggestions that might improve it. The later history would make that tension impossible to ignore.

There was also a family life. Wiles married Nada Canaan, a molecular biologist, in nineteen eighty-eight. They had three daughters. In interviews he described family life as a necessary counterpart to his intense work. Children want stories and attention; they are not obligated to care that their parent is trying to solve Fermat's equation. We should not invent a private conversation to make this sound charming. Wiles himself made the point in public recollections. His famous paper would later be dedicated to Nada, Clare, Kate, and Olivia, a visible record that the years were not inhabited by mathematics alone.

At some points Wiles worked at Oxford as well as at Princeton. In nineteen eighty-nine he was elected to the Royal Society. The credentials and appointments are worth mentioning because they complicate the myth that Fermat's Last Theorem was solved by someone who sacrificed all professional work for a dream. His earlier results were already recognized. The great unknown was whether his new line of argument could be made rigorous.

What did he actually do during those years? The technical achievement was to translate the conjecture about elliptic curves into a problem about Galois representations, then find ways to control the objects that represented possible answers. This sounds as remote from Fermat's equation as modular forms once did. There is a good reason it worked. It moved the question into a setting where different collections of possible mathematical structures could be compared precisely.

A Galois representation records the action of symmetries of algebraic numbers on a vector space or a closely related module. That description is abstract, so begin with a simpler symmetry. Consider the two square roots of two: positive square root of two and negative square root of two. There is an algebraic operation that exchanges them while preserving the rational arithmetic relations they satisfy. Galois theory studies enormous families of such symmetries and the ways they constrain equations.

An elliptic curve carries special points of finite order under its addition law. For a chosen prime p, the points annihilated by multiplication by p form, over an algebraic closure and in characteristic zero, a group resembling a two-dimensional vector space over the integers modulo p. Galois symmetries permute these points, and that action can be written using two-by-two matrices. We have attached a representation to the curve. Instead of comparing every curve directly with every modular form, we can compare their associated representations.

There is a second, related object. Rather than looking only at points of order p, examine compatible points of orders p, p squared, p cubed, and so on. This records finer and finer p-adic information, yielding a representation with coefficients in a p-adic ring. A modular form also has Galois representations associated to it, through the work of mathematicians including Eichler, Shimura, Deligne, and others. If the two representations can be shown to be of the same appropriate kind, the modularity of the curve follows.

Now imagine that we know the representation modulo p, a coarse shadow of the complete object. We want to understand all the ways that shadow might be lifted to richer p-adic representations while obeying specified arithmetic conditions. One can organize these allowable lifts by a universal deformation ring, conventionally called R. On the modular side, a Hecke algebra, conventionally called T, organizes the arithmetic operations and modular forms corresponding to the same residual data. There is a natural map from the deformation problem toward the modular one. If R and T turn out to be the same ring under the right conditions, the feared extra deformations have nowhere to hide. The admissible Galois representations are accounted for by modular forms.

This is a compressed account of a sophisticated argument. The point is not that two letters magically prove a three-hundred-year-old theorem. The mathematical work is in defining the deformation conditions, ensuring the objects have the properties required, and proving the comparison. Even the statement R equals T depends on hypotheses that cannot be dropped casually. But a general listener can see the strategy. Counting or classifying elliptic curves directly had been out of reach. Wiles recast the task as a comparison of two algebraic mechanisms, and that comparison became tractable.

A crucial piece of the argument began with three-torsion, the part of an elliptic curve's finite-order points killed by multiplication by three. Work of Robert Langlands and Jerrold Tunnell made certain odd, irreducible two-dimensional representations modulo three modular. Starting from that known case, Wiles could try to lift modularity to the full curve. When the mod-three representation was reducible, he used a clever three-five switch, finding an auxiliary curve with related five-torsion and more favorable three-torsion. This was not a generic assertion that three equals five, nor a numerical shortcut. It was an argument with carefully selected elliptic curves and the Galois representations on their torsion points.

Step by step, the picture began to connect. A residual representation known to be modular could become the starting point for a modularity lifting theorem. A modularity lifting theorem could establish the modularity of a semistable elliptic curve. A semistable modularity theorem, combined with Ribet's theorem, could eliminate the hypothetical Frey curve. Eliminating the Frey curve would prove Fermat's Last Theorem.

All these steps were difficult. One of them, in nineteen ninety-three, was still not valid.

### The room in Cambridge

Wiles was sufficiently confident by the spring of nineteen ninety-three to plan a public announcement. He arranged a three-part lecture series at a number theory meeting organized in Cambridge, the city where he had grown up and completed his research training. He spoke on successive days, leading his specialist audience through the ideas about Galois representations, modular forms, and elliptic curves. The lectures themselves were not disguised as elementary number theory. Their significance lay in a long technical argument, not in an easy explanation of an old puzzle.

On the final day, June twenty-third, he stated the consequence: Fermat's Last Theorem. Kenneth Ribet and other mathematicians could recognize how the semistable modularity result would connect to the work already done. The claim was sufficiently important to make news far beyond the institute. Reporters had a theorem they could describe in one sentence and a mathematician whose biography seemed to fit the world's favorite account of genius: a boy fascinated by a question, an adult who worked secretly for years, and a spectacular answer.

The public narrative began before the mathematical process was finished. That is a crucial distinction. A lecture announcement is not the same thing as a published, checked proof. In ordinary mathematical practice, claims are examined in detail by colleagues, referees, and later readers. The complexity of Wiles's argument made such scrutiny especially demanding. It involved several branches of mathematics, many delicate local conditions, and a central comparison between algebraic structures. Most of the people who heard the announcement could not have checked every link of the proof on the spot, even if they knew exactly what the theorem meant.

Wiles circulated the manuscript for careful examination. During the review process, a serious gap emerged. The problem concerned an argument that had been intended to control certain arithmetic groups through a method inspired by Matthias Flach and Victor Kolyvagin. These were not decorative technicalities appended to an otherwise complete proof. Controlling those groups was part of what the modularity-lifting method needed. Without the missing estimate, a crucial connection could not be justified.

The difficulty had a particular shape. Wiles had a framework for comparing the deformation ring and the Hecke algebra. He needed sufficient arithmetic control to show that this comparison was as strong as required. One approach involved an extension of techniques from Euler systems, arrangements of related arithmetic classes that can impose bounds on elusive arithmetic objects. The extension Wiles had hoped for did not work in the form required. The apparent bridge did not reach the other side.

We need to say this accurately, because popular retellings sometimes minimize the flaw as a typo or one missing line that any competent mathematician would repair over lunch. It was a real mathematical gap. It remained open for many months, despite intense work. Another simplification is equally misleading: that the entire seven years of research had collapsed and nothing of value remained. Much of Wiles's framework survived; the outstanding issue was concentrated in a key part of the argument. That is both why repair remained imaginable and why it was so hard.

For Wiles, this was an abrupt change in the character of the work. The preceding seven years had been private, driven by the problems he chose and by his own judgments of progress. After the Cambridge lectures, his result was a public claim. The mathematics was now being evaluated under exceptional attention. Rumors and reports spread while the detailed situation was known to only a limited circle. Wiles later described the exposure as painful, but we need not speculate beyond his recorded comments about what every day felt like. The difference between private uncertainty and public uncertainty is clear enough.

At this point a person writing a movie would probably invent an antagonist, a hostile rival ready to humiliate him. The documentary record is less convenient and more interesting. Other mathematicians were trying to understand a difficult proof. If an argument contains a gap, pointing it out is part of the discipline by which mathematics becomes reliable knowledge. The people who had helped develop the field had every reason to want the theorem established, but wanting a claim to be true is not the same as knowing that it is proved.

### Richard Taylor and the missing ingredient

Wiles eventually sought the help of Richard Taylor, a former doctoral student who was now an accomplished number theorist. Taylor came to work with him on the troublesome part of the proof. They investigated ways to repair the Euler-system approach and other possibilities. The work was collaborative; the accepted mathematical record would reflect that collaboration explicitly.

The relationship matters for a biography because it complicates the romantic isolation of the earlier seven years. Wiles had chosen an independent attack on the problem, but the resolution of the crisis depended on another mathematician's time, ideas, and sustained attention. Taylor was not a helpful bystander who checked a finished calculation. He became a coauthor of one of the two papers that established the result. The nature of the contribution is visible in the paper's title, *Ring-Theoretic Properties of Certain Hecke Algebras*, and in its introduction, which identifies the missing algebraic ingredient that the companion argument required.

The breakthrough came in September of nineteen ninety-four. Wiles later gave a vivid account of the moment when he realized how to combine pieces of two approaches he had previously pursued. He had tried to use Iwasawa-theoretic methods and had set them aside after they failed to yield the necessary conclusion. He had then turned to an approach inspired by Kolyvagin and Flach, only to find that its crucial extension failed. Looking again at why the second approach did not work, he recognized something that allowed the first approach to be used in a new way.

In his recollection, the insight arrived on September nineteenth. He described staring at what he had found, hardly believing its simplicity or beauty. These are Wiles's later words about his experience, not access to a private diary and not evidence that the solution was fully written, refereed, and published in one afternoon. The breakthrough made a complete proof possible; further mathematical writing and verification still mattered.

What emerged was a revised strategy for the ring comparison. Rather than obtaining the necessary control from the attempted Euler-system argument, Wiles and Taylor used algebraic constructions that let them compare the deformation rings and Hecke algebras through auxiliary levels and carefully chosen primes. Later work developed and generalized these ideas under the name Taylor–Wiles patching. One should be cautious about pretending that the word *patching* makes the proof elementary. The construction combines information from many related settings, using limits and numerical criteria to produce the ring-theoretic properties required. It is a method for controlling an entire space of representations, not a piece of tape laid over a crack in a manuscript.

The coauthored paper establishes, in the relevant setting, a complete-intersection property of certain Hecke algebras. Roughly, a complete intersection is an algebraic structure whose defining equations fit together with the expected degree of independence. In this problem, that strong structural property helps make the deformation–Hecke comparison work. Being merely a Gorenstein ring, a weaker property available by known methods, had not been enough. The distinction is technical, but the logical role is simple: the original proof needed a stronger algebraic statement, and the revised argument supplied it.

That is why the successful repair became a mathematical contribution in its own right. A proof that only said *Fermat is true* would have been a wonderful conclusion to a long-standing puzzle. The proof Wiles and Taylor made possible added machinery that other mathematicians could carry into many problems involving modularity, Galois representations, and arithmetic geometry. It was not just the final answer that survived; the methods became part of number theory's working equipment.

Wiles submitted a revised proof in the autumn of nineteen ninety-four. The final published result appeared in the *Annals of Mathematics* in May nineteen ninety-five as two adjacent articles. Wiles's *Modular Elliptic Curves and Fermat's Last Theorem* occupied more than a hundred pages. The companion article, *Ring-Theoretic Properties of Certain Hecke Algebras*, was written by Richard Taylor and Andrew Wiles. Their separate publication was a precise division of authorship, not permission to forget one author's role when retelling the story.

The dedication of Wiles's main article reads, simply, *For Nada, Clare, Kate and Olivia*. It is an instructive line to place next to the theorem. Years of mathematical concentration took place within a life populated by family, collaborators, institutions, and friendships. The paper contains no account of every sacrifice, and a biographer need not supply one. The dedication is the author choosing whom to name.

### What was actually proved

It is worth returning to the logic, now that the historical suspense has ended. Wiles's theorem, with the supporting Taylor–Wiles result, established that semistable elliptic curves over the rational numbers are modular. That was enough to prove Fermat's Last Theorem because Frey's hypothetical curve, after the requisite reductions, would be semistable and Ribet's theorem would rule out its modularity.

The theorem about elliptic curves was not a conclusion guessed from the absence of counterexamples. It was a proof in the mathematical sense: an argument showing that, given established results and explicitly stated conditions, the desired conclusion follows. It was long, highly specialized, and difficult even for other mathematicians working in number theory. Yet its relation to Fermat's elementary equation can be described without hiding the logic. Assume a forbidden solution exists. Build the Frey curve. Use semistable modularity to conclude that the curve is modular. Use level lowering to conclude that the required modular form cannot exist. Contradiction. Therefore the forbidden solution does not exist.

It is equally important to say what Wiles did not prove in nineteen ninety-five. The original modularity conjecture concerned *every* elliptic curve over the rationals, not only semistable ones. Wiles's proof covered the semistable case, an achievement sufficient for Fermat and powerful well beyond it. Later mathematicians extended the methods. Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond, and Richard Taylor completed the proof of the full modularity theorem, published in two thousand and one. The later theorem should not be described as if Wiles had already proved it in nineteen ninety-five, and the later authors should not be treated as mere custodians of a finished result.

What about Fermat? Did the seventeenth-century mathematician really have a proof that would have fit on a larger page? We do not know what argument Fermat had in mind, and we do not have a valid general proof from him. It is possible to recognize Fermat's genuine contributions without accepting his marginal assertion as proof of an unseen demonstration. The fact that the eventual proof involved twentieth-century algebraic geometry and modular forms tells us something about the proof we have; it does not establish what was in Fermat's head. Historical claims and mathematical claims require different evidence.

And what about the idea that a theorem becomes settled only when no human being could ever conceivably find a mistake? Mathematics operates through arguments examined, reconstructed, and used by other experts. There is no magical moment when a printed page acquires immunity from criticism. The episode of the nineteen ninety-three gap is a particularly striking demonstration of why scrutiny matters. A public claim was corrected, rather than protected by prestige. The accepted proof is accepted because the missing argument was supplied and the mathematics endured subsequent examination, not because a famous person repeated the announcement.

### The work after Fermat

After nineteen ninety-five, Wiles could no longer be simply a specialist known chiefly to number theorists. He was a public figure. He received major prizes and honors, including a knighthood and, in two thousand and sixteen, the Abel Prize. The Abel citation recognized both his proof of Fermat's Last Theorem and the opening it created in number theory through the modularity conjecture for semistable elliptic curves. In Oslo, the honor belonged to a mathematical result nearly a quarter century old, but also to an area of research that had continued to expand.

His work did not end with that proof. Wiles continued research, teaching, and mentoring. He held senior positions at Princeton before returning to Oxford as a Royal Society Research Professor. The University of Oxford opened its Andrew Wiles Building in two thousand and thirteen, a setting for present and future mathematics rather than a museum of a finished problem. His earlier work in Iwasawa theory and the arithmetic of elliptic curves remained important even apart from the Fermat result. Many of his students and collaborators went on to substantial research careers of their own.

In two thousand and twenty-six, a biographical memoir of John Coates appeared under Wiles's name. It is a telling detail. The mathematician whose own life is routinely compressed to a childhood puzzle and a triumphant blackboard announcement was writing about the teacher who had helped give him the tools to do research. The memoir presents Coates as a working mathematician in a wider community, moving between universities, collaborators, and problems. That is also the more accurate way to understand Wiles.

His achievement contains a paradox that is not a contradiction. The work was deeply personal to him. It was also dependent on a network of ideas reaching far beyond one person. Fermat and Diophantus were part of the distant ancestry. Germain and Kummer transformed problems about powers into algebraic number theory. Taniyama, Shimura, and Weil proposed a connection between curves and modular forms. Frey, Serre, and Ribet showed how the conjecture could settle Fermat. Langlands, Tunnell, Mazur, and many other mathematicians developed the machinery needed for representations and modularity. Katz helped examine Wiles's developing proof. Taylor helped make the crucial repair. Faltings, Darmon, Diamond, and other readers contributed to the critical process by which the argument became reliable.

None of this diminishes the originality of Wiles's work. Originality in mathematics does not mean thinking without predecessors. It means seeing, and then rigorously establishing, something that the existing collection of ideas does not yet supply. Wiles found a path through a landscape other mathematicians had built. He created essential parts of the path, and other people could then travel it, test it, and build from it.

### The question that changed shape

The story began with a boy who could say a sentence about cubes and fourth powers and understand what it meant. It took decades for that sentence to become connected to enough mathematics to be answered. Much of what Wiles learned as a student did not look like direct progress on the question. Studying elliptic curves with Coates, proving statements in Iwasawa theory with Mazur, working with p-adic representations—these were not detours away from mathematics toward a hobby. They were mathematics. When Ribet's theorem made the childhood question newly accessible, Wiles was able to recognize that the tools he had been developing could matter.

There is an appealing moral available here about persistence, but it needs qualification. Persisting at a false proof does not make it true. Secrecy is not a universal recipe for research. A lifetime devoted to a problem does not guarantee a result. Many excellent mathematicians have worked for years on questions that remain open. The feature of Wiles's story that is worth holding onto is not the idea that determination can overpower any obstacle. It is that curiosity can survive while the person changes, and that the right preparation can make an old curiosity into a different sort of question.

The other moral is about the gap. The nineteen ninety-three announcement and the nineteen ninety-five papers are not competing versions of an uncomplicated triumph. They tell us how mathematical certainty is produced. An argument that almost works does not establish a theorem. A public claim is subject to correction. A collaborator can supply what individual effort could not. A failure of one approach can reveal why another might succeed. The proof survived because its authors kept working and because other mathematicians insisted that the argument, not the story, carry the conclusion.

The margin that Fermat said could not contain his demonstration has become a symbol of mathematical mystery. Wiles's proof would not have fitted in it either. But there is a more revealing contrast. Fermat left a claim. Wiles, Taylor, and the larger community left a body of mathematics that could be read, challenged, checked, and extended. That is what turned a sentence about whole numbers into a theorem.

And somewhere in that history remains a smaller moment, a boy in a Cambridge library finding a problem that anyone could ask. The answer did not come from staying ten years old. It came from growing into a mathematician capable of asking something much harder, and then following that harder question all the way back.

## Nonspoken mathematical and historical companion

### The elementary assertion and exponent reduction

Fermat's Last Theorem (FLT) asserts that for every integer \(n>2\) there are no positive integers \(a,b,c\) such that

\[
a^n+b^n=c^n.
\]

A reduction to cases \(n=4\) and odd prime \(p\) uses divisors of \(n\). For any odd prime \(p\mid n\), set \(A=a^{n/p}\), \(B=b^{n/p}\), and \(C=c^{n/p}\); then \(A^p+B^p=C^p\). If \(n>2\) has no odd prime divisor, \(n\) is a power of two and is divisible by four. Fermat proved the fourth-power case using infinite descent (the precise historical route goes through the impossibility of certain right triangles and related equations). Be careful not to infer that checking all small primes would prove the theorem.

### Elliptic curves and an exact finite-field example

An elliptic curve over \(\mathbb{Q}\) can be given by \(E:y^2=x^3+Ax+B\), with \(4A^3+27B^2\ne0\), and a chosen point at infinity. On \(E:y^2=x^3-x\), reduction modulo five gives \(7\) affine solutions and the point at infinity: \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_5)=8\). Hence

\[
a_5(E)=5+1-8=-2.
\]

At a prime of good reduction, the modular form corresponding to \(E\) has a Fourier coefficient \(a_p\) equal to \(p+1-\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\). More precisely the elliptic curve corresponds to a normalized weight-two newform of level equal to its conductor, with matching Hecke eigenvalues at primes of good reduction. The one example illustrates the relation; it is not evidence that every curve is modular by itself.

### The Frey–Serre–Ribet contradiction

For a primitive hypothetical FLT solution \(a^p+b^p=c^p\) with odd prime \(p\ge5\), an associated Frey curve can be expressed as

\[
E_{a,b,p}:\quad y^2=x(x-a^p)(x+b^p).
\]

The curve is semistable (after using the appropriate normalizations and hypotheses). Serre formulated the modular obstruction, and Ribet proved the needed level-lowering result in 1986. Under modularity of semistable rational elliptic curves, one obtains a modular form whose associated mod-\(p\) representation descends to the impossible weight-two level-two setting. The familiar slogan "there is no weight-two cusp form at level two" abbreviates substantial local and global representation-theoretic arguments; level two does not emerge by a naive division of an integer.

### What the 1995 papers establish

The main article by Wiles proves semistable modularity over \(\mathbb Q\), using mod-three modularity, modularity lifting, and the three-five argument. The joint article of Taylor and Wiles supplies a crucial ring-theoretic result about certain Hecke algebras. *Deformation rings* classify suitably constrained lifts of residual Galois representations; *Hecke algebras* organize modular eigenforms. Statements comparing \(R\) and \(T\) require their local and global deformation conditions and are not universal equalities for any two rings with these names. The corrected proof uses ring-theoretic control subsequently generalized as Taylor–Wiles patching.

The semistable theorem is sufficient for FLT but is not the full modularity theorem for all elliptic curves over \(\mathbb Q\). The latter was completed in work by Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor published in 2001.

### Historical cautions

- The announcement occurred **June 23, 1993**, at the end of Wiles's three Newton Institute lectures; the accepted proof was completed in **1994** and published in **1995**. Do not collapse these into a single date.
- The gap was mathematical and substantial. It was neither merely a typographical error nor evidence that the entire seven-year project contributed nothing.
- Wiles's September 19, 1994 revelation is known from his retrospective recollection. Avoid presenting paraphrase as verbatim contemporaneous dialogue.
- The childhood library episode comes from Wiles's accounts and the opening of his 1995 paper. His early life in Ibadan is documented; do not infer details of the Nigerian environment beyond the available biographies.
- Richard Taylor's role is established by coauthorship of the 1995 companion paper. Earlier mathematical contributions by Frey, Serre, Ribet, Taniyama, Shimura, Weil, Langlands, Tunnell, Coates, Mazur, and others are not optional background to the logical account.
- Public biographical sources disagree about the year assigned to Wiles's Cambridge doctoral degree; narration refers to his **late-1970s doctoral research**, which does not depend on settling that inconsistency.
- The technical discussion deliberately avoids claiming that an elementary description of modularity lifting is a substitute for the proof.
