# 037000 — Évariste Galois: Why can some equations never be solved by radicals?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** algebraic equations, permutations, and the emergence of group theory. **Background:** elementary algebra, introduced again when needed.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine being handed a polynomial equation of degree five. You know its coefficients. You know it has five complex roots, counting multiplicities. You can approximate those roots with a computer to as many digits as you like. But there is another question, one that occupied mathematicians for centuries. Can you express every root using only the coefficients, arithmetic operations, and a finite succession of square roots, cube roots, and higher radicals? Quadratic equations have such a formula. Cubic and quartic equations do too. For a general quintic, the answer is no. The reason is an obstruction in the symmetries among the roots. Évariste Galois helped discover how to make that obstruction exact.

He became one of the most romanticized figures in mathematics. A brilliant French teenager, a political revolutionary, a rejected manuscript, a fatal duel before his twenty-first birthday, and pages supposedly written in one feverish night. Much of that story contains real events, but it is an unreliable guide to the mathematics. Galois's ideas were developed across several years; he published work while alive; and later mathematicians carefully reconstructed and extended the theory from manuscripts that did not resemble a modern textbook. The life is dramatic enough without making the mathematics appear from nowhere. To understand his achievement, we need to follow both the person and the idea.

Évariste Galois was born in Bourg-la-Reine, near Paris, on October twenty-fifth, eighteen eleven. France was still living with the consequences of the Revolution and Napoleon's empire. His father, Nicolas-Gabriel Galois, was active in local public affairs and became mayor. His mother, Adélaïde-Marie Demante, taught him at home in his early years. In eighteen twenty-three he entered the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris, where the discipline and politics of schooling did not suit his temperament. He eventually became absorbed in advanced mathematics, reading books by Legendre and Lagrange well beyond his assigned curriculum.

His parents belonged to the politically engaged middle class. His father, Nicolas-Gabriel Galois, served as mayor of Bourg-la-Reine, and his mother taught him herself until he was twelve. That early education emphasized classical languages and religion rather than advanced mathematics. At the Lycée Louis-le-Grand, the young Galois did not at first look like the inevitable founder of a new algebra. He did well in several subjects, then became increasingly impatient with the school's prescribed sequence. Teachers could see his ability and also complained about his lack of method. Their reports show a student whose pace of thought did not fit comfortably into examinations and classroom discipline. The picture is more complicated than the familiar image of an institution simply incapable of recognizing brilliance.

It helps to place that education inside a mathematical problem already centuries old. In ancient and medieval algebra, an equation was not chiefly a graph to be plotted. It was a problem of extracting an unknown number from known numerical relations. The familiar quadratic formula gave a model of success. For x squared plus b x plus c equal to zero, complete the square. Move c to the right, add one quarter b squared to both sides, and take a square root. You obtain x equals minus b plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four c, all divided by two. No numerical iteration is required. The root is assembled by a finite recipe from the coefficients.

During the sixteenth century, mathematicians including del Ferro, Tartaglia, Cardano, and Ferrari found radical formulas for cubic and quartic equations. These procedures were not simple, but they showed that degree three and degree four were accessible in principle. The next natural target was degree five. Repeated effort failed to produce a comparable general formula. At first this could mean either that nobody had yet found the right trick or that the desired kind of formula did not exist. Those possibilities are mathematically different. Discovering the first would produce an algorithm. Establishing the second would require a theorem about all possible algorithms of the permitted form.

Before Galois, Paolo Ruffini argued that the general quintic was not solvable by radicals, and Niels Henrik Abel gave a decisive proof of the general impossibility in the eighteen twenties. Galois's contribution was different and deeper in a structural direction. He sought a criterion for an individual polynomial: which equations are solvable by radicals, and what feature distinguishes them from those that are not? That question requires understanding relations among roots even when the roots cannot be written explicitly.

Start with a small example. Suppose x squared minus five x plus six equals zero. Its roots are two and three. The coefficients tell us that the two roots add to five and multiply to six. If I exchange the names of the roots, those two relations still hold. This is a symmetry. More generally, for a quadratic with distinct roots alpha and beta, the sum and product are determined by the coefficients. Exchanging alpha and beta preserves every rational relation that comes from those coefficients. There are two possible permutations: do nothing, or swap the roots. Together they form a group of order two.

The word group has a precise meaning. A group is a collection of reversible operations that can be composed, with an identity operation, inverses, and an associative law. Permutations are a natural example. If I first swap two labeled roots and then perform another relabeling, the composite is another permutation. Every permutation has an inverse. The set of all permutations on n objects forms the symmetric group on n letters, written S n. A polynomial of degree n may have fewer allowed root permutations than the full symmetric group, because those permutations must respect all algebraic relations defined over the coefficients' base field.

Here is the crucial distinction. We are not asking which rearrangements leave the product of the linear factors looking the same. Every permutation of the roots does that, because multiplication is commutative. We are asking which permutations are realized by algebraic automorphisms of the field obtained by adjoining all the roots, while fixing the original rational numbers or other specified coefficient field. An automorphism must preserve addition and multiplication and send each rational number to itself. Any polynomial identity with rational coefficients must remain true after the automorphism. That restriction can rule out permutations that superficially appear possible.

Consider x squared minus two. Its roots are positive square root of two and negative square root of two. The field formed from rational numbers and square root of two consists of numbers a plus b times square root of two, where a and b are rational. There are exactly two automorphisms fixing the rationals: leave square root of two unchanged, or send it to its negative. The second choice also sends a plus b square root of two to a minus b square root of two. It respects sums and products because the square of either choice is two. This is the simplest nontrivial Galois group.

Now consider x cubed minus two. It has one real root, the real cube root of two, and two nonreal roots obtained by multiplying by primitive complex cube roots of unity. We must adjoin both the real cube root and those roots of unity to obtain a field containing all three roots. The splitting field then has six automorphisms over the rationals. They act as all six permutations of three roots, forming S three. The quadratic and cubic examples already show why the idea is richer than merely counting roots. The group records which symmetries can coexist with the arithmetic relations.

There is a useful way to hear the noncommutativity of S three. Label the roots one, two, and three. First exchange roots one and two, then exchange roots two and three. The result sends the old root one to three. Perform the exchanges in the opposite order and the old root one goes somewhere else. The final permutations differ. The order changes the result. This is why the word nonabelian is meaningful, and why it becomes a serious obstruction in a radical-solvability problem. A radical step that merely flips one sign has a commuting symmetry group of order two; more complicated equations can display nested operations whose order cannot be ignored.

There is a second structural feature hidden in the quadratic formula. Its square root is the square root of the discriminant, b squared minus four a c. If that discriminant is already a square in the base field, the equation splits there and the root-exchange symmetry disappears. If it is not a square, adjoining its square root gives a genuine two-element Galois group. The same symbolic formula therefore describes two different field situations. The written radical expression alone does not tell us the symmetry group until we specify the coefficient field.

For a cubic with rational coefficients, the discriminant again records an important part of the root symmetry. If an irreducible cubic has a square discriminant, its Galois group is the cyclic group of order three, consisting of even permutations. If its discriminant is not a square, its group is S three. For x cubed minus two, the discriminant is minus one hundred eight, which is not a rational square, so the group is S three. The familiar cubic formula works despite that noncommutativity because S three has a solvable normal-subgroup chain. This will become the essential distinction at degree five.

The formulas for cubic equations also carry a historical warning. Sometimes a cubic has three real roots, but its classical radical formula runs through complex numbers before returning to real answers. This was called the irreducible case, or casus irreducibilis. It showed early algebraists that restricting intermediate calculations to real numbers could hide useful solution methods. Modern Galois theory handles this cleanly: a formula by radicals may live temporarily in a larger field, including complex roots of unity, even when the final roots are real. The field, rather than the visible appearance of the final answer, is the natural setting of the theory.

Galois lived amid a rapidly changing political and educational world. In eighteen twenty-nine he published a paper on continued fractions and submitted work on equations to the Academy of Sciences, where Augustin-Louis Cauchy was involved in reviewing it. That same summer his father died by suicide amid a local scandal, a devastating family event. Galois twice failed to gain admission to the prestigious École Polytechnique. Accounts of those examinations sometimes turn them into a fable about examiners unable to recognize genius. The surviving evidence supports a more complicated portrait: an impatient and difficult student under immense strain, whose mathematical ideas were advanced but whose explanations could be hard to follow.

One episode puts the school's conflicts in human terms. In eighteen twenty-seven Galois discovered advanced mathematics and began working rapidly through books that were beyond the normal syllabus. His mathematics teacher Louis Richard recognized the scale of his ability. Others worried that he neglected subjects and methods they considered essential. He twice failed the competitive entrance examination for the École Polytechnique. Later legends describe an examiner and a blackboard eraser as if one dramatic insult explains everything; the actual records give no secure basis for that theatrical scene. Disappointment at the examinations occurred alongside the growing discovery that he wanted to do original mathematics rather than repeat a curriculum.

He entered the École Normale instead. Meanwhile France underwent the July Revolution of eighteen thirty, which replaced Charles the Tenth with Louis-Philippe. Galois was committed to republican politics and deeply frustrated by the school's attempts to keep students away from the upheaval. After a public dispute with its director, he was expelled. His political involvement later led to arrests and imprisonment. Mathematics was pursued in a life full of interruption, agitation, and personal anger.

The year eighteen twenty-nine also brought a family catastrophe. His father was driven into a public scandal involving forged satirical verses attributed to him and died by suicide. Galois was still a teenager. The surviving accounts connect this loss with his increasing political anger, though no biographer can reduce his subsequent choices to one cause. The July Revolution the following year gave young republicans new hopes, followed quickly by disillusionment with the new monarchy. Galois belonged to political associations and took positions that carried real risk. Mathematics was one part of his life; republican agitation, loyalty to friends, and anger at authority were others.

And yet the mathematical work was not a single burst immediately before his death. In eighteen thirty Galois published on the theory of numbers, describing what we now recognize as important steps toward finite-field theory. This is another side of his achievement. Arithmetic modulo a prime behaves like arithmetic in a small field. For example, modulo five, the numbers zero through four can be added and multiplied, and every nonzero number has a multiplicative inverse. More surprisingly, there are fields with four, eight, nine, and other prime-power numbers of elements. Their structure can be understood through polynomial equations over smaller fields.

To see the idea, work modulo two, so one plus one equals zero. Consider the polynomial t squared plus t plus one. It has no root among zero and one, because substituting either gives one. We can therefore introduce a new symbol alpha satisfying alpha squared plus alpha plus one equals zero. In characteristic two, that says alpha squared equals alpha plus one. The resulting field has four elements: zero, one, alpha, and alpha plus one. Multiplication simplifies using alpha squared equals alpha plus one. This concrete construction shows how adjoining a root creates a larger arithmetic universe with its own symmetries.

In that four-element field, squaring is itself an automorphism. It leaves zero and one fixed, sends alpha to alpha squared, and sends alpha squared back to alpha. The reason is that squaring respects addition in characteristic two: the cross term two a b vanishes. In general, raising to the p-th power is the Frobenius map in characteristic p. For a finite field with p to the n elements, successive Frobenius powers generate a cyclic Galois group of order n over its prime field. Galois's original work predates today's polished vocabulary, but this thread connects his name to the arithmetic of finite fields used throughout modern coding theory, cryptography, and computational algebra.

Return to the original problem of radical formulas. Why should the structure of the group decide whether radicals will work? Taking a square root introduces a very controlled kind of ambiguity: the result can be either of two signs. Taking an n-th root introduces a finite family of choices differing by roots of unity. By adjoining radicals one at a time, we build a tower of field extensions. After adjoining the necessary roots of unity, the automorphism groups of these stages are abelian, meaning their operations commute. The cumulative symmetry group can therefore be broken into a chain whose successive quotients are abelian. Groups with such a chain are called solvable groups.

This is the algebraic heart of the criterion: for a polynomial over a field of characteristic zero, solvability by radicals is equivalent to solvability of its Galois group. A full proof needs careful treatment of splitting fields, roots of unity, normal extensions, and the correspondence between fields and subgroups. But the basic mechanism is already visible. Each radical extraction permits a limited kind of symmetry. A long tower can combine those symmetries, yet it cannot generate arbitrary algebraic complexity. If the full symmetry group is too entangled to be decomposed into abelian stages, no radical recipe can capture all its roots.

Why does a ladder of fields translate into a ladder of groups? Imagine starting from rational numbers, then adjoining the square root of two, and then adjoining the square root of three. At the first step the sign choice for square root of two creates a symmetry of order two. After the second step there is another independent sign choice. The whole construction has four automorphisms, and they form a commutative group. This is a particularly simple radical tower. More elaborate towers may have noncommuting groups overall, but they have a chain that can be analyzed through successive abelian pieces. That chain is what solvability formalizes.

The converse is equally important. If the group has an appropriate solvable chain, Galois theory lets us construct a tower of radical extensions sufficiently large to contain the roots. The proof is not merely the observation that known formulas happen to produce solvable groups. It is a classification: the algebraic symmetry is enough to tell us when radicals are possible. The word possible refers to expression by a finite sequence of allowed operations, not to the efficiency or simplicity of the resulting expression. A solvable polynomial may still have a dreadful-looking formula.

The group S three is solvable. It has a normal subgroup A three containing the three even permutations, which is cyclic of order three. The quotient S three over A three has order two. We therefore obtain a chain of groups in which the successive quotients have orders three and two, both abelian. That matches the existence of cubic formulas. S four is also solvable, though its normal-subgroup chain is longer. This matches Ferrari's formula for quartics. But for degree five a different phenomenon becomes possible.

Inside S five sits A five, the group of all even permutations of five objects. It has sixty elements. Unlike the smaller alternating group A four, A five is a nonabelian simple group: it has no nontrivial proper normal subgroup. Its operations do not all commute, and it cannot be broken into a chain of normal subgroups with abelian quotients. Since A five is a normal subgroup of S five, S five is not solvable. Thus any polynomial whose Galois group is the full S five cannot have all its roots expressed by radicals over the rationals.

Why does A five create such a strong obstruction? Its simplicity is the key. In a group, a normal subgroup is a collection preserved by conjugation, the process of transporting an operation by another group operation and its inverse. Normal subgroups are what allow quotient groups to be formed. If there are no nontrivial proper normal subgroups, there is no way to decompose the group through intermediate normal stages. A five has that property, and it is noncommutative. So any attempted solvable series for A five would be forced to stop at a nonabelian quotient.

One can even see the outline of a proof from counting. The sixty elements of A five divide into conjugacy classes of sizes one, fifteen, twenty, twelve, and twelve. A normal subgroup has to be a union of conjugacy classes and must contain the identity. Its size must divide sixty by Lagrange's theorem. Try adding the numbers fifteen, twenty, twelve, and twelve to the mandatory one, in any proper combination. None of the resulting proper sums is a divisor of sixty. The only possible normal-subgroup sizes are one and sixty. This is a striking instance in which a concrete finite count rules out an entire hierarchy of algebraic constructions.

The modern definition of solvability is also more flexible than the older question about radicals. A group is solvable if its derived series eventually reaches the identity, where the derived subgroup is generated by commutators. A commutator measures the failure of two group elements to commute. For A five, the derived subgroup is all of A five, so repeatedly taking commutators never reduces its complexity. This makes the impossibility intuitive: the noncommutativity is structurally persistent.

This is a remarkable kind of negative knowledge. Rather than trying every clever formula and failing again and again, one can prove that the entire class of formulas is insufficient. The obstruction is not computational weakness. You could calculate a quintic root to a million digits and still be unable to express it in a finite radical formula. Numerical approximation and algebraic solvability ask different questions.

Let us examine a particular quintic so the statement has a definite object. Consider x to the fifth minus six x plus three. Eisenstein's irreducibility criterion with the prime three tells us this polynomial cannot factor into lower-degree rational polynomials: all the nonleading coefficients are divisible by three, while the constant term three is not divisible by nine. Irreducibility forces its Galois group to act transitively on its five roots. Since the group order is divisible by five, it contains a five-cycle.

Next look at the graph of the polynomial on the real line. Its derivative is five x to the fourth minus six. This derivative has exactly two real zeros, one negative and one positive, so the polynomial has two turning points. The values at suitable intervals show it crosses the axis three times. That leaves two nonreal roots, which form a complex-conjugate pair. Complex conjugation fixes the three real roots and swaps the two nonreal roots, giving a transposition in the Galois group. A five-cycle together with a transposition generates the full symmetric group in this prime-degree setting, so the Galois group is S five. No solution by radicals exists for this particular polynomial.

The example deserves a caution about what the result says. It does not assert that every equation of degree five is unsolvable by radicals. The equation x to the fifth minus thirty-two equals zero has the real root two, and its other roots are obtainable using fifth roots of unity. Special quintics have solvable Galois groups. The result is that there is no formula by radicals that works for arbitrary quintics, and many concrete quintics, including the one we just studied, are not solvable by radicals at all.

There is a second caution about history. The compact modern presentation through splitting fields, automorphism groups, normal subgroups, and the fundamental theorem of Galois theory developed through later generations. Galois introduced the essential group-theoretic analysis of equations, but the definitions and proofs familiar from a current algebra textbook were refined by mathematicians such as Jordan, Dedekind, and Artin. History is more exact when it traces both the originating idea and its later formalization.

The fundamental theorem of Galois theory expresses a beautiful reversal. Suppose L is a finite Galois extension of a base field K, and G is its group of automorphisms fixing K. Every intermediate field between K and L corresponds to a subgroup of G, and every subgroup corresponds to an intermediate field. Larger fields correspond to smaller subgroups. Normal intermediate extensions correspond to normal subgroups, and their Galois groups are the appropriate quotients. This gives two parallel ways to navigate the same structure: add numbers to a field, or remove symmetries from a group.

The field-group correspondence gives a way to draw an abstract algebraic map. Put the large splitting field at the top of a page, the rational numbers at the bottom, and the intermediate fields in between. Then draw the subgroup lattice upside down. Every intermediate field appears opposite the group of symmetries that fixes it. Taking a fixed field loses symmetries as it adds numbers. Taking a subgroup preserves only those automorphisms compatible with the additional equations. This dual organization is one reason the theory is so powerful. It converts questions about algebraic expressions into finite combinatorial structure, and sometimes converts finite group questions into questions about field extensions.

For the field generated by square root of two and square root of three, the three proper quadratic intermediate fields are generated by square root of two, square root of three, and square root of six. The last one is easy to forget: multiplying the two radicals produces another quadratic extension. The four-element automorphism group has three different subgroups of order two, and the three quadratic fields correspond to them. This small lattice is a complete, concrete instance of the fundamental theorem.

A small example makes that reversal audible. Consider a field containing both square root of two and square root of three. Starting from the rationals, adjoining both gives four combinations: a plus b square root of two plus c square root of three plus d square root of six. There are four automorphisms, determined by choosing the signs of the two independent square roots. They form a group in which every operation has order at most two. The intermediate field containing only square root of two corresponds to the two automorphisms that leave square root of two fixed while possibly changing the sign of square root of three. Add the second square root, and the permitted symmetry shrinks to the identity. Enlarging a field reduces its remaining symmetries.

There is also a computational story here. If we can factor a polynomial modulo carefully chosen primes, the factor degrees often reveal cycle patterns in its Galois group. For example, a factorization into one irreducible quadratic and one irreducible cubic modulo a suitable prime corresponds to an element whose permutation has a two-cycle and a three-cycle. Cubing that element leaves a transposition. An irreducible reduction of a quintic modulo another suitable prime gives a five-cycle. These observations can help certify that a polynomial's group is S five without ever writing exact formulas for its roots. A nineteenth-century idea becomes a practical method in modern computational algebra.

Finite fields reappear here for a second reason. In a finite field, the Frobenius automorphism acts on the roots of a polynomial, and its cycles correspond to degrees of irreducible factors. So factoring an integer polynomial modulo primes samples the arithmetic symmetry of its roots. This is not identical to the finite-field construction from Galois's early paper, but it shows how different branches of his work meet in later mathematical practice.

The conceptual power is easy to miss if we treat this as only a recipe for solving polynomials. A root is an object whose definition is constrained by algebraic equations. Instead of staring at an object directly, study all transformations that preserve the relations defining it. Then translate those transformations into structural information about the object. That strategy reaches far beyond nineteenth-century algebra. Symmetry groups would later organize geometry, physics, topology, and number theory.

Galois's political life intensified during eighteen thirty-one. He was arrested after a public gathering and later imprisoned following a demonstration. His scientific memoir was reviewed by Siméon-Denis Poisson, who judged the arguments insufficiently clear and developed for the Academy to accept. That report can feel especially painful in retrospect, but it is important to distinguish a correct mathematical insight from a submission that had already given readers every needed justification. Poisson's report and Liouville's later edition illustrate a practical problem in mathematical research: a breakthrough needs enough exposition for other specialists to evaluate it and carry it forward.

Imprisonment changed the conditions under which he could work. Following arrests connected to republican activity, he spent months in Sainte-Pélagie prison. Biographical accounts preserve distressing episodes of drinking, isolation, and a suicide attempt from which fellow prisoners intervened. These are accounts of a young man in severe difficulty, not evidence that suffering caused mathematical originality. Meanwhile a mathematical memoir was judged insufficiently developed by Poisson and Lacroix. The report did not say that its author had no ideas; it said the exposition did not allow the referees to assess the argument adequately. Galois's frustration was understandable, but the historical record also makes room for legitimate difficulty in reading work that had not been fully explained.

In early eighteen thirty-two, a cholera epidemic affected Paris, and Galois was transferred from prison to another establishment. He became involved in a troubled personal relationship. The circumstances of the duel on May thirtieth remain disputed; various romantic and political interpretations have circulated, often with more confidence than the documentary evidence supports. What is certain is that he was shot and died on May thirty-first, aged twenty. The shortness of that life helps explain the legend but should never replace an account of the actual mathematics.

When cholera spread through Paris in eighteen thirty-two, some prisoners were moved out of Sainte-Pélagie. Galois was transferred to a private establishment, where he became attached to Stéphanie-Félicie du Motel. Their correspondence suggests a painful and troubled relationship. The exact chain of events leading to the duel remains disputed, and the motives attributed to particular people have changed across retellings. It is important to resist the image of a young mathematician deliberately arranging his own death so that his work would become immortal. He was an eighteen-thirties Parisian youth caught in political and personal conflicts, with mathematical manuscripts still unfinished.

On May twenty-ninth, Galois wrote a letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier reviewing important parts of his research and asking that other mathematicians examine the work. Chevalier published the letter later in eighteen thirty-two. Joseph Liouville eventually presented substantial manuscripts in eighteen forty-six, helping the mathematical community engage with them. The gap between discovery and assimilation became part of the history of Galois theory itself.

His letter to Auguste Chevalier addressed a trusted friend rather than an imagined audience. He asked his friend to pass work to mathematicians who might understand it, naming people whose judgment he respected. This was a practical attempt to preserve research amid uncertainty. The subsequent legend of a complete theory written in one frantic night exaggerates the role of the letter. He had been publishing and revising algebra for years, and the final communication gathered parts of that continuing effort. Galois died after the duel at twenty. His letters and mathematical notes survived through the actions of friends and later editors, who turned scattered pages into a body of mathematics others could study.

One of the most striking features of the subsequent story is the way Galois's language became more influential when mathematicians adapted it. Camille Jordan's eighteen seventy treatise on substitutions and algebraic equations gave systematic form to many ideas about permutation groups and their applications. Later abstract algebra made groups, fields, and rings into independent objects of study. The theory of equations became one chapter in a much broader mathematical language. Galois did not see that future, but his structural move helped make it possible.

Let us make a final comparison between equations and geometry. Suppose I hand you a square. You can describe it by its four corners and four sides. Or you can describe it by the eight rigid symmetries that preserve it: four rotations and four reflections. The second description does not replace the first. It reveals organization that a list of coordinates may conceal. Galois proposed an analogous viewpoint for polynomial roots. The roots may be algebraically inaccessible as individual formulas, yet their permissible exchanges can still be studied exactly.

That change of viewpoint answers the guiding question. Why can some equations never be solved by radicals? Because the form of solution you permit has an algebraic structure, and the equation's root symmetries may exceed that structure. A failure to find a formula can be elevated to a proof that no such formula exists. The mathematics is powerful because it tells us what can be done, what cannot be done, and which property makes the difference.

Galois developed bold ideas under personal and political pressure. He struggled to communicate those ideas within the mathematical institutions of his time, and the manuscripts he left required difficult editorial work. The mathematics survived because people copied, published, taught, and rebuilt it. His enduring legacy lies in that collaboration across time: a new method of understanding equations by symmetry, transformed into a foundation of modern algebra.

## Display-only mathematical companion

### Roots and automorphisms

For a separable polynomial \(f\in K[x]\), let \(L\) be its splitting field. The Galois group is

\[
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)=\{\sigma:L\to L\mid \sigma\text{ is a field automorphism},\ \sigma|_K=\operatorname{id}\}.
\]

Each automorphism permutes the roots, but only permutations preserving all polynomial relations over \(K\) can occur.

### Running examples

For \(f=x^2-2\), \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\), and
\[
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)=\{\operatorname{id},\sigma\},\qquad \sigma(\sqrt2)=-\sqrt2.
\]

For \(f=x^3-2\), \(L=\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2,\omega)\), \(\omega^2+\omega+1=0\), and \(\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\cong S_3\).

### Four-element field

\[
\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[\alpha]/(\alpha^2+\alpha+1),\qquad
\alpha^2=\alpha+1,\qquad \operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb F_4/\mathbb F_2)=\langle x\mapsto x^2\rangle\cong C_2.
\]

### Radical-solvability criterion

Over a field of characteristic zero, \(f\) is solvable by radicals if and only if its splitting-field Galois group is solvable. A finite group \(G\) is solvable when there is a chain
\[
G=G_0\triangleright G_1\triangleright\cdots\triangleright G_r=\{e\}
\]
with abelian quotients \(G_i/G_{i+1}\).

\(S_3\triangleright A_3\triangleright\{e\}\) is solvable; \(S_5\) is not solvable since its nonabelian simple normal subgroup \(A_5\) is not solvable.

### An unsolvable quintic

\[
f(x)=x^5-6x+3.
\]

By Eisenstein at \(p=3\), \(f\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). Its derivative \(5x^4-6\) has two real critical points, and the signs of the local extrema show that \(f\) has exactly three real roots. Complex conjugation therefore acts as a transposition on the remaining two roots. Transitivity and the degree-five condition yield a five-cycle; in degree five, any subgroup containing a five-cycle and a transposition is \(S_5\). Consequently its roots are not all expressible by radicals.

### Fundamental theorem of Galois theory

For a finite Galois extension \(L/K\), the map \(H\mapsto L^H\) reverses inclusions, establishing a bijection between subgroups \(H\leq\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\) and intermediate fields \(K\subseteq E\subseteq L\).
