# 037000 — Évariste Galois I: A young republican in the schools of Paris

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** family, public education, the July Revolution, and equations. **Background:** secondary-school algebra, introduced again where needed.

In July of eighteen thirty, students in Paris heard the noise of revolution in the streets. The Bourbon king, Charles the Tenth, had tried to curb the press and limit the electorate. Barricades went up. At the École Préparatoire, the teacher-training institution later known as the École Normale, students were ordered to stay inside. Among them was a young republican and mathematician named Évariste Galois. Accounts of the episode say he tried to get over the school wall to join the fighting. Whether or not that attempt would have changed anything, the frustration mattered: Galois believed the country's future was being decided just beyond the gates, while the institution preparing him to teach insisted that he remain indoors.

This was neither the beginning of his life nor the source of his mathematical ideas. It is, however, a good place to begin a history of Galois as a person. The familiar legend runs directly from an adolescent genius to a rejected paper and a fatal duel. The real sequence includes a household with political commitments, years of schoolwork, arguments over teaching, a series of publications and revisions, and a revolution that displaced one monarchy with another. He died at twenty, but he did not live outside history.

Évariste was born on October twenty-fifth, eighteen eleven, at Bourg-la-Reine, south of Paris. His father, Nicolas-Gabriel Galois, became the town's mayor after the fall of Napoleon. His mother, Adélaïde-Marie Demante, gave him his early lessons at home. She taught classical languages and religion; she was not preparing him by means of an advanced course in algebra. In this family, education was a serious matter, and local civic life was not an abstraction. The boy's first world was neither a university nor a mathematical academy, but a provincial household watching the French state change its form again and again.

Napoleon's defeat had restored the Bourbon dynasty, but it had not erased the political divisions left by the Revolution. Constitutional liberals, royalists, republicans, and supporters of the Church contested schools, newspapers, elections, and municipal office. Galois's father occupied a public position within that climate. The importance of this background becomes clearer when we reach the family disaster of eighteen twenty-nine: there were real costs to being the visible representative of one political camp in a small town.

At twelve Évariste was sent to the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris. The school offered the education necessary to compete for elite state institutions. It also demanded obedience and steady performance across a curriculum far broader than mathematics. Galois did not arrive as the universally recognized mathematical prodigy of the legend. He made progress in classical subjects before mathematics became his consuming interest. In eighteen twenty-seven he began reading demanding works by Legendre and Lagrange, often faster than the prescribed lessons allowed. Louis Richard, one of his mathematics teachers, recognized an exceptionally able student. Other teachers saw someone who resisted classroom methods and failed to explain enough of his reasoning to those around him.

That tension mattered for more than examination marks. In France, the École Polytechnique was a principal route into technical and scientific careers connected to the state. Galois tried twice to enter and twice failed. Stories later attached an insulted examiner, and sometimes a thrown eraser, to the second attempt. The evidence does not establish that scene. An entrance examination tests communication under constraints as well as independent invention; the surviving accounts show both striking ability and serious difficulties in meeting an institution's expectations. They do not license a simple verdict that all his examiners were fools.

During eighteen twenty-nine, two kinds of setback converged. Galois was beginning to send mathematical papers into the professional world. His first article, on continued fractions, appeared in print; he also submitted a memoir on algebraic equations to the Academy of Sciences. Augustin-Louis Cauchy handled work associated with this submission, but the documentary history is more complicated than a story in which a famous professor merely lost a finished masterpiece. Galois would submit, revise, and resubmit work through several different channels. His mathematical ideas were developing over time.

In the same year his father died by suicide after a local campaign of hostile, fabricated verses. The episode was linked to the religious and political tensions of Bourg-la-Reine. His son was still seventeen. It is reasonable to say that a family catastrophe and disappointment at the Polytechnique affected the conditions under which he lived and studied. It is not responsible to explain every later act of defiance as the inevitable outcome of either event. The record gives us experiences and choices, not access to a private psychological formula.

Galois entered the École Préparatoire, which trained teachers rather than admitting students to the engineering elite of the Polytechnique. While he continued trying to reach mathematicians through the Academy, the government of Charles the Tenth became increasingly confrontational. In eighteen thirty another submission was associated with a competition for the Academy's grand prize. The prize went to Jacobi and, posthumously, Abel. Galois's papers were not assessed in that competition. This is part of a history of scientific publication: the importance of an idea and the fate of its physical manuscript are not the same question.

The July Revolution replaced Charles the Tenth with Louis-Philippe, whose constitutional monarchy disappointed republicans who had hoped for something more radical. At the École Préparatoire, Galois publicly criticized its director, who had kept students from joining the insurrection. He was expelled after a bitter public exchange. The sequence says much about the time: the state trained teachers, teachers were expected to obey institutional rules, and a student who treated a revolution as a summons to action could quickly lose his place.

He joined the artillery branch of the National Guard, which had republican associations; the new government dissolved that branch at the end of eighteen thirty. Early in eighteen thirty-one Galois also tried to teach higher algebra independently. A relatively large audience came to the first meeting, but it diminished. He needed a professional place in mathematics and, increasingly, a way to live outside the school from which he had been expelled. At the same time he was becoming more deeply involved in political organizing.

A letter written by the older mathematician Sophie Germain in April eighteen thirty-one gives a view from the professional community. Writing to Guillaume Libri, she acknowledged Galois's promise but described his behavior with alarm, and she reported that he and his mother had little money. Her words are evidence that his circumstances were visible and troubling to contemporaries; they are not a diagnosis of the young man. Nor do they support a myth in which every adult refused to recognize his ability. Several mathematicians tried, with mixed results, to read or circulate what he had written.

One might imagine that Galois alternated between an orderly mathematical career and an unrelated political life. There is no such neat division in the evidence. He wrote mathematics in the margins of institutional upheaval, and he tried to change institutions whose rules affected him directly. The problems in algebra that interested him were inherited from a long history, but his opportunities to present them depended on the politics, friendships, examinations, publishers, and learned societies of France in the early eighteen thirties.

To understand what this young man had been trying to communicate, we can now leave the classroom and street for a sustained look at his subject. The following examples are modern explanations: the field and group language in which we teach Galois theory today was developed and polished by later mathematicians. They show the kind of structural problem that his writings opened, not the exact lecture that a Parisian student would have heard in eighteen thirty.

Imagine being handed a polynomial equation of degree five. You know its coefficients. You know it has five complex roots, counting multiplicities. You can approximate those roots with a computer to as many digits as you like. But there is another question, one that occupied mathematicians for centuries. Can you express every root using only the coefficients, arithmetic operations, and a finite succession of square roots, cube roots, and higher radicals? Quadratic equations have such a formula. Cubic and quartic equations do too. For a general quintic, the answer is no. The reason is an obstruction in the symmetries among the roots. Évariste Galois helped discover how to make that obstruction exact.

It helps to place that education inside a mathematical problem already centuries old. In ancient and medieval algebra, an equation was not chiefly a graph to be plotted. It was a problem of extracting an unknown number from known numerical relations. The familiar quadratic formula gave a model of success. For x squared plus b x plus c equal to zero, complete the square. Move c to the right, add one quarter b squared to both sides, and take a square root. You obtain x equals minus b plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four c, all divided by two. No numerical iteration is required. The root is assembled by a finite recipe from the coefficients.

During the sixteenth century, mathematicians including del Ferro, Tartaglia, Cardano, and Ferrari found radical formulas for cubic and quartic equations. These procedures were not simple, but they showed that degree three and degree four were accessible in principle. The next natural target was degree five. Repeated effort failed to produce a comparable general formula. At first this could mean either that nobody had yet found the right trick or that the desired kind of formula did not exist. Those possibilities are mathematically different. Discovering the first would produce an algorithm. Establishing the second would require a theorem about all possible algorithms of the permitted form.

Before Galois, Paolo Ruffini argued that the general quintic was not solvable by radicals, and Niels Henrik Abel gave a decisive proof of the general impossibility in the eighteen twenties. Galois's contribution was different and deeper in a structural direction. He sought a criterion for an individual polynomial: which equations are solvable by radicals, and what feature distinguishes them from those that are not? That question requires understanding relations among roots even when the roots cannot be written explicitly.

Start with a small example. Suppose x squared minus five x plus six equals zero. Its roots are two and three. The coefficients tell us that the two roots add to five and multiply to six. If I exchange the names of the roots, those two relations still hold. This is a symmetry. More generally, for a quadratic with distinct roots alpha and beta, the sum and product are determined by the coefficients. Exchanging alpha and beta preserves every rational relation that comes from those coefficients. There are two possible permutations: do nothing, or swap the roots. Together they form a group of order two.

The word group has a precise meaning. A group is a collection of reversible operations that can be composed, with an identity operation, inverses, and an associative law. Permutations are a natural example. If I first swap two labeled roots and then perform another relabeling, the composite is another permutation. Every permutation has an inverse. The set of all permutations on n objects forms the symmetric group on n letters, written S n. A polynomial of degree n may have fewer allowed root permutations than the full symmetric group, because those permutations must respect all algebraic relations defined over the coefficients' base field.

Here is the crucial distinction. We are not asking which rearrangements leave the product of the linear factors looking the same. Every permutation of the roots does that, because multiplication is commutative. We are asking which permutations are realized by algebraic automorphisms of the field obtained by adjoining all the roots, while fixing the original rational numbers or other specified coefficient field. An automorphism must preserve addition and multiplication and send each rational number to itself. Any polynomial identity with rational coefficients must remain true after the automorphism. That restriction can rule out permutations that superficially appear possible.

Consider x squared minus two. Its roots are positive square root of two and negative square root of two. The field formed from rational numbers and square root of two consists of numbers a plus b times square root of two, where a and b are rational. There are exactly two automorphisms fixing the rationals: leave square root of two unchanged, or send it to its negative. The second choice also sends a plus b square root of two to a minus b square root of two. It respects sums and products because the square of either choice is two. This is the simplest nontrivial Galois group.

Now consider x cubed minus two. It has one real root, the real cube root of two, and two nonreal roots obtained by multiplying by primitive complex cube roots of unity. We must adjoin both the real cube root and those roots of unity to obtain a field containing all three roots. The splitting field then has six automorphisms over the rationals. They act as all six permutations of three roots, forming S three. The quadratic and cubic examples already show why the idea is richer than merely counting roots. The group records which symmetries can coexist with the arithmetic relations.

There is a useful way to hear the noncommutativity of S three. Label the roots one, two, and three. First exchange roots one and two, then exchange roots two and three. The result sends the old root one to three. Perform the exchanges in the opposite order and the old root one goes somewhere else. The final permutations differ. The order changes the result. This is why the word nonabelian is meaningful, and why it becomes a serious obstruction in a radical-solvability problem. A radical step that merely flips one sign has a commuting symmetry group of order two; more complicated equations can display nested operations whose order cannot be ignored.

There is a second structural feature hidden in the quadratic formula. Its square root is the square root of the discriminant, b squared minus four a c. If that discriminant is already a square in the base field, the equation splits there and the root-exchange symmetry disappears. If it is not a square, adjoining its square root gives a genuine two-element Galois group. The same symbolic formula therefore describes two different field situations. The written radical expression alone does not tell us the symmetry group until we specify the coefficient field.

For a cubic with rational coefficients, the discriminant again records an important part of the root symmetry. If an irreducible cubic has a square discriminant, its Galois group is the cyclic group of order three, consisting of even permutations. If its discriminant is not a square, its group is S three. For x cubed minus two, the discriminant is minus one hundred eight, which is not a rational square, so the group is S three. The familiar cubic formula works despite that noncommutativity because S three has a solvable normal-subgroup chain. This will become the essential distinction at degree five.

The formulas for cubic equations also carry a historical warning. Sometimes a cubic has three real roots, but its classical radical formula runs through complex numbers before returning to real answers. This was called the irreducible case, or casus irreducibilis. It showed early algebraists that restricting intermediate calculations to real numbers could hide useful solution methods. Modern Galois theory handles this cleanly: a formula by radicals may live temporarily in a larger field, including complex roots of unity, even when the final roots are real. The field, rather than the visible appearance of the final answer, is the natural setting of the theory.

At the end of eighteen thirty, Galois knew that he wanted to understand equations through the relations among their roots, and he had already published mathematical work. He did not yet have a stable institutional position, and the political commitments that brought him out of the school threatened to take much more from him. In the final nineteen months of his life, his prison cell, his friends, and the difficulty of getting a manuscript read became as important to the surviving history of the mathematics as any examination room.
