# 047000 — Paul Dirac: What sort of mathematics could make antimatter possible?

**Track:** History · People and ideas. **Theme:** mathematical form, relativity, quantum theory, and the people behind a restrained public persona. **Background:** algebra, squares, negative numbers, and a qualitative understanding of waves.

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Suppose I tell you that the total energy of a moving particle must satisfy a certain equation. The square of its energy equals the square of its momentum, multiplied by the square of the speed of light, plus the square of its rest energy. It is a beautifully compact rule from relativity. But the square creates a problem: taking a square root can give either a positive answer or a negative one. What might a negative energy mean? Is it a mistake, an artifact of the formula, or a clue about the physical world?

This was one of the questions that helped Paul Dirac change theoretical physics. He did not begin by asking how to predict an undiscovered particle. He was trying to make two extremely successful theories, quantum mechanics and special relativity, live in the same mathematical house. When he found an equation he believed was properly constructed, the equation brought consequences he did not initially know how to interpret. The story is about the power of mathematical structure, but also the danger of confusing what a structure permits with what nature has actually shown.

Dirac was born in Bristol in nineteen hundred two. His father, Charles, had come from Switzerland and taught French; his mother, Florence, was English. Biographical accounts describe a household in which the father imposed strict linguistic and behavioral expectations. Dirac recalled that his father required French at meals, while the children were otherwise speaking English. Accounts of the family's dining arrangements and of Paul's unhappy childhood come from later testimony, not from a transcript of daily life. They help explain why a reserved man became known to the public through stories of his silence, but we should not reduce a scientific career to a psychological story about one parent.

At the Merchant Venturers school, science and mathematics were practical disciplines. During the First World War, the school's organization enabled younger pupils, including Dirac, to progress quickly through advanced work. He chose electrical engineering at the University of Bristol, not theoretical physics. That training matters. Engineers must attend to whether an equation can describe a real mechanism and whether the symbols on a page correspond to things one could, at least in principle, measure. When Dirac graduated in nineteen twenty-one, employment was difficult to find. A scholarship to Cambridge was insufficient to support him, and problems of financial eligibility obstructed an immediate move. Bristol allowed him to study mathematics without paying fees. Only in nineteen twenty-three did he begin research at Cambridge, under Ralph Fowler.

The new physics arrived while Dirac was preparing himself to contribute. Classical physics had pictured a particle as following a trajectory, with a position and a momentum at every instant. Quantum experiments resisted that familiar picture. In Werner Heisenberg's emerging theory, the quantities that replaced classical variables did not always behave like ordinary numbers. Multiplying them in one order could give a different result from multiplying them in the reverse order.

Let's make that strangeness concrete without pretending we can explain the whole of quantum theory in a sentence. Think of two operations on a piece of writing. One operation doubles every number. Another adds one to every number. Double first, then add one to three, and you get seven. Add one first, then double, and you get eight. The operations do not commute. In ordinary arithmetic, three times four equals four times three. Operations on a space need not have that property. Quantum observables are represented by operators, and their noncommutation helps encode limits on what can be specified together.

To see what such an operator difference means in the actual quantum setting, think of a wave function as a changing curve drawn above a horizontal axis. One operation multiplies the height at every point by its horizontal position. Another measures how steeply the curve changes at that position; mathematically, it differentiates the function. Differentiate first and then multiply by position, and you obtain the position times the slope. Multiply by position first and then differentiate, and the product rule adds one extra copy of the original curve. The difference between the two orders is therefore a particularly simple operation. This fact has a precise quantum counterpart involving position and momentum. It does not mean that every classical operation secretly involves uncertainty, but it shows how a noncommuting algebra can still obey exact, testable rules.

There is a more general teaching lesson here. A theory may retain familiar words, position and momentum, while changing the mathematical kind of thing those words refer to. If you assume they remain ordinary numbers and use every ordinary-number algebraic identity, you erase the new theory's content. Dirac's gift was not merely quick algebra. It was the ability to take the new objects seriously enough to ask what relationships among them survive.

Dirac recognized that the difference between the two multiplication orders was not a nuisance one could simply discard. It was central to the new mechanics. He related the quantum commutator, the difference between doing two operations in opposite orders, to the older mathematical idea of the Poisson bracket. That connection let him translate familiar mechanics into a new language while preserving the relationships that mattered. He was not alone in this effort: Heisenberg, Max Born, Pascual Jordan, Erwin Schrödinger, and others were developing complementary formulations. Dirac's contribution was to make a powerful, general operator language available and to show how it related to what had come before.

Now return to the conflict with relativity. In introductory quantum mechanics, an equation for the wave function often contains a first derivative with respect to time and a second derivative with respect to space. Its energy resembles kinetic energy proportional to momentum squared. That works very well when particles move slowly compared with light, but Einstein's relation between energy, mass and momentum has a different form. A straightforward attempt to insert relativistic energy leads to an equation with second derivatives in time, now known in a broader context as the Klein–Gordon equation. That equation is mathematically useful and remains part of modern field theory. But it did not meet Dirac's initial hopes for a single-particle electron theory with a probability density that behaved in the way he wanted.

Dirac pursued a more demanding idea: make the equation first order in time and first order in space, while ensuring that its square reproduces the relativistic energy rule. If you imagine an ordinary number multiplied by momentum, its square gives the square of momentum. But there is also a mass contribution, and when you square a sum, cross terms appear. These unwanted mixed terms must cancel. Ordinary numerical coefficients cannot do that job in the required way. Matrices can.

Here is a tiny model you can carry in your head. Picture a two-entry column, rather than a single number, describing a state. Introduce one operation that swaps its entries, and another that leaves the first entry alone but changes the sign of the second. If you perform the swap and then the sign change, you get the negative of the result from doing them in the opposite order. Mathematicians call such operations anticommuting. If you take a sum of these two operations with suitable coefficients and square it, the mixed terms cancel. What remains is the sum of two squares, just the shape relativity requires.

This little construction works as an illustration in one spatial direction. For the full three-dimensional electron, the algebra is more demanding. Dirac's matrices must represent several anticommuting directions as well as the mass term. The familiar formulation uses four-component wave functions and four-by-four matrices. The components are not four separate physical particles. They are a mathematical way to encode how the electron's state transforms, including spin and the distinction between positive- and negative-energy solutions.

Notice the logic. The equation is not chosen because its symbols look elegant. It must reproduce an independently known relativistic relation when squared; it must give sensible quantum evolution; and its predictions must meet experiment. Mathematical consistency narrows the range of choices. It does not replace observational testing.

The resulting equation, published in nineteen twenty-eight, explained electron spin in a relativistically coherent manner and gave the correct structure for important aspects of the electron's magnetic behavior. But the negative-energy solutions did not disappear. Dirac wrestled with their interpretation. His early hole theory treated a hypothetical filled collection of negative-energy electron states as a background. A missing electron in that sea would behave, in crucial respects, like a positively charged particle. The historical path from that proposal to modern antiparticles was neither immediate nor clean. Dirac first entertained an identification with the proton; this did not work. In nineteen thirty-two, Carl Anderson identified the positron experimentally in cosmic-ray tracks. The success belongs to a conversation between mathematics, experimental apparatus and several researchers, not to a story in which a single line of algebra produced a particle by magic.

We can unpack one key consequence. An electron carries negative electric charge. A positron has the same mass but positive charge. If a positron meets an electron under suitable conditions, their energy can become photons; conversely, enough energy in an appropriate physical process can create an electron–positron pair. The exact conservation requirements matter. Energy, momentum and charge have to balance. The equation helped reveal a deep symmetry in the types of matter that can exist, but modern quantum field theory describes particles and antiparticles without literally filling every negative-energy electron state in the old sea picture.

Dirac's manner became almost as famous as his mathematics. Many stories feature his refusal to offer unnecessary words. Some are based on contemporaries' recollections; others grew through repetition. The safest conclusion is that colleagues often experienced him as remarkably economical in speech, and that the economy of his published arguments had a lasting influence. A biography should not treat every witty Dirac anecdote as documented dialogue, nor infer that he lacked affection or human connection because he disliked chatter.

He worked and traveled within an unusually international mathematical community. At Cambridge he interacted with Fowler, and he developed relations with scientists across the European centers of quantum theory, including Copenhagen and Göttingen. His nineteen thirty-three Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Schrödinger, recognized new atomic theory. When he went to Stockholm, his mother accompanied him; biographical accounts note his estrangement from his father. Honors came while the intellectual revolution he had helped produce was still becoming intelligible to the wider scientific world.

In the mid-nineteen thirties he met Margit Wigner, the sister of the theoretical physicist Eugene Wigner. They married in nineteen thirty-seven. Margit had two children from an earlier marriage, and the household eventually included two more children. These details counter the picture of Dirac as nothing but a solitary machine for producing symbols. His family and academic obligations continued alongside his work. His stepson Gabriel Andrew Dirac later became a mathematician known for graph theory, a distinct career that should not be confused with Paul's physics.

Dirac went on to investigate magnetic monopoles, quantum electrodynamics, and other difficult questions. His nineteen thirty-one argument for magnetic monopoles is especially revealing. A magnetic monopole has not been experimentally established as a fundamental particle. But Dirac showed that, if even one such monopole existed under the assumptions of his model, electric charge would be constrained to occur in discrete units. The conclusion is conditional. It is intellectually valuable precisely because the condition can be stated honestly. It connects an otherwise puzzling feature of electric charge to the geometry of quantum wave functions.

The idea can be described without the full mathematics. A wave can accumulate a phase, an angle that matters when different paths come together and interfere. If a particle can travel around a region containing a hypothetical magnetic monopole, the phase acquired along different descriptions must ultimately agree. The requirement that the same physical state not depend on a hidden choice of mathematical description forces a whole-number condition. That is where charge quantization enters. It is a remarkable instance of mathematical consistency producing a physical restriction, though not a proof that monopoles exist.

Dirac held the Lucasian chair of mathematics at Cambridge for decades. In the early nineteen seventies he moved to Florida State University, where he continued working. He died in nineteen eighty-four. Over a long career he remained attached to the search for mathematical formulations that could organize physical facts with precision. At times his conviction that beauty was a guide to theory encouraged ideas that the available experiments did not establish. His successes and less successful later proposals should both remain visible.

Return to the two operations that failed to commute. There was a practical lesson in that small piece of algebra. We are accustomed to thinking that an equation describes a world whose objects we already understand. Sometimes, though, the requirement that one equation be compatible with several principles forces us to enlarge the kinds of mathematical objects we use. Once we enlarge them, the theory may predict phenomena we have not yet seen. Dirac's life is a particularly clear case of that process, carried out by a person shaped by family conflict, engineering education, financial constraints, intense intellectual friendships and a lifetime of careful work. The equation and the life are not the same thing. Each makes the other easier to understand.

## Mathematical and historical companion (not spoken)

### Relativistic factorization

For a free electron, the relativistic dispersion relation is

\[
E^2 = c^2\lVert\mathbf p\rVert^2 + m^2c^4.
\]

Dirac sought a Hamiltonian linear in spatial momentum,

\[
H = c\sum_{j=1}^3\alpha_j p_j + \beta mc^2,
\]

with matrices satisfying \(\alpha_i\alpha_j+\alpha_j\alpha_i=2\delta_{ij}I\),
\(\alpha_i\beta+\beta\alpha_i=0\), and \(\beta^2=I\).
Then \(H^2=(c^2|\mathbf p|^2+m^2c^4)I\) for commuting components of free-particle momentum. In three spatial dimensions the standard irreducible complex representation for the full Dirac algebra uses four-by-four matrices.

An illustrative **one-dimensional** factorization uses Pauli matrices \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_z\):
\[
H_{1D}=cp\,\sigma_x+mc^2\sigma_z,\qquad H_{1D}^2=(c^2p^2+m^2c^4)I_2.
\]
This illustration is not the full 3D Dirac equation.

### The historical distinction

Dirac's 1928 equation, evolving hole-theory interpretation, Anderson's 1932 positron observation and subsequent field-theoretic treatment are separate developments. A **negative-energy mathematical branch** is not itself a direct measurement of an antiparticle.
