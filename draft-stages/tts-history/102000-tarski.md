# 102000 - Alfred Tarski: Truth, exile, and a school of logic

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Focus:** Jewish Warsaw under changing political regimes, the Polish school of logic, the challenge of defining truth, a displaced family in World War II, the making of Berkeley's logic program, and the reach of model theory and algebra. **Background:** formal languages, models and geometric examples are explained in speech.

What does it mean to say that a mathematical sentence is true? Not simply that someone believes it, or that a professor has written it on a board. A proof is a sequence of steps licensed by stated rules. Truth asks whether a statement holds in the structure those rules are meant to describe. For centuries philosophers and mathematicians had argued about these words, often with examples from ordinary language. In the twentieth century Alfred Tarski made a daring move. He tried to define truth precisely for certain formal languages, while explaining why the attempt becomes dangerous when a language is allowed to declare its own sentences true or false. This work emerged from a city whose intellectual life was exceptionally creative and violently threatened. Tarski's life was also a story of who gets to become a professor, who must leave home, and what it takes to build a research community after a catastrophe.

He was born in Warsaw in January nineteen oh one, when the city belonged to the Russian Empire. His birth surname was Tajtelbaum, written in several forms in different languages and records. He grew up in a Polish Jewish family in a place where languages, national projects, and political loyalties overlapped. The city's educational culture was not the same as the country in which it now lies. Polish universities and public institutions were constrained by imperial rule. Revolution and the First World War altered those structures, and an independent Polish state emerged in nineteen eighteen. The young Tarski entered higher education amid these changes. It is important not to imagine his intellectual development inside a timeless, insulated Warsaw classroom.

The University of Warsaw assembled scholars of mathematics, philosophy and logic whose work often crossed the boundaries of those disciplines. Tarski studied with figures including Jan Łukasiewicz, Stanisław Leśniewski, Tadeusz Kotarbiński, Wacław Sierpiński and Zygmunt Janiszewski's mathematical successors. Students could move between research on infinity, formal inference, and questions about the meaning of scientific statements. Where another university might have separated philosophy and mathematics into insulated traditions, Warsaw produced arguments about foundations that were simultaneously technical and conceptual. Tarski did not arrive to rescue logic from confusion. He learned within a community already capable of extremely sophisticated work.

He completed his doctorate in nineteen twenty-four under Leśniewski. Around that period, he changed his surname to Tarski and converted to Catholicism. Those facts are documented, but historians should be careful about turning them into confident statements about private motives. Antisemitism was a real barrier in parts of Polish academic life, and changes of name or religious affiliation took place within that environment. Yet the exact mixture of personal belief, professional calculation and social pressure cannot be established by simply looking at two entries in a biographical table. The larger pattern is less ambiguous: a scholar who soon acquired an international reputation still found the road to a secure senior appointment in Poland blocked.

From the mid-nineteen twenties until nineteen thirty-nine Tarski taught mathematics at secondary-school level while holding more limited university teaching positions. He published important work without the salary or status ordinarily associated with a professorship. In nineteen twenty-nine he married Maria Witkowska. They had two children, Ina and Jan. Their family life belonged to the same prewar Warsaw as the extraordinary seminars and mathematical conversations. These relationships will matter when we reach nineteen thirty-nine, because the familiar narrative of a scholar escaping Europe can easily overlook the family that could not escape with him.

In his early research Tarski worked in set theory, geometry and the logical analysis of mathematical expressions. With Stefan Banach he contributed to the celebrated paradoxical decomposition of a sphere. In its most familiar form, the Banach–Tarski theorem says that, in a mathematical setting using the axiom of choice, a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be divided into finitely many sets and reassembled by rigid motions into two balls each the same size as the original. It sounds like the creation of matter from nothing. But the pieces involved are not physical slices with ordinary measurable volume. Their bizarre structure is possible because the mathematical assumptions include sets far beyond objects one could cut out and place on a table. The result is a theorem about geometry and set theory, not a proposed manufacturing method.

The theorem also illustrates the Polish environment's willingness to ask questions that appear absurd until the assumptions are made explicit. If a mathematical intuition says that volume must be preserved, then the precise issue is: for which kinds of sets is volume defined, and which principles are we allowed to use? Much of Tarski's mature work proceeded this way. A comfortable word such as truth, consequence, or definability was not satisfactory merely because mathematicians used it successfully. He wanted to know what it required, and whether the requirements could be expressed without silently assuming the answer.

To begin, imagine a formal language in which we can state facts about the natural numbers. Its sentences are finite arrangements of symbols with rules for forming expressions, not whatever phrases happen to sound plausible in conversation. We also have an intended mathematical structure: the natural numbers with specified operations and relations. The sentence saying that two plus two equals four is true in that structure. A sentence saying that two plus two equals five is false. But defining truth for every sentence, including ones with quantifiers such as for all numbers or there exists a number, requires a systematic account of what the symbols mean in a structure.

The first temptation is to add a predicate inside the language and declare that it applies to precisely the sentences that are true. But now remember the old liar problem, the sentence that says of itself that it is not true. If the language can describe its own sentences with enough flexibility, a universal truth predicate may lead to contradiction or impossibility. Tarski's approach was to distinguish the language being studied from the language in which we study it. The first is called the object language; the second, the metalanguage. We may be able to define truth of sentences in the first by using resources of the second, while not expecting the first language to define its own unrestricted truth predicate.

A simple example helps. We want the definition to entail that the quoted sentence snow is white is true exactly when snow is white. This is not itself the whole definition, and it is not a trick where we explain truth by repeating the word true. The quoted expression is an object we are naming; the unquoted statement describes a condition in the world. For a suitably formalized language, Tarski showed how a recursive construction can connect complicated formulas to their simpler components and their satisfaction by assignments in a mathematical structure. Once such a construction is in place, truth is no longer a free-floating label attached by intuition. It is defined in a way that can be mathematically studied.

His long Polish paper on the concept of truth appeared in nineteen thirty-three. A German version appeared in nineteen thirty-five, and later translations helped the work reach a much larger audience. The historical sequence matters. The book did not appear in fully modern English terminology, instantly adopted by everyone. It was developed in conversation with the Warsaw school, with earlier work on syntax and semantics, and with the wider foundations crisis associated with Hilbert, Gödel, and the Vienna Circle. Gödel's incompleteness results concerned what sufficiently expressive formal systems can prove about arithmetic. Tarski's results about undefinability concerned what those systems can express about their own truth. The results are related, but they answer different questions.

Now consider a second question. Suppose a conclusion follows from a set of assumptions. What makes this relation logical consequence rather than an accidental fact about our chosen interpretation? Tarski argued for describing consequence in terms of all interpretations, or models, in which the assumptions are true. If there is no model where the premises hold but the conclusion fails, then the conclusion follows semantically. This mathematical approach to logical consequence was another powerful contribution. It did not eliminate philosophical debate about which expressions count as logical, or how rich a class of models we should consider, but it made the central relation exact enough to support a technical research program.

By the late nineteen thirties, Tarski had enough international recognition to be invited abroad. Yet that reputation did not give his family a secure future. In August nineteen thirty-nine he traveled to the United States for a meeting associated with the Unity of Science movement. Germany invaded Poland in September; the Soviet Union invaded from the east later that month. Tarski remained in America, separated from Maria, Ina and Jan in Warsaw. His departure saved his life, but it was not an uncomplicated emigration plan carried out with a family and household. It was a sudden rupture. Extended members of his family were killed during the Holocaust. Many colleagues from the Polish research world were imprisoned, driven from their posts, or murdered.

Maria's survival with the children required determination under the occupation, and the family was not reunited until nineteen forty-six. In this account, the word exile should refer to an actual experience rather than serve as atmosphere for a story of scientific success. A person can continue publishing while wondering whether the people closest to him are alive. Universities in the United States offered Tarski temporary appointments; he taught and worked at Harvard, the City College of New York, and the Institute for Advanced Study before finding a permanent institutional home at the University of California in Berkeley. During those years there was no guarantee that the Warsaw school could simply be rebuilt somewhere else.

There is a particular irony in the history of Tarski's appointments. In Poland he produced research influential enough to circulate through the international community, yet remained dependent on school teaching and temporary university work. In California the same mathematician became an institution builder, with graduate students and resources that made long research programs possible. The change cannot be explained by a sudden transformation in his mathematical ability. It reflects the structures through which universities recognize, hire, and support researchers. The lesson is not that one country lacked serious mathematics and another possessed it. The Warsaw school had developed some of the century's strongest work. The institutions that nurtured it had been politically constrained and then devastated by war.

The personal stakes of rebuilding were immense. Some colleagues reached safety in other countries, while others did not survive. For an émigré scholar, an international conference could carry several meanings at once: the chance to discuss a proof, to find news about a friend, or to encounter someone who remembered a city that no longer existed as it once had. The mathematical school Tarski built in Berkeley was not a replica of Warsaw. It used techniques and habits learned there while becoming a new community with its own students, arguments, and institutional responsibilities.

Berkeley appointed him in the early nineteen forties, and he eventually became a professor in its mathematics department. Once Maria and the children joined him, the family could begin a different life. His work there helped turn Berkeley into a center of mathematical logic. He cultivated an environment in which precise proof, algebraic technique, philosophical questioning, and graduate education reinforced one another. Robert Vaught became a major collaborator in model theory. Other students and colleagues developed work in set theory, algebra and foundations that traveled far beyond California. Tarski's impact was not only a result of theorems bearing his name. It was also the creation of a setting where people could learn to make new ones.

What became known as model theory takes the relationship between a formal language and its interpretations as a subject in its own right. Think of a set of axioms that describes a family of structures, rather than specifying one concrete object. We can ask whether the axioms have a model; whether all their models satisfy some property; whether two structures satisfy the same sentences; or whether a theory determines its models up to an appropriate notion of equivalence. The distinction between a statement as a string of symbols and a statement interpreted in a structure was no longer just philosophical housekeeping. It became a machine for mathematical discovery.

Tarski also contributed to the logical theory of algebra and geometry. An especially striking result concerns the first-order theory of real closed fields, which provides a formal setting for many questions about real numbers involving addition, multiplication, equality and inequalities. He developed a decision method: in principle, for statements of the suitable language, there is an effective procedure to determine truth. The existence of a decision method is not the same as the existence of a fast algorithm. General procedures can be computationally expensive. But the result showed that a broad and important piece of geometry could be handled with exact logical methods. In later decades, research on quantifier elimination developed into algorithms and software for reasoning about polynomial conditions.

Imagine a geometrical claim: every real number satisfying some collection of polynomial inequalities also satisfies another. A quantified statement may describe points we cannot picture in advance. Quantifier elimination translates such questions into algebraic conditions that can be systematically checked under suitable assumptions. It can reduce the problem of infinitely many potential real values to a finite symbolic procedure. This is one reason Tarski's history belongs beside the history of computation even though he is usually introduced as a logician. Making a question decidable, and then studying how costly it is to decide, became part of an ongoing conversation among logic, mathematics and computer science.

One can see this in the subject called algebraic logic. Instead of treating logical operations only as lines of symbolic text, mathematicians study algebraic systems that model those operations. Relations can be combined, reversed or projected, and the resulting identities become algebraic laws. Tarski's work with collaborators and students helped develop relation algebras and cylindric algebras, allowing ideas from mathematical logic to interact with algebra and set theory. This was not an afterthought to a single celebrated truth theorem. It was part of his sustained interest in changing representations of a question until its structure became clearer.

The educational consequence was substantial. A student trained in these methods could move between logical syntax, interpretations, geometric structures and algebraic equations. Each translation could reveal something the previous vocabulary had concealed. That breadth also makes Tarski hard to fit inside an introductory label such as philosopher of truth. His philosophy mattered, but so did the technical depth that made his questions fruitful for other mathematical specialists.

There were also important collective and institutional limitations. Being a brilliant organizer did not mean that Tarski treated every colleague in exactly the same way or that his seminar culture felt equally welcoming to everyone. Mathematical institutions had hierarchies of seniority, language, reputation and access. Berkeley's logic group was built through appointments, students' labor, publication opportunities, and the ability to draw people from other countries. His students later remembered an exacting teacher whose demands sharpened their work. Accounts of his personality vary, and they should not be collapsed into a convenient legend about how great mathematicians ought to behave.

For the broader public, the Banach–Tarski paradox may remain the most astonishing association with his name. For philosophers, the semantic conception of truth is likely more important. Mathematicians working in model theory may value the language of structures and definability, while specialists in algebra and computer science encounter Tarski through decision problems and the algebra of relations. These reputations can make him seem like several separate researchers. But a common project connects them: take concepts that are used informally in reasoning, state the conditions under which they make sense, and then investigate their limits and power with the tools of mathematics.

This project also sets a boundary on what mathematics can promise. The existence of a mathematically adequate truth definition for particular formal languages does not yield a universal formula for determining the truth of every scientific claim or every sentence in ordinary speech. The undefinability result points in the opposite direction for sufficiently expressive languages. Similarly, a decision procedure for real algebra does not make all of mathematics decidable. Every theorem has hypotheses. Tarski's achievement was partly to show how much can be accomplished once the hypotheses are explicit, and how misleading it is to forget them after the result becomes famous.

Tarski remained associated with Berkeley for the rest of his life. He received major honors, traveled, lectured and continued participating in a widening international logic community. He died in October nineteen eighty-three. By then the political map of his youth had changed repeatedly and the people of the Warsaw school had been scattered across continents. Some had survived and helped establish new centers; others had been lost. The Berkeley program was therefore both an intellectual successor and something new, shaped by American institutions and by the particular research priorities of students who came after the war.

To close, return to the small example of a true mathematical sentence. It is easy to say that two plus two equals four, but Tarski invites us to ask what kind of sentence we have written, in what structure it is interpreted, and which language allows us to state the rules of interpretation. Those questions once sounded like philosophical complications. They became methods for proving new mathematical results. His personal history shows another kind of structure: a network of teachers, families, universities and borders that can support thought or be violently broken. Tarski contributed to the reconstruction of a scientific community while helping that community see with unprecedented clarity how a formal language can describe a world, and where the description must stop.

## Historical companion (not narrated)

### Chronology

- **1901–1924:** Born in Warsaw under Russian imperial rule; independence of Poland; studied mathematics and philosophy under Warsaw scholars, received doctorate in 1924, changed surname from Tajtelbaum to Tarski in 1923.
- **1924–1939:** Research and publication in logic, set theory and geometry; secondary-school and temporary university teaching; marriage to Maria Witkowska in 1929 and two children; 1933 Polish monograph on formal truth (German version 1935).
- **1939–1946:** United States trip at the outbreak of the war; separated from his family in Poland; US temporary appointments; joined Berkeley in 1942; family reunification in 1946.
- **1940s–1983:** Berkeley research school, algebraic logic, decision procedures, model theory, graduate teaching; died 1983.

### Mathematical companion

**Object language and metalanguage.** Tarski distinguishes a language being studied from a language in which its expressions and interpretations can be described. A *T-sentence* has the form “\(\ulcorner P\urcorner\) is true if and only if \(P\)” with appropriate naming and translation. This is a criterion of material adequacy, not a standalone recursive definition for an arbitrary language.

**Undefinability.** For a sufficiently expressive consistent formal theory of arithmetic, a truth predicate for its own intended sentences cannot be defined in the same way without restrictions. Do not conflate this theorem with Gödel's incompleteness theorem; their proof ideas and philosophical uses overlap without being identical.

**Banach–Tarski.** In three-dimensional Euclidean space, the axiom of choice permits a paradoxical equidecomposition using nonmeasurable sets. It does not license creation of physical matter or ordinary finite-volume pieces.

**Real closed fields.** Quantifier elimination yields decision procedures for first-order statements in the language of ordered rings interpreted over real closed fields. The existence of a finite procedure says nothing by itself about practical time complexity. Tarski's original procedure was not an efficient modern general-purpose solver.

**Historical cautions:** Tarski's surname and religion changed in a society where antisemitism was present, but personal motives are not established by the archival dates alone. He left for an American conference before World War II began; he did not relocate his whole family by choice. Preserve the roles of Banach in the paradoxical decomposition and of earlier Warsaw teachers in the formal logic program. Truth definitions for specified formal languages are not algorithms for settling arbitrary natural-language claims. The 1933 Polish and 1935 German versions of the truth paper should not be collapsed into one publication date.
