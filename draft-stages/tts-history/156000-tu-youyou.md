# 156000 - Tu Youyou: The Fever, the Archive, and Sample 191

**Track:** History - People and ideas. **Background:** No prior pharmacology, chemistry, or medical training. A standalone biographical investigation of Tu Youyou, the Chinese antimalarial research network called Project 523, the discovery of artemisinin, and the continuing challenge of using a powerful medicine responsibly.

On October fourth, nineteen seventy-one, a research group in Beijing recorded a result that must have seemed almost implausibly good. An extract prepared from a plant had prevented the measurable growth of malaria parasites in an experimental mouse model. The test reported one hundred percent inhibition. The sample had a number: one hundred and ninety-one.

That number did not mean that someone had already discovered a medicine that would cure every patient. The animals were not people. A laboratory extract was not a standardized drug. An early assay could be misleading, and this particular laboratory had seen apparent successes fade when the experiments were repeated. But sample one hundred and ninety-one gave a reproducible direction to a search that had often looked like a collection of failures. It was a foothold.

The research leader was Tu Youyou, a forty-year-old pharmacologist who had spent her career in a Chinese institute devoted to medicinal plants. The result had required her to look backward into old medical books and forward into experimental chemistry. It also required colleagues who identified plants, prepared extracts, tested them in animals, tried them in patients, isolated compounds, and established structures. Many were working inside a national research program whose existence was not public knowledge. When Tu's name became internationally famous decades later, the achievement could be described in a single sentence. Its actual history takes longer to tell.

To understand why that laboratory result mattered, begin with the disease. Malaria is not a bacterium or a virus. It is an infection by microscopic parasites of the genus Plasmodium. Certain Anopheles mosquitoes transmit the parasites when they feed. A parasite first establishes itself in the liver, then releases forms that invade red blood cells. In repeated cycles, parasites multiply, blood cells rupture, and infected people may experience bouts of fever, chills, exhaustion, and anemia. Some infections become life-threatening very quickly. A mosquito can take up sexual forms of the parasite from an infected person and transmit their descendants to someone else. Malaria is a disease of human bodies, mosquito ecology, and the landscapes in which people and insects meet.

People had treated malarial fevers for centuries without knowing the full parasite life cycle. Quinine from cinchona bark eventually became an important drug. In the twentieth century, synthetic antimalarials, especially chloroquine, supplied new means of controlling infection. But parasites are evolving populations. A drug kills susceptible organisms more efficiently than resistant ones; the survivors can become common when the same medicine is used repeatedly. By the nineteen sixties, resistance to chloroquine was a growing problem, particularly in parts of Southeast Asia. The old assumption that treatment could be made routine was coming apart. Soldiers were affected during the Vietnam War, and so were civilians whose suffering rarely entered the military histories.

The Chinese government began a confidential national antimalarial effort on May twenty-third, nineteen sixty-seven. Its name, Project Five Twenty-Three, referred to that date. The request emerged from wartime urgency and the search for medicines effective against resistant parasites. The program drew scientists from military units, universities, pharmaceutical laboratories, botanical institutions, and centers of traditional medicine. The Cultural Revolution made the setting especially difficult. Research institutions were disrupted, many experienced scholars were sidelined, and the ordinary channels of publishing and international exchange were strained or closed. Yet a large scientific network continued to work. This contrast matters: a secret project can simultaneously coordinate impressive resources and damage the open criticism and attribution on which research depends.

Tu Youyou was not born into that history. She was born on December thirtieth, nineteen thirty, in Ningbo, a port city in Zhejiang province on China's eastern coast. She grew up with four brothers in a family that valued education. As an adolescent, she had tuberculosis, and illness interrupted her schooling. In later accounts she connected that experience to her interest in medicine. It is an understandable connection, although we should not turn a childhood illness into a complete explanation for a scientific career. She also grew up in a country going through war, revolution, and enormous changes in its educational institutions. A young woman who wished to study pharmacy was entering a world in which both modern laboratory medicine and traditional practice would become matters of national interest.

From nineteen fifty-one to nineteen fifty-five, she studied pharmaceutical science at Beijing Medical College, in the institutional lineage of today's Peking University health sciences programs. She did not train as a clinical physician and later receive a pharmacology appointment by accident. The study of medicines was her professional route from the beginning. After graduating she joined the Institute of Chinese Materia Medica in the Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Beijing. She remained associated with that research community for decades.

The name of the institution can make the work sound purely historical, as though the research consisted of reciting ancient prescriptions. The laboratory reality was different. Studying medicinal plants involves botanical identification, extraction, separation, assays of biological activity, the chemistry of individual molecules, and careful attention to dose and adverse effects. A plant is a chemically crowded organism. Its leaves, stems, roots, and flowers may differ; its chemical contents may change with season, growing conditions, and handling. Calling something a traditional remedy tells us where to search. It does not yet tell us what will work or how safely it can be given.

Tu also received additional training in Chinese medical theory and practice between nineteen fifty-nine and nineteen sixty-two. The course was designed for researchers already trained in modern medicine or pharmaceutical science. She later described how this study broadened the resources available to her. It made her one of the people able to move between a body of classical Chinese texts and the practical questions posed by modern experimental pharmacology. That combination would become crucial, but only after considerable frustration.

In nineteen sixty-nine, representatives of Project Five Twenty-Three approached her institute. Tu was assigned to organize and lead a group searching traditional medical knowledge for possible antimalarials. She was not chosen as a celebrity; she was a researcher with the skills the project needed. She had experience with plants and extracts, understood the literature, and could coordinate chemical and biological experiments. The appointment brought urgency and responsibility, but also a personal cost.

Tu had two small daughters. According to her own later account, one was four years old and the other only one when she accepted the assignment. Her husband was away on an institutional training program. To free time for fieldwork and research, she left her younger daughter with relatives in Ningbo and arranged for her older daughter to live away from home under the care of a teacher's household. The separation lasted for years. Tu recalled that, when she visited after a long absence, her younger daughter did not recognize her. Another daughter hid behind her caregiver when Tu returned to collect her. These are details from Tu's autobiographical recollection, and they ought not be turned into sentimental proof that the discovery required a particular family arrangement. They show what participation in this kind of urgent, state-directed scientific work could demand of a household.

The research problem looked enormous. A modern screening laboratory can evaluate many substances under standardized conditions, but Project Five Twenty-Three was working with natural materials and reports accumulated across centuries. Traditional Chinese medical texts describe patterns of fever and other symptoms in categories that do not map cleanly onto modern parasitology. A remedy recorded for periodic fever might act against malaria parasites; it might reduce inflammation or discomfort without killing the parasite; or it might do neither. The surviving text might use a plant name that covered more than one species. The preparation might be crucial. The dose might be missing.

Tu's group began with a survey rather than with a single celebrated herb. In her account they searched more than two thousand recipes and compiled a working collection of six hundred and forty for further investigation. They consulted medical literature and practitioners. The compilation circulated to other teams in the national program. This is one of the first ways the story differs from a solitary-discovery legend: before there was a promising numbered sample, there was a shared reference resource built from many people's knowledge.

Experiments initially gave disappointing results. Among the plants tested was qinghao, a name associated with plants in the Artemisia group. Reports of extracts inhibiting parasites were inconsistent. A result that looked promising on one attempt did not reliably return on the next. It is easy, looking backward from a Nobel Prize, to treat every failed trial as a step that obviously led toward success. At the time, contradictory assays also could have meant that the plant was worthless as an antimalarial source. A responsible investigator had to be willing to find that out.

Tu went back to the books. In a work attributed to the fourth-century physician and writer Ge Hong, a handbook of prescriptions for emergencies, she found an instruction for qinghao that differed from the way many medicinal herbs were prepared. It described soaking the plant in water and expressing the liquid, rather than boiling it for a prolonged time. What caught her attention was not a secret ancient formula for artemisinin. No one in the fourth century had identified the modern compound or established its effects in controlled trials. What the text supplied was an experimental question: might heat be reducing the activity of the preparation?

There is an important distinction between noticing that an old recipe was cold and proving that a particular active molecule was heat-sensitive. The first was a historical clue. The second required tests. Tu's team changed the extraction conditions, separated plant materials and chemical fractions, and compared solvents, including low-boiling organic solvents. The work did not consist of copying a traditional preparation and administering it unchanged. It involved reconstructing a workable laboratory procedure, checking whether activity remained, and locating the part of a complex mixture that produced it.

Think about extracting caffeine from tea leaves, although the chemistry here was more demanding. If you steep leaves in water, you dissolve a mixture of substances. Change the temperature, and you change how rapidly different materials move from plant tissue into the solvent. Change the solvent, and you change which molecules dissolve most readily. Separate an extract into chemical fractions, and you can ask whether one fraction carries the biological activity while another carries unwanted effects. An extraction method is a measurement tool as much as a method of collection: it defines what reaches the assay.

With the revised protocol, the Beijing group tested a new extract made from qinghao. On October fourth, nineteen seventy-one, sample one hundred and ninety-one produced complete inhibition of parasite growth in the reported mouse assay. Subsequent work on the neutral fraction also yielded strong results in infected monkeys. This was the breakthrough, but it was not a purified drug, and it did not establish safety or cure in humans. It established something enormously useful: an experiment worth repeating, refining, and sharing.

By March nineteen seventy-two, Tu presented the findings at a Project Five Twenty-Three meeting in Nanjing. Other groups could follow the trail. The results did not end a search; they turned a diffuse national effort toward a concrete line of investigation. Now people had to determine which botanical material was reliable, which chemical fraction worked, how to produce enough for further tests, and whether patients could actually benefit.

One of the reasons this stage is scientifically interesting is that the familiar phrase 'active ingredient' conceals a whole chain of questions. An extract may be effective because of one molecule, several molecules acting together, or a molecule altered during processing. An organic solvent can introduce residues. Plant material collected in the wrong season can have a different chemical profile. A substance that inhibits parasites in a mouse may also injure the mouse. A substance that works when swallowed in a laboratory mixture may not dissolve when compressed into a tablet. Chemistry, biological testing, and pharmaceutical formulation cannot be skipped simply because a source looks promising.

The program moved toward clinical testing in nineteen seventy-two. In Hainan, between August and October, a small group of twenty-one malaria patients received qinghao extract under several dosing arrangements. Tu's later account gives the breakdown: infections with the falciparum species, the vivax species, and one mixed infection. The investigators tracked fever and parasites visible in blood samples. She reported that all twenty-one recovered from the fever and that parasites were no longer detected after treatment. Additional patients were treated at a Beijing military hospital. These were encouraging early clinical observations, not evidence that a large, controlled modern trial had already established a universally safe cure. They made the next stage of research both possible and urgent.

While clinical work continued, chemists worked to isolate the responsible compound from the mixture. Separation is not simply filtering out a solid. If several molecules are dissolved in an extract, researchers can exploit differences in how strongly they bind to a material, how readily they dissolve in a chosen liquid, or how they crystallize. In chromatography, for instance, a complex mixture is passed through a medium that holds different components with different strengths. Fractions emerge separately. Each can be tested. If one fraction inhibits parasites, the investigator concentrates on that fraction, purifies it again, and asks whether the biological effect follows the same material.

In November nineteen seventy-two, Tu's group reported obtaining crystals of a substance with antimalarial activity. Other Project Five Twenty-Three laboratories also isolated active crystalline material during this period. It became known in Chinese as qinghaosu and internationally as artemisinin. A plant name had now become the name of a molecule. That is a major conceptual transition. Scientists can weigh it, characterize it, test it at defined concentrations, change its structure, and eventually manufacture reproducible medicines. They no longer have to depend solely on a variable preparation of the entire plant.


The path from an active extract to a reproducible medicine was neither straight nor especially comfortable. During the Cultural Revolution, pharmaceutical workshops could be inaccessible or closed. Tu recalled that her group worked with improvised vats when they needed to prepare larger quantities of extract. They spent long hours with organic solvents in spaces without adequate ventilation. Members of the team, including Tu, developed symptoms from exposure. We should not romanticize laboratory hazards as proof of dedication. They are part of the material history of a program being pursued urgently with inadequate equipment.

The need to move into human testing presented a difficult choice. Animal toxicity results were not entirely consistent; the malaria season was passing, and waiting might mean delaying field observations for another year. In July nineteen seventy-two, Tu and two team members voluntarily took the extract under hospital supervision. She later described a week of monitoring without observed adverse effects, followed by a dose-escalation study involving five more volunteers. Such self-experimentation has a long place in medical history, sometimes ending badly. What it meant in this case is that a small group took risks to obtain some preliminary safety information. It did not substitute for rigorous evaluation of dose, long-term effects, or safety across different patient groups.

That episode also has something to tell us about the relationship between a laboratory and the society surrounding it. In one frame, the project had national priority and access to a large research network. In another, a group struggled to obtain facilities that a modern clinical research program would regard as basic. Neither description cancels the other. An institute can mobilize trained people and still lack reliable equipment. A government can fund a search and simultaneously disrupt scientific life. The work of Project Five Twenty-Three unfolded within those contradictions.

The next chemical problem was to determine what artemisinin actually was. A purified substance is not automatically understood. It may be possible to measure its mass and infer which elements it contains before anyone knows how those atoms connect. Artemisinin had a molecular composition of fifteen carbon atoms, twenty-two hydrogen atoms, and five oxygen atoms. It did not contain the nitrogen associated with some existing antimalarial drug families. The molecule belonged to a class of natural products called sesquiterpene lactones, and it contained a chemically distinctive peroxide bridge, an oxygen-oxygen bond within its ring structure.

The full structural determination required specialists from several Chinese institutions. Tu's institute collaborated with the Institute of Materia Medica, with organic chemists in Shanghai, and with physicists and crystallographers at the Chinese Academy of Sciences. X-ray crystallography helped establish the arrangement of the atoms in three dimensions. By the mid-nineteen seventies, investigators could describe the chemical architecture of a compound that had first emerged as an effect in a biological assay. That knowledge mattered because a structure can be checked, synthesized, and modified. The drug no longer had to be treated as an anonymous chemical fraction that happened to clear parasites.

The difference between a molecule and a medicine became painfully concrete in nineteen seventy-three. Researchers prepared artemisinin tablets for a clinical test, but the results disappointed them. The tablets were so hard that they did not dissolve as needed. Here was an instructive failure: a molecule could be highly active in an assay and ineffective in a particular pharmaceutical form. A patient does not benefit from a drug locked inside a tablet that passes through the body without releasing a useful dose. The team examined the formulation problem and tried capsules. A limited number of patients were treated near the end of that malaria season, with promising results. This step was not an incidental manufacturing detail. It was a reminder that pharmacology includes the journey from a chemical structure to a concentration at the correct tissue at the correct time.

Around the same period, Tu investigated chemically modified forms of artemisinin. One product was dihydroartemisinin, derived by reducing part of the original molecule. This derivative was highly active against malaria parasites and became important in the family of medicines that followed. Other researchers developed compounds including artesunate and artemether, adapted for different formulations and clinical settings. There is no single timeless 'artemisinin pill.' There is a family of related compounds, each with particular chemical properties, methods of administration, and practical strengths.

How does a molecule with a peroxide bridge interfere with a malaria parasite? The answer remains more complex than the standard story of one drug binding to one protein and blocking it. Inside red blood cells, malaria parasites consume hemoglobin. This brings them into contact with iron-containing heme and with biochemical systems that must manage the consequences. Evidence indicates that artemisinin's peroxide bond is crucial to its activity, and chemical activation involving iron and heme can generate reactive species that damage parasite components. But the full network of targets and the importance of different mechanisms under different conditions have been debated and investigated for decades. The careful description is not that researchers discovered one simple lock and one simple key. It is that the molecule disrupts vulnerable parasite biology rapidly, and its unusual chemical structure is central to that effect.

This is a good place to distinguish two kinds of scientific success. One is explanatory: can we give a precise molecular mechanism? The other is therapeutic: does a well-defined treatment, used appropriately, clear infections and improve outcomes? Mechanistic understanding can improve a medicine, but an incomplete mechanism does not invalidate reliable clinical evidence. The artemisinin discovery advanced through animal work, preliminary human observations, chemistry, further clinical studies, and public-health deployment. Each answered a different question. None could legitimately answer every other one.

A second set of problems was botanical. The Chinese term qinghao had not always denoted one species with modern taxonomic precision. Researchers needed to identify the plant source that reliably contained artemisinin, Artemisia annua, often called sweet wormwood. They also had to discover which parts of the plant were richest in the compound and when those parts should be harvested. Imagine two laboratories following what they believe is the same recipe, but one uses a different Artemisia species and the other collects its leaves at a different stage of growth. Their results need not agree. Standardizing the plant was as necessary as standardizing the extraction solvent.

Now consider an important limit of the ancient-text episode. Ge Hong's instruction was an intellectual clue, not a certification that drinking a simple infusion of sweet wormwood is equivalent to receiving a modern antimalarial. The traditional name covers variable plant material. The quantity of active compound in an infusion cannot be assumed. Purification, dosing, formulation, and clinical testing are precisely what the subsequent decades of research supplied. The scientific achievement was not proving that every old remedy was valid, or that inherited knowledge and laboratory methods were enemies. It was finding a promising question in a historical source and subjecting it to increasingly demanding experimental tests.

The discovery also exposes the difficulty of assigning credit to large, partly secret projects. For decades, many reports circulated within Project Five Twenty-Three before they circulated in international scientific journals. Results moved among laboratories at national meetings. Papers sometimes appeared under collective names. In nineteen seventy-seven, an early publication on qinghaosu appeared under the name of a cooperative research group rather than a conventional list of individual authors. Foreign researchers encountering the chemistry later had to reconstruct who had done which work, sometimes using reports and testimony that had been unavailable at the time.

The names of some of these colleagues are recoverable, and they change the picture. The historical reconstruction by Xin-zhuan Su and Louis Miller describes Zeyuan Luo at the Yunnan Institute of Drug Research and Zhangxing Wei at the Shandong Institute of Chinese Traditional Medicine as leaders of teams that obtained highly active artemisinin crystals using information shared through the national program. Guoqiao Li at the Guangzhou University of Chinese Traditional Medicine undertook influential clinical investigations, including comparisons that helped establish the need for combination therapy. These are not substitute heroes in a different version of the story. They are part of the chain by which an effective laboratory extract became a treatment.

The attribution problem is not settled simply by declaring that everybody contributed equally. Tu's leadership in the traditional-text search, the changed extraction method, and the early active extract is a specific achievement supported by her research record and by historical investigation. The clinical research and drug's subsequent development also depended on other teams. Researchers elsewhere in China investigated efficacy against resistant malaria, built formulations, improved derivatives, and made the medicine more practical. The scientists who identified molecular structure were doing another kind of indispensable work. Credit ought to be specific enough that the contributions remain visible.

When international investigators learned of artemisinin, they were encountering a discovery developed in a research system quite different from the one in which they were accustomed to reading papers. Some information had been restricted because of war and state secrecy; some had circulated only in Chinese. A team could know a result well through repeated internal meetings while outside researchers did not even know where to begin looking for it. The first substantial English-language accounts arrived years after the original laboratory work. In nineteen eighty-one, Tu was selected to describe the Project Five Twenty-Three work to a visiting group associated with the World Health Organization. More researchers could now ask how the compound had been found, what studies supported its use, and how their own work might contribute.

An important question follows. If artemisinin was so powerful, why was it not simply given by itself whenever someone had a fever? Malaria treatment is a race against a rapidly multiplying parasite population. Artemisinin derivatives act quickly, reducing the parasite burden in the blood by orders of magnitude over the early days of treatment. But the drugs are eliminated relatively quickly from the body. Some parasites can remain after the artemisinin component has done its work. Using the compound alone can expose the parasites to a period of weakening drug pressure and increase the chances that a surviving population returns or evolves resistance.

For this reason, modern therapy generally combines an artemisinin derivative with a partner medicine that persists longer and clears the remaining parasites. These are called artemisinin-based combination therapies. There are different recommended combinations for different settings, and proper diagnosis and dosing remain necessary. It is a practical application of evolutionary thinking: if a pathogen can find ways around one attack, use independent pressures where possible, rather than relying on a single vulnerable defense. Combination therapy does not abolish resistance, but it can make successful treatment more robust and help preserve useful drugs.

The success of artemisinin also belongs to the history of manufacturing and health systems. A molecule found in a plant must be obtained in sufficient quantities. Growers must produce reliable raw material; chemists must extract or manufacture active ingredients; quality-control laboratories must check what goes into the medicines; governments and health organizations must procure them; clinics must diagnose malaria and provide complete courses. None of this is glamorous compared with sample one hundred and ninety-one. Without it, however, the discovery would remain confined to experimental records. Later advances in plant cultivation, chemical processing, and partially synthetic production helped support a worldwide demand for artemisinin derivatives.

The earliest clinical work occurred in China and in places affected by war and resistant malaria. Over time, the greatest routine demand for artemisinin-based therapies came from malaria-endemic regions elsewhere, notably across much of sub-Saharan Africa. The disease's geography makes a simple heroic ending impossible. A treatment can prevent immense suffering without removing the mosquitoes, reducing poverty, creating reliable public-health infrastructure, or eliminating every parasite reservoir. Drugs are part of control and treatment alongside mosquito protection, diagnosis, surveillance, and other medical interventions.

By the time Tu received international recognition, decades had passed since the first effective extract. The Lasker-DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award honored her in twenty eleven. Four years later, she received half of the twenty fifteen Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning a new malaria therapy. The other half of that year's prize was divided between William Campbell and Satoshi Omura for work on treatments for roundworm infections. These were separate discoveries recognized in the same year, not a single collaboration in which all three laureates discovered artemisinin.

The ceremony put a scientist from Ningbo on a world stage after a career that had mostly unfolded within one Chinese research institution. It also created a familiar temptation to recast the entire program as a competition between an overlooked individual and an unappreciative establishment. Tu's career certainly complicates conventional stories about academic prestige. She did not have a doctorate or a record of study abroad, and the public spotlight reached her late. Yet institutions in China had recognized the work over the preceding decades, and the Project Five Twenty-Three network had been essential to it. Scientific recognition moves at different speeds in different communities. A Nobel Prize concentrates public attention on one name; it cannot contain the full institutional history.

Tu's own account of the prize repeatedly returned to the wider collective. She described the discovery as the result of work by Chinese scientists and stressed the value of combining traditions of observation with modern research methods. That view was not merely diplomatic courtesy. It was consistent with the record of a project in which a signal found by one group could be pursued by others equipped to do different tasks. At the same time, acknowledging the collective should not make Tu disappear. It was her particular chain of decisions, from consulting Ge Hong's handbook to changing the extraction procedure and testing the neutral fraction, that turned an old clue into a reproducible experimental lead.

There is still a harder, less celebratory question about what happens when a miracle drug becomes ordinary. A medicine used at scale changes the selective environment of the organisms it treats. Parasites do not remember that a discovery saved lives. They reproduce, vary, and sometimes survive what killed their ancestors. Malaria parasites showing partial resistance to artemisinin derivatives have been identified in parts of Southeast Asia and Africa. In current medical usage, partial resistance usually means slower clearance of parasites from the blood, not that the entire drug family has suddenly stopped working. Where partner medicines remain effective, combination therapy can still cure most infections. Where resistance to partner drugs develops as well, treatment becomes much more difficult. The World Health Organization has warned that this is a serious threat requiring careful surveillance and treatment policy.

This last development does not reverse Tu's achievement. It shows why effective medicines have histories after their discovery. Research must continue on resistance, alternative partners, new compounds, and the conditions that determine whether treatment reaches patients. The relevant scientific community is global, and the patients most affected often live far from the universities and ceremonies that commemorate past discoveries. One of the most responsible ways to honor a drug's origin is to keep improving its use instead of treating it as a finished monument.

Return once more to the numbered sample in Beijing. Its meaning changed as the evidence accumulated. At first it was one measurement in a small-animal experiment, encouraging but provisional. Then it became a guide for purification and a reason to carry out careful clinical observations. It led to a molecule, and that molecule led to a family of therapies distributed around the world. Different people, institutions, and disciplines moved the project forward at every stage. Tu's distinctive contribution was to recognize that a failed extraction might conceal a successful biological source, and to devise a better experiment by bringing a historical text into conversation with chemistry.

There is a certain intellectual courage in returning to a question after the assays have disagreed. It is not the confidence that the old book must be right. It is the willingness to ask whether the present experiment has been designed correctly. An instruction about soaking rather than boiling did not explain malaria, and it did not contain the structure of artemisinin. But it suggested something that the researchers could test. The result, if it was going to matter, had to survive the laboratory, the field clinic, the chemical structure determination, and eventually the experience of people far from the original project.

The story of Tu Youyou is therefore not a fable about one culture defeating another, or about ancient wisdom making modern science unnecessary. It is a biography of a scientist working across several kinds of evidence, in a time of war and political upheaval, at considerable personal and institutional cost. It is also the story of colleagues whose contributions became entangled with secrecy and collective authorship, and of an effective medicine that remains under evolutionary pressure. The lesson is not that every numbered sample has a discovery hidden inside it. It is that a promising result only becomes knowledge when people keep asking what, exactly, has been measured, and what would have to be true for that result to help someone live.

## Nonspoken companion: scientific and historical notes

### The discovery in distinct stages

1. **Screening and hypothesis.** Tu's group searched classical and later prescriptions, prepared a selection of 640 from more than 2,000 investigated prescriptions, and later reconsidered extraction of qinghao after poor reproducibility. Ge Hong's instruction was evidence of a historical preparation method, not evidence of a characterized compound or effective modern dose.
2. **1971 experimental lead.** On 4 October 1971, low-temperature ether-derived sample 191 gave complete suppression in the reported *rodent* malaria assay. In the Nobel lecture Tu describes oral dosing in mice at 1 g/kg for three days; do not convert this assay into a human clinical cure rate. Confirmatory monkey tests followed in December 1971–January 1972.
3. **1972 early clinical work.** Tu's hospital-supervised self-testing with colleagues in July preceded a small Hainan series of 21 patients (9 falciparum, 11 vivax, 1 mixed) and another 9 patients in Beijing. These were preliminary, nonrandomized evaluations, not modern proof of universal efficacy or safety.
4. **Molecule and modification.** Artemisinin (qinghaosu) was isolated from *Artemisia annua* as an antimalarial substance in 1972, with collaborative molecular-structure elucidation in the 1970s. Dihydroartemisinin work followed in 1973. Further derivatives and formulation advances involve many research groups.
5. **International clinical deployment.** Combination treatments, drug supply, clinical monitoring, and surveillance of resistance are later achievements. Today's ACTs are not equivalent to an uncontrolled preparation of the entire plant.

### Mechanism and formulation

Artemisinin is a sesquiterpene lactone containing a peroxide bridge and empirical formula **C15H22O5**. Its endoperoxide is important for activity. Models involving heme/iron-initiated formation of reactive intermediates and damage to multiple parasite components have substantial experimental support; a fully exclusive target or mechanism is not established by the discovery narrative. In blood-stage infections, ACTs pair a rapidly acting derivative with a longer-acting partner drug to clear parasites remaining after the early treatment phase. Compare WHO's 2025 partial-resistance definition (delayed parasite clearance, which is not in itself equivalent to ACT treatment failure).

**Drug-delivery distinction:** even a potent drug can fail if its tablet does not disintegrate. The 1973 tablet/capsule episode concerns bioavailability and formulation, not loss of antimalarial activity in the purified compound.

### Historiography and attribution

- Tu's own 2015 Nobel autobiographical account is detailed but retrospective and should be read alongside the 2011 independent documentary reconstruction by X. Su and L. H. Miller, the 2011 *Journal of Clinical Investigation* profile, and histories of collective Project 523 work.
- The 1977 report appeared under a collaborative-group byline. Independent histories identify additional teams in Yunnan and Shandong as having obtained active crystals early in the wider project; the precise sequence is not fully resolved by Tu's retrospective autobiography. Secrecy, the Cultural Revolution, and limited international translation complicate historical priority reconstruction. Credit the 1971 extraction breakthrough specifically to Tu and her research group; credit subsequent structural determination, clinical trials, derivatives, and manufacturing to broader networks rather than silently assigning all stages to one scientist.
- Tu's daughters' reactions are taken from her Nobel autobiography, not independently documented private testimony. They are presented as her recollection.
- Avoid the claim that number 191 literally means 190 consecutive failures of an otherwise uniform experiment. It is a sample identifier; a simple count of failures is not established by the record.
- Historical *qinghao* cannot be identified uncritically with a uniform modern *A. annua* preparation. Plant taxonomy, part, harvest date, dose, and processing matter.
- The project used human self-testing and small uncontrolled clinical trials. Reporting those events is not an endorsement of their replication under present-day clinical-research ethics.
- Annual malaria totals, treatment-course figures, and resistance locations are time-sensitive; keep numerical statistics in dated source notes rather than treating them as timeless biographical claims.
