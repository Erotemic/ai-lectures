# The Sun Also Rises: Bullfighting, Craft, and the Ethics of Watching

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

One of the most sustained forms of attention in The Sun Also Rises is Jake Barnes's attention to bullfighting. The scenes are easy to treat as a symbol: the bullring is danger, masculinity, grace, or death. Each association has some support. But Hemingway gives the sport enough practical texture that interpretation should begin with what the participants actually do and with the difference between performing a craft and merely appearing to perform it.

Jake knows which movements expose a matador to danger and which simulate danger for a crowd. He respects the coordination of timing, bodily control, and style. Pedro Romero becomes important because his work is not just flamboyant. In Jake's account, Romero maintains line and discipline while staying close to the animal. To describe a practice well, Jake must be able to notice small differences that an inexperienced visitor would miss. His technical knowledge supplies a model of artistic criticism: admiration is earned through exact attention, not through loud applause.

Montoya, the hotelkeeper, shares this valuation. Their relationship is based on the serious discussion of the sport, and the approval Jake receives matters to him. Montoya wants Romero protected from fashionable visitors who may distract him or turn him into a celebrity before his skill can mature. When Jake helps Brett meet the young matador, he endangers that trust. The episode matters precisely because Jake knows better. The person who distinguishes genuine craft from empty show is not thereby immune to sacrificing craft for private desire.

Bullfighting also intensifies the novel's contrast between the tourists and the people who work in a local tradition. The fiesta brings outsiders seeking excitement. Some understand little of the dangers or history. Others, like Jake, develop specialized knowledge. Yet knowledge alone does not erase the asymmetry between an American visitor and those for whom the festival is a lived social practice. Jake has his own purposes in Spain. He is drawn to a world he regards as possessing clearer standards than the unsettled relationships in Paris.

There is a problem with romanticizing these standards. Bullfighting involves pain and the deliberate killing of animals; the festival also includes danger to humans. The novel acknowledges injuries and death, while Jake's aesthetic judgments place the matador's performance at the center. A modern reader may question whether the beauty of technique can justify the practice. That is an ethical inquiry distinct from explaining what the art means to Jake and Montoya. Serious criticism can hold both lines of thought without pretending that one cancels the other.

The violence among Jake's friends creates a second comparison. Cohn is a trained boxer, and when he attacks Jake, Mike, and Romero, his physical skill becomes domination. Romero is beaten but later returns to the bullring and performs with discipline. Hemingway sets unregulated interpersonal violence beside a stylized form of danger. But the comparison should not become an automatic defense of the ring. It reveals Jake's values: he respects the skill governed by practiced standards and distrusts violence motivated by wounded vanity. Those preferences are legible even if a reader rejects the sport itself.

Notice too how Brett's gaze changes the scene. She admires Romero's performance and is attracted to him. Her excitement brings him into the emotional economy of the expatriate group. Jake's expertise gives him the power to introduce them. He cannot stay simply a connoisseur; he becomes an actor in the very drama he believes he can analyze from a distance. This is a recurrent pattern in the novel. The better Jake understands a practice, the more painful it is when he fails its standards.

For a close-reading exercise, identify one paragraph in which Jake evaluates a bullfighter's movements. List the observable actions before you list the words of praise or contempt. Ask which technical claims a reader could verify from a description and which depend on Jake's authority. Then compare that paragraph to his judgment of Cohn or Brett. Are the grounds of evaluation equally explicit? Where does direct physical knowledge give way to private interpretation?

One can also compare the fishing interval with the bullring. Both activities have specialized vocabularies and rituals, and both let Jake inhabit a world where timing and skill matter. But their social pressures differ. Fishing with Bill offers companionship and relative quiet. Bullfighting in Pamplona offers public admiration, competition, and proximity to the powerful desires of spectators. The second setting exposes Jake to a test his private refuge did not impose.

Hemingway's bullfighting chapters teach more than a set of metaphors. They show how a narrator can make expertise narratively persuasive and then test whether that expertise extends to ethical conduct. The great irony is that Jake's judgment about performance may be far more dependable than his judgment about what he owes the people around him.
