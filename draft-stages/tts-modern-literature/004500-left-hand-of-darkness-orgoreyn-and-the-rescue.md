# The Left Hand of Darkness — The Story, Part 2: Orgoreyn and the Rescue

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Orgoreyn initially seems like the answer to Genly Ai's diplomatic difficulties. Its officials receive him and listen to the idea of joining the Ekumen. Where King Argaven offered suspicion and volatile royal pride, the Orgota government presents committees and administrators who can discuss policy in an orderly fashion. Genly visits the capital and speaks with influential men. He begins to believe that the political opening he wanted has finally appeared.

But the government is not one thing. Different officials compete for advantage, and a secret security apparatus operates behind the apparent openness of its institutions. The Orgota treat their citizens through an elaborate system of registration and authority. Political enemies can disappear into the machinery of the state. Genly notices some of these features, but he has traveled a long way and wants to believe that negotiations are succeeding.

Estraven is in Orgoreyn too. Exiled from Karhide, he has tried to survive by taking work and forming uncertain alliances. He hears what the officials say about Genly and sees how their hospitality might be converted into a trap. Estraven warns the envoy that the people courting him are not necessarily the people who can protect him. Genly finds the warnings difficult to trust, partly because the circumstances make Estraven appear to be playing another political game. The distance between the two men continues, though Estraven has far more immediate knowledge of what is happening.

In Orgoreyn the official kindness has a particular rhythm. A visitor may be invited to a generous meal, congratulated on his status, and assured that a decision is nearly at hand, while security officers prepare quite a different future for him. Genly is not simply failing to persuade a hostile king. He is being led toward greater dependence on people who can withdraw their protection instantly. Estraven understands the danger because exile has forced him to live among citizens whose movements and words are closely watched. His warnings to Genly become more urgent as the room for escape narrows.

The Orgota authorities eventually arrest Genly. Their public hospitality has not made him free. He is transported to a remote labor camp, one of the places where inconvenient people are sent out of sight. There he is questioned, drugged, and subjected to conditions that make recovery difficult. Drugs interfere with the Gethenian sexual cycle and with Genly's own body. Prisoners are moved and worked under exhausting control. Genly's status as an envoy offers him no protection once the government has decided it prefers his disappearance to an honest negotiation.

Estraven learns where Genly has been taken. He might protect himself by remaining invisible, or by accepting that the envoy has been lost. Instead he undertakes a rescue. He uses contacts, careful planning, and dangerous measures to get Genly out of the camp. By the time they escape, Genly is severely weakened. Estraven must carry much of the practical burden of their flight, finding food, shelter, and a route that neither country's government will expect them to take.

The ordinary border crossings are too dangerous. Karhide's authorities still regard Estraven as an exile who may be killed if he returns. Orgoreyn is searching for its escaped prisoner. Between the nations lies an immense frozen region called the Gobrin Ice. Crossing it is possible but terrifying. It means traveling for weeks with a loaded sled over snow, crevasses, storms, and terrain where an error may be fatal. Estraven decides that the ice is their best chance. Genly will need to recover enough to share the work.

They prepare what they can, collecting supplies and calculating how long the journey might take. Genly has arrived on Winter expecting to speak for worlds he knows and a wider civilization he represents. Now he is dependent on one person, an exile whom he has distrusted almost from the moment they met. Estraven does not ask for a formal confession. He builds the possibility of survival one practical choice at a time. Genly begins to see that the person he had treated as a political puzzle is the person putting his own life in danger to save him.

The next part follows their journey over the glacier. Much of it is made of walking, hauling, sleeping, waking, and doing all of those things again. There is little space for the drama of court politics. Their lives depend on weather, food, stamina, and whether they can learn to speak honestly to one another before the ice takes away their remaining chances.
