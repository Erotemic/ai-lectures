# Kindred — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Dana Franklin is a Black woman living in California in 1976. She is a writer, married to Kevin, a white writer, and the couple are making a home together. On her twenty-sixth birthday Dana suddenly feels dizzy and disappears from the room. When she can see again she is standing beside a river in Maryland in the early nineteenth century. A white child is drowning. She pulls him from the water and revives him. His father arrives with a gun and points it at Dana. She feels the same terrible dizziness and is back in California, with Kevin staring at her in disbelief.

There is no device for Dana to operate, no explanation of how she traveled, and no safe means of choosing the year or destination. She discovers that she is being pulled toward danger involving the boy, whose name is Rufus Weylin. Her own fear for her life seems capable of sending her back. For Rufus, years may pass between her appearances. For Dana, sometimes almost no time passes at all. She starts to understand that she may be spending fragments of her life inside a long life she cannot control.

The second summons brings her to Rufus's bedroom, where he has started a fire out of anger. She saves him again. She also meets people living outside the plantation. Among them are Alice Greenwood and Alice's family, free Black people whose lives are nevertheless threatened at every turn by men with legal and physical power over them. Dana sees white patrolmen beat people, invade a home, and act as though their violence requires no explanation. When she is attacked herself, fear carries her back to 1976. Her modern clothes, education, and confidence offer only limited protection in the earlier world.

On a later journey, Kevin is holding Dana when she disappears, and he travels with her. They find Rufus injured after falling from a tree. Dana and Kevin become entangled in the Weylin plantation. Rufus's father, Tom Weylin, is harsh and suspicious, but he allows them to stay for a time. Kevin's whiteness gives him room to move and speak that Dana does not possess. Dana must learn the routines of the household: what servants and enslaved field workers do, how a person addresses an owner, when a question might be punished, and how quickly an apparently manageable situation can turn violent.

Some of the enslaved people on the Weylin plantation have made small networks of care under conditions they did not choose. Sarah, the cook, keeps the household running and has already endured the sale of members of her family. Carrie, her daughter, communicates without speech and is part of the life Dana comes to know. Nigel, who has grown up under Weylin's authority, wants a future that includes his own choices. Dana is learning the individual names and circumstances of people whom the owners treat as labor and property, and she begins to fear not only what may happen to her but what will happen to them after she disappears again.

Dana teaches people to read because literacy matters to her and because she cannot accept that it should be forbidden. When Weylin discovers her helping an enslaved boy named Nigel, he whips her. Dana is sent back to California by the danger, but Kevin is too far away to travel with her. She has returned to the life they share, yet he remains stranded in the nineteenth century. Dana has no way of finding him except by being summoned again. The interval in their marriage becomes a time when ordinary hours in California conceal years of danger for Kevin.

When Dana returns, Rufus has grown older and become more dangerous. He is obsessed with Alice Greenwood, who has married a man named Isaac. Rufus assaults Alice; Isaac retaliates violently, and the couple try to escape. Dana intervenes to keep Rufus alive, knowing that Rufus has become necessary to her own existence. She has discovered that Alice and Rufus will be ancestors in the family line that leads to her. If Rufus dies before the necessary child is born, Dana fears she may erase herself. This knowledge does not turn his behavior into something she can excuse. It forces her to keep saving a man whose growing power is ruining other lives.

Alice and Isaac are captured. Alice suffers grave injuries and is enslaved. Rufus purchases her and uses his ownership to force her into a relationship with him. Dana has to live among people who know the power he holds and who also know that opposing him can cost them everything. She tries to persuade Rufus to act less cruelly, tries to help Alice survive, and tries to find Kevin. Rufus lies about delivering letters. Dana attempts to escape and is whipped again. At last Kevin returns to the plantation after living years in the nineteenth century. They make a desperate attempt to leave together, and Rufus fires at them as they flee. Dana and Kevin get back to 1976.

Return to California is not an ending for Dana; the past draws her back once more.

Dana has returned to California with Kevin, but the years they spent apart have changed them. Kevin experienced a long stretch of the nineteenth century that Dana could not share. She has been beaten, threatened, and forced to watch people treated as property. They still love one another, but neither can simply fold the experience into an anecdote about a strange accident. Dana is drawn back again, this time without Kevin.

She finds Rufus lying drunk and endangered, and again her arrival appears connected to his immediate need. She helps him recover. His father, Tom Weylin, grows ill and dies despite Dana's attempts to help. Rufus turns his grief and anger on the people under his authority, including Dana. By now he is a grown man with the power to buy, sell, punish, and separate families. Dana has watched him change from a frightened child into someone who uses that power to satisfy his own desires.

Alice is living on the plantation as Rufus's enslaved mistress. The relationship has been forced upon her. She resents Rufus and knows that every small act of resistance may threaten her children. Dana cannot give Alice a simple plan for freedom. She tries to provide information, assistance, and companionship, but she also carries the knowledge that Alice must give birth to a particular descendant if Dana's own family is to exist. When Alice's daughter Hagar is born, Dana recognizes the child whose life connects the past to her present. Hagar is not a symbol to her mother. She is a little girl Alice wants to protect from the man who controls both their lives.

Rufus continues making choices that destroy the trust he claims to want. He sells enslaved people for reasons that can be as petty as jealousy. Dana protests when a man is sold for showing her attention. Rufus strikes her, and eventually she wounds herself badly enough to force a return to California. She has begun using the danger to her own life as the only exit available, though every return to freedom may require another injury.

During a later visit Dana finds the plantation in crisis. Alice has tried to run away with her children. Rufus has deceived her into believing that he has sold them. He has actually sent them away, but he uses their absence to punish her. Alice, unable to endure what she believes has happened, dies by suicide. Dana has spent years in different stretches of time trying to keep Alice alive. The news leaves her grieving and furious. Rufus is also devastated, but his grief does not erase his responsibility for what he has done.

With Alice gone, Rufus turns toward Dana. He tells her how much she resembles Alice and wants her to take the dead woman's place. He holds Dana close to him, increasingly treating her availability as another thing he can command. Dana has already helped ensure Hagar's birth. She no longer needs Rufus to survive for the sake of that child, although she does not know what killing him would mean for her own escape. One night, when Rufus attempts to force himself on her, she resists and stabs him. He dies. In the moment of mortal danger, Dana is pulled back into her own time.

But she does not return intact. Her arm has become trapped at the point where Rufus held her as she vanished. In California she arrives with part of the arm destroyed. Kevin finds her, and they must explain a horrifying injury to people who cannot believe where it happened. The story opens with the loss already present. Now we understand what that damage cost and what Dana had to do to survive.

Afterward Dana and Kevin try to investigate the historical record. They travel to Maryland and seek surviving evidence of the Weylin family and the people who lived on the plantation. The record offers some fragments and leaves enormous gaps. Dana cannot find official documentation that would make her lived experience legible to a stranger. She and Kevin have returned to an era where their marriage is legal and their freedoms are greater, but the past has not become distant. Dana's missing arm is an enduring part of her body.

This is where Kindred ends: a couple living in 1976, a woman who knows how and why one branch of her family came into being, and an injury that cannot be undone by understanding it. Rufus is dead. Alice is dead. Hagar survived long enough to become an ancestor. Dana is home. She has learned more about the people who came before her than she ever intended, and no one can make that knowledge painless by calling it history.
