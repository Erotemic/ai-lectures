# The Left Hand of Darkness — The Story, Part 3: The Ice and the Last Border

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Genly Ai and Estraven set out onto the Gobrin Ice with a sled packed for a journey that may take months. They are escaping Orgoreyn, where Genly was imprisoned, and heading toward Karhide, where Estraven is still under sentence of exile. The route offers no comfortable refuge. It is a landscape of snowfields, hidden crevasses, fierce winds, and long distances that cannot be shortened by urgency.

At first their partnership is mostly practical. The sled must be loaded correctly; the path must be tested; food and strength must be measured against the number of days ahead. Estraven knows more of life on Winter, and Genly gradually becomes a better traveling companion. Both men get tired and irritated. They also become capable of helping one another without interpreting every gesture as a political sign. They are moving through danger that cares nothing for the categories Genly once used to understand the people of Gethen.

As the journey continues, they talk. Genly asks about Estraven's family and the relationships he has left behind. Estraven asks about other worlds and the reasons anyone would spend years inviting strangers into a wider human community. They exchange stories and explain the customs that have shaped them. Genly starts to recognize the limits of his assumptions about sex and gender. Estraven is neither the man nor the woman that Genly's habits keep trying to imagine, and friendship becomes easier once Genly stops expecting one of those familiar answers.

They also discuss mind-speech, a form of direct mental communication that Genly can teach. The ability is not simply another diplomatic technique. When Estraven hears Genly's mind-speech, the voice carries a disturbing resemblance to someone from Estraven's past. The encounter brings old grief into their growing intimacy. The two do not suddenly understand everything about one another. But they are able to share things they could not share at the beginning of the journey.

They discover a kind of domestic routine on the ice. They have to pack the tent, harness themselves to the sled, judge the day’s likely distance, and decide how much of their food can be eaten. Snow and fog can make the world look entirely without features. The same person who was hard to approach in a palace can be an intimate companion when both travelers have spent days hauling a load through cold that hurts their hands and faces. They begin to know each other's limits and habits as well as their opinions.

The crossing is long, physically punishing, and sometimes monotonous. Food runs low. They endure weather that could end their lives in a night. Each day brings a new calculation: whether to continue, whether to wait, whether the land ahead is stable enough to cross. In their isolation the political mission that brought Genly to Winter seems very far away, though it is the reason both men are here. Estraven's life has been uprooted twice for trying to help a visitor whose country cannot guarantee his safety.

Eventually they leave the ice and approach the Karhidish border. Genly has reached the country where he first began his mission, and there may finally be a way to contact his ship. Estraven's position is more perilous. The banishment order has not disappeared. He moves toward the border despite the danger and is shot by guards. Genly cannot save him. The man who guided him across the ice, freed him from imprisonment, and made his mission possible dies before he can see the result.

Genly brings the truth of their journey to Karhide. The circumstances have changed. The government's calculations change too, and King Argaven eventually accepts the possibility of contact with the Ekumen. Genly is able to summon the ship that has waited for him. Visitors arrive from other worlds, making real what had previously sounded like one stranger's improbable story. The planet's first steps toward joining the Ekumen are no longer only a promise in an envoy's report.

But Genly's final personal journey is not to a diplomatic reception. He goes to Estraven's home region and meets people who remember the exile as a family member rather than a political figure. He carries what he can of Estraven's story back to those who loved him. The mission has succeeded, but its success came through an act of trust that cost its most devoted advocate his life.

At the end, Gethen has not transformed overnight. Karhide and Orgoreyn still exist; their histories and differences cannot be erased by the arrival of a ship. Genly has learned what it means to speak with someone he once systematically misunderstood. He returns to the larger human community carrying the knowledge that the world did not open because he was right about the future. It opened because Estraven believed that a relationship between strangers was worth risking everything to make possible.
