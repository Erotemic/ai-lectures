# The Lord of the Rings — The Story, Part Two: The Two Towers

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

When the Fellowship broke beside the great river, its members did not instantly understand what had happened. Frodo had gone east with Sam. Two younger hobbits, Merry and Pippin, were missing. Aragorn, who had inherited the responsibility of leading the company after Gandalf's fall, still had to choose between following the Ring and rescuing friends whose captors might be carrying them toward a different evil.

He finds Boromir dying among the bodies of enemies he has killed. The warrior has defended Merry and Pippin against a large attack, but the hobbits have been taken. Boromir confesses that he tried to seize the Ring from Frodo. His last concern is whether he has failed his people as well as his friends. Aragorn does not abandon him to that judgment. With Legolas and Gimli he lays Boromir in a boat upon the river. The man who had wanted a weapon to save Gondor is now carried away from every place he hoped to defend.

The three remaining hunters read the tracks of the raiders. The enemies who seized the hobbits are not a single disciplined force. Some come from Mordor; others are servants of Saruman, the wizard who has made Isengard into a military workshop. Their leaders quarrel over where prisoners should be taken and who has authority to claim them. Merry and Pippin listen from captivity. They know enough to understand that the enemy's divided loyalties may give them an opportunity. But the raiding force runs swiftly, and the hunters are already far behind.

## Three hunters and two captives

Aragorn, Legolas and Gimli pursue across the open plains of Rohan. Their fatigue grows as the distance stretches. Their purpose is not grand military strategy; it is the refusal to leave two friends to their captors. They meet mounted warriors under Éomer, a leader of the Rohirrim. Éomer's riders have attacked the Orc company and killed its members, believing there were no living captives to save. He is skeptical of the hunters' account, yet lends them horses and tells them where the fighting occurred.

At the battlefield there is no sign of hobbit bodies. This absence may mean hope or merely a failure to find the dead. Aragorn searches the edges of the site and discovers clues that the captives escaped into Fangorn, the immense forest beside the field. For Merry and Pippin the escape had come during the confusion of the battle. They ran under cover of darkness into trees that the Orcs feared.

Fangorn is not a tame wood. Its old trees carry anger about being cut and burned, and some of its creatures are much older than the peoples now fighting their wars. The hobbits encounter Treebeard, an Ent: a being whose body, voice and patience are bound to the life of trees. His speech moves slowly because events are not small to him merely because they happened long ago. He knows of Saruman's transformation of Isengard from a place of learning into an engine of destruction. Trees have been felled for furnaces, and living things treated as fuel.

Treebeard's memory also includes absence. He recalls the Entwives, whose ways of tending the world differed from those of the Ents and whose whereabouts are unknown. There is affection and regret in the memory, but no simple road back to the former life. The old forest's anger is not a modern program imposed upon animated trees. It comes from specific losses: cut trunks, vanished companions, and the sense that Saruman regards living growth only as material for his furnaces. Merry and Pippin listen to a grief whose timescale they cannot share, yet whose demands they can understand.

Treebeard has not yet committed the Ents to war. Many are cautious, and their meetings can take an astonishing length of time. Merry and Pippin listen while they gather for an Entmoot, where the forest's guardians try to determine whether the attack on their world can continue unopposed. The decision finally comes. Ents and other powerful tree-beings move toward Isengard, not as soldiers following a human general but as inhabitants of a forest defending itself.

Meanwhile, the three hunters encounter a mysterious white-clad figure in Fangorn and initially fear it is Saruman. It is Gandalf, returned from death and changed. He tells them something of his battle with the Balrog, which carried him into depths beneath the mountains and eventually upward to a peak. He has been sent back to finish what he was given to do. He is now Gandalf the White, no longer Saruman's subordinate in the work of opposing Sauron.

Gandalf knows Merry and Pippin are safe with Treebeard, at least for the moment. He leads Aragorn, Legolas and Gimli into Rohan. The kingdom's king, Théoden, has been declining in strength under the poisonous advice of his counselor Gríma, called Wormtongue. Saruman has cultivated influence at the center of the kingdom while assembling an army to attack it. Théoden is neither a simple fool nor a king free from grief. His son has died, his people are endangered, and distrust has been given the sound of prudent counsel. Gandalf confronts the court, Wormtongue's influence breaks, and the king decides to act.

Éomer, previously under suspicion, is restored to the king's service. Théoden prepares to move his people toward safety and war. His niece Éowyn watches. She is capable of courage and action, yet the role assigned to her is to hold the household and lead those who cannot fight. She is also responsible for people who have suffered while the men of the court have argued over command. Her story is not over; at this point her horizon appears to her to be closing.

## The fortress and the flooded tower

The Rohirrim do not have the luxury of choosing ideal ground. Saruman's army has moved west and south, burning settlements and pressing toward the valley. Many of Rohan's people seek refuge at the stronghold called the Hornburg in Helm's Deep. Théoden and the defenders ride there, expecting to survive behind stone walls while reinforcements can be gathered. Saruman's troops arrive in overwhelming numbers, with weapons and explosive devices designed to break fortifications.

During the night the defenders hold a narrow place that seems impossible to hold. Gimli fights in close quarters where his axe is effective. Legolas uses his bow from high positions. Their friendly competition over how many enemies they have defeated does not cancel the danger; it is one way companions speak to each other when terror leaves little room for ordinary humor. Aragorn moves where the line is weakest and Théoden faces the possibility that the last mounted defense of his people will die inside the valley.

The people inside the caves are not a decorative audience for a handful of heroes. Families, elders and children have fled there because the war has reached their homes. The soldiers' task is to buy them a morning. Gimli, more at ease among stone than in a large open field, sees the caves' remarkable formations and later tries to tell Legolas how beautiful they are. Even amid siege the place is not merely a shelter to be used up. It belongs to a landscape worth saving from destruction. The conversation about beauty will become part of the friendship between the Dwarf and the Elf.

The wall is breached. The defenders withdraw toward the fortress. For a time the night seems endless. At dawn, Théoden and Aragorn ride out rather than wait for destruction. Gandalf returns with Erkenbrand and a force from Rohan, and the attackers find themselves squeezed between riders and a forest that was not there before: living trees from Fangorn have moved into the valley. The enemy army is broken. Many who flee into the trees never come out again.

Victory is not a return to peacetime. There are dead to bury, homes to restore, and an enemy whose larger purpose has not been defeated. The companions ride with Gandalf and Théoden to Isengard expecting to confront Saruman at the center of his strength. They find something almost comically different: the place has been flooded and torn apart. Treebeard and the Ents have broken dams and released water through Saruman's works, extinguishing the furnaces and wrecking the machinery of war. The tower of Orthanc still stands, hard and inaccessible, with Saruman and Wormtongue trapped within.

Merry and Pippin are outside, healthy enough to enjoy food and tobacco amid the wreckage. Their reunion with the older companions is joyful and slightly absurd. The hobbits who were carried away as helpless prisoners have assisted in bringing down the very war machine that captured them. Treebeard cannot be ordered like an army, but he and the Ents have made an alliance through a convergence of threats.

Saruman refuses to admit defeat. He speaks from his high window with a voice capable of making his intentions seem reasonable and his listeners' own doubts seem foolish. He tries to separate Gandalf from the others, then Théoden from his people. Gandalf refuses the terms. Saruman's power as leader of the wizardly order is broken. Wormtongue, in anger, throws a heavy object from the tower. It proves to be a palantír, a seeing-stone whose long history has given it power to communicate across distances. The object is recovered and held carefully. What seems like a trophy from a ruined fortress is in fact another route by which a hostile will may reach into private judgment.

Pippin cannot stop thinking about it. During the night he looks into the stone without permission. Sauron, who has been using another seeing-stone, confronts the young hobbit across the connection. Pippin cannot understand all the conclusions his interrogator draws from the encounter. Gandalf understands enough to be terrified: the enemy has seen a hobbit connected with the company, and the direction of his next movements may depend on an error about who holds the Ring. Gandalf takes Pippin and rides toward Minas Tirith, the fortified capital of Gondor. The story in the west is moving into a larger war.

## Two hobbits east of the river

Meanwhile, Frodo and Sam have been making their own way through a far less hospitable country. They climb ridges and descend steep ravines, repeatedly losing time because they cannot find a safe route through the Emyn Muil. They are also being followed. At night the watcher approaches, pale eyes catching what little light there is. The hobbits catch Gollum, the creature who possessed the Ring for centuries before Bilbo found it. He is thin, hungry, ingenious, and desperate to recover what he calls his precious possession.

Sam distrusts him immediately. Frodo recognizes that Gollum is dangerous but also sees something of himself in the creature: a life deformed by the Ring, and perhaps a life that still retains some choice. Frodo makes Gollum swear by the object he desires that he will guide them without betraying them. The promise is fragile, since swearing by the Ring may be another way of tightening its hold over him. Yet Gollum does know paths the hobbits cannot find alone.

He leads them into the Dead Marshes, a flat region of water, mud, and strange lights. Beneath the pools seem to lie faces from a battle fought generations before. Frodo and Sam move through a place where history has become a physical hazard. They must keep their eyes from the lights and their feet from treacherous ground. At night vast flying shapes bring fear across the sky. They are watched from above by the same larger power that is preparing to assault Gondor.

Finally the travelers reach sight of the Black Gate into Mordor. The gate is defended and too heavily watched for three small figures to pass. Frodo is ready to attempt a desperate entry anyway; refusing to act seems as dangerous as acting. Gollum proposes another route, farther south, through a mountain pass that he claims can lead them into Mordor without crossing the open gate. He does not tell them everything he knows about the place. Frodo accepts the guidance because no other viable road is visible.

As they cross Ithilien, a country that once belonged more securely to Gondor, they move through abandoned gardens, broken walls and wild greenery. This is territory contested by soldiers rather than wholly claimed by either side. The hobbits witness the ambush of a company of Southrons traveling with a great elephant-like beast. In the aftermath they encounter a fallen enemy soldier whose death makes him seem less like an emblem of Sauron's forces than a man far from home. Sam wonders what brought him to this war. The question does not remove the danger the armies pose, but it interrupts the easy belief that every body on the other side of a battle belongs to an incomprehensible species of evil.

The hobbits have been observed by Rangers of Ithilien under Faramir, a captain of Gondor and Boromir's younger brother. His men take them to a hidden refuge by a waterfall. Faramir is skeptical of their story and protective of his military secrets. He understands enough to suspect that Frodo carries the object Boromir hoped would save Gondor. The discovery creates a decision like his brother's, but Faramir reaches a different answer. Even if the Ring could help his people defend themselves, he refuses to claim it. He is not indifferent to Gondor's suffering; he is unwilling to redefine his duty as possession of an enemy's instrument of domination.

Faramir's questions are personal as well as military. He knows his brother Boromir went north and did not return, and he has received signs of his death. He cannot easily understand why these two small travelers should be connected with the disaster. As Frodo's account unfolds, Faramir begins to see what Boromir may have wanted from the Ring and why its offer could have overwhelmed him. Faramir's restraint is not a simple rejection of his brother. He grieves for Boromir and understands the city's need, but refuses to let grief decide what may be done in its name. Sam, who has been trying to protect Frodo by remaining quiet, reveals more about the Ring than he intended. The captain's refusal is therefore made with knowledge of the stakes rather than ignorance of the weapon before him.

Faramir treats the hobbits with increasing respect and offers provisions. His men catch Gollum fishing in a forbidden pool near the refuge. Faramir might have him killed. Frodo intervenes, despite knowing the guide's loyalty is uncertain. Gollum regards the capture as betrayal, not rescue. Faramir warns Frodo that the road toward Cirith Ungol is dangerous, and that Gollum's knowledge may conceal a plan. The hobbits leave with little comfort and no alternative they can trust more.

## The stairs and the dark passage

The route turns toward Minas Morgul, a city transformed into a place of fear under the power of the Ringwraiths. Frodo feels an overwhelming urge to approach its gates, as though the Ring is pulling him toward the very place he must avoid. He and Sam hide while a great enemy force leaves the city, led by a terrible figure whose presence drains courage. The invasion of the west is beginning while their own path narrows to a cliffside stair.

Gollum guides them up steep, exhausting steps. Frodo is worn by carrying the Ring and by hunger. Sam's concern for him is constant, yet Sam is less able to separate fear for Frodo from hatred of the guide. Gollum's behavior changes from moment to moment. He may seem almost affectionate toward the sleeping hobbits, then retreat behind suspicion and craving. The travelers stop near the mouth of a dark tunnel. Sam wakes and accuses Gollum of sneaking about. Whatever hesitation Gollum may have felt does not survive into an open admission of his purpose.

The tunnel belongs to Shelob, an enormous spiderlike creature that predates many of the political powers now fighting. Gollum has led the hobbits toward her as prey, imagining that when they are dead he may take the Ring. Inside, the passage becomes a suffocating dark. Frodo holds the light Galadriel gave him, and for a time it pushes Shelob back. Sam follows, drawing on courage that does not cancel his terror. They reach what seems to be an opening, but Gollum attacks Sam from behind. By the time Sam escapes the struggle and finds Frodo, Shelob has struck him with a sting and is carrying him away.

Sam attacks the creature. He is a small, exhausted hobbit facing something built for slaughter, but he wounds her with the sword Frodo carried and raises the Elven light. Shelob withdraws, terribly hurt. Sam turns to Frodo and finds him cold and unresponsive. There is no breath or movement that he can detect. The last purpose left in Sam's world appears to have vanished.

He makes an agonizing decision. If Frodo is dead and the Ring remains beside him, the mission will end as soon as an enemy arrives. Sam takes the Ring, intending to continue alone, though he scarcely knows where he is. He has no training for the role of Ring-bearer, no map of the ground ahead, and no confidence that he can resist the temptation that destroyed people much greater than he.

Orcs approach before he can get far. Sam hides, putting on the Ring to avoid discovery. He hears the soldiers discussing the body they have found. One tells the other that Shelob's victims are paralyzed, not necessarily dead. Frodo is alive. The enemy takes him into the tower guarding the pass, where prisoners are questioned and valuables searched. Sam is outside, carrying the Ring, with the terrible knowledge that he has left his friend in the hands of enemies.

Here The Two Towers ends. The book has shown armies moving toward war, a kingdom briefly saved, a wizard's industrial power broken, a stone carrying perilous messages across the world, and two hobbits brought almost to the threshold of Mordor. Neither thread is resolved. The captains of Gondor cannot know whether the Ring approaches its destruction. Gandalf cannot know whether Frodo is alive. And Sam, who began the journey hoping simply to serve his master, must now attempt a rescue on which the entire quest may depend.
