# Season of Migration to the North — The Story

This is a complete plot narration of Tayeb Salih's novel, including the ending. It precedes analysis lecture `011000`. The work includes sexual violence, coercive marriage, suicide, and murder; this account names those events without recreating graphic scenes.

## Returning to the village

A young Sudanese man comes home after seven years in England. He has completed his studies and is pleased to be back in the village beside the Nile, where his grandfather still lives and where familiar faces welcome him. His travels have given him a degree and an acquaintance with Europe, but the place to which he returns seems at first to have held still. There are palm trees, fields, meals, jokes, arguments, the ordinary traffic of people who know one another's families. He feels that he belongs here in a way he did not belong abroad.

The narrator never gives us his name. He describes his pleasure in the river and in the continuity of the village, and he is delighted to be known as someone who has returned with an education. The local people want to hear about England. There is curiosity, but also a gentle sense that their lives cannot be measured only by what a visitor has learned overseas. The narrator answers their questions and enjoys the warmth of his reception.

One man does not respond as the others do. His name is Mustafa Sa'eed. He has settled in the village relatively recently and seems prosperous, reserved, and competent. He works his land, takes part in community affairs, and has married a local woman, Hosna bint Mahmoud. He has children. He is treated as a practical member of the village rather than a mysterious intellectual. Yet he seems notably unimpressed by the narrator's European education. The narrator finds this irritating. The stranger occupies a position he cannot easily read. Why should a man who belongs here only by arrival have so little interest in another man's journey north?

One evening the men are together in a convivial gathering. Mustafa, who has been drinking, unexpectedly recites English poetry with the fluency and confidence of someone deeply familiar with it. The moment breaks the identity he has presented. The farmer who appeared to have no reason to care about the narrator's years in Europe has himself lived somewhere the narrator thought was part of his own distinctive story.

The narrator presses him. Mustafa is reluctant at first, but the concealment cannot be sustained. He eventually tells the young man about his earlier life. The village story has acquired a second, darker story inside it.

## The young Mustafa

Mustafa grew up in Sudan without the kind of warm, rooted family identity the narrator takes for granted. He was exceptionally gifted in school. His intelligence opened doors in the educational system established under British colonial rule. Where other pupils struggled with the language and examination structure, he advanced with startling ease. He passed through schools, went to Cairo, and continued on to England. His talent was recognized and rewarded, but he learned early that education could also become a path away from every place that might have held him.

He was young when he began collecting academic honors and professional opportunity. He became a scholar of economics and moved within university and London society. To the institutions that recognized him, he could appear a triumph of education: a brilliant Sudanese student whose success validated the route they had made available. Mustafa's own experience was not so simple. He had crossed into a world eager to view him through its own fantasies of distant countries, danger, sexuality, and the exotic East.

He learned to perform those fantasies. In London he cultivated rooms and stories that could make him seem like the figure others expected. He fashioned an atmosphere of Eastern luxury and mystery, set beside his impeccable intellectual credentials. There was a calculating pleasure in discovering how readily people accepted what he wanted them to see. He could use stereotypes as instruments of seduction, turning the people who held them into participants in his performance. But the performance also trapped him. He was neither merely a victim of European projections nor a man untouched by what he deliberately did with them.

Several relationships with English women became destructive. Ann Hammond, Sheila Greenwood, and Isabella Seymour are among the women whose lives become entangled with his self-invented persona. Their deaths by suicide are recounted through Mustafa's account of those years. He likes to describe himself as dangerous, capable of conquering people emotionally in the manner of a soldier crossing territory. We should not mistake that description for the objective truth of what those women were or why they acted. Their lives reach us mostly through a man's recollection, and the way he turns them into incidents in his own legend is part of the violence of the telling.

With Jean Morris, the relationship becomes a different kind of struggle. Mustafa encounters a woman who resists his practiced methods of charm and domination. Their courtship and marriage are full of provocation, resentment, and attempts to humiliate one another. He wants to make her submit to the identity he has prepared; she refuses the part he assigns her and forces him to confront his own rage. Their marriage culminates in his killing her. He stabs her. No metaphor about colonial conquest can make that killing something other than the murder of a person.

Mustafa is brought to trial. The courtroom in England becomes another place in which competing stories are told about him. He can be represented as an exceptionally gifted outsider, an embodiment of colonial injury, a sexual threat, or a man whose actions must be judged as individual crimes. People who have known him argue about his intentions and character. He receives a prison sentence and eventually emerges from the ordeal alive, but the life he had built is destroyed. His route back toward Sudan does not restore the years he spent in London or undo the lives lost there.

## The confession and its price

The narrator listens. He is disturbed, fascinated, and unwilling to let Mustafa's story rest. The two men are alike in ways that make the differences unsettling. Both studied abroad and returned to Sudan. Both know how English language and education can confer status. Both have lived between worlds. Yet the narrator wants to believe that his travels have left him able to come home, while Mustafa has made foreignness into a secret that organizes his whole life.

The confession does not produce intimacy or understanding in the ordinary sense. It creates an obligation that the narrator neither requested nor knows how to refuse. Mustafa does not give his life to the village as a public narrative. He gives it to one man who now must carry knowledge he cannot easily share. Back among his neighbors, Mustafa continues to farm and conduct his affairs. The story of Jean Morris, the other women, the trial, and the years in England belongs to a different register from the village's everyday account of him.

The narrator is not constantly at home. He takes a job connected with education and travels between the village and his work. This means he can withdraw from the unsettling familiarity of Mustafa's presence. Yet the question of who Mustafa really is persists. When he looks at the village now, he is less certain that knowing a place means knowing its people. The man who seemed utterly out of place can perform the part of a settled husband and farmer, while the man who grew up there is increasingly uncertain what his own education has made him.

Then the Nile rises in a period of severe flooding. Mustafa disappears. His fate remains uncertain. He may have drowned by accident or deliberately entered the river; the novel does not resolve the matter in a way that makes a reliable report possible. The family he leaves behind must live with his absence regardless of how it came about.

Mustafa has named the narrator as someone to look after Hosna and their two sons. He has also left him access to a locked room. These gestures bring the earlier private confession into the practical arrangements of village life. The narrator has become a witness to a life and a custodian of part of it. He is also someone whose conduct might matter to the surviving wife and children.

## Hosna and the village

Hosna bint Mahmoud is not a character in Mustafa's London legend. She is the woman he married after coming home, a mother with children and a future whose course has not yet been decided. Her husband's disappearance makes her vulnerable. In the village there are men whose jokes and habitual authority disclose how little weight some of them give a woman's refusal.

An older man, Wad Rayyes, wants to marry Hosna. He is considerably older than she is, and he has a history of marriages. He speaks about the arrangement as though his desire and social standing are sufficient. But Hosna does not want to marry him. Her answer is not mysterious or ambiguous. She refuses. The narrator hears of the proposal, and at points Hosna appeals to him for help. She knows the local expectations will be used against her. She also knows the narrator's connection to Mustafa may give him a special position.

The narrator is troubled, but he does not respond decisively. He has other work and reasons to leave the village. He can describe the situation, resent the men who discount Hosna's wishes, and imagine that someone else will intervene. He is not a simple villain. His failure is more ordinary and perhaps more damaging: he knows enough to recognize danger, but not enough about his own responsibility to act when action is still possible.

Hosna's family and the older man push ahead. She is compelled to marry Wad Rayyes despite her opposition. The community has the ordinary mechanisms of consent turned backward. An arrangement that ought to depend on her decision is treated as a matter for men and relatives to settle. It is possible to hear this part of the novel as a dispute about tradition, but the story is more specific than a contest between a timeless village and a modern city. Named people make decisions. Named people ignore clear refusals. People who might have helped find reasons not to take responsibility.

News comes that Hosna and Wad Rayyes are dead. After the forced marriage, he attempts to rape her. She kills him and then dies by suicide. The details reach the narrator after the event, through other people's accounts and the devastated reactions of the village. A woman who wanted to remain unmarried, care for her children, and decide the conditions of her own life has been pushed into a situation that destroys her. The story refuses to let her death be treated simply as a sensational ending to Mustafa's enigmatic career.

The narrator returns in shock and anger. He questions the people around him. Some condemn Hosna; some struggle to understand. He must face the fact that he had received a responsibility from Mustafa and then failed to protect the person who needed it. He cannot undo the marriage, the violence, or his own absence. He also has to recognize that the village he had imagined as the secure opposite of London possesses its own forms of coercion.

## The locked room

Mustafa's room has been kept closed. It stands inside the village house as a physical remnant of a life that does not fit the outward identity of farmer and husband. When the narrator finally enters, he finds a space curated around the absent man: books, papers, possessions, and the traces of his English years. It is a room arranged almost as an imported fragment of Europe. Mustafa had carried the north south with him, concealing it behind the appearance of a complete return.

A mirror becomes one of the most disturbing objects. In the room the narrator sees an image of himself and for a moment almost confuses it with Mustafa. The scene condenses his fear: the stranger's journey might be his own, or the stranger might have occupied the path he narrowly escaped. The room does not merely answer a mystery about another man. It threatens to make every answer the narrator gives about himself uncertain.

He examines the remains of Mustafa's books and self-documentation. He understands that the London story had not been left behind. It had been preserved in a private enclosure, alongside the family who were expected to live around it. Yet the stored papers do not settle the moral questions. A document can record success, scandal, desire, and pain while failing to tell us what those facts meant to the people whose lives were affected. Mustafa controlled the presentation of himself in the house as he once controlled it in London.

The narrator's growing identification with Mustafa becomes intolerable. He does not want to be the other man's continuation. He cannot return to the innocence of arriving from England and finding everything at home exactly as he remembered. Too much has happened in the village to permit that account.

## In the river

Near the end the narrator enters the Nile to swim. The river is the setting of the childhood security with which the story began. It is also the river in which Mustafa vanished. In the water the narrator moves away from the bank and finds himself caught between conflicting impulses. It becomes difficult to swim back. He contemplates yielding to the current, letting the river close over him as it may have closed over Mustafa.

The book might have ended as a neat repetition: two educated men, both estranged from themselves, disappearing into the same water. Instead the narrator reaches toward a decision that is neither complete reconciliation nor triumphant recovery. He wants to live. He cries for help. The cry goes outward, toward other people who may hear him. He cannot guarantee that they will save him; the novel ends before any rescue is securely reported. But the act of calling breaks the secrecy and self-enclosure that have shaped so much of Mustafa's story.

The young man who came back to a stable village has learned that his home is inhabited by dangers and responsibilities he preferred not to see. The stranger whose life seemed an exotic aberration has forced him to confront the distance between education and wisdom, between condemning violence and interrupting it, and between knowing another person's secrets and helping the living.

That is the story of *Season of Migration to the North*. It moves from a homecoming to a confession, from London back to a Sudanese village, from the disappearance of one man to the forced marriage and death of a woman, and from a locked room to a cry in the river. The narrator begins by thinking the river proves he belongs. He ends by needing someone on its banks to answer him.
