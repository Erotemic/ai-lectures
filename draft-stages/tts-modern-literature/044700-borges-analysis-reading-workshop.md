# Borges: A Close-Reading Workshop for Unreliable Documents and Perfect Patterns

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

When reading Borges, the most useful question is often not what a symbol means but who has placed it in front of us. A letter, book title, passage from an encyclopedia, or geometric diagram may look like information. Its significance depends on whether we understand the agent who selected it and the reasons the narrator reports it. This lecture offers a set of close-reading moves that can be practiced on the stories without treating them as riddles with one permanently concealed answer.

Start with the first pages of Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius. Make a small map of the acts of verification. Two friends remember a reference; one copy of an encyclopedia appears not to contain it; another does. There are names and bibliographical details that suggest a research trail. Do not begin by deciding whether Tlön is physically real in the story. Instead ask what each new piece of evidence licenses. An extra entry in one copy proves, within the narrative, that this copy differs from the other. It does not yet prove that Uqbar is a country or that its literary world exists. A large part of Borges's effect comes from making those different claims feel as though they arrived together.

Next read Pierre Menard as a report on a supposed writer. Mark every moment when the narrator moves from something observable, such as identical words, to something inferred, such as the greater philosophical seriousness of one author's version. The important joke is not that we can never attribute meaning to a text. It is that the critic's evidence and conclusion are not the same kind of statement. The writerly technique is to present the leap with the gravity of scholarship, then let the reader discover how much of the leap was supplied by the reviewer.

Now turn to The Library of Babel. Distinguish the library's stipulated rules from its residents' beliefs. The printed alphabet and the regular size of the books are premises of the imagined world. The existence of a reliable master catalog, an all-knowing librarian, or a sacred book may be the hope of a character. The story becomes clearer when we do not treat every intense conviction as a revelation from the narrator's omniscient creator. What the inhabitants want the library to be is itself part of the evidence about their lives.

Funes the Memorious asks for a different kind of care. The narrator presents an account of perfect memory while admitting that he himself reconstructs the past from imperfect recollection. Try listing what he witnessed directly and what Funes told him. Ask where his interpretation of Funes's thinking begins. The story may support a philosophical claim about abstraction, but it does not turn an invented person's behavior into a clinical data set. The form of testimony limits the certainty of the claims that the testimony seems to make.

In Death and the Compass, separate observation, hypothesis, and intervention. A scholar has been killed. The detective infers a theological pattern. Later signs confirm his expectation. Once we discover that a murderer has deliberately generated those signs, our reading must change. The question is no longer simply whether the hypothesis fits the observed geometry. It is whether the observations were independent of the investigator's known preferences. If you have learned to ask who controls the evidence, you have learned the technique that Lönnrot neglected.

The Garden of Forking Paths involves two frames. The outer editorial apparatus claims to clarify military history. The inner statement presents Yu Tsun explaining an act whose reason remains hidden until the end. After reading, reconstruct what the first frame promises and what the last paragraph actually supplies. The coded signal succeeds, but the reasons for the delay in a military offensive, the reliability of the editor, and the moral weight of the murder need not be collapsed into one satisfied conclusion.

Finally read The South twice without deciding in advance which reality is definitive. On the first reading, pay attention to the ordinary causal sequence of injury, treatment, release, railway journey, and provocation. On the second, attend to the shift in texture as travel becomes dreamlike and the landscape seems to accommodate Dahlmann's preferred image of himself. The second reading does not cancel the first. It identifies how the story makes two patterns available and how a reader's desire for one clear ending can become another unexamined assumption.

A robust close reading should be capable of saying both what the text establishes and what it leaves uncertain. It should be able to quote or identify the particular formal decision behind an inference, explain at least one plausible alternative, and distinguish factual claims from evaluative judgments. Borges rewards sharpness of attention, but not the belief that every allusion must fit one master code. When a story shows a scholar being destroyed by his own perfect pattern, we should hesitate before trying to impose a perfect pattern of our own.

One last practice: take a story whose ending has changed your understanding, and describe the earlier scene again without changing any of its reported events. The exercise reveals the power of interpretation. A scar remains a scar; a murder remains a murder; a book remains a book. The narrative has changed the position from which those things can be understood. That repositioning is often Borges's most consequential invention.
