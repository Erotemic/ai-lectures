# The Trial — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On his thirtieth birthday, Josef K. wakes in the furnished room where he has been living and finds that breakfast has not arrived. He rings. Instead of his landlady's cook, a stranger comes in. Another stranger is waiting in the next room. They tell him that he is under arrest.

K. is a capable official at a bank, accustomed to having his instructions obeyed and his position respected. He wants to know the charge. He wants credentials, a named superior, a recognizable office, some ordinary procedure by which to check a claim. The two men provide none of these things. They know enough to treat him as a person whose case already exists, but not enough, or not with enough interest, to explain it. They take his breakfast and examine his belongings. When he protests, they say that their business is not to discuss the law.

An inspector eventually appears in a neighboring boarder's room. K. continues asking what he has done. The inspector offers no accusation. Instead, he tells K. that the arrest need not stop him from going to work. He can continue his ordinary life. His freedom, such as it is, now belongs to a process whose existence has been asserted without being justified. K. leaves for the bank, but he can no longer say what ordinary life means.

The novel was left unfinished by Franz Kafka and published after his death. Its familiar sequence of chapters represents an editorial ordering of material that Kafka did not prepare as a finished book. We can still tell the principal events, including the written ending, without pretending that every transition is an author-approved, fixed chronology. What follows is the story readers encounter in the common book order.

## The arrest spreads beyond the morning

K. lodges with Frau Grubach and several other tenants. After the arrest he becomes intensely concerned about how the morning's proceedings might affect his reputation. He speaks to his landlady and to Fräulein Bürstner, a young woman whose room was used by the officials. He tries to reconstruct the event for her, arranging furniture and demonstrating where the men stood. His account does not remain an innocent request for sympathy. He presses his attention on her and kisses her without waiting for an invitation. The scene exposes a man whose own sense of entitlement is not eliminated by the humiliation he has suffered.

He later receives instructions to appear for an inquiry on a Sunday, but the summons offers little practical help in finding the place. He searches the streets and enters a building in an ordinary working neighborhood. The court is not in a monumental courthouse. It occupies rooms above domestic spaces, reached through stairs and corridors. K. pushes through a crowd into a hearing room, where a magistrate and an audience seem to expect that the case is already intelligible to everyone except the accused.

K. makes a forceful speech against the proceedings. He denounces the officials' corruption, the absurd arrest, and the contempt shown to ordinary people. He wants to expose the institution by addressing the room as if it were a public meeting. But the audience's reactions are unreliable. Those who appear to be opposing groups may belong to the same apparatus. The court does not have to defeat him in an open argument to preserve its advantage. It can absorb his protest as another thing that happened inside its own hearing.

On a later visit, K. enters an empty room used by the court. A woman connected with the court caretaker talks to him about its officials and the favors they demand. A law student takes her away; other officials have their claims upon her. The arrangements are exploitative, but K.'s indignation is mixed with his own attraction and possessiveness. He is trying to find a route into the institution through someone who may have even less power than he does.

Behind the courtroom are the court offices, crowded into suffocating attic rooms. Petitioners wait. Clerks work. The air seems impossible to breathe. K. becomes dizzy and must be helped out. He learns that the court is not one place easily distinguished from the city. It is a network of rooms, intermediaries, and rules that appears in the least expected buildings and overwhelms people who attempt to move through it.

## Punishment behind a door

At the bank, K. discovers another connection between his life and the case. From a storage room he hears cries. Inside, a man is whipping the two warders who took part in his arrest. They say that K.'s complaints brought this punishment on them. The warders' original misconduct was petty but real. The official retaliation is cruel and hidden. K. tries to intervene, then retreats because he is afraid the disturbance will damage him professionally. When he opens the storeroom again the following day, he finds the same awful scene, as though the punishment has never advanced beyond the moment he first saw it.

It is not explained whether the scene is supernatural, an impossible repetition, or something more like the logic of K.'s nightmare made physical. It matters that a rule presented as accountability produces only another wrong. K. can protest the warders' treatment and still fail to act when the consequences of his protest threaten his own standing.

His position at the bank begins to suffer. He is distracted, obsessed with the legal process, and increasingly frightened that colleagues will exploit his weakness. He has been permitted to continue working; the permission is almost part of the trap. A person can be made less and less able to perform his ordinary responsibilities without being formally imprisoned.

## The lawyer and his household

K.'s uncle learns of the case and is alarmed about the family's reputation. He takes K. to a lawyer named Huld, an invalid who is said to know the peculiar workings of the court. Huld talks about influence, petitions, secret relationships among officials, and the difficulty of obtaining reliable information. He may know things that K. needs. He also has every reason to keep K. dependent on the promise that only an insider can help.

In Huld's house K. encounters Leni, a nurse and household servant. Their attraction quickly distracts him from his uncle's efforts to secure legal assistance. Later, Leni explains that many accused men come under her care, suggesting that the status of being accused gives them a disturbing attraction in this world. The novel repeatedly mingles desire with institutional dependence. It seldom lets K. approach another person without also calculating what that person might do for his case.

Huld's progress is painfully slow. K. receives accounts of important contacts and elaborate drafts, but no clear result. The lawyer's influence operates in rooms the accused cannot enter. The more K. worries, the more tempted he becomes to believe that his rescue is being prepared beyond his sight.

## Three ways of not escaping

A manufacturer who does business with the bank learns about K.'s trouble and advises him to see Titorelli, a painter who makes portraits for court officials. K. finds the artist in a cramped room, surrounded by girls who seem to know his business better than he wants them to. The studio itself has an access route to court offices. The supposedly private expert occupies another edge of the same structure.

Titorelli says he can explain the kinds of acquittal available. An actual, final acquittal is a possibility named in theory, but the painter knows no clear practical route to one. There is an apparent acquittal, by which a defendant may be released for the moment without the case truly disappearing. There is also protraction, keeping the case going at a low level so that immediate punishment is deferred. Both require continuing contact with officials. Neither gives K. the conclusion he wants: a recognizable decision that he is free.

The discussion makes him more aware of the machinery but not more capable of leaving it. As K. departs, the painter presses unwanted pictures upon him, several landscapes so similar that they offer little genuine choice. K. has acquired knowledge and a purchase he did not need. He has not acquired justice.

## Another accused man

Later, K. finds a merchant named Block at Huld's house. Block has been a defendant for years. He has spent money, neglected his business, and consulted several lawyers, sometimes secretly. His life has narrowed around the demand to be ready whenever someone in the court requires his attention. He remains frightened of displeasing Huld, who demonstrates the power of making a desperate client beg for even a small sign of progress.

K. is appalled at Block's degradation. He decides to dismiss Huld and take more direct responsibility for his defense. But the confrontation is not a clean declaration of independence. He watches the old lawyer humiliate Block and sees what years of dependence can make of a person. The scene suggests K.'s possible future, while showing the cruelty in his own mixture of pity and contempt. He wants to be unlike Block, but he does not know a way out of the same system.

K. can no longer keep the case separate from his professional duties. An important visitor to the bank, an Italian client, is to be shown the city's cathedral. K. prepares for the meeting and goes there, expecting to perform a normal business courtesy. The visitor does not come. The great church is dark and almost empty.

## The cathedral

A priest speaks from a pulpit and addresses K. by name. He is connected to the prison chaplaincy. The encounter feels planned by a court that has placed its officers everywhere, yet nothing here resembles an ordinary trial. The priest speaks about K.'s situation and warns that he is deceiving himself about his progress.

He tells K. a story often called Before the Law. A man from the country seeks entry to the Law. A doorkeeper refuses to admit him now, while suggesting that an entry might be possible later. The man waits. He tries questions and gifts. The doorkeeper accepts the gifts without letting him through. Years pass. The man grows old. Shortly before death he asks why, in all the years he has waited, no one else has come to this entrance. The doorkeeper tells him that it was intended for him alone and that it will now be closed.

K. reacts strongly. He believes the man was deceived. The priest presents other possible interpretations: that the doorkeeper may have told the truth, that the man made false assumptions, that duties and permissions have different meanings within the law. Every explanation seems able to coexist with its opposite, and none changes the result. A person has died outside a doorway while the authority guarding it continued to exist.

The priest does not give K. an instruction that would settle the case. He leaves K. with a parable in which a door can be meant for someone and still never open. K. departs the cathedral with more interpretation and no verdict.

## The last night of the year

The end comes on the eve of K.'s thirty-first birthday. Two men arrive at his lodging. They are dressed formally and conduct themselves like performers of a procedure whose steps have been rehearsed. K. does not obtain an accusation, a hearing, or an explanation. He goes with them through the city. There are moments when he might resist, attract notice, or appeal to a passerby, but action repeatedly collapses into hesitation.

The men take him to a deserted quarry. They position him and produce a knife. At the last moment K. sees a figure at a distant window. He cannot know whether the figure is a friend, a witness, or someone who might still help. The men kill him. The final humiliation is not only that he has been condemned without a known charge. It is the shame that seems to remain as his last human possession.

We do not learn that K. was convicted of any particular offense. We do not discover the institution's purpose or the origin of its authority. The ending is part of what Kafka wrote, but the manuscript as a whole remained unfinished. It therefore gives us a conclusion to K.'s life without granting the procedural explanation a reader of a conventional legal thriller might expect.

The story begins with a bank official insisting that the world should offer him an intelligible account of his arrest. It follows him into hearing rooms that resemble apartments, offices where air cannot be breathed, a lawyer's bedroom, a painter's studio, a church, and a quarry. Along the way he becomes less able to distinguish between investigating an accusation and organizing his entire life around it. At the end the original question—what did he do?—is still unanswered. We have learned many things about the people and practices that claim the power to judge him. We have not learned the secret that would make the proceeding fair.

## Textual and interpretive note

This original narration follows the commonly published sequence of *Der Process*/*Der Prozess* (*The Trial*), written chiefly in 1914–15 and first published after Kafka's 1924 death by Max Brod in 1925. Kafka did not finish or definitively order all chapters, and critical editions make different editorial choices.
