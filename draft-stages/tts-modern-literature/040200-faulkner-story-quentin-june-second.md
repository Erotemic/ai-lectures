# The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part Two: Quentin’s Last Day

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The second part of The Sound and the Fury begins not after Benjy's Saturday but nearly eighteen years before it. The heading reads June second, nineteen ten. We are in Massachusetts with Quentin Compson, the eldest Compson son, near Harvard University. By the time this day ends he will be dead. The novel lets us accompany him for hours before the whole significance of his actions becomes clear. It is not the suspense of whether a hero will escape a threat. It is the distress of being near someone who is preparing to end his life while thinking continually about what he cannot repair.

Quentin wakes to a watch that belonged to his grandfather. His father has given it to him with a bitter account of time and futility. Quentin breaks off its hands, but the movement does not stop. The watch becomes one of the day's governing objects. Measuring time is supposed to make a day manageable. For Quentin it makes the distance between what happened, what he remembers, and what he intends almost impossible to bear. A clock can be deprived of its visible hands without relieving the mind that hears it.

It helps to know the central event of his recent family life. His sister Caddy has had sexual relationships and become pregnant. The family has arranged a marriage with Herbert Head, a man who does not know the full circumstances and whose social standing seems capable of protecting the Compson name. Quentin has confused his sister's autonomy with the collapse of all order. He loves her; he also cannot bear the thought that she has a life independent of the identity he has assigned to her. These feelings are entangled in his mind, where jealousy, fear, affection, shame, and fantasies of rescue continually exchange places.

At one point Quentin tells his father that he and Caddy have committed incest. The claim is false. It is an attempt to make the unbearable sexual history of his sister into a story in which he can occupy a central, tragic role. His father does not accept the confession. Instead of recognizing a son in crisis, Mr. Compson responds with a weary skepticism about purity, desire, honor, and the importance of anything people pursue. Quentin hears these ideas without being freed by them. His father's cynicism collides with his own rigid ideal of an unchanging sister. Neither offers the living Caddy room to be herself.

Quentin also remembers Dalton Ames, associated with Caddy's sexual history. These memories are not straightforward hearings of evidence. Quentin's imagination returns to scenes of confrontation, to questions asked and answers anticipated, to the possibility of violence, and to the humiliating possibility that no declaration by him will change what Caddy chooses. In his distress he turns a woman's experience into an accusation against himself. That is why the most emotionally charged passages are not simply descriptions of something Caddy has done. They are records of Quentin's failing effort to make the world obey an inherited moral language.

In the present-day streets around Cambridge and Boston, he tries to move through ordinary actions. He walks, rides, looks into shops, meets fellow students, pays attention to a river, and makes purchases. The route is not random. Quietly, he obtains heavy flat-irons, objects associated with his plan to drown himself. He encounters water over and over. The Charles River is both a place in a real city and the dark destination toward which his thoughts keep turning. Nothing in a shop window or a morning's errand can lift that private direction from his mind.

There is a strange, moving episode with a young Italian girl whom Quentin cannot easily communicate with. He tries to help her find where she belongs, and she follows him. Adults begin to read the situation differently. A brother or guardian arrives; Quentin is suspected of taking the child; there is police involvement before the misunderstanding is cleared up. This episode matters partly because it puts Quentin's bewilderment into an external, social form. He believes himself to be acting harmlessly; other people see a suspicious man escorting a girl. His own certainty about his intentions gives him no control over what others conclude.

In another part of the day he moves among university acquaintances, including Gerald Bland, whose easy social confidence and manner with women become intolerable to him. Quentin gets into a fight. His body is injured; the encounter leaves embarrassment and confusion, not heroic resolution. The campus world that should represent his education and future has become another setting in which he measures himself against an ideal of masculine authority he cannot inhabit. The Compsons sold land to finance his attendance at Harvard. Quentin will not complete his first year.

His relation to time is built from more than clocks. He remembers Caddy as a child in the stream and as a young woman at the edge of departure. He hears conversations out of order. Names and phrases repeat until they seem to detach from their first occasions. His consciousness can move from a bench or a shop to the Compson household with no formal transition. Sometimes words fracture and conventional punctuation disappears. The language is not a puzzle that will yield a single tidy equation once every flashback is dated. It is a mind trying to escape its own chronology and failing.

Notice the other Quentin. In the later events of the novel, Caddy has a daughter given this very name. The choice memorializes the brother after his death, but it also makes the family repeat a name while refusing to face what the first bearer suffered. The younger Quentin, the girl, will have her own conflicts with Jason, the surviving brother. She is not present to tell this Harvard story. At this point in the novel the name belongs to a young man whose world has contracted around an idea of disgrace.

Quentin's suicide is not presented as a conclusive philosophical insight. Nothing his sister has done makes his death inevitable or justified. The novel shows how an ideology can deepen distress: an ideal of chastity that cannot account for another person's freedom; a code of family honor that places a disproportionate burden on women; a father's intellectual detachment that lacks care; and Quentin's inability to distinguish emotional pain from an unalterable sentence. These pressures do not exhaust the meaning of his mental state. They help us see why the thought of a future is disappearing from him.

He prepares himself carefully. Clothing, objects, and small arrangements matter to him at the end because they offer a semblance of control. The day continues to unfold in scraps of observed life even as his intention becomes unmistakable. Later sections and the larger family history establish that he drowns himself in the Charles River. The first-person account stops short of becoming a reporter's description of the death. No one can narrate what Quentin does not survive to tell.

One important difference between Benjy and Quentin emerges here. Benjy's memories arrive through sensory association without a clear past-tense boundary. Quentin knows about clocks and the prestige of abstract explanations; that knowledge is no protection against obsessive returns. He tries to abolish time by removing the watch's hands, while the watch continues ticking. He tries to abolish the difference between Caddy's choices and his own by claiming to be responsible for her sexual history. Both efforts fail. The world outside his mind persists.

The section leaves us with an appalling contrast. In one place, the family has spent its land and its hopes to send a son into elite education. In another, a son cannot imagine inhabiting the future that the money was meant to purchase. It is tempting to call Quentin the victim of the family's decay, but that would ignore the ways his own possessive beliefs reproduce the family's damage. Faulkner refuses a single sentence that excuses or dismisses him. We are asked to witness both his suffering and the forms of power he imagines having over someone else.
