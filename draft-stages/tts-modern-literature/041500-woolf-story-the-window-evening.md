# To the Lighthouse — The Story, Part Two: Dinner and the Night

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

By dinner time the house has become a problem of timing. Mrs. Ramsay wants the guests together, the food at its best, the meal to become one of those rare occasions which people may carry in memory for years. Paul Rayley and Minta Doyle are late from the shore. The cook, Mildred, has made a special beef dish, and it cannot wait forever. Mrs. Ramsay's irritation is a hostess's practical irritation: food, chairs, guests, and the habits of unreliable young people must all be brought into alignment.

When the gong calls everyone to dinner, they come in from separate rooms and separate thoughts. The gathering is not instantly harmonious. Some people are tired; others are socially uneasy; several are silently judging the company. Charles Tansley is cramped by the thought that he is being patronized. Lily Briscoe notices that Mrs. Ramsay expects her to provide the kind of sociability that keeps a dinner from collapsing into silence. Lily dislikes that demand. She also understands how much labor has gone into the occasion. Her resistance and her sympathy can exist together.

The table is arranged with light, flowers, food, voices, and a dish carefully prepared in the kitchen. Mrs. Ramsay admires a bowl of fruit which seems briefly to make the fragments of the evening hold together. The meal changes as people begin talking: a guest says the wrong thing, another makes an awkward attempt to recover, conversation wanders into poetry and politics. Paul and Minta return, and the sense spreads that they are engaged. Mrs. Ramsay feels a kind of triumph. She has wanted this union. But the reader has been allowed enough access to other minds to know that her designs are not the whole of the young couple's future.

Lily looks at Mrs. Ramsay across the table. Her hostess can make a group feel, for a time, like a group. One cannot see all the work beneath that effect; it appears to happen naturally. Lily is drawn to this capacity, even as she refuses the idea that her own painting should yield to marriage or domestic duty. Her canvas waits outside the dinner room, unfinished. The painting and the dinner are rival and allied kinds of making. A dinner vanishes as soon as it is over, but its form may persist as a remembered relation among people. A painting can outlast its maker, but only if she finds a form that is not merely borrowed from somebody else.

The meal is not a utopia. Mrs. Ramsay's instinct to put others at ease sometimes depends on asking women to absorb men's discomfort. Tansley's exclusion is partly a matter of class, but it does not cancel his contemptuous dismissal of women's art. Mr. Ramsay is capable of affection and humor, yet he makes the emotional weather of the household depend heavily on his own need to be assured. The scene becomes beautiful without becoming morally innocent. In its beauty there is work, inequality, and constant repair.

Later the guests disperse. Mrs. Ramsay moves toward the nursery and finds a domestic emergency that would be invisible in a lofty summary of family life. A boar's skull hangs on the wall. Little Cam is frightened by it, but James does not want it removed. Their mother wraps the skull in her shawl, turning it into something the frightened child can bear while assuring James that the object remains. It is a small feat of imaginative diplomacy. She does not insist that one child's fear must cancel the other's attachment. Her solution changes how the thing is seen, not what it is.

James still asks whether they will go to the lighthouse tomorrow. His mother will not promise the weather, but she preserves the possibility. For James the trip has become a test of whether his parents can be trusted, whether adult truth must always arrive as defeat. For his father it is a matter of forecast. For his mother it is now one of the many hopes she cannot guarantee, even though she has devoted herself to making other things possible.

While she attends to the children, Mrs. Ramsay thinks about the dinner and the people who have gathered. Paul and Minta's prospective marriage reassures her that something she has helped begin will continue beyond her. But a feeling of continuity does not make her certain about the world. The novel repeatedly allows her mind to rise toward an apparent final statement and then retreat from it. A moment of happiness may feel complete; immediately the thought of its ending presses against it.

When she rejoins her husband, the evening narrows to two people sitting together. Mr. Ramsay reads. She is with her knitting. They have lived long enough together that conversation is not always the medium of understanding. He wants words from her. He knows she loves him but also feels she rarely says so in the direct way he wishes. She is reluctant to perform an avowal on command. She watches him, reads a little, considers the room and the darkness, and says something about the weather. She feels she has communicated something without a formal declaration. Whether the final assurance belongs entirely to her own perspective is a question close readers and textual editors debate. The end of the section turns on the fragile possibility of mutual understanding between people who need different kinds of proof.

Woolf has given us an entire evening without forcing it toward a conventional climax. The engagement may matter later, but it does not resolve the lives at the table. The dinner is a fleeting achievement; the nursery scene a minor triumph; the marriage a mixture of intimacy and disappointment. Nothing stops time. No one knows who will die. The first section closes while the household still exists, while the children are children, while Mrs. Ramsay can reach for the next domestic task.

It is tempting, knowing the novel's reputation, to call this dinner a monument built in advance of grief. The characters do not experience it that way. They are simply trying to get through an evening, perhaps to make it pleasant. This difference between lived immediacy and the meaning a later reader gives it is central to the story. At the table, nobody has a privileged view of what will follow. They possess neither a map of the decade ahead nor the certainty that this ordinary night will come to stand for an entire lost world.
