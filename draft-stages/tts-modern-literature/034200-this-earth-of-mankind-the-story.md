# This Earth of Mankind — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A young man has been told that education will enlarge his world. He studies European science, reads newspapers, learns to write in Dutch, and discovers that the modern age can transmit messages across oceans. He has reason to be proud. His teachers are preparing him for a future in which knowledge seems to carry its own authority. Then he enters a household that teaches him how little knowledge matters when a court has decided that some people do not count as equals.

His name is Minke. We meet him in Java near the beginning of the twentieth century, when the islands that will one day be Indonesia are governed as the Dutch East Indies. He comes from Javanese nobility and attends an elite Dutch-language school, an educational opportunity almost entirely denied to people classified by the colonial state as Native. His origin gives him certain privileges within Javanese society. It does not make him equal to Europeans in the colonial order.

Minke is intelligent, ambitious, self-conscious, and often impatient with inherited deference. He is delighted by discoveries and inventions that appear to make the world newer every year. He also writes for newspapers under a pseudonym. Print promises him something remarkable: his thoughts might reach people who have never seen his face. For now, he wants to use the tools of modernity without giving up the advantages of his upbringing. He has not yet discovered how directly these aims can conflict.

A fellow student, Robert Suurhof, invites Minke to meet a young woman said to be extraordinarily beautiful. The invitation leads to a household outside Surabaya, on a large property at Wonokromo. The people who live there will change his life.

## The family at Wonokromo

The young woman is Annelies Mellema. Her father is a Dutchman, Herman Mellema. Her mother is a Javanese woman called Nyai Ontosoroh. Annelies has an older brother, Robert Mellema, whose contempt for Native people includes contempt for much of his own family. The household is wealthy and the business surrounding it is carefully managed. But the distribution of respect within it bears little relation to the actual work that makes the estate prosper.

Minke first notices Annelies. She is beautiful, young, and painfully lonely. He is drawn to her immediately, and she seems relieved to have the companionship of someone who treats her as a person rather than as a problem to be administered. Their closeness begins awkwardly. Minke can be presumptuous, and Annelies is more vulnerable than he initially understands. He will have to learn how much of her emotional life is hidden behind the elegance of the house.

Then he meets Nyai. The title marks a socially precarious position: she is Herman's concubine, not his legally recognized wife. Outsiders expect a woman in this position to be uneducated and dependent. Nyai surprises Minke by speaking Dutch fluently, reading, discussing literature, and managing the property with extraordinary competence. She runs the dairy business, understands its accounts and employees, and can meet visitors without shrinking before them. Herman, by contrast, has become erratic, embittered, and largely absent from the work of the estate.

Nyai is not the woman whom Minke's education has taught him to expect. She seems more modern in practical judgment than many of the Europeans who claim modernity as their birthright. Yet the society around her considers her less respectable and gives her fewer legal protections than people whose achievements are much smaller.

The reason for her position is part of the household's history. Born Sanikem, Nyai was sold into concubinage by her father while still a young teenager. His ambitions for his own career mattered more to him than his daughter's wishes. Herman brought her into his home, and she decided that if she could not choose the conditions of her life, she would at least learn the skills needed to survive them. She studied language and business. She gradually became indispensable. The estate's success came to depend on her labor and judgment.

For a time her partnership with Herman held a form of domestic happiness. But Herman had an existing wife and a son in the Netherlands, facts he had concealed. When that son, Maurits, confronted him, the household began to unravel. Herman withdrew from family life. Nyai was left to hold the work and children together. She resolved that Annelies should have the freedom to choose a husband rather than be disposed of as she herself had been.

This history matters because the house is not merely a romantic setting. It is a place where love, coercion, commerce, race, and law have become knotted together. Nyai has built something real. She is still not allowed to possess the dignity that should accompany it.

## Learning to love, and learning what love cannot settle

Minke begins spending time at Wonokromo and eventually moves into the household. He is fascinated by Nyai's knowledge and by Annelies's attachment to him. The latter is so isolated that his presence changes her health and mood. He finds this devotion moving, but it also gives him a responsibility he does not always know how to handle.

Annelies reveals a history of sexual abuse by her brother. This deepens our understanding of her dependence on Minke and her need to feel safe. The story does not make the abuse a romantic explanation for all that she is; it shows the dangers within a supposedly respectable family, and the way a young woman can be trapped by the very people who ought to protect her. Robert's hostility toward Minke grows more threatening. He treats his European ancestry as a claim to superiority and resents a Native young man who refuses to be properly subordinate.

Minke has friends outside this household. Jean Marais, a French veteran and artist who lost a leg in colonial warfare, speaks to him with a worldly perspective learned at great cost. Jean's family history does not fit the European stories of benevolent civilization that Minke hears in school. A teacher, Magda Peters, recognizes Minke's intelligence and encourages him as a writer. She is an ally, although being an enlightened teacher does not grant her control over the institutions that employ her.

Minke's writing under a European-sounding pseudonym gives him a route into public life. Readers can admire a piece of prose before learning that the writer is Native. Once that identity becomes known, admiration and prejudice collide. His school performance is excellent, but association with a concubine's household invites gossip. Officials can praise the value of education while treating his private conduct as proof that he does not belong among respectable graduates. Minke slowly realizes that merit is not the same thing as acceptance.

His relationship with Annelies deepens. They begin to imagine a future together. Nyai regards him as a man who might honor her daughter's wishes, though she is painfully aware of the dangers surrounding them. The private drama seems to have the shape of a marriage plot: two young people have found one another despite hostile relatives and social gossip. If only they can persevere, perhaps they can make an independent household.

But there are forces in this story that cannot be defeated by affection.

## A death brings the household to court

Herman Mellema dies in a nearby brothel under suspicious circumstances. The family is dragged into a criminal inquiry that becomes public spectacle. Reporters spread the scandal. Minke's movements and his relationship with the women at Wonokromo become matters for discussion. The categories of respectability that once operated through gossip are now enforced by courtroom procedure.

Nyai and Minke must navigate questioning that treats Native people as morally suspect and Europeans as entitled to define the terms of judgment. Nyai answers with dignity, and when she speaks about her own history she exposes the violence concealed behind the word concubine. Her ability to address the court in Dutch and her command of the facts do not cause the institution to recognize her as a social equal. The courtroom can hear a person's argument while denying her the standing that would give the argument force.

Minke's school expels him amid the controversy, though support for him and his demonstrated ability eventually allow him to return under conditions. He completes his examinations with exceptionally high marks. His achievement is genuine. It confirms that the hierarchy of intellect taught by racial prejudice is false. But the same achievement also sharpens an uncomfortable question: why did he need to prove exceptional talent to claim the ordinary freedom of being judged fairly?

Minke and Annelies marry. Their wedding gathers friends, acquaintances, and some of the people who have watched the young man grow. For a short time the celebration appears to fulfill the promise of education, love, and personal courage. Minke has stood up to prejudice, established his ability, and won the affection of a woman who wants to live with him. Nyai has managed to give her daughter a choice she herself did not receive.

Then the law intervenes again, this time with a cruelty the criminal proceedings could only foreshadow.

## The marriage the law refuses to see

Maurits Mellema, Herman's son from his legally recognized Dutch marriage, asserts his claims to the estate and to the family's legal affairs. The Dutch colonial legal system privileges that recognized European lineage. Nyai's years of management do not translate into the rights that her work has earned. Annelies's position becomes especially dangerous. She is treated as a minor subject to legal guardianship and to decisions taken far away from the people with whom she lives.

For Minke, the blow is intimate and political at once. He is Annelies's husband in the life they have made together. The authorities refuse to recognize the marriage as a sufficient barrier to the decision being imposed upon her. A colonial court orders that Annelies be sent to the Netherlands under the guardianship arranged through her father's European family. Her wishes, her mother's wishes, and Minke's wishes do not decide the matter.

Minke looks for legal help. Nyai looks for allies. They appeal to officials, discuss strategies with attorneys, and mobilize public opinion. Minke writes to newspapers, including readers he can reach through Malay rather than only through the Dutch-language channels that once made him proud. His ability to write has begun to take on a different purpose. He is no longer simply demonstrating that a Native student can master European civilization. He is asking what use civilization is if its laws make an ordinary family powerless.

The appeals fail. Annelies becomes ill and withdrawn under the strain. Nyai has spent years building the competence and confidence that should prevent her children from suffering her fate. She now watches a version of that fate come for her daughter. What was done to Sanikem by a father with social ambitions will be done to Annelies by an international legal order claiming authority and procedure.

Darsam, a devoted member of the household, and other local defenders attempt to prevent the removal. There is resistance and bloodshed, answered by colonial military force. The willingness to fight does not turn this into an adventurous rescue. The defenders are outmatched, and neither love nor courage can change the order issued by the court.

At last, Annelies is taken from Wonokromo. She leaves in the care of the people who have been given authority over her, not those to whom she has entrusted her life. Minke and Nyai can only watch. She is to cross the sea to the Netherlands, a country that has already exercised enormous power over her without ever needing to know her as a person.

## The end of the first book

There are three things to hold in mind as the story closes. The first is the marriage: it happened in the emotional and social lives of the people concerned, and the law's refusal to recognize it does not make their commitment imaginary. The second is Nyai's labor: she learned, built, and protected, yet the rights that should have accompanied those acts were denied to her. The third is Minke's education: it did not deceive him in everything. Science, language, and the press are powerful. But he has learned that being equipped with those powers does not guarantee that the authorities will allow him to use them freely.

The novel finishes with defeat, not with the later history of Minke's political career. *This Earth of Mankind* is the first volume of the four-book Buru Quartet. It is important to stop where this book stops. The expulsion of Annelies is not a temporary obstacle that the story can resolve by turning one more page. It is the event that forces Minke to see the colonial order as something more than a collection of prejudiced individuals.

At the beginning he trusted education to bring him into the modern world. At the end he knows that the modern world contains institutions capable of making injustice orderly, articulate, and legally binding. The same young man can still write. But he will no longer be able to write from the comfortable assumption that knowledge and justice naturally move together.

## Editorial notes

This plot lecture covers *Bumi Manusia* (1980), **only the first** of the four Buru novels. It does not borrow Annelies's later fate or Minke's subsequent political activity from other volumes. Concubinage and colonial legal categories are situated in the novel's fictional case, not presented as a complete statement of Dutch East Indies statutory law.
