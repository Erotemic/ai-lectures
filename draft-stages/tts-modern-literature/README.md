# 20th-Century Literature: Story, Then Analysis

The track currently covers **forty literary works** (several receive extended multi-lecture treatment). For each work, complete plot narration precedes criticism: usually one continuous story lecture, with extended multi-volume treatment where the work itself warrants it. The story account introduces the people, setting, main events, and ending; it does not require that the listener has read the book. The analysis can then explore form, historical context, and competing critical readings without explaining the plot again.

## Science fiction as literature: Sagan and Vonnegut

- `018200` then `019000`: Carl Sagan, *Contact* (1985): a full story orientation to the **novel** (five international travelers, the base-eleven pi ending), followed by analysis of scientific evidence, witness testimony, science and religion, public institutions, and communication. Do not substitute the 1997 film plot.
- `019200` then `020000`: Kurt Vonnegut, *Slaughterhouse-Five* (1969): the authorial frame, Dresden, Billy Pilgrim's life and reported Tralfamadorian encounters, followed by analysis of traumatic narrative time, fatalism, black comedy, and historical memory. ## Mainstream twentieth-century landmarks: Fitzgerald, Orwell, Herbert

The next three complete pairs deliberately strengthen mainstream and genre-defining coverage without undoing earlier geographic and formal breadth:

- `020200` then `021000`: F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* (1925). The story follows Nick's account from his arrival at West Egg to the deaths of Myrtle, George and Gatsby and the nearly empty funeral. The analysis examines time, narrative mediation, wealth, class impunity, racism and antisemitic stereotypes in the narrated world, and the contradictions in Gatsby's dream.
- `021200` then `022000`: George Orwell, *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949). The story follows Winston's Ministry work, private record, relationship with Julia, the trap, torture and final capitulation. The analysis distinguishes evidence from command, explores historical roots and language, and treats the appendix's historical tense as debatable rather than proof of an offstage revolution.
- `022200` then `023000`: Frank Herbert, *Dune* (1965). The single story lecture covers **the first novel**, not its film versions or sequels, from the imperial trap through Paul and Jessica's Fremen lives, the spice monopoly and Paul's victory with its unresolved religious consequences. The analysis treats ecology, political economy, Bene Gesserit engineering, Indigenous and Islamic resonances, narrative perspective and the danger of a messianic leader.

In each case, narration is a complete spoiler-containing account before interpretation. There is no runtime or word quota: the plots determine how much explanation and pacing they require. Later books and adaptations are not silently imported into first-novel summaries. The pack is wholly within Agent A's authoring boundary.

## Editorial principles

- No target, minimum, or maximum duration. One complete story can be short or long, according to its needs; do not split to hit a time box.
- Story orientation before analysis, including spoilers and an intelligible ending. Do not assume the listener knows character names or context.
- Original expository narration, not a narrated copy of copyrighted prose. Preserve the identity of the plot and avoid invented dialogue attributed to the author.
- Distinguish historical facts, plot details, critical arguments, and present-day synthesis. Culture, language, caste, colonialism, and gender need specific treatment rather than a single global template.
- Use sub-thousand numeric IDs for insertions while retaining existing analysis IDs and URLs.
- `spoken_words` and duration are descriptive measurements, never stylistic constraints. `track.json` lists only manuscripts that exist and can be played.
- Source catalog and sidecars are outside the spoken region. Every source ID must resolve and every claim anchor must match the extracted narration exactly.

## Further blind spots and familiar classics

Representation is not a tally to complete mechanically. Important omissions remain in Indigenous, South and Southeast Asian, African-language, Middle Eastern, and Eastern European traditions, as well as other kinds of genre fiction and experimental prose. The next selections should prioritize documented literary influence, major formal achievement, cultural significance and lasting critical study. Geographic or demographic range is valuable but is not a selection quota. Landmark gaps should be evaluated against those criteria before adding further exploratory works; every work needs its own plot and source review. There is no runtime or word-count requirement.

## Special treatment: Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings*

**`026200`–`027200` — eleven dedicated lectures.** Tolkien's novel is one continuous work internally arranged in six books and published in three volumes. We use those volume boundaries as natural narrative divisions, rather than splitting at an arbitrary speaking duration:

- `026200`: an accessible entrance to Middle-earth without requiring *The Hobbit* or lore familiarity
- `026300`: *The Fellowship of the Ring*, complete story of Books I–II, including Bombadil and the Barrow-downs
- `026400`: *The Two Towers*, Books III–IV, both the Rohan/Isengard and Frodo/Sam branches
- `026500`: *The Return of the King*, Books V–VI, including the Paths of the Dead in the novel, the Scouring of the Shire, and the Grey Havens
- `026600`: the Ring and the corruption of apparently virtuous political aims
- `026700`: mercy, Gollum, providence and Frodo's limit, grounded in Tolkien's correspondence
- `026800`: languages, ruins and how deep time becomes perceptible
- `026900`: fellowship, class, service and friendship across inherited hostility
- `027000`: war, environmental injury, the Scouring and the different possibilities of return
- `027100`: kingship, gender, peoples, the moral status of Orcs and the representation of outsiders
- `027200`: the creation of the book, philology, interlacing, fantasy inheritance and Tolkien's rejection of one-to-one allegory

This is a single literary work, not three separate novels. The narrator introduces geography and characters when needed; the plot lectures contain full spoilers and run in sequence *before* criticism. The analysis lectures are thematically self-contained after that context. No rigid length target or word quota was used. Metadata lengths describe the files after authoring.

## Major-modernist landmark: James Joyce, *Ulysses* (036200–037300)

A dedicated **twelve-lecture section** adds *Ulysses* (1922), bringing the total to 37 works. The five original-prose narrative lectures together retell all 18 episodes, from Stephen's morning in the tower to Bloom's return and Molly's final monologue. Seven additional lectures analyze its shifting narrative styles, Homeric architecture, Bloom and Stephen, women's voices, political belonging and prejudice, close-reading strategies, and publication/censorship/legacy. No runtime target governs the section.

The catalog section `ulysses-a-day-in-dublin` preserves the narrative-before-analysis sequence. Episode names are commonly used critical labels, not a claim about printed chapter headings in the first edition. Molly's monologue is Joyce's composed fiction, not Nora Barnacle's transcribed voice. The first book was published by Sylvia Beach in Paris on 2 February 1922; the U.S. prosecution of *The Little Review* editors was a distinct 1921 event and the Woolsey importation decision was in 1933. The 1984 Gabler text is a significant edited edition, not the only uncontested text.

Source, editorial, and lesson-level distinctions: `notes/ulysses-special-section-036200-037300.md` and `metadata/036200.json`–`metadata/037300.json`.

## Major-modernist landmark: Marcel Proust, *In Search of Lost Time* (038100–039400)

A **fourteen-lecture section** explores one continuous seven-volume novel. Seven original spoken story narrations first cover each volume in order, without assuming familiarity with the text; seven sustained critical lectures then analyze involuntary memory and habit, jealous desire and evidence, class and the Dreyfus Affair, sexuality and secrecy, fictional artists, narrative architecture, and the history of composition, publication, and translation. The section adds one work to the canon: **38 works, 107 lectures**.

The final three volumes were published **posthumously**, and the sixth volume has substantial variants among posthumous printings and a subsequently recovered author-corrected typescript. The editorial note discusses this evidence, keeps the narrator distinct from Proust, distinguishes fictional speculation about Albertine from established actions, and avoids confusing the deceased narrator's grandmother with his still-living mother. The 14 source sidecars identify individually grounded claims; `notes/proust-special-section-038100-039400.md` records the source trail and interpretive limitations. This addition prioritizes demonstrated literary significance rather than representation quotas. No fixed spoken length or duration was imposed.

## The Sound and the Fury: Faulkner landmark section (`040100`–`041200`)

William Faulkner's *The Sound and the Fury* (1929) is a central work of American literary modernism. This twelve-lecture treatment is selected for its historical influence, formal experimentation, and sustained critical importance. It does not convert the four sections into one authoritative family testimony. The first four lectures follow Faulkner's published section order; a fifth assembles the family chronology. The seven studies then treat competing experiences of time, Caddy's absent narrative voice, Quentin's crisis and masculinity, Dilsey and racial labor, Southern political economy, close reading, and textual history.

|---|---|
| `040100`–`040400` | Full plot, one lecture for each of the four dated sections, including the ending |
| `040500` | Chronological retelling and names: Quentin the brother versus Miss Quentin the daughter |
| `040600` | Time, sensory memory, psychological obsession, accounting and Easter time |
| `040700` | Caddy's absent first-person viewpoint and the limits of the brothers' accounts |
| `040800` | Quentin's shame, honor ideology, false confession and psychological crisis |

The main textual witness is the original 1929 four-section novel; the 1945 Compson Appendix is a distinct later composition, not part of the original ending. ## Virginia Woolf, *To the Lighthouse*: modernist landmark (041400–042500)

A dedicated **twelve-lecture section** treats Woolf’s 1927 novel as a complete formal and human work. Four original story narrations follow *The Window* across the afternoon and dinner, *Time Passes* across the unoccupied house and wartime losses, and *The Lighthouse* through the boat journey and Lily’s picture. Eight analyses then develop Woolf’s narrative time and shifting perspectives, Mrs Ramsay’s domestic authority, Lily’s artistic independence, Mr Ramsay’s epistemic and emotional needs, the interlude’s physical history, gender/class/empire, close-reading practice, and the novel’s actual publication and editorial history. All major deaths and the ending appear **before** the analyses; no reader is assumed to know the book.

Primary witnesses are the three public-domain 1927 American-edition parts at Wikisource; a Cambridge synopsis confirms the asymmetric three-part architecture. The original British and American editions differ, and the 1930 British impression includes further authorial revisions. The ending of *The Window* is treated as potentially focalized through Mrs Ramsay rather than certified access to her husband’s thoughts; this is especially important when reading different editions. Mrs McNab, Mrs Bast, and George are credited for the practical restoration of the house. The narrator’s memories are not simply Woolf’s biography, and a fictional guest’s reading is not the author’s judgment. Source IDs and exact claim anchors are in `metadata/sources.json` and `metadata/041400.json` through `metadata/042500.json`; the editorial dossier is `notes/woolf-to-the-lighthouse-041400-042500.md`. No fixed spoken length, audio, or binary images are included. The catalog now holds **40 works and 131 lectures**.
