# Ficciones: Pierre Menard — A Book Written Twice

Imagine reading a memorial essay about a recently deceased writer. The essayist wants to restore the writer's reputation, rescue neglected work from vulgar misunderstandings, and establish the greatness of an almost impossibly ambitious project. The title promises an author of Don Quixote. But the author's name is not Miguel de Cervantes. It is Pierre Menard.

Borges constructs the entire story as an account of Menard's intellectual career. The narrator catalogs the visible publications: scholarly articles, experiments in versification, and learned contributions that imply an active, cultivated literary life. Another observer has supposedly misrepresented the list. The tone is that of the jealous defender of a literary reputation, complete with social names and carefully judged praise. This is already comic, because the narrator's conviction that he is being scrupulously fair makes his snobbery increasingly visible.

Then he turns from the public works to the private undertaking. Menard has been writing passages of Don Quixote. That statement might suggest a modern translation, an adaptation, a literary hoax, or even a transcription. The narrator rejects all of these. Menard's aim is to create certain passages that are verbally identical to Cervantes's original. He does not wish to copy them as an exercise or to simulate a lost manuscript. He wants to arrive at exactly the same words through a separate act of authorship, as a French writer living centuries after Cervantes.

There are two possible methods. One is to become Cervantes by reconstructing his language, worldview, and historical conditions. This seems promising in theory and nearly impossible in practice. More important, Menard rejects it as too easy, a kind of theatrical impersonation. The harder method is to remain Pierre Menard: to have his own life, to inhabit the modern world, and nevertheless to write a portion of the seventeenth-century novel word for word. The project's apparent absurdity is also its precision. Menard is not trying to improve the words. He is testing what it would mean for the same sentence to be produced by two different histories.

The narrator then performs the experiment in reading. He places the two versions side by side and insists that the modern one is richer. The letters have not changed. What has changed is the entire field of associations the narrator brings to them. An attitude to history that seems unsurprising in Cervantes is suddenly interpreted as startling when attributed to a twentieth-century writer familiar with modern philosophy. Another passage acquires an ironic or philosophical weight from the fact that it appears to be the deliberate choice of a later author. The comedy comes from a reviewer whose confidence increases as his evidence for textual difference dwindles to zero.

The joke is not that words mean nothing. It is that the same material words can enter a different conversation. A reader who knows the supposed date, country, and intellectual history of an author reads differently from a reader who knows none of those things. The narrator's excesses can be ridiculous; the underlying question remains serious. We rarely encounter a book as a naked string of sentences. We meet it inside a story about its making, its author, and its audience.

At the end, the narrator recommends an audacious technique: reading an earlier book as though a later writer had written it. The technique invents relations between works that chronology seems to prohibit. It can be an exercise in insight or a mechanism of wishful projection. Borges does not rescue us by providing a sober critic who settles the dispute. The ridiculous admirer has discovered a genuine pressure point in literary scholarship.

The story appeared in 1939, and its form mattered. Instead of giving Menard a conventional dramatic scene, Borges writes a scholarly obituary for an invented achievement and trusts that the apparent apparatus of serious criticism will make the fiction possible. We can laugh at its absurdity without ignoring what it teaches. The act of writing may end, but a text does not finish acquiring histories when its first author puts down the pen.
