# The Stranger — Part Two: Judgment, the Chaplain, and the Unfinished Life

The second part of The Stranger begins after Meursault has killed a man on the beach. He is arrested, questioned, and eventually placed on trial. It would be natural to expect an inquiry centered on the dead man: who he was, why he was at the spring, what threat he posed, and what Meursault intended. Instead Camus gives us a judicial process increasingly preoccupied with the accused man's behavior at his mother's funeral and with his refusal to express the feelings that officials believe he should have had.

During preliminary questioning a magistrate attempts to draw out remorse and religious feeling. Meursault describes what occurred, but he cannot offer the kind of moral transformation the magistrate seeks. The magistrate confronts him with a crucifix and finds his lack of religious belief baffling. Meursault's lawyer is concerned that the apparent indifference to his mother's death will damage the defense. The case begins to take a recognizable shape: the defendant is judged not only for the act charged but for the kind of person the authorities believe the act reveals.

Prison changes Meursault's daily experience. At first he misses the sea, cigarettes, women, and physical freedom. Marie visits, but their contact is restricted. Over time he learns ways of enduring confinement through memory and minute observation. Ordinary habits that had seemed like the entire substance of life become memories of a world no longer available to him. The movement is quiet but significant. A man who once expected nothing in particular from tomorrow now measures the passing hours in a cell. Freedom was not an abstract idea when he possessed it, but losing it exposes the importance of simple bodily possibilities.

The trial turns acquaintances into witnesses. The director and caretaker of his mother's home are asked about his conduct during the funeral. Marie's account of their swimming and movie outing appears in the courtroom. Raymond's affairs with the woman he mistreated enter the case, along with details of the beach conflict. Céleste offers an account more sympathetic to Meursault. Testimony does concern the shooting, yet the emotional organization of the prosecution suggests that his social nonconformity is itself a kind of crime.

The prosecutor assembles a story with beginning, motive, and moral meaning. A son who did not weep at his mother's funeral, who went to a comedy soon afterward, and who seems unmoved by conventional expressions of guilt is presented as someone capable of terrible violence. The narrative may persuade because it fits familiar assumptions about how an innocent or repentant person should behave. But a convincing story about character is not identical with proof of a particular intention at a particular moment. The court's transformation of behavior into evidence is part of the novel's critique.

This critique must not make us forget the victim. Meursault did kill a man. The case is not simply the persecution of a harmless eccentric for drinking coffee at a vigil. The legal proceedings expose a serious tension between the facts of the act, the social expectations imposed on the accused, and the racial hierarchy of colonial Algeria. The dead man has no proper name in Meursault's narration and little presence as someone with his own future, relationships, and grief-stricken family. The more insistently the courtroom analyzes Meursault's soul, the less space remains for the other person's life.

Meursault is sentenced to death. He confronts the prospect of execution and the uncertainty of appeals or last-minute changes. The narrative does not depict him being executed. It remains with a living man trying to understand what it means to know that his life may be ended by others. He imagines the public square, the machine, the crowd, and the possibility that any condemned person must hope for an exception. The temporal scale that once seemed flat has contracted. Each night, each morning, each unconfirmed hope becomes consequential.

A chaplain seeks to visit and offer religious consolation. Meursault resists. When they finally confront one another, the chaplain insists on possibilities of meaning and hope that Meursault will not accept. The encounter erupts into anger. Meursault rejects a promise of another life as an answer to the present one. He is not offering a tidy textbook argument in a seminar. He is a frightened, furious man who has been sentenced to die, unwilling to let a stranger supply the feelings or beliefs he is supposed to possess.

After the outburst, he experiences a kind of reconciliation with the world. The universe does not explain his fate, congratulate him, or promise to protect him. Its indifference no longer seems to require a lie of consolation. The night, the sky, his bodily life, and the memory of his mother become present to him in a different register. Some readers find release in this acceptance; others hear self-absorption and a continuing moral blindness. Both responses have textual grounds. Acceptance of one's mortality does not erase responsibility for a man's death.

Meursault's final wish is for a hostile crowd to witness his execution. This is not the execution itself, and it is not evidence that the victim has received justice in any simple sense. It is a disturbing desire to meet public judgment without pretending that a crowd's approval is possible. He imagines a final contact with others through their hatred. The title's separation remains in place: he is outside the social forms used to judge him, yet his own language has kept another man outside the circle of individual recognition.

The novel closes before the machinery of the sentence completes its work. That choice is essential. The final event is a change in Meursault's understanding, not a reported death. It leaves the reader with three truths that do not cancel each other: a person has been murdered; a court has misused conventions of grief and belief to construct an identity for the murderer; and the murderer, awaiting death, has encountered the limits of the meanings he once took for granted. The Stranger is enduring because none of those truths can comfortably absorb the others.
