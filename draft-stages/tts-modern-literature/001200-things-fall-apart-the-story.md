# Things Fall Apart — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine arriving in a village where a man's reputation is known before he speaks. People remember the wrestlers he defeated, the harvests he has managed, the titles he has earned. They also remember the father whose life he has tried desperately not to repeat. This is Umuofia, a group of Igbo villages in what is now southeastern Nigeria, before British colonial rule has taken hold there. The man is Okonkwo. We will follow him through the end of Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart, including its ending. For now, his life is full of work, responsibilities, relationships, and rules that he believes he understands.

Okonkwo was not born to wealth. His father, Unoka, enjoyed music, conversation, and the pleasures of living, but he left debts and little property. Okonkwo remembers the embarrassment of being associated with a man other people could dismiss as unsuccessful. He sets about becoming everything his father was not. As a young man he defeats a renowned wrestler, Amalinze the Cat. He becomes a formidable farmer, cultivating yams with a stubbornness that nearly destroys him during a season of terrible weather. He builds a compound with houses for his wives and children. He earns standing among the men who make decisions for the village.

Nothing about this success makes him feel safe. He fears the appearance of weakness so fiercely that affection often comes out as anger. His eldest son, Nwoye, does not resemble his father's ideal of toughness. The boy responds to stories told by his mother and to gentler kinds of companionship. Okonkwo scolds and strikes him, hoping pressure will create a harder child. His second wife, Ekwefi, has endured the deaths of many babies; her surviving daughter Ezinma is the child who can talk to Okonkwo with a directness few others dare. He wishes she had been born a son. Ezinma is close to her mother, and both parents are frightened when she falls ill. Their worry is not an abstract debate about strength. It is the immediate terror of losing someone they love.

Umuofia has ways of deciding disputes and answering injuries from other communities. When a woman from Umuofia is killed in the neighboring village of Mbaino, the people demand compensation. Mbaino sends a girl to become a wife for the dead woman's husband and a boy named Ikemefuna to live in Umuofia. Okonkwo is ordered to take the boy into his household. Ikemefuna does not know when he will be allowed to return home. He grows close to Nwoye, teaching him games and work, and Nwoye flourishes in his company. After three years, the stranger has become a member of the household in every way that seems to matter to the children.

At the New Yam Festival, the year's first harvest is an occasion for thanks and visiting. Okonkwo's impatience makes him an uncomfortable companion at a celebration that other members of his family enjoy. At a wrestling match the village gathers to watch young men test themselves before cheering neighbors. These moments explain why the people around Okonkwo cannot be understood only through his anger: they have routines, pleasures, rivalries, and customs of their own. When an egwugwu hearing concerns a husband whose wife has returned to her relatives after his violence, the judges hear the parties and direct him to make peace rather than presenting his force as unquestionable.

There are festivals, wrestling matches, visits, debates, marriages, and judgments. During the Week of Peace, a sacred period before planting, Okonkwo beats one of his wives and is punished for violating a communal prohibition. He objects to being judged for a domestic matter, but his neighbors insist that the peace of the community is not his private possession. When families bring a domestic dispute before masked ancestral judges called the egwugwu, the hearing is elaborate and public. Villagers listen as the representatives of the ancestors question the quarrel and deliver a decision. The ceremony is frightening, theatrical, and thoroughly woven into ordinary social life. The community is not without disagreement, and its traditions are not reducible to Okonkwo's temper.

At home, Ekwefi tells Ezinma a story about a cunning tortoise who tricks birds into sharing their feast. The child knows the rhythms of her mother's voice. The two live through moments of comfort as well as danger. Chielo, a priestess who can speak casually to Ekwefi in the daytime, later arrives in the voice of the Oracle of the Hills and Caves to demand Ezinma's presence. She carries the child into the night. Ekwefi follows despite the danger, keeping the moving figures in sight through the villages and toward the shrine. Okonkwo eventually appears, having come to guard them. He does not deliver a speech about his love. He is simply there when they need him.

Now the story returns to Ikemefuna. A decision comes from the Oracle: the boy must die. The respected elder Ezeudu warns Okonkwo not to join the execution because Ikemefuna calls him father. Okonkwo nevertheless accompanies the men who lead the unsuspecting boy into the forest. Ikemefuna has been told that he is being returned to his own family. When one man strikes him with a machete, he runs toward Okonkwo for protection. Okonkwo kills him rather than allow the others to see him hesitate. He returns to a house where the children will eventually understand that someone they loved has disappeared forever.

Nwoye senses that something in his world has broken. Okonkwo is disturbed too. He cannot eat or sleep properly and tries to dismiss his grief as softness. His friend Obierika asks why he had to participate in the killing when the Oracle had not commanded his personal involvement. There is no easy answer, and their disagreement is allowed to remain. Okonkwo resumes work, but the village does not become uncomplicated again.

The next great public gathering is Ezeudu's funeral. His titles and standing require a large ceremony. Men fire guns, drums sound, and the village assembles. Okonkwo's gun explodes accidentally and kills one of the dead man's sons. This is not an intentional murder, but it is still a serious offense against the earth. The punishment is exile for seven years. Okonkwo and his family must leave Umuofia. His compound is destroyed as a ritual consequence of the accidental death, and he must begin again in the land of his mother's people. He goes carrying what can be saved, leaving behind the village in which he had built almost everything that mattered to his public identity.

In exile, Okonkwo must begin rebuilding the standing he had spent his adult life earning.

Okonkwo and his family arrive in Mbanta, the village of his mother's relatives. They are not abandoned. His uncle Uchendu and the other kinfolk help him find land and begin farming again. They understand the burden of exile, although Okonkwo can hardly bear it. For seven years he is cut off from the status, household, and plans he had built in Umuofia. He imagines returning with enough wealth to reclaim the life that was interrupted.

Uchendu talks to him about the meaning of seeking refuge among his mother's people. A child knows the father's household in times of prosperity, the older man says in substance, but turns toward the mother in suffering. Okonkwo hears the words, but consolation does not easily penetrate his resentment. He works hard. His wives and children make a life in Mbanta. His old friend Obierika visits from Umuofia, brings money earned from selling Okonkwo's yams, and reports what is happening outside the village.

The news begins with a story from Abame, another Igbo village. A stranger with white skin appeared there on an iron horse, which the people had never seen before. The Oracle predicted that he would bring destruction, and villagers killed him and tied up his bicycle. Later, armed white men arrived and attacked the village when people had assembled in the marketplace. Many were slaughtered. The story is terrifying partly because the people receiving it do not yet know the larger system that brought the strangers there. A single encounter has turned into mass violence.

Then missionaries arrive in Mbanta. They announce a religion centered on one God and tell the villagers that the many gods they worship are false. Some people laugh. Others ask questions or quarrel over the missionaries' claims. The visitors are granted a piece of land in the Evil Forest, where people believe malevolent forces will destroy the church. The missionaries survive, and their survival gives their message an unexpected force. People who had been pushed to the margins of village life begin to join them. A faith that makes room for those whom the community has treated as contaminated, rejected, or unimportant can seem like a different future.

Nwoye hears their singing and begins to visit. He has never stopped carrying memories of Ikemefuna. He is also troubled by the abandonment of newborn twins under an old custom. The missionaries' words do not answer every question he has, but their community offers him a place where he is not required to become his father. When Okonkwo learns that his son has joined the Christians, he reacts with fury and violence. Nwoye leaves. He will later use the Christian name Isaac. Okonkwo treats the change as a personal betrayal and almost as if the boy were dead.

Not every convert is equally welcome to everybody else. The Christians themselves quarrel over whether they should accept people designated as osu, a hereditary outcast status in the old system. Their leader, Mr. Kiaga, insists that the converts must not reproduce that exclusion. This matters to people who had been told that their place in society was fixed before they were born. But the new church also creates new conflicts. One convert, Okoli, is accused of killing a sacred python. The village responds with alarm. The church has to decide whether its members will deliberately insult the beliefs of neighbors among whom they must continue to live.

For Nwoye, leaving is not an adventurous escape into an easy life. It means accepting the loss of his father's approval and many of the ties through which he has understood his home. He finds people willing to teach him a new language of faith and the possibility of schooling, but his departure leaves his mother and siblings to live with Okonkwo's rage. Okonkwo thinks that a son who rejects the gods of his ancestors has rejected him as well. He tries to imagine a family line that can continue without Nwoye and thinks of Ezinma's marriage as a way to restore his status once he returns to Umuofia.

Okonkwo watches the changes with disgust. He sees religious disobedience, the breakup of family obligations, and a dangerous softness in people who once seemed willing to defend the old ways. Yet life in Mbanta does not stop at his anger. Farming continues. Marriages are arranged. People weigh dangers and opportunities in different ways. Some want no part of the newcomers; some see them as a threat that can still be contained; some have already decided that the church is their home. There is not one response, but many.

As the end of the seven-year exile approaches, Okonkwo prepares a large feast for his mother's relatives. He is genuinely grateful that they have sheltered his household. There is food, ceremony, and a public acknowledgment of what their help has meant. Some of the elders worry aloud about the generations coming after them and whether the old bonds will survive. Okonkwo thinks chiefly of return. He has planned to rebuild his compound in Umuofia and to take titles that the accidental killing postponed. He expects to resume the story of his life almost where it stopped.

Obierika has tried to prepare him for what is waiting. The church has reached Umuofia too. It is no longer simply a collection of outsiders teaching unfamiliar words. There are converts, schools, new paths to employment, and a new court backed by armed force. A man can be tried for breaking rules that have no standing in the village's own judgments. People who used to have to persuade their neighbors may now call on foreign authority instead.

Seven years pass before the family can return to Umuofia.

When Okonkwo returns to Umuofia, the village he remembers has not vanished, but its balance has changed. His compound must be rebuilt. His friends welcome him, yet the celebrations he once imagined are muted by larger events. A Christian church has attracted converts. People have begun to trade with Europeans, and some families benefit from new schools and businesses. A colonial court claims power over disputes formerly settled within Igbo institutions. Prisoners are held and punished by men known as court messengers. The old village is still alive, but another government is establishing itself over it.

One missionary, Mr. Brown, has tried to avoid open war with the clan. He speaks with elders, attempts to understand why they believe what they believe, and makes schools and medical services part of his mission. He persuades some families that formal education may be useful even if they reject his religion. His approach has limits; he still represents a system of foreign belief and expanding influence. But he is careful enough that many villagers are willing to live alongside the church.

Mr. Brown's successor, Reverend Smith, is far less willing to tolerate compromise. He sees the situation in stark oppositions and encourages a more militant community of believers. Enoch, one of the converts, publicly unmasks an egwugwu during a village ceremony. The act is a grave insult to the masked ancestral judges and the people they represent. The egwugwu retaliate by destroying Enoch's compound and burning the Christian church. Violence now has a momentum that neither side can control through ordinary village negotiation.

Okonkwo welcomes the willingness to fight. He has feared that his people have become incapable of defending themselves. When the District Commissioner calls the leaders of Umuofia to a meeting, they go expecting to discuss the crisis. Instead they are arrested. The court messengers shave their heads, humiliate them, beat them, and demand money for their release. While the men are imprisoned, the larger community struggles to raise the fine. The colonial authorities can now impose punishment without asking the community to accept their account of justice.

After their release, the elders call a gathering. Okonkwo wants resistance. He has already imagined the worst: that the village will submit because everyone has learned to fear the consequences of defiance. At the meeting, speakers are beginning to address the assembled men when court messengers arrive and order the crowd to disperse. Okonkwo draws his machete and kills one of them. He is ready for the others to rise beside him, for the meeting to become an uprising, for his people to do what he believes honor requires.

But the others do not follow. Some people ask why he killed the messenger. The remaining messengers escape. The gathering's reaction tells Okonkwo something he cannot bear to acknowledge: he has acted on an imagined consensus that no longer exists. Whatever the villagers think of colonial rule, they do not agree with him about this act or about the immediate future. His old authority, already damaged by exile, has failed at the very moment he thought it would be restored through violence.

The District Commissioner comes to arrest Okonkwo. He does not find him in his house. Obierika and the other villagers lead him to a tree. Okonkwo has hanged himself. In the beliefs of his community, suicide is an abomination against the earth. The people who knew him cannot simply cut the body down and bury it according to ordinary custom. They must ask strangers to handle what is left. Obierika, grief-stricken and furious, tells the commissioner that Okonkwo had been one of the greatest men in Umuofia and that the outsiders have driven him to his death.

The commissioner hears these words while thinking about a book he intends to write on his work in the region. He has plans to describe the peoples under his authority. To him, the dead man's life is potentially useful as a brief episode in a larger account of colonial administration. The reader has just lived through Okonkwo's childhood ambitions, family life, achievements, terrors, transgressions, exile, and failed return. The commissioner imagines reducing all of that to a few lines.

That is where the story ends. We have followed the events of Okonkwo's life as far as they can go. Ikemefuna is dead, Nwoye has left his father's religion, Ezinma's future lies beyond the book's closing pages, and the community must find ways of living under a power that does not recognize the village as the center of its own history. The ending leaves the people of Umuofia still living, still disagreeing, still facing what comes next. It does not offer another scene in which Okonkwo can change his mind. His choice has brought his part of the story to a final stop.
