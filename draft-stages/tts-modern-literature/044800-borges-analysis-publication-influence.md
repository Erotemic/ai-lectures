# Borges: Ficciones, Its Editions, Translations, and Literary Afterlife

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The story of Ficciones begins before the book with that title existed. Some of its most important pieces appeared separately in magazines, others in an earlier collection, and the familiar volume itself changed after its first publication. That history matters because Borges made invented bibliographies and altered editions part of his fictional method. In his case, a real table of contents can become as instructive as an imaginary one, provided we do not confuse the two.

Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote appeared in the Argentine journal Sur in 1939. Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius followed in 1940. A group of stories was gathered as El jardín de senderos que se bifurcan, The Garden of Forking Paths, in 1941. Its form included fictional criticism, cosmological fable, and a spy tale that presents a theory of time inside a murder. Borges was building an approach to prose fiction that made an essay's framing devices, a detective story's demands, and a philosophical argument's precise formulations serve the same literary object.

In 1944, Editorial Sur published Ficciones, bringing the earlier group together with a section called Artifices. The original publication contained fourteen pieces in the combined book. The more familiar 1956 edition added three stories: The End, The Sect of the Phoenix, and The South. The Approach to Al-Mu'tasim has a particularly complex editorial history because it circulated earlier as a piece of fictional criticism and appears or disappears in different collected arrangements. One should verify a particular edition's contents before asserting that a given printed volume includes all of the same texts.

There are also translation questions. Readers of the Spanish originals encounter choices of register, verbal economy, names, and grammatical structures that translations must reconstruct in another language. The English titles themselves vary. Funes el memorioso has appeared as Funes the Memorious and as Funes, His Memory. The collection is titled Fictions in some modern English editions, even though the Spanish Ficciones is often left in place in literary history. These changes are not simply mistakes; they show how a text acquires a second life as it enters new institutions and a new network of readers.

The major expansion of Borges's international visibility came substantially after the first Spanish publication. He shared the 1961 International Publishers' Prize, commonly called the Formentor Prize, with Samuel Beckett. English-language collections including Ficciones and Labyrinths appeared in 1962. Translators, editors, professors, and other writers helped introduce stories that had been composed many years earlier to audiences who experienced them as an intervention in the literature of the 1960s.

That shifted chronology is important for intellectual history. A story written in the shadow of fascism and Argentine cultural debates could be read later as a model of postmodernism. Readers recognized metafiction, intertextuality, the fiction of authorship, and the instability of interpretive systems. They sometimes promoted a picture of Borges as a pure cerebral artist whose world was only the library. More recent scholarship has questioned the adequacy of that picture by placing him back in the history of his city, his language, his literary partnerships, and his political commitments.

What sort of influence can we document? Borges supplied forms that later writers admired and adapted: imaginary books reviewed as though real, stories built around an impossible library, narratives whose endings rewrite their beginnings, and scholarly essays that make their references part of the fiction. Writers working in metafiction and experimental prose found him a major precursor. But a resemblance between two works is not automatically proof that one author borrowed from another. Demonstrable influence requires testimony, citations, correspondence, shared institutions, or textual evidence stronger than a familiar motif.

The writer's literary afterlife also complicates simplistic accounts of Latin American literature. Borges's influence cannot be reduced to magical realism or to a geographical quota within a global canon. He worked with detective fiction, philosophy, gaucho literature, and forms of the essay, and he transformed the possibilities of the short story. His importance is formal and historical: generations of writers learned that a work of fiction could consist partly of the commentary on an imaginary book or the investigation of a proposition that can never be settled.

There is a final reason to attend carefully to editions. The literary text one teaches is not always a timeless object. It is a particular arrangement of pages, chosen and transmitted through authors, publishers, translators, and readers. Ficciones itself reminds us to question the authority of bibliographies, yet a responsible history of the book depends on bibliographical work. We can enjoy the fiction of a nonexistent novel while still tracing the actual appearance of Borges's stories in Sur, in the 1941 volume, and in the later editions.

By the end of this section we have a picture of influence that is more substantial than the praise of brilliance. Borges gave twentieth-century literature a set of repeatable inventions, and he changed how many writers thought about the boundaries of story, essay, philosophy, and criticism. He also left us a method for testing our own admiration: ask which evidence is real, which interpretation is ours, and what a beautiful explanation might be hiding.
