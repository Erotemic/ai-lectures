# Waiting for Godot: French, English, and the Changing Performance Text

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A familiar edition of Waiting for Godot can give the impression that there is one settled English script, transferred from a French original as faithfully as words permit. The actual textual history is more instructive. Beckett composed En attendant Godot in French between October 1948 and January 1949. It was published in French in 1952 and first performed in Paris on January 5, 1953, under Roger Blin's direction. Beckett then translated his play into English himself. The first English-language book edition appeared from Grove Press in New York in 1954; the first major English-language staging was directed by Peter Hall in London in August 1955.

These are more than bibliographic coordinates. The author who writes a play in French and then writes it again in English is making dramaturgical decisions. Translating a conversational exchange is not a matter of exchanging one dictionary entry for another. A joke may depend on syntax, rhythm, an accidental double meaning, or the ordinary speech of a particular place. If the joke cannot survive literally, the playwright must choose what performance effect to preserve. Beckett was not an outside translator; he was revising his own theatrical material in another language.

Lawrence Graver's study of Godot emphasizes that the French and English versions are both authorial works. The English text is often tighter, and changes reflect what Beckett learned from the early performance process. A phrase may be shortened not because the translator has misunderstood it but because the author has discovered what an actor can make work aloud. This is an important lesson in reading drama: the page is not only literature recorded in print. It is also a score intended to be realized by bodies and voices.

The English publishing history adds another complication. The Grove Press edition in America preceded the British Faber and Faber edition, which came in 1956. The British publication was affected by cuts and alterations connected with the Lord Chamberlain's censorship of the play. A fully restored British text did not appear until later, in 1965. Consequently, two mid-century English copies may differ for reasons that have nothing to do with a simple progression from error to correction. Publishing market, theatrical licensing, censorship, and authorial revision all enter the record.

Beckett continued to work on the play after it had become famous. His 1975 Berlin production of Warten auf Godot, at the Schiller Theater, generated detailed production notes and revisions of movement, delivery, and stage arrangement. Scholars have studied these documents as evidence that Beckett's theatrical practice developed over time. The relevant question is not whether the first text was wrong. It is how repeated production changed the author's understanding of an exact theatrical design.

This should alter how we talk about authorial intention. It is fair to report a specific staging decision in a documented Beckett production, and fair to compare two published versions when we have the texts. It is not fair to claim that a choice in a later rehearsal proves that every earlier audience saw the same choice. Nor does a later revision automatically supersede every earlier version for every scholarly purpose. A historian may care about the Paris premiere, an editor about the first English printing, and a director about the revised performance score. These are different questions.

For listeners who know only one version, there is still a useful way to read. Attend to places where a tiny verbal difference could change an actor's relationship to an object or partner: a pause before an answer, the length of a joke, an instruction to look, the timing of a repeated phrase. The play's philosophical atmosphere emerges from these granular particulars. It can be transformed by a slight change in who speaks first, or how long a performer stands still after an apparent decision.

The work's original language matters socially and historically as well. Beckett was an Irish writer living in France and writing across literary languages. It would be misleading to describe the French play as simply the work of a French playwright, or the English play as a translation performed by someone unconnected to its composition. Bilingual authorship complicates the traditional label of a national literature. It also discourages us from treating English idiom as proof of what the original French meant.

There are limits to what we can demonstrate without placing specific editions side by side. A lecture based on secondary textual scholarship can describe broad differences and historical causes, but should not invent line-by-line variants. If precise comparison matters, a reader needs a defined pair of editions, the exact passages, and a record of any changes caused by performance or censorship. Without that evidence, phrases such as 'Beckett originally intended' are too strong.

This history also helps explain the play's international reach. Godot travels without needing a detailed local setting, but that does not make its language weightless. Every new performance must produce its pauses, rhythms, social relations, and bodily jokes anew. The tree and the road may appear simple; making that simplicity communicate in a particular language is complex artistic work.

What persists across versions is not a single hidden philosophical statement. It is an unusually disciplined dramatic arrangement: two men wait; others arrive changed; a messenger defers arrival; declarations of departure fail to produce departure. The text history teaches a paired lesson. The arrangement is recognizable and resilient, yet its effects depend on exact words and stage decisions. A great play can be durable without becoming textually or theatrically fixed.
