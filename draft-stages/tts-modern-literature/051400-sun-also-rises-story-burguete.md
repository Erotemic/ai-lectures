# The Sun Also Rises — The Story: Fishing, Distance, and the Road to Pamplona

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Jake Barnes and Bill Gorton travel south from Paris toward Spain, meeting Robert Cohn along the way. Their journey has the agreeable texture of a holiday: trains, hotels, conversations about food and money, the prospect of cold water and fish. But their party is already divided by expectations. Cohn hopes for more time with Brett Ashley, and her arrival is delayed. Jake and Bill would rather proceed into the countryside. The first question in Spain is therefore not where to go, but whom to wait for.

In Pamplona, Brett and Mike Campbell fail to appear when expected. Cohn remains in the town, unable to detach himself from the possibility of seeing Brett. Jake and Bill leave for the fishing country near Burguete. This separation creates one of the novel's most revealing contrasts. The men in town are suspended in romantic anticipation. In the hills, Jake and Bill encounter a world of practical routines. They find lodging, make arrangements for meals, travel to the river, cast lines, and come back tired in a way that is not the same as despair.

The fishing passage has a different rhythm from the Paris nights. Things happen in a sequence governed by daylight, the stream, and the movements of fish. Jake is attentive to the material detail of the activity. He describes equipment and terrain more comfortably than he speaks about the emotional wound separating him from Brett. The physical business gives him a domain where competence can be practiced and recognized without confession. Bill jokes, and their companionship is sometimes affectionate in indirect ways. The scenes are neither a cure for the war nor an empty diversion. They show an interval in which life feels possible.

Their time in Burguete also exposes the limits of the cliché that Hemingway's characters are simply intoxicated expatriates. Jake can pay attention carefully. He can enjoy food and landscape without turning everything into a performance for an audience. Bill's good humor is not only an escape from feeling; it is one means of sustaining friendship. The small pleasures have real weight. But the novel does not suggest they will permanently solve the conflict waiting in Pamplona.

News arrives that Brett and Mike are coming. Jake and Bill return to the city, where festival preparations are under way. Jake stays at the hotel run by Montoya, a man who shares his serious respect for bullfighting. Their relationship is not just a commercial arrangement between guest and host. Montoya recognizes that Jake understands the sport beyond the fashionable thrill of watching danger. That recognition offers Jake something he has sought throughout the book: standing earned through knowledge and attention rather than charm or sexual conquest.

To understand the importance of Montoya, remember that Jake is an outsider. He is an American in Spain and does not become Spanish through admiration. He nevertheless develops a genuine apprenticeship in the practice of watching bullfighting. He can distinguish technique from tricks and spectacle from skill. Montoya values this distinction too. Their friendship is delicate because it depends on conduct. A person who appreciates a cultural practice can still betray the confidence of those who sustain it.

As the party gathers in Pamplona, the romantic conflicts return with greater force. Brett has arrived with Mike, who intends to marry her. Cohn has not forgotten their earlier time together and keeps hoping for an exclusive bond. Jake continues to love her and tries not to turn that love into a public demand. The men are capable of genuine affection and of astonishingly petty cruelty. Mike directs some of his most vicious remarks at Cohn. His jealousy takes a form sharpened by antisemitism already present in the way the group describes him.

The group watches the bulls being unloaded. The animals are powerful and dangerous; the practical work around them is unfamiliar to visitors who have come for spectacle. The episode provides an early reminder that the festival is not simply a backdrop for romantic farce. The bulls will injure and kill. The people who handle them possess skills that the tourists do not. Jake is especially concerned with whether his companions understand the difference between respectfully watching a demanding local practice and using it as an occasion for personal drama.

Brett attracts attention in the town. She drinks, moves among friends, and resists attempts by the men to define her relationship to each of them. Cohn's expectations and Mike's resentments build. Jake's apparent composure is strained. He may be a knowledgeable spectator at the bullring, but he cannot stand outside what is happening among his companions. His decisions will soon affect the life of a young bullfighter whom Montoya wishes to protect.

The journey from Burguete to Pamplona is therefore not just a movement from peace into chaos. It stages two forms of order. In the hills, attention and mutual companionship make a temporary shelter. In Pamplona, an exacting art demands discipline from its practitioners even while the crowd drinks, celebrates, and loses control. Jake is drawn to both sorts of order. His tragedy is that he cannot keep his private desires from reaching into them.
