# Ulysses: Bloom, Stephen, and the Family That Cannot Quite Form

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

One of the pleasures of Ulysses is that James Joyce allows a young intellectual and a middle-aged advertising salesman to inhabit the same city without turning one into a mere lesson for the other. Stephen Dedalus is twenty-two, strikingly learned, short of money, and nearly helpless in certain ordinary affairs. Leopold Bloom is thirty-eight, curious about countless practical things, socially awkward, and capable of a kind of attention that Dublin's talkative men often mistake for weakness. Near the end, Bloom helps Stephen after a drunken confrontation with soldiers, brings him home, and offers him cocoa. Their meeting suggests a father and son, but it never becomes the complete reconciliation that the analogy seems to promise.

Why is this incompleteness important? Because the novel wants to know how people care for one another when resemblance does not erase difference.

## The young man who can explain everything

Stephen knows how to turn an event into an argument. He has read philosophical systems, theatrical traditions, and Catholic theology. In the tower he interprets Buck Mulligan's mockery as an injury to his mother's memory. At school he turns a history lesson into a judgment about human destiny. On the strand he thinks about the foundations of perception. In the library he builds a theory of Shakespeare's life and work.

His intelligence is real. Joyce does not treat literary education as a fraud. But Stephen's brilliance is inseparable from a fear of dependence. He needs a salary but despises the headmaster who pays it. He wants friendship but resents the laughing company that offers it on terms he cannot bear. He is alienated from his father yet cannot stop thinking in familial patterns. He rejects a religious duty at his mother's bedside and remains haunted by her final request.

The idea of being free from social authority is attractive to him precisely because so many authorities have claims upon him. He wants to become a writer, which means deciding what the past may demand from a living artist. But people do not become independent by declaration alone. Stephen still needs food and shelter; he still needs a person who will come back for him when he is injured.

## The older man who notices the small thing

Bloom's habits look less impressive. He worries about advertising space, funeral subscriptions, bills, social misunderstandings, and what people will think of his appearance. He is capable of technical speculation that is half practical invention, half daydream. He is also unusually alert to the needs of people who are absent from the scene. At Paddy Dignam's funeral he considers what the widow and children will require. At the maternity hospital he thinks about the woman giving birth. In Nighttown he follows a drunk man whose companions have not undertaken to protect him.

Care is not the same as moral perfection. Bloom conducts a pseudonymous flirtation with Martha Clifford. On the beach he masturbates while watching Gerty MacDowell. He can objectify women, indulge fantasy, resent Molly, and avoid open communication. Joyce's achievement is not to present a flawless humanitarian. It is to make a human being's ethical strengths visible without erasing his evasions and appetites.

If Bloom's goodness matters, it is because it appears in small choices that could easily have been otherwise. He might have remained silent when the Citizen baited him. He might have left Stephen to the police. He might have treated a widow's material situation as someone else's problem. The day shows him choosing attention more than once, alongside many occasions when he withdraws or hesitates.

## Two forms of fatherlessness

Stephen's mother has died. His father Simon lives but is an unstable source of security. Bloom's own father died by suicide, and Bloom carries the stigma and grief of that death. His son Rudy died as an infant. The two men meet at an intersection of different losses: one has a difficult father and a dead mother, the other a dead father and a dead son. The pattern is powerful, but it is not symmetrical.

It is tempting to say that Stephen is the son Bloom needs and Bloom the father Stephen needs. Joyce provides just enough suggestive detail to make the hypothesis emotionally persuasive. The young man's vulnerability reminds Bloom of lost parenthood. Bloom's attention supplies Stephen, briefly, with something other than mockery or instruction. But to make either man wholly satisfy the other's need would be to violate what the novel actually records. Stephen declines Bloom's invitation to stay and leaves the house.

That refusal is not simply a failure to recognize a perfect solution. It may protect Stephen's independence, or express his unease with Bloom, or reflect exhaustion and habitual restlessness. The narrative does not give an omniscient verdict. Bloom has offered hospitality. Stephen has not made a promise. We can value the encounter without pretending the future has been settled.

## The city keeps measuring people incorrectly

Other people habitually describe Stephen and Bloom by ready-made categories. Stephen is a clever young man who should get himself into print, a drunk who needs controlling, a possible genius to be teased. Bloom is an advertiser with peculiar interests, a husband whose wife's conduct can be whispered about, a person marked as Jewish, an object of suspicious jokes.

These categories may contain partial truths, but they are often used to prevent further knowledge. The Citizen's prejudice is an extreme form of the same reduction that appears in milder social comedy. Stephen's own theories can also reduce people to roles within an argument. He can use Shakespeare and his family as elements of an interpretive machine, just as others use Stephen as the designated young intellectual of a conversation.

The novel's changing styles resist such a conclusion. We are not allowed to view either man through just one set of social terms. Bloom's breakfast is as much part of him as his defense of love against hatred. Stephen's tired and frightened body is as much part of him as his learned Shakespeare theory. The meaning of each life grows from accumulating relations, not from one decisive label.

## Is Bloom really the hero?

The word hero can mislead if it implies mastery, victory, and superiority. Bloom loses arguments, avoids conflict, forgets items, suffers humiliation, and goes home to an uncertain marriage. Yet in a novel whose mythic framework invokes Odysseus, he has a claim to the title because he keeps responding to human need. His acts are limited. He cannot make Stephen stay. He cannot undo his son's death. He cannot know the future of his marriage. He can feed a cat, attend a funeral, intervene on a street, and make cocoa.

Stephen can also be understood generously. His refusal to kneel at his mother's deathbed may be a defense of conscience that remains painful precisely because the person he loved asked him to do otherwise. His intellectual suspicion can expose pretension and historical violence. To say he needs kindness is not to say he must surrender his right to judge.

The central relation thus does not divide the novel into cleverness and goodness. Both men are capable of wit, perception, fantasy, error, and sympathy; they practice these capacities differently. Their differences give the meeting substance. A perfectly matched pair could become a symbol. Two awkward men asking each other questions over cocoa remain people.

## The home that waits beyond the encounter

There is a third person essential to this account: Molly Bloom. A reading focused on Bloom and Stephen can turn Molly into the place to which the older man returns, or the unfaithful woman whose behavior makes his kindness heroic. The final monologue refuses that use of her. Molly is neither prize nor obstacle. She remembers, desires, resents, and makes judgments in a voice that the two men cannot fully control.

That ending also alters our view of Bloom's longing for a restored family. The dead Rudy cannot be replaced, the living Milly is becoming independent, and Molly's private life will not become transparent because her husband wishes it. Family is not an equation in which one missing member can be canceled by a new variable. It is a set of relationships whose pleasures and injuries cannot be neatly totaled.

The gift Bloom gives Stephen is temporary and concrete. He offers his time when the young man most needs someone to stay nearby. The gift Stephen gives Bloom is harder to describe: for a night, Bloom can direct some of his paternal attention toward a living person. It does not heal the original loss. But an act need not cure grief to be worthwhile.

That is one way to understand the novel's humanism. Not as a theory that every person is good underneath, and not as a demand to forgive every injury. It is a commitment to seeing what a person does and suffers when the city's preferred story about him is incomplete. Two people can matter to one another without becoming each other's destiny.
