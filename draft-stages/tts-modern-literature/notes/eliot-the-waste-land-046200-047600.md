## Listening order and shape

This is an extended **poetry** section, not a novel retelling or a performed reading of Eliot’s poem. First come an accessible listening guide and five self-contained, complete movement-by-movement orientations. Nine analytical lectures follow: montage and voice; sound and prosody; ritual and mythic method; women and coercive relations; London, class, empire and trade; cross-tradition religious references; composition and publication; close-reading practice; reception and influence. A listener may follow these without prior reading, though side-by-side access to a legitimately obtained poem makes the close-reading workshop more useful. The manuscripts are original explanatory prose and do **not** reproduce the poem or create an audio book of it.

## Bibliographic and edition distinctions to protect

- The poem first appeared in *The Criterion* (October 1922) and *The Dial* (November 1922), **without Eliot’s Notes**. Its American standalone book appeared via Boni and Liveright in **December 1922**, with Notes. The British Hogarth Press (Leonard and Virginia Woolf) first produced its British book edition in **September 1923**. Fine day-of-publication reporting differs across sources; avoid claiming an exact December release day without a specified bibliography. The epigraph, printed apparatus and later dedication need edition-specific verification rather than an assumption of first-appearance uniformity.
- The poem consists of five titled parts, with the familiar text's line ranges I: 1–76; II: 77–172; III: 173–311; IV: 312–321; V: 322–433. **Part IV is ten lines.** Keep the boundaries distinct and do not turn the poem into a single literal narrator’s story.
- The draft was longer and included the discarded opening and other passages. Ezra Pound made substantive cuts, while Vivien Eliot’s marginal interventions also survive. Valerie Eliot’s 1971 facsimile transcript documents this history. The editorial dossier relies on accessible facsimile descriptions and scholarly abstracts; the 1971 facsimile was **not** exhaustively examined in full.
- Eliot's Notes **are primary statements about his references and proposed structure**, not unquestionable proof of a single correct interpretation. His tarot imagery deliberately adapts a traditional deck. Notes link the Fisher King to the poem, but that name is not uttered as a named person in the verse of Part V. Likewise, Tiresias appears and is important, but the poem does not become an unambiguous transcript of Tiresias's continuous eyewitness account.
- Avoid claiming an objectively achieved restoration at the end. The thunder offers instructions and storm conditions change, but it does not certify that everyone drinks, every broken relation is repaired, or that ethical imperatives have been practiced. The last invocation of peace is **not** a narrated triumph.
- The Buddhist Fire Sermon and Augustine's *Confessions* are distinct religious sources. The *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* supplies a threefold thunder teaching; Eliot changes the order and glosses it as “give, sympathize, control.” The Sanskrit teaching retains its own context independent of Eliot's poetry; modern cross-tradition comparison should not conflate Buddhist, Christian and Hindu doctrines.
- The poem's representation of the typist and the young man depicts unequal sexual agency. It should not be simplified into two equally willing people having boring sex. Avoid turning Lil's pub interlocutor into authoritative judgment about Lil, and avoid treating foreign identities, gender stereotypes, or the global borrowing of spiritual material as culturally neutral.

## Evidence basis and limits

The 1922 book text, including Eliot's own Notes, was consulted through a public-domain US transcription and university/Poetry Foundation annotated editions. Published scholarship, institutional archives and accessible educational material support historical claims. For exact lines, edition-sensitive quotations, critical variants and revision decisions, consult the specified edition and facsimile: the review here **does not claim direct collation** of all Criterion, Dial, Boni and Liveright, Hogarth, 1971 manuscript and modern scholarly editions. Some Cambridge chapters are represented by publisher-visible abstracts, not complete chapters. The original poem is public-domain in the United States as to the 1922 text, but copyright is jurisdiction-dependent, and no long excerpts are included here.
