# Editorial dossier — Ernest Hemingway, *The Sun Also Rises* (051300–052200)

## Purpose and structure

The novel is selected for the influential combination of economical prose, dramatic omission, first-person selection, precise physical description, and its portrait of expatriate social life after the First World War. **Three complete story narrations** come first: Paris; Burguete and the return to Pamplona; the fiesta, its aftermath, San Sebastián, and Madrid. **Seven analyses** then address style and omission, Jake's selective account of Robert Cohn, Brett's decisions and gender, bullfighting, social economy, dialogue and close reading, and publishing history. The phrase "lost generation" is examined rather than used as a total character diagnosis.

## Factual and plot checks

- **Jake Barnes** is an American newspaperman living in Paris after war service. His sexual injury remains largely indirectly narrated; do not assert a precise clinical diagnosis not established by the fictional text.
- **Robert Cohn** is Jewish, Princeton-educated, and a trained boxer. Prejudice toward him exists in the fictional social circle. The lectures distinguish his possessive behavior and actual assaults from the antisemitism in other characters' evaluations.
- **Brett Ashley**, **Mike Campbell**, **Bill Gorton**, **Frances Clyne**, **Count Mippipopolous**, and **Montoya** have distinct relations to Jake and Cohn. In particular, Brett and Cohn spend time at San Sebastián before the Spanish trip, while Jake and Bill do the fishing near Burguete.
- **Pedro Romero** is a nineteen-year-old matador in Jake's narration. Jake helps Brett meet him, which strains his relationship with Montoya. Cohn strikes Jake and Mike and also beats Romero. The bullfighting and injury sequence is told before the critical discussion.
- **Brett** leaves the fiesta with Romero but summons Jake to **Madrid** after ending the affair. The novel ends with Jake and Brett riding in a taxi; it does not show them reconciling as partners.

## Edition and source distinctions

1. [Original 1926 novel, accessible transcription](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67138/pg67138-images.html). This supplies the original epigraphs and can be consulted for scene-level wording. Text should be checked against the edition quoted in teaching materials.
2. [SparkNotes, full-book summary](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/sun/summary/). Secondary cross-check for cast, plot order, and ending. Separate its commentary from the novel itself.
3. [Morgan Library & Museum first-edition record](https://www.themorgan.org/printed-books/91742). Confirms the **1926 Charles Scribner's Sons** edition. An independent [University of Utah rare-books exhibition](https://openbook.lib.utah.edu/tag/ernest-hemingway/) gives publication **October 22, 1926**.
4. [Library of America, interview with scholar/editor Robert W. Trogdon](https://www.loa.org/news-and-views/1773-robert-w-trogdon-on-ernest-hemingway8217s-paris-years-8220a-magical-time-for-modern-literature8221/). Discusses the two epigraphs and Hemingway's complex relation to the *lost generation* label.
5. [Library of America, corrected text](https://www.loa.org/books/704-the-sun-also-rises-paperback-and-loa-ebook-classic/). Robert W. Trogdon edited an edition consulting manuscripts/typescripts and first publication. **Notably, the corrected text prints the famous closing sentence with a period, while many familiar editions print a question mark.** Narration discusses its ambiguity without claiming punctuation is uniform across editions.
6. Original **US title**: *The Sun Also Rises* (1926). Its **British publication title** was *Fiesta* (1927). The two epigraphs are Gertrude Stein's remark and a passage from *Ecclesiastes*; neither alone supplies a full reading of the novel.

### Limits and responsible interpretation

No claim is made that Hemingway invented economical prose, nor that later books with short sentences must be indebted to this novel. Biographical parallels to Hemingway's 1925 travels are real but **do not** authorize equating fictional speakers with real-life acquaintances in every detail. The original novel was accessible, though no exhaustive collation against the Library of America critical edition was performed. Verify exact page numbers, punctuation, and quotation marks against a chosen text before making printed companion material.

The study keeps bullfighting's historical aesthetic and animal suffering in view without assuming that admiration of Jake's technical vocabulary requires ethical approval of the sport. It also examines the class and national position of tourists in the Spanish festival. Brett's actions are read through Jake's selective narration rather than presumed as direct access to her mind.
