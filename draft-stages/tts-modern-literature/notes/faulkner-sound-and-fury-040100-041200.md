# The Sound and the Fury: complete narrative and criticism — editorial dossier

## Why it belongs in the landmarks track

William Faulkner's *The Sound and the Fury* (1929) occupies a central place in twentieth-century Anglophone modernism. Its formal achievement is not simply telling an otherwise ordinary plot out of sequence. It builds four incommensurate ways of experiencing and accounting for time, including a section whose sensory transitions resist the conventional signals of an explanatory narrator, an obsessively recursive consciousness, an economically calculating narrator, and an externally narrated Easter morning. The novel is a foundation for studying nonlinear narration, the ethics of narrative perspective, and the social history of the U.S. South. The decision to include it rests on literary influence, formal accomplishment, critical endurance, and its teaching value, not on a coverage quota.

The section is **complete as a first major treatment**: five connected, spoiler-complete story-orientation manuscripts followed by seven original analytical manuscripts. They are intended to be listened to in order but not artificially shortened to a duration target. The prose is original secondary narration and criticism, not a verbatim performance or reproduction of the novel. The spoken manuscripts contain no citation URLs; documentary records are in metadata and this non-spoken dossier.

## Source hierarchy and how to use the citations

The novel itself is the primary evidence for fictional events and narrative arrangement. University scholarly resources and university course transcripts provide corroboration or *attributed* interpretations. Archival accounts by Faulkner are evidence for what he said **later** about composition and intended layout, not proof of all readers' interpretations. Publisher and academic catalogs supply bibliographic context, but short blurbs and abstracts do not establish the substance of books or articles behind paywalls.

**Primary text and dates:** William Faulkner, *The Sound and the Fury* (1929), public-domain electronic witness at [Project Gutenberg eBook 75170](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/75170). University of Virginia's [Digital Yoknapatawpha: novel record](https://faulkner.iath.virginia.edu/shanti/content/sound-and-fury) identifies Jonathan Cape and Harrison Smith, New York, **October 7, 1929**, and the four novel section dates. The UVa research site's [chronology-method essay](https://faulkner.iath.virginia.edu/the_art_of_faulkner_technique.html) cautions that translating remembered events into one calendar is interpretive, not mechanically determined. Some UVa text-record pages may not load through automated fetches; this is stated in the catalog rather than concealed.

**Gibson family, perspective and critical context:** UVa's [Gibson family entries](https://faulkner.iath.virginia.edu/shanti/content/gibsonfamily) cover Dilsey, Versh, Frony, T. P., and Luster. Wai Chee Dimock's fully available Open Yale Courses lectures [6](https://oyc.yale.edu/american-studies/amst-246/lecture-6), [7](https://oyc.yale.edu/american-studies/amst-246/lecture-7), and [8](https://oyc.yale.edu/american-studies/amst-246/lecture-8) supply substantial interpretations for Benjy, Quentin and Jason. When an interpretation has a named modern scholar, the narration names the scholar rather than presenting the view as Faulkner's settled intention. The novel itself is not proof of the appropriateness of modern diagnostic terms.

**Material textual history:** [Faulkner at Virginia on italics and color](https://faulkner.lib.virginia.edu/display/wfaudio09_1.html) and [Faulkner on his title](https://faulkner.lib.virginia.edu/display/wfaudio26_2.html) record the author's retrospective remarks. Jonathan Berliner's [Cambridge discussion](https://cambridgeblog.org/2024/04/faulkners-material-texts/) and the University of Illinois library's [2012 essay on the fourteen-color edition](https://publish.illinois.edu/litlanglibrary/2012/07/18/faulkners-the-sound-and-the-fury-as-he-realized-it/) distinguish author proposal from later editorial reconstruction. The [Library of America article](https://blog.loa.org/2010/09/william-faulkner-and-sound-and-fury-its.html) discusses first reception, manuscript history and the book's publishing circumstances. The [Compson Appendix](https://faulkner.iath.virginia.edu/shanti/content/appendix-compson), written in 1945 for *The Portable Faulkner* (1946), is separate from the original 1929 text; later edition practices vary. The [2026 Norton Critical Edition](https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9781324072324-the-sound-and-the-fury-eabd9c54-a8c5-4a86-aef1-b030f427761a) is listed as a publisher description only, not as fully inspected criticism.

**Title, wider recognition and reception:** The phrase comes from [*Macbeth*, Act 5 scene 5](https://www.folger.edu/explore/shakespeares-works/macbeth/read/5/5/). Faulkner's public remarks say that he was drawn to the sound of the phrase, not that the tragic hero and the novel's people were intentionally a literal parallel. Sources concerning influence include an [Oxford Research Encyclopedia abstract](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61883/chapter-abstract/547846196) and an [academic article abstract on critical reception](https://webbut.unitbv.ro/index.php/Series_IV/article/view/2889), both **abstract only**; generalized claims of direct influence on specific later authors require separately verified evidence. [NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1949/faulkner/speech/) records Faulkner's **1949 prize** with his **1950 speech/ceremony**. The independent [SparkNotes plot outline](https://www.sparknotes.com/lit/soundfury/plot-overview/) is a low-priority factual orientation cross-check, not a substitute for reading the novel.

The precise bibliography is in `metadata/sources.json`. Each `metadata/040100.json`–`metadata/041200.json` identifies its resources and exact-text claim anchors, linked by stable source IDs. A unique text anchor is a way to locate a claim in the transcript. It is **not**, by itself, independent proof of an interpretation or of a disputed remembered incident.

## Guardrails on plot, historical claims and reading

1. **Four dated sections in published order:** Benjy April 7, 1928; Quentin June 2, 1910; Jason April 6, 1928; the final third-person section April 8, 1928. Chronological order of the *section days* would be 2, 3, 1, 4, but the scenes within Benjy's and Quentin's consciousness are not one agreed uniform chronology.
2. **Two people called Quentin:** Quentin Compson is a son who dies by suicide in 1910; Miss Quentin is Caddy's daughter and his namesake. A passage naming Quentin without context can refer to either.
3. **Quentin's incest claim is false:** His claim that he and Caddy committed incest is not a confirmed secret or an unresolved historical fact in the novel. Distinguish it from his distress, his desire to keep Caddy close, and his own unreliable reconstructions of her sexual history.
4. **Suicide and causal claims:** Quentin carries out suicide, but the fiction does not give warrant for a single clinical diagnosis or for declaring his death an honorable necessity. The second section conveys his final day; do not make him personally narrate a death he cannot recount.
5. **Benjy's disability:** The text shows profound intellectual disability and restricted communication, but assigning a specific modern diagnosis exceeds what the text supports. The representation's language and the family's decision to castrate him are historic fictional events; they should not be naturalized as ethical or medically appropriate.
6. **Caddy's absence:** Candace/Caddy remains crucial but never receives one of the novel's four narrative sections. Her portrayal through her brothers' testimony is an explicit object of criticism; a brother's judgment is not neutral evidence.
7. **Miss Quentin's departure:** She escapes the household with money Jason concealed. The 1929 novel does not certify a later life or final moral destiny for her. Later Compson supplements are distinct texts.
8. **Jason's theft and accounts:** Jason intercepts money intended for Miss Quentin, uses substitutions to deceive Mrs. Compson, and resents Caddy for a lost job prospect. His narration is a self-interested account, not a fiscal audit or an omniscient judgment.
9. **Dilsey and racial narrative asymmetry:** The final section grants Dilsey narrative prominence but **is third person**, not her full first-person testimony. Explore the book's respect for her endurance alongside its constraints as a white author's representation of Black interior lives. The Gibson family is not an anonymous single servant.
10. **Southern class and historical slavery:** The Compsons are a white family whose wealth and inherited conception of order are structured by the racial regime of the South. Distinguish actual history from what a Compson character claims to understand about that history. Jason's racist and antisemitic remarks are character attitudes, not statements endorsed by this course.
11. **Material editions:** Faulkner originally wished for multicolored type as a way to mark Benjy's temporal movement. The familiar printed novel used other typographic means. The modern fourteen-color arrangement is an **editorially realized** interpretation, not the 1929 first edition printed as Faulkner had written it.
12. **1945/1946 Appendix:** Published *after* the 1929 novel and not part of the original fourth section. Later genealogical details can diverge from implications of the original text. Do not silently retrofit them into a factual account of the 1929 ending.
13. **Macbeth and author intention:** The Shakespeare line supplies the title; how far an audience should read an analogy to Macbeth is an argument, not a statement directly settled by title attribution.
14. **No fixed length and no audio:** The section is written for TTS as original connected exposition. Manuscript lengths are descriptive metadata, not a minimum, cap or target; no voice audio, illustrations or binary assets are included.

## Recommended reading order and follow-on studies

Read or listen to the complete story orientation before evaluating narrative judgments. On return to the novel itself, keep the original section dates, the printed typographical shifts, the identity of each focal character, and the difference between a speaker's certainty and the text's evidence. Read a declared edition when comparing exact typography or interpretive annotations. A further primary-text reading workshop with explicit page markers **for a declared edition** could extend this section; so could a sourced comparative study of Faulkner with Joyce and Woolf. Neither is a prerequisite to treat the current twelve-lecture section as complete.
