# Editorial dossier: Albert Camus, *The Stranger* / *The Outsider* (`049100`–`049900`)

## Why this work follows Rushdie

Published in 1942 as *L'Étranger*, Camus's short novel is important for the stylistic restraint of its narrator, its role in debates about the absurd, its critique of conventional social judgment, and the scrutiny it has received as a French-colonial Algerian narrative. Those contributions, not a categorical label of "existentialist novel," justify extended study. The two continuous story lectures come first and include the entire ending, then seven independent analyses attend to its narrative omissions, ethics, colonial setting, philosophy, relationships, English textual history and close-reading. The length follows the content and contains no mechanical duration constraint.

## Essential distinctions

**What happens.** Meursault's mother dies in a home. After the funeral he resumes ordinary life, begins a relationship with Marie, assists Raymond in abusing and humiliating a woman, and later kills a man on the beach. The narration describes one initial shot followed by four more shots into a body already fallen; the heat and sun are part of his perspective, not a physical force compelling the killing. Part Two covers imprisonment and a trial in which behavior at the funeral becomes conspicuous evidence of moral deficiency. He is sentenced to death, rejects the chaplain's assurances, and approaches the end of his life. The novel does not show an execution: neither the lecture nor sidecar should supply one as a final scene.

**Colonial narrative boundaries.** The murdered man is not given a personal name in Camus's novel; the narrative instead primarily identifies him through his being Arab. Likewise, the woman abused by Raymond is not an independent narrative center. This is not solved by making up a name in a retelling. In **Kamel Daoud's later novel** *The Meursault Investigation* (first issued in Algeria in 2013), the murdered man is named Musa and a brother, Harun, speaks; this is an artistic counter-narrative, **not a name hidden in Camus's 1942 text**. The colonial setting is part of the story's moral and representational significance even when first-person narration leaves it implicit.

**Philosophy and ethics.** Camus published *The Myth of Sisyphus* in the same year. Readings through his philosophy of the absurd can illuminate the encounter with mortality and the refusal of consolation, but they cannot logically justify Meursault's participation in Raymond's abuse or the killing. Camus resisted simple identification as an existentialist. The character's voice, the author's philosophical essays and later scholarly interpretations are three different things.

**Translations.** The novel was first issued in French by Gallimard in 1942. English translations are not interchangeable renderings of identical philosophical meaning; wording around the first sentence, affect, relationships and local setting can shape interpretation. The Albert Camus Society lists the English version by Stuart Gilbert (1946), Matthew Ward (1988) and Sandra Smith (2012). The title may appear as *The Stranger* or *The Outsider* in different English-language markets. Any quoted wording in a future close-reading or slide must identify the specific translator and edition. This lecture set paraphrases in original prose and does not reproduce extended copyrighted language.

**Courtroom epistemology.** The prosecutor's moral interpretation of funeral conduct is part of the **fictional trial**. It should not be reported as a verified general statement about French-Algerian evidentiary law. The killer's lack of conventional verbal emotion and the victim's death are both real within the fiction; criticism should not collapse either into the other.

Their URLs, access labels and scope appear in `metadata/sources.json`; five exact-text claim anchors per lecture are in `metadata/049100.json`–`metadata/049900.json`.

The complete copyrighted French novel and every English edition were **not collated** for this draft. The editorial record thus explicitly avoids claiming edition-level textual verification. The philosophical article's reading is a specific scholarly interpretation, not a universal consensus. Keep attribution of ideas to their original author and treat later critical responses as interpretive interventions rather than corrections to the novel's events.
