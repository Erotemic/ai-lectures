# To Kill a Mockingbird — The Story, Part Three: After the Verdict

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At the center of Harper Lee's *To Kill a Mockingbird* is a trial. A Black man called Tom Robinson has been convicted of assaulting a white woman, Mayella Ewell, despite a defense that raised serious questions about the accusation. The lawyer who defended him is Atticus Finch. His children, Jem and Scout, watched from the balcony. Their friend Dill was there too. When the verdict was announced, Jem could scarcely believe it. Scout, younger and still trying to assemble the meaning of what she has seen, watched the Black spectators rise in respect as Atticus left the courtroom.

The children now have to go back to ordinary days in Maycomb. They eat, quarrel, attend school, visit neighbors, and listen to the adults explain what happened. But Jem's idea of the town has been shaken. He expected a jury to consider evidence and return the verdict that followed from it. Instead, the court has convicted Tom. The people who made that decision can return to their homes without appearing any different from the neighbors he knew before.

The Black community sends gifts of food to Atticus's house, an expression of gratitude that moves him, though the families contributing can scarcely afford the expense. Miss Maudie invites Jem, Scout, and Dill for something to eat and speaks about the people who did try to help Tom. She is trying to offer the children a reason not to surrender to despair. It is a difficult consolation. Tom remains convicted, and the family whose life is at stake cannot solve the problem by saying that a few people acted decently.

There is an appeal to be pursued, but Bob Ewell, Mayella's father, is not content with the verdict. Although the court accepted his family's accusation, Atticus exposed contradictions in his testimony and made him look foolish in public. Bob is angry and wants Atticus to suffer for humiliating him. He confronts Atticus in town, spits at him, and threatens him. Atticus refuses to fight. He wants to believe that Ewell's anger will spend itself without further harm. The people who know Bob are not all so confident.

Meanwhile, Scout tries to return to the concerns of school and family. Aunt Alexandra, her father's sister, continues to live at the Finch house and tries to teach Scout the manners and obligations expected of a respectable young woman. Scout is most comfortable in overalls and dislikes the idea that polite behavior requires pretending not to notice things. Yet she also begins to recognize that Alexandra worries about the family. Even the adults who seem opposed in principle must share the same rooms when there is danger or grief.

Tom Robinson never receives the freedom the children hoped a just verdict would give him. News arrives that he has been shot and killed while trying to escape from prison. The reported number of shots is seventeen. Atticus goes with Calpurnia to tell Helen Robinson, Tom's wife. The news reaches Scout indirectly as adult voices break into her everyday world. There is no possibility of an appeal that can bring Tom back. The trial has ended in a conviction and now in a death, leaving Helen and the children to carry the loss.

Some townspeople speak of the killing as the unsurprising result of Tom's attempting to flee. Others recognize how little chance he had been given. Mr. Underwood, who runs the local newspaper, writes an editorial about the killing. Atticus had tried to persuade the court to give Tom a fair hearing; now the town is left with statements and reactions rather than a living man whose case could still be contested.

The Ewell family is not finished with the people connected to the trial. Helen Robinson, trying to support her children, takes work with Link Deas, Tom's former employer. Bob Ewell begins frightening her on her way to work. Link Deas confronts him and orders him to stop. There is also an incident at the home of Judge Taylor, who presided over the trial, suggesting that someone is trying to get inside. The threats have a recognizable direction: people who contradicted or embarrassed Bob Ewell are being made to feel vulnerable.

Not everything that happens in Maycomb is announced as part of the conflict. Some of it appears in the small, disorienting things Scout hears at school. Her teacher, Miss Gates, condemns the persecution of Jewish people in Nazi Germany. Scout agrees that such cruelty is wrong. But she has also heard the teacher express contempt for Black people in Maycomb after Tom's trial. Scout cannot easily reconcile the two attitudes. The teacher condemns injustice at a distance and accepts something closely related in her own community. Scout asks Jem about the contradiction, but the trial remains too painful for him to discuss. He wants her to leave the subject alone.

As autumn arrives, the school prepares a Halloween pageant. Scout will take part wearing a costume shaped like a ham, made bulky by wire and fabric. She is meant to appear onstage at the right moment but falls asleep and misses her cue, an embarrassment that feels enormous at the time. After the festivities, she and Jem start home on foot in the dark. Scout is still wearing the awkward costume. Jem, nearly a teenager now, accompanies her along the familiar road.

Their walk should be ordinary. The trees and houses belong to a neighborhood they have crossed for years. But they begin hearing footsteps behind them. At first it might be someone playing a trick. Then the sounds come closer. Jem becomes concerned. Before they can get home, a man attacks them. The children cannot see clearly what is happening. Scout's costume restricts her movement and her view. Jem struggles with the attacker and is badly hurt. Scout hears frightening noises, stumbles, and tries to make sense of bodies moving in the dark. Another figure enters the struggle. The violence stops.

Someone carries Jem toward the Finch house. Scout follows, shaken and unsure who has rescued them. At home a doctor is called. Jem's arm is broken, and there are other injuries to examine. Atticus is frightened for both children. The sheriff, Heck Tate, comes to investigate. Outside, a man has been found dead. It is Bob Ewell. The violence that followed the trial has reached the Finch children at last.

Scout sees a pale, quiet man standing back in the room, almost hidden from attention. For a moment she does not understand who he is. Then she recognizes Arthur Radley. Boo Radley, the neighbor she and Jem once described as a monster, has come out of the house. He heard or saw the attack and intervened. He carried Jem home. The children had spent years inventing terrifying explanations for the life inside the Radley place. In the end, the man from that house protected them when an actual danger appeared on their street.

Atticus and Heck Tate disagree about how to account for Bob Ewell's death. Atticus initially fears that Jem may have killed Ewell while defending himself and Scout. He does not want a special exception made for his son. But Tate has examined the scene and knows what happened well enough to understand that Arthur Radley was the rescuer. The sheriff insists that Ewell fell on his own knife. He is not prepared to drag Arthur into a public inquiry that would turn a private, frightened man into the center of town attention. Atticus slowly realizes that Tate is determined to protect the person who saved his children.

Scout understands something of that determination too. For years she had wished Arthur would emerge so she could satisfy her curiosity. Now that he is standing in front of her, she no longer asks him to explain himself or perform the part she invented for him. She speaks to him gently and guides him to Jem's room. He looks at the sleeping boy, then asks to go home. Scout takes him by the arm and walks with him down the street, allowing him the dignity of looking as though he is escorting her rather than being escorted.

At the Radley porch she pauses. From there, she can see the Finch home and the route she and Jem have taken so many times. She can imagine the children's games on the street, the changing seasons, the night of the fire, the gifts left in the tree, and the recent walk in the darkness, all as events a neighbor might have watched from that side of the road. She returns home alone. She has finally met Arthur Radley and learned that the most frightening stories about him were wrong.

Atticus is still awake. Jem is recovering, and Scout is exhausted. Her father stays with the children, and the ordinary intimacy of the house resumes: a parent with a book, a child struggling to remain awake, the knowledge that tomorrow will bring another day. The book closes with Scout's childhood world both restored and permanently altered. Her brother is alive because Arthur came to their aid. Tom Robinson is dead, and nothing that happens on the Radley porch can reverse his death. The town remains Maycomb, full of the same people and houses. Scout's understanding of those people will never again be quite so simple.

## Listening companion

**Purpose:** The conclusion of the plot-first orientation. Full ending spoilers. The following `001000` lecture is an interpretation of the novel's form, history, and social implications; it can be heard directly after this account without presuming the listener has read the novel recently.

**Key events:** The community's response to the verdict; Tom Robinson's death; Bob Ewell's threats to people associated with the trial; Scout's conflicting school lessons; the Halloween attack; Arthur Radley's intervention; the sheriff's decision; the walk to the Radley porch.

**Important distinction:** The book does not say that a rescue compensates for Tom Robinson's killing. Both events are true within its fictional world, and the later analysis asks what the novel gains and risks by bringing the stories together.

**Reading anchors:** Lee, *To Kill a Mockingbird*, chapters 22–31. See the National Endowment for the Arts *Big Read* guide for the intersecting plotlines, and the original novel for the language and detail of the final scenes.
