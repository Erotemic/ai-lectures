# One Hundred Years of Solitude — The Story, Part 1: The Founding Family

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

One Hundred Years of Solitude tells the story of a town and a family over generations. Many people have the same names, and some lives echo other lives so closely that a newcomer can lose track of who belongs to which century. For this telling, it will help to follow the family in approximate chronological order. We will begin with the founders of Macondo and end, in later episodes, with the last child born to their descendants. This account includes the entire ending of Gabriel García Márquez's novel.

José Arcadio Buendía and Úrsula Iguarán are cousins who marry despite family fears that the marriage of close relatives might produce a child with a pig's tail. Their early life is marked by a quarrel. After another man mocks José Arcadio Buendía, he kills him. The dead man's ghost returns, and the couple finally abandons their home, traveling with a group of people through difficult country until they establish a settlement beside a river. They call it Macondo. At first the town seems remote from the wars, industries, and governments that shape the wider world.

José Arcadio Buendía is curious about nearly everything. He is fascinated when a band of traveling gypsies arrives bearing inventions and marvels. Their leader, Melquíades, shows the villagers magnets, scientific equipment, and objects the town has never seen. José Arcadio Buendía becomes convinced that experimentation can transform his future. He pours energy into attempts at alchemy and discovery, often leaving Úrsula to manage the household and its practical needs. When the gypsies introduce ice to Macondo, the encounter becomes one of the childhood memories that will stay with the family for generations.

José Arcadio Buendía and Úrsula have children. The eldest boy, José Arcadio, is impulsive and physically powerful. Another son, Aureliano, is quieter and capable of extraordinary concentration. Their daughter Amaranta grows up amid complicated affections and rivalries. A girl named Rebeca also joins the household; she arrives with the remains of her parents and the habit of eating earth. Over time the household becomes a crowded center of Macondo's life, filled with relatives, servants, visitors, and the people who come asking Úrsula for help.

Another kind of change arrives with a magistrate from outside, Don Apolinar Moscote, who brings government expectations into a town that had believed it could govern its own affairs. José Arcadio Buendía resents the intrusion, yet the families also begin to intermarry. The peaceful, self-contained picture of Macondo is already changing before the Colonel takes up arms. Úrsula travels in search of the son who left with the gypsies and finds routes to settlements the founders had not known. New traders and visitors follow. The town is joining a larger map.

Rebeca brings an illness of forgetting. People in Macondo stop sleeping. At first the wakefulness sounds like a blessing, a chance to work and talk through the night, but soon memory begins to unravel. The villagers label objects with their names and functions, afraid they may forget what ordinary things are for. Melquíades returns and brings a remedy. He later lives in the family house and leaves behind manuscripts in a script that nobody can yet decipher. Those papers will survive long after the people who first saw him have died.

José Arcadio, the eldest son, leaves with the gypsies. Years later he returns as a huge, worldly man and marries Rebeca, scandalizing the family and interrupting her expected marriage to a musician named Pietro Crespi. Amaranta has also developed an intense attachment to Pietro, and the rivalries among the young women leave wounds that endure long after the immediate choices have been made. Aureliano marries a young woman named Remedios Moscote, but she dies while still very young. The death removes one of the people who had brought warmth into his increasingly remote life.

The settlement grows and becomes entangled with politics. Macondo is no longer so isolated that events in the nation can be dismissed as rumors. Aureliano is drawn toward the Liberal cause in civil conflict, while the government represents Conservative authority. He becomes Colonel Aureliano Buendía, a military leader who will launch many rebellions, face imprisonment and execution, and acquire a reputation far beyond Macondo. His wars will stretch across years and leave a trail of children, losses, and wounded relationships. Even when he comes home, he may feel less at home than before he left.

José Arcadio Buendía, the founder, loses his hold on the ordinary world. His obsession with time, invention, and the figures of the past grows into a condition his family can no longer manage indoors. He is tied to a chestnut tree in the courtyard, where he spends many years speaking with presences others cannot see. Úrsula continues to hold the household together around him. This is what the first generations leave their descendants: a growing town, a house of remarkable endurance, unfinished love affairs, a war about to consume the region, and manuscripts that appear to contain a future nobody understands.
