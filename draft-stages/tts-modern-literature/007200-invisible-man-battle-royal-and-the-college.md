# Invisible Man — The Story, Part 1: Battle Royal and the College

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man is narrated by a man who never gives us the name by which he was born. When he begins speaking, he is living underground in a city, drawing power illegally to keep an astonishing number of electric lights burning around him. He insists that he is invisible, although he has a body and people can see him. He means that people who encounter him repeatedly fail to recognize the person who is actually present. From this underground room he begins telling us how he arrived there.

As a young man in the segregated South, he is an ambitious and capable student. He believes that careful speech, good manners, and hard work will earn him the opportunity to improve his position. After giving a successful school speech, he is invited to repeat it for a gathering of prominent white men. The evening turns into something terrifying. He and several other young Black men are blindfolded and forced to fight one another for the amusement of the audience. Afterward they are told to scramble for money on an electrified rug. Humiliated and injured, the narrator is finally allowed to deliver his speech. He has learned something about the men who claim to reward him, but he also receives a briefcase and a scholarship to a Black college. He goes away believing that the scholarship may open the life he wants.

At college, the narrator respects the institution's president, Dr. Bledsoe, and imagines himself someday becoming a leader of similar standing. He is given the duty of driving a wealthy white trustee, Mr. Norton, around the area. Norton asks to see more than the carefully prepared scenes of the campus. Their drive takes them near the home of Jim Trueblood, a poor Black farmer whose account of an incestuous assault within his own family has drawn scandal. Norton becomes fascinated by Trueblood's story, even while the narrator is desperate to remove him from an encounter that could embarrass the college.

The day grows more difficult. Norton becomes ill and the narrator takes him to a place called the Golden Day, where Black veterans gather and where a visiting college trustee is not received with the deference the narrator expects. Some of the men are former professionals, soldiers, or intellectuals whose lives have been damaged by racism and psychiatric confinement. The narrator cannot control what is said in front of Norton. His drive has become a sequence of unexpected disclosures, and he returns to campus fearing punishment.

The narrator has also heard a powerful speech honoring the college's Founder, a figure whose image dominates the institution's ceremonial life. The speaker, Reverend Homer A. Barbee, is blind. He describes the Founder's achievements in grand, reverent language while the students are expected to listen as though a shared destiny has been settled for them. The narrator wants to participate in that future. The president's anger after the disastrous drive is therefore more than the loss of a job: it threatens the whole picture of himself he has been encouraged to cultivate.

Bledsoe is furious. The president tells the narrator that his first obligation was to protect the institution's image in the eyes of powerful supporters. Truth, in this account, is less important than managing what wealthy white men are allowed to see. The narrator protests that he followed Norton's wishes. Bledsoe does not accept the excuse. He expels him, but offers letters of introduction to important men in New York City, where the narrator may find summer work and perhaps earn the right to return to school. The narrator leaves with the letters and a new determination to prove his worth.

In New York he visits offices and waits for answers that never arrive. The letters do not produce jobs. At last, the son of one of the men addressed agrees to explain why: Bledsoe has written not recommendations but warnings that the narrator is troublesome and unworthy of help. The supposed route back to college was a method of sending him away without allowing him to understand what had happened. The young man's faith in Bledsoe is shattered. He has obeyed the college president's instructions and discovered that the president used his trust against him.

The young man takes the employment that is offered to him instead: a job at a paint factory called Liberty Paints. Its pride is a product advertised as brilliantly white, and the factory has its own complicated hierarchy of workers and supervisors. The narrator enters a new world of machinery, rules, and people whose power over him has little connection to his intelligence or education. His first hopes in the South have fallen apart. The next stage of his story takes place in a factory where, once again, he will be made to perform a role he scarcely understands.
