# Brave New World - The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In London, centuries after the world familiar to us has passed away, children are not born into families. They are manufactured through a coordinated system of reproduction and conditioning. The people who run it regard this as a tremendous achievement. No one need risk the instability of pregnancy, parenthood, or unpredictable upbringing. Human beings can be made for the places society has assigned them, educated into desires appropriate to their work, and protected from most of the discomforts that once made ordinary life so difficult.

A visiting group of students tours the Central London Hatchery and Conditioning Centre. Their guide explains how embryos are sorted and manipulated. Some will become Alphas, people trained for intellectual and administrative roles. Others are destined to be Betas, Gammas, Deltas, or Epsilons, occupying positions farther down the hierarchy. For many of the lower castes, a laboratory process produces large numbers of nearly identical individuals. Conditioning begins before birth and continues through childhood. Small children learn to recoil from things society does not want them to enjoy, and to repeat principles that discourage solitude, stable attachment, and independent judgment.

The calendar itself marks the years after Ford, a figure associated with mass production and industrial efficiency. Citizens are taught to consume, stay cheerful, accept their place, and distrust feelings that might disrupt the machinery. A drug called soma makes distress easier to escape without the inconveniences of an ordinary hangover. Sexual relationships are expected to remain casual. Marriage and the word mother provoke embarrassment or disgust. Many people in this World State are sincerely content. The system does not need to order every person to feel happy every minute; it has organized childhood and desire so that a great many people will want what it supplies.

This is the world of Aldous Huxley's Brave New World. Its characters will discover that even a carefully arranged happiness can leave questions no one is allowed to ask.

## Bernard, Lenina, and Helmholtz

Bernard Marx is an Alpha who does not fit easily among the other Alphas. He is smaller than expected, painfully aware of how others judge him, and resentful of the easy confidence with which his peers inhabit their privileges. He dislikes the casualness of social life, or at least the humiliation he experiences within it. Yet his criticism is inconsistent. He often wants the approval of the very people he despises.

Lenina Crowne is a young woman who works within the reproductive and conditioning system. She is attractive, friendly, and mostly at ease with the rules that Bernard questions. Her friend Fanny advises her not to become too attached to one man. Lenina has a relationship with Henry Foster and has become curious about Bernard. She cannot always understand why he resists forms of recreation that seem obviously pleasurable to her.

Bernard has a friend named Helmholtz Watson, a gifted writer and lecturer whose job is to compose language that efficiently creates emotional effects in listeners. Helmholtz has everything Bernard envies: good looks, social success, and confident ability. But he feels confined by his work. He knows he can make words produce the prescribed response, and suspects that words could do much more if there were anything sufficiently important to say. Bernard suffers because people reject him. Helmholtz suffers because a world arranged to satisfy everyone gives his talent almost nothing worth expressing.

Bernard invites Lenina on a trip to the Savage Reservation in New Mexico, a territory outside the ordinary life of the World State. Its people still age, form families, experience illness, practice religious rituals, and live under conditions that the visitors find shocking. Bernard obtains permission from the Director of the Hatchery. During their conversation, the Director unexpectedly recalls having visited the same place years before with a young woman who disappeared during a storm. He never found her. He seems disturbed at having spoken so personally, and soon threatens Bernard with exile to Iceland for his nonconformity.

Bernard and Lenina leave for the Reservation. On arrival, the contrast is overwhelming. Lenina sees worn bodies and poverty, things her society has worked to make invisible. They watch a ceremony involving pain and sacrifice. Neither knows quite what to do with the fact that these lives continue without the familiar comforts and rules of London. The Reservation is not portrayed as a simple paradise beyond technology. It is a difficult place, and its inhabitants have their own exclusions and cruelties.

## John and Linda

They meet John, a young man whose appearance sets him apart from many people on the Reservation. John grew up there with his mother, Linda, who came from the World State. Linda is the woman the Director lost years before. She became pregnant, could not return, and raised her son in a society that treated both of them as outsiders. Linda retained the sexual customs of her old home, which provoked hostility among women on the Reservation. John was rejected in turn. He learned to hunger for a place in the community and to fear the ways he was not allowed to belong.

John's education has been assembled from fragments. He knows some of the World State through his mother's memories. He has a book of Shakespeare and reads it intensely, trying to understand love, jealousy, suffering, honor, and courage through stories written for a world long gone. The words give emotional shape to feelings his neighbors and his mother cannot fully explain. He imagines London as a place where the life Linda describes might make room for him.

Bernard realizes that John is the Director's son. In a civilization where birth and family are objects of shame, this fact could ruin a powerful man's career. Bernard receives permission to bring John and Linda to London. John is eager to see the society he has imagined. Linda wants relief from years of deprivation and shame. Lenina wants to return to the ease and cleanliness of her home.

Back in London, the Director prepares to condemn Bernard publicly for his eccentric behavior. Bernard instead presents Linda and John. Linda identifies the Director as the father of her child. The assembled people react with horrified amusement. Their leader's authority collapses under a word that should have been ordinary but has become obscene to them. The Director disappears from his position.

Bernard's status changes overnight. He has brought home the man people call the Savage, and everyone wants access to him. Invitations arrive. People who once ignored Bernard flatter him. He enjoys the privileges while finding reasons to believe his earlier complaints have been vindicated. For a time John is a fashionable object, displayed at gatherings and discussed as a marvel of cultural difference.

Linda, meanwhile, retreats almost completely into soma. Years of harsh life are followed by a chemically suspended comfort in which she rarely has to confront the world. John sees her slipping away. The society that regards aging and sorrow as problems to be removed has no easy place for someone who wants to grieve a mother before she is gone.

## A love that cannot find common words

John is strongly attracted to Lenina, and Lenina is attracted to him. But they use incompatible ideas of what intimacy means. John imagines love through Shakespeare, with loyalty, restraint, suffering, and idealization. Lenina expects sex to be a cheerful and ordinary expression of liking. She has never been taught that casual desire might appear to another person as a threat to something sacred.

When Lenina approaches him directly, John reacts not with tender explanation but with disgust and anger. He uses language of shame and purity against her, and his behavior becomes frightening. His ideals do not make him morally impeccable. The distance between them is genuine, but John answers it in ways that hurt a person who has not set out to hurt him.

John also meets Helmholtz, who is fascinated by the strange power of Shakespeare's language. Helmholtz can admire passion and conflict as resources for art, but he sometimes laughs at the old social arrangements on which Shakespeare's tragedies depend. Neither man can simply transfer his experience into the other's world. Still, Helmholtz and John recognize in each other a dissatisfaction deeper than Bernard's wish to be admired.

Then John learns that Linda is dying. At the hospital, she is heavily drugged, drifting through memories and barely recognizing him. Children are being shown the dying as part of their conditioning, taught to treat death as an ordinary event without emotional weight. John is horrified. His mother's death is not simply a biological conclusion to him. It is the loss of the person with whom he shared his earliest life, complicated by resentment, shame, and affection he does not know how to express.

After Linda dies, John encounters a group of lower-caste workers receiving their soma ration. He sees the drug as an instrument of enslavement. He tries to stop the distribution and throws the tablets away, calling on the workers to recognize their freedom. The result is a disturbance, not the moral awakening he has imagined. The people he is addressing want the relief they have been taught to expect. Helmholtz joins the effort to defend John, while Bernard hesitates. Police arrive and disperse the crowd with calming measures, including soma vapor. John, Helmholtz, and Bernard are taken to see one of the World Controllers, Mustapha Mond.

## The Controller's answer

Mond is not a fool who has accidentally misunderstood the human cost of the State. He is an intelligent man who knows much of the history and knowledge kept from ordinary citizens. He explains that the system has chosen stability. Art that produces dangerous passions, research that might upset settled arrangements, religious longing that grows out of fear and suffering, and the claims of personal liberty all threaten the equilibrium. Happiness, predictability, and social peace have been bought by restricting what people can know or desire.

Helmholtz and Bernard are told they will be sent to islands where people who cannot conform live away from the central system. Bernard is distraught at the loss of his position and comforts. Helmholtz finds the prospect attractive: at last he might have something worth writing about. The same punishment has different meanings for two men who both called themselves dissatisfied.

John confronts Mond over the price of the World State's achievements. To John, a life from which suffering, dangerous choice, moral struggle, great art, and genuine love have been systematically removed is diminished, whatever its comfort. Mond does not deny the cost. He insists that society chose the arrangement because people can be made happy in it. The argument does not close because neither man accepts the other's definition of a humanly good life.

John asks to join the exiles, but Mond refuses. John is to remain where he can be observed. He therefore tries another kind of escape. He goes to an abandoned lighthouse in the countryside, seeking a hard, isolated existence in which he can work, fast, pray, and punish himself for what he believes are his sins. He wants to make his life his own by rejecting the comforts that the State has engineered.

But the World State cannot leave him alone. Reporters discover him. His attempts at purification become entertainment. Visitors come to watch a man whom they consider exotic perform the difficult and painful things he does to himself. The society that never knew how to hear his grief turns that grief into a spectacle.

When Lenina appears among the visitors, John's desire and fury become entangled with his self-punishment. He attacks her and strikes out at himself. The crowd's excitement swells into a collective sexual frenzy, and John is caught in the event he most wanted to resist. The next day he wakes horrified by what happened and by his inability to keep the boundary he had drawn around himself. He dies by suicide in the lighthouse.

The book ends without a successful rebellion or a lesson learned by the State. Mond has not been defeated. Bernard and Helmholtz have been sent away. Linda is dead. Lenina has been harmed. John sought freedom first in an imagined civilization and then in isolation, and found no place where his understanding of love, grief, and moral life could survive intact.

We have followed a system that claims to have solved human unhappiness and a young man who cannot live by its solution. The question now is not simply whether Huxley's world is oppressive. It is what his society counts as happiness, how its citizens acquire their desires, and why John's resistance is both necessary to understand and tragically flawed.
