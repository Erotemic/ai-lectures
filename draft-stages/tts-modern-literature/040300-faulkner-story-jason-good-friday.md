# The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part Three: Jason’s Good Friday

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The third section of The Sound and the Fury opens on Good Friday, April sixth, nineteen twenty-eight. It is one day before Benjy's Saturday, but the story has traveled forward from Quentin's last day in nineteen ten. Now the man telling us about the Compson household is Jason, the remaining adult brother who manages its money. He is not difficult to follow in the way Benjy and Quentin are. He speaks in sentences whose targets are immediately clear. He complains, calculates, mocks, and blames. If the earlier sections force us to work out what a speaker cannot explain, this one requires a different effort: we must decide how much to believe a speaker who explains everything to his own advantage.

Jason begins in a state of anger that seems to have no resting point. His mother is ill, or says she is ill; Benjy makes demands he finds intolerable; servants do not obey him quickly enough; and his teenage niece, whom the family calls Miss Quentin, repeatedly escapes his supervision. She is Caddy's daughter. The name was taken from the Harvard student who died before she was born. Jason treats her as living evidence that his sister ruined his prospects. In his account she is not a young person whose circumstances deserve attention. She is an expense, a danger, a liar, and an insult to his authority.

That authority is more fragile than it sounds. Jason works in a farm-supply store and depends on a household whose social standing is a residue of its past. The Compsons are not prosperous aristocrats in possession of a functioning estate. They are people who talk as though a lost place in society entitles them to obedience. Jason knows how little is left, and he measures every injury in money. When he looks back to Caddy's marriage with Herbert Head, he remembers a promised bank job. Herbert withdrew the offer after the marriage failed. Jason has turned that disappointment into a story in which Caddy owes him the life he imagines he would have had.

The most consequential thing Jason does with money is deliberately hidden from his mother. Caddy has been sending funds to help support her daughter. Mrs. Compson does not want to accept her disgraced daughter's assistance, and Jason pretends to enforce the refusal. He shows his mother fraudulent substitutes for the checks and allows her to burn them in a performance of injured family honor. Meanwhile, he keeps the real proceeds. The deception allows him to steal from both the mother and the girl while telling himself he is the one person who works and suffers. Money, in his mind, becomes proof that he is right; control of the money becomes permission to punish.

The niece is not docile. Miss Quentin slips out of the house, meets people her uncle dislikes, and refuses his moral lectures. A traveling show is in Jefferson, and Jason grows alarmed that she is meeting one of its performers. He pursues her through town, interrogates people, and watches her with an intensity that he regards as vigilance. The reader sees coercion. The more he polices her, the more she seeks ways around him. The fact that she resists does not make every choice safe; it does make Jason's claim to be her selfless guardian untenable. He is largely uninterested in her welfare apart from what it can do to his own reputation and authority.

Jason's language reveals his world as clearly as any formal flashback. He insults women, Black employees, religious figures, customers, and his own family. He talks as if exploitation were an ordinary privilege of the competent and complaint a mark of inferior people. His self-pity is constant even when he is in control. Faulkner makes this voice energetic and sometimes darkly comic, but the energy must not be confused with moral credibility. Jason is capable of reporting accurate facts. He is also capable of arranging those facts into a case in which he is always the wronged party.

A commercial side of his life makes the self-deception more visible. He follows cotton prices and speculates in the market, imagining quick gains that will compensate him for what the family has cost. His decisions do not deliver the mastery he boasts of. He becomes enraged by losses and suspicious of strangers, while finding other people to blame for his calculations. The old family estate has been reduced by years of expenditure and bad judgment; his own response is not to rebuild it through honest work but to gamble, hoard, and embezzle. He can see figures on a page much better than he can see an ethical obligation.

At home his mother continually reinforces the family's mythology. She praises the supposedly dutiful Jason and laments the other children. The love she extends him is not the kind that asks him to become more generous. It is an alliance in grievance. He agrees to protect her from unpleasant facts, and in doing so gains her support for cruelty and concealment. The mother who refuses Caddy's money on principle is being deceived by the son she trusts. It is a miserable domestic bargain: she keeps her self-image, he keeps the money, and Miss Quentin is denied what her mother has tried to provide.

Dilsey, the aging Black cook, is present throughout these conflicts as the person expected to make the household function. She and her family have looked after the Compson children for years. Jason orders and insults them; they answer, comply where necessary, or quietly protect others. The narrator is not interested in their history except where it intersects with his complaints. Yet even in his version we can detect how much the house depends on the labor he refuses to respect. He imagines his money makes him master of events, but he could not manage the household's most basic needs alone.

Benjy also figures in Jason's anger. He is a brother with continuing needs, not a profitable asset. Jason speaks about him with contempt and treats his presence as a threat to social respectability. An adult man who has not been given authority over his own body and who has lost the sister who understood him now lives under the control of a brother who values almost nothing about him. Jason's version of practical reason is therefore an especially severe judgment upon the family. It counts resources and obscures the people whose lives those resources sustain.

As Good Friday continues, Jason moves between the store, the house, and the streets where he suspects Miss Quentin has gone. He watches the performer's movements, invents accusations, and returns to the familiar rhythm of argument. Faulkner does not give him a sudden confession. He has little reason to doubt that his resentment is justified, and it has become easier for him to preserve the structure of his life than to consider the harm he does. The narrative's relative clarity is itself an irony. Unlike the brothers whose minds shift through time, Jason can tell a continuous story. But he is least willing to allow a competing account any weight.

The catastrophe of Easter morning has not happened yet. Miss Quentin has not yet taken the stored money and escaped. By ending Jason's section on Friday, before Saturday and Sunday gather the consequences, the novel lets us occupy his justification before we see its failure. He believes he owns his niece's future and her mother's financial help. He believes he alone is being cheated. What he cannot imagine is that she might refuse the role of permanently indebted prisoner that he has written for her.

Faulkner's structure has now given us three different responses to the same family history. Benjy responds to absence with uncomprehending grief. Quentin responded to his sister's adulthood with impossible demands upon time, purity, and himself. Jason responds with bills, threats, and theft. The apparent maturity of his language is no guarantee that he understands more. Indeed, the precision of his accusations may help him avoid knowing the person he has become.
