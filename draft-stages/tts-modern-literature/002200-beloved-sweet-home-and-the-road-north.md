# Beloved — The Story, Part 1: Sweet Home and the Road North

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Before we arrive at the haunted house in Toni Morrison's Beloved, we need to know where its inhabitants came from. The book does not tell their history from beginning to end. Memories emerge in fragments, and some events are disclosed long after we have seen their effects. For this telling, we will follow the lives that lead to the house first, then return to the events of 1873. Everything that follows reveals major parts of the novel, including the death at its center.

Sethe is enslaved at a Kentucky farm called Sweet Home. The name sounds pleasant, and for a time its owner, Mr. Garner, prides himself on treating the enslaved men who work there as if they possess adult dignity. But his supposed kindness cannot change the fact that he owns people, controls their work, and decides their futures. Among the men are Paul A, Paul F, Paul D, Sixo, and Halle. Halle works to buy freedom for his mother, Baby Suggs, and eventually marries Sethe. Sethe has children, and for a time she knows a kind of domestic life in the midst of captivity. Nothing about that security can be relied upon.

After Mr. Garner dies, a new authority arrives: a man whom the enslaved people call schoolteacher. He has nephews who work alongside him, and he studies the people he has been given power over as if they were specimens to be measured, classified, and trained. Sethe becomes the object of their scrutiny. She discovers them writing down supposed human and animal traits. The men also take the breast milk she has been saving for her baby. When Sethe tells Mrs. Garner what happened, the men whip her so severely that her back is left covered with scars. She is pregnant. Her children have already begun the journey toward freedom, and she is desperate to catch up with them.

The people who plan the escape have very different ideas of how much they can risk. Some have been imagining a place beyond Sweet Home for years; others have made private bargains with hope that they barely dare speak aloud. Sethe's husband Halle has used his labor to buy Baby Suggs's freedom, and Sethe believes he will come with her when the time arrives. That belief matters. She is not leaving merely to save herself but trying to gather the family she has been allowed to create under another person's authority. The people they mean to leave behind are armed, organized, and accustomed to treating any attempt at freedom as theft.

An escape has been planned. Not everyone makes it. Sixo is captured and killed; his death is terrible, and the people carrying out the killing believe they can do it without moral consequence. Paul D is captured and sent into a brutal chain of ownership and punishment. Halle does not join Sethe as promised. Later, Paul D tells her that Halle had seen what happened to her and had been so broken by it that he appeared to lose his mind. Sethe has lived for years without knowing whether her husband was dead or alive. She must leave without him.

She moves toward the Ohio River, pregnant, injured, her feet damaged beyond the comfort of ordinary walking. On the road she encounters Amy Denver, a poor young white woman escaping an oppressive situation of her own. Amy is not a magical rescuer. She is young, talkative, and unaware of much that Sethe has suffered. But she stops to help. She attends to Sethe's wounds and remains beside her as the baby is born in a small boat near the river. Sethe names her new daughter Denver after the girl who helped deliver her. The child's name will preserve an encounter whose importance the two women could not have anticipated.

On the other side of the river, a man named Stamp Paid helps carry Sethe and the baby toward Cincinnati. People who have escaped slavery and people who organize escape routes support one another, though they live under constant threat. Sethe reaches the house at 124 Bluestone Road. Baby Suggs, already free, is there. So are Sethe's other children. For the first time, the mother can hold her family in a place where she hopes nobody has the right to claim them. Baby Suggs welcomes her, bathes her, and helps her heal.

Baby Suggs has become a source of strength for people in the surrounding Black community. In a wooded place called the Clearing, she leads gatherings where people are encouraged to cry, laugh, move, and care for bodies that slavery taught them to despise. She does not have a church building or a formal office. She has her own presence, her experience, and the trust of neighbors who have survived things difficult to recount. At one point a large celebration at her house leaves some neighbors feeling envious of the abundance. Feelings among the community are not simple. People support one another and also hurt one another.

Sethe spends twenty-eight days with her children in freedom. She learns the pleasures of working for wages, of choosing where to sleep, of believing she has a future beside them. She has arrived exhausted and battered, but she has arrived alive. The peace is brief. Schoolteacher knows where she has gone. He is coming to the house with men who intend to take Sethe and her children back to Kentucky. The next part of the story begins when their arrival turns twenty-eight days of hope into the terrible memory that will inhabit the house for years.
