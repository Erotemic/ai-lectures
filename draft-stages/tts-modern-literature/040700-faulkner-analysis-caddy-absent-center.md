# Faulkner: Caddy, the Character Everyone Tries to Own

Candace Compson, called Caddy, is a principal force in The Sound and the Fury. She cares for Benjy when the adults fail to understand his needs. Her childhood courage and independence become signs of a freedom her brothers alternately cherish and fear. Her pregnancy and collapsed marriage reshape the household. Her exile leaves both an adult woman and a child in painful circumstances. Yet she does not receive one of the novel's four narrative sections. The book tells us an extraordinary amount about what she means to other people while denying us sustained direct access to her own interior account.

That artistic decision is sometimes described by saying Caddy is absent. But absence can be misleading. She appears in remembered scenes, she speaks in dialogue, she acts, and the reader can see how she comforts one brother or challenges another. The essential absence is more specific: she does not control the telling. We encounter her most frequently through the recollection, fear, desire, or accusation of men who claim a special stake in her body and choices. The gap between a woman who lived and a woman whose meaning others declare is the core of the form.

Benjy is the first to reveal this. He does not offer a theory of femininity or family honor. Caddy is a familiar smell, a voice, a source of touch and guidance. His distress when she changes or departs is real. So is the care she gave him. Yet the fact that Benjy needs stability does not mean Caddy could or should have remained permanently a child or a caregiver. A reader can be moved by his grief without turning it into a verdict that her independence was wrong. The novel's power depends on allowing both the need and the limit to exist.

The famous childhood image of Caddy climbing a tree supplies a vivid measure of the brothers' differences. She wants to see what the adults conceal in the house. She acts. The boys below see her clothes and respond from positions that already anticipate their adult relationships to her. In later readings of the novel, the muddy underclothes have often been turned into a sexual symbol. But the tree also marks a child's curiosity and willingness to do what the boys cannot do from where they stand. The symbolic pattern should not erase the literal agency of the child in the scene.

Quentin's attachment is explicitly moral and sexual in a way Benjy's is not. He identifies his sister's virginity with the legitimacy of the Compson name and, more deeply, with his own identity. When Caddy's choices contradict that ideal, he imagines the violation as something he has to own. His false confession of incest to his father is an effort to replace a history in which Caddy chose another man with a history in which Quentin is at the terrible center. The imagined offense is a grotesque method of recovering possession. It is not evidence that the offense occurred.

The father's response reveals another kind of failure. His skepticism toward the importance of virginity could, in principle, free Caddy from an oppressive standard. Instead, he often addresses Quentin with abstractions and weary contempt. He has little interest in helping either child find a workable language for care or responsibility. His cynicism is not equivalent to respect for a daughter's autonomy. It can leave the existing social arrangements untouched while denying suffering any seriousness.

Jason's version is the bluntest. He believes Caddy cost him a bank job promised through her husband. This belief becomes an inexhaustible claim against her daughter and the whole household. He steals Caddy's financial support and treats the niece as a dangerous investment. A woman who has been expelled for damaging the family's reputation remains financially indispensable to its survival, while the son considered responsible keeps her money. The hypocrisy is not accidental background. It is a concrete method by which control is maintained.

Caddy's daughter, Miss Quentin, intensifies this contradiction. She is another young woman whose sexuality and movements are monitored by men. Jason presents her resistance as proof of moral inheritance from her mother, a story that absolves him of considering what he has done to her. The daughter eventually steals the hidden cash and escapes. Her action is not guaranteed to produce a good life. It does, however, prevent Jason from ending the novel as the successful author of her future. She leaves a narrative that has tried to reduce her to debt and shame.

A scholarly account of Caddy should resist two tempting simplifications. One is to call her simply pure, as though her primary worth lay in the tenderness she gave Benjy before she grew up. That view reproduces the brothers' refusal to acknowledge her adulthood. The other is to call her simply liberated, as though social power and economic exclusion no longer affected her once she left home. Her choices occur in a world where a family's name can be used to deny her access to her own child, where marriage is an economic transaction, and where her support must pass through a hostile brother.

Nor can we pretend that the novel provides complete private testimony from Caddy by means of inference. We hear fragments, but some crucial matters remain unspoken or contested. Quentin's memories of her lovers are not objective transcripts. Jason's accusations are certainly not a neutral record. Even Benjy's affectionate perceptions cannot communicate her motives in full. Part of interpreting the novel well is keeping open the distinction between what someone says about Caddy and what the text actually establishes that Caddy says or does.

It is worth considering why a novel centered on a woman's life might refuse to enter her consciousness directly. The structure can expose the way a patriarchal family turns a person into a symbol of its own honor. It can make the reader feel the violence of not hearing the account that would most disturb the brothers' explanations. But a critic can also ask whether the author has repeated some of the very exclusion he illuminates. These readings are not mutually exclusive; the absence can be a powerful technique and a genuine limitation at the same time.

Faulkner later wrote a genealogy of the Compson family, usually called the Compson Appendix, for The Portable Faulkner in the nineteen forties. It supplies additional imagined history and revisions of emphasis. It should not be silently treated as a missing chapter of the original nineteen twenty-nine text. Readers can compare what that later document says about Caddy with what the original novel permits, but the comparison should preserve the chronology of Faulkner's own writing. An author's later framing does not erase a first edition's formal exclusions.

Caddy's absence asks us to consider a general rule of narrative. A character can be central to everyone's explanation and still not possess the power to explain herself. Sometimes the story most urgently needed is the one the text refuses to provide. In The Sound and the Fury, that refusal generates much of the book's emotional force. It also prevents us from treating the brothers' sorrow, jealousy, or grievance as a final judgment upon the woman around whom their memories circle.
