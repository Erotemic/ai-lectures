# Beloved — The Story, Part 2: The Woodshed and the Woman at the Door

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Schoolteacher and his party arrive in Cincinnati to reclaim Sethe and her children. The news does not reach the house in time. Some neighbors have been hurt by the ostentatious feast at Baby Suggs's home, and the community that once acted as a web of warning and protection does not intervene quickly enough. Sethe sees the men who intend to return her family to slavery. She gets the children into a woodshed. Before they can be taken, she tries to kill them. The oldest daughter, a little girl who has already begun to crawl, dies from a wound to her throat. Sethe is stopped before she can kill the others. The surviving children are hurt and frightened; the baby Denver is still in her arms.

For Sethe, the act follows an unbearable conviction: the children will be better dead than owned. Other people around her do not accept that conviction as a simple answer. The enslavers, suddenly confronted with the aftermath, decide the children are of no further use to them. Sethe is arrested and imprisoned with Denver. People sympathetic to her cause help secure her release. When she comes back to 124, the life she had begun there has ended. Baby Suggs withdraws from the world. Neighbors avoid the family. The oldest daughter's grave receives a stone on which Sethe manages to have a single word carved: Beloved.

Years pass. The house develops a furious haunting. Objects move and sounds break out without visible cause. Sethe's sons, Howard and Buglar, eventually leave. Baby Suggs dies. Sethe continues to work, and Denver grows into a young woman who has never managed to become part of the town around her. She knows that something happened to her older sister, but pieces of the story reach her in different ways. Her mother's attachment to the dead child is woven into their daily lives. In 1873, Sethe and Denver are the only people living at 124, unless one counts the ghost.

Then Paul D comes to the door. He is a man Sethe knew at Sweet Home, someone who remembers the life she had before the river. His journey has been long and painful. After capture he was sold and eventually imprisoned on a Georgia chain gang. The men there were locked into degrading routines and forced labor, chained together until a violent storm helped create an opportunity to escape. He has made his way through several lives since then, keeping the most painful parts of himself closed away. When he sees Sethe, he recognizes her, and she recognizes someone who can say the names of people she believed she had lost.

Paul D enters the house and confronts its supernatural disturbance. The ghost erupts into an upheaval of noise and shaking, and then the house becomes quiet. He begins to imagine staying. Sethe is tempted by the possibility that ordinary companionship might return. Denver is more guarded. The ghost, whatever it was, had been familiar to her, and Paul D is a stranger claiming room in a household that has always centered on her mother and the dead sister. Still, the three of them go to a carnival. They spend an afternoon among people rather than in isolation, and for a moment the future does not look as narrow as it had before.

When they return to 124, a young woman is sitting outside near the house, exhausted and strangely helpless. She says that her name is Beloved. She gives few clear answers about where she has come from. Her skin and behavior suggest an adult, but her speech, appetite, and demands often seem childlike. Sethe and Denver take her in. Denver is immediately drawn to her. Beloved seems to know things she should not know, and she asks questions that call up Sethe's buried memories. Her interest in the past is insistent, almost hungry.

Beloved asks Sethe about earrings, family memories, and the things a baby might have heard long ago. She delights in receiving descriptions of the life before she arrived. Denver tells her about the river and Amy Denver, drawing on the birth story her mother has repeated. At first these exchanges give Denver something she has almost never had: a person her own age who seems willing to listen. But the more the women talk, the more Denver understands that Beloved's desires are directed above all toward Sethe. In a family already shaped around one dead child, a living daughter has again become an observer.

The house that had grown quieter fills again with disturbance, this time in human form. Beloved attaches herself to Sethe and wants stories, attention, and closeness. Denver wants Beloved for herself, and Beloved often treats Denver as an obstacle rather than a friend. Paul D feels pushed from one room to another as if the house itself were rejecting him. Eventually Beloved pressures him into a sexual encounter in a small outbuilding. He is ashamed and troubled, unable to explain what has happened to him. His relationship with Sethe weakens.

At the same time, people outside the house have begun talking again about the old story. Stamp Paid carries a newspaper clipping about Sethe's act and shows it to Paul D. Paul D struggles to believe what he is told, but the truth cannot be dismissed. He asks Sethe about it. She tries to explain why the arrival of schoolteacher left her believing she had only one choice. Paul D hears both what she says and the terrible thing she did. He leaves.

Sethe is now left with her daughter, the mysterious young woman, and the memory of the dead child. She begins to recognize Beloved not simply as a guest but as the return of someone she once lost. Whether the young woman is literally the murdered daughter, an embodiment of that daughter, or something the novel refuses to settle entirely, the people in 124 begin living as though a dead child's demand for her mother has come back into the room. The balance of the household is about to turn dangerous.
