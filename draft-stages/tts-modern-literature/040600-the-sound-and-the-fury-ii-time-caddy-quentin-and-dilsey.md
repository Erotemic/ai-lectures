# The Sound and the Fury II: time, Caddy, Quentin, and Dilsey

## Faulkner: Four Dates, Several Kinds of Time

Open The Sound and the Fury and read only the dates printed at the heads of its four sections. April seventh, nineteen twenty-eight. June second, nineteen ten. April sixth, nineteen twenty-eight. April eighth, nineteen twenty-eight. If we arrange those four days chronologically, we get the second section first, then the third, then the first, then the fourth. Faulkner could have told the story that way. He did not. And while simply rearranging the dates is a useful orientation, the deeper experiment is that the four sections inhabit four different relationships to time.

Benjy's section makes a sensory contact into a passage between decades. A golfer calls for a caddie; Benjy remembers Caddy. A landscape changed by the sale of family land makes the childhood pasture present. The texture of clothes or the smell of a body is linked with another scene. These shifts may be marked typographically, but not in a consistently explanatory way. The sentences do not announce that Benjy is remembering something that occurred in a particular year. They let an event become present again as sensation. This is a literary representation, not a clinical description of how all intellectually disabled people perceive.

To a reader accustomed to narrators who distinguish long ago from yesterday, Benjy's prose feels disorienting. But it is not formless. Physical association is doing the connective work that a historical explanation would normally do. When Caddy is associated with trees, the scent matters because it returns her to a role she once had in Benjy's world. The author's formal choice makes the reader feel why change can be frightening without supplying Benjy with an argument he could not articulate. The past is alive, but it is not available for revision.

Quentin's time is different. Quentin knows about dates, clocks, university schedules, his family's history, and the future he has been given. His crisis is not an inability to distinguish then and now. It is an inability to stop the past from defining him. The watch he breaks is a perfect object because breaking the hands does not stop its mechanism. The scene can be read as an attempt to refuse measurement, but the day continues. Quentin has a plan for his death; the future is painfully available as something he is declining to enter. His thinking fragments because the meaning of each present sensation is already charged with unresolved memories of his sister.

The older brother's recollection is also language struggling against itself. Words repeat, a proposition seems to collapse before its end, names crowd in, and pieces of dialogue from different occasions collide. Benjy's sensory passages do not operate on this plane of self-accusation. Quentin wants an account that will make pain coherent. That very desire disrupts his ability to follow ordinary continuity. He seeks a statement so absolute that the world cannot contradict it, but every actual meeting with another person demonstrates that a life cannot be mastered by an idea.

Jason's narration appears to solve the technical problem. He goes to work, pursues Miss Quentin, complains about people, and explains what he thinks caused his misery. We can readily follow the movement of Good Friday. Yet this clarity is a trap. His words offer a continuous chronology because he wants every event entered as evidence that someone owes him something. He understands the past through accounting: the promised bank job, the money Caddy sends, the sums he diverts, and the financial markets in which he speculates. He can order events but not admit their moral implications. A neat causal account is not necessarily a truthful account.

What is it, then, to understand time? The first three narrators give three answers that fail in different ways. Benjy cannot secure continuity against change. Quentin cannot stop measuring an injury according to an ideal of purity. Jason cannot relinquish the accounting of grievance that makes every hour a repetition of blame. None of these failures is explained merely by the fact that the book's chapters are arranged out of chronological order. The structure draws the reader into the characters' distinct modes of being trapped.

The fourth section changes the medium of narration. We move in third person through Easter Sunday, in and around Dilsey's work, Miss Quentin's flight, and the Black church service. The writing no longer depends on one brother's uninterrupted account of himself. But it does not grant an absolutely transparent or complete knowledge of all lives. It gives us events in a wider field and asks us to notice whose actions, rather than whose explanations, keep the household and the story going.

Easter is another kind of time. The Christian calendar returns each year to death and resurrection. The service attended by Dilsey takes the events of a particular Sunday and places them in a larger ritual history. This is not the same as the Compsons' repeated invocation of family ancestors. The ritual calls a congregation into participation and gives suffering a meaning not supplied by household status. Yet sacred recurrence does not cancel the legal, economic, and racial circumstances of that day. Dilsey must still go home to work. If the sermon offers a wider horizon, its material consequences should not be invented.

The final carriage journey is a small laboratory of time. Luster takes Benjy along a path in a different direction; Benjy cries; Jason redirects the carriage; Benjy grows calm. Repetition restores a pattern of sensations, but it does not undo the years that produced his grief. The square contains a monument whose symbolism touches the South's public history, though Benjy does not provide a history lesson about it. The ritual of the familiar route may be comforting and politically ambiguous at once. The same place can be a stable world for one person and a reminder of a violent social order for another.

A useful comparison is to distinguish three tasks that readers often conflate. The first is locating a remembered event in the family's chronology: was this Caddy's childhood, wedding day, or the later household? The second is interpreting why the memory enters this narrator's consciousness here. The third is evaluating the narrator's account: what does the scene reveal that the narrator fails or refuses to understand? An edition with detailed chronology can help with the first question. It cannot automatically answer the second or third.

The Digital Yoknapatawpha project, developed at the University of Virginia, has mapped Faulkner's fictional events and explicitly acknowledges that many such dates require interpretive decisions. That is a useful scholarly model. We should be confident about the four printed section dates, but cautious about treating each remembered fragment as a court record with an uncontested timestamp. Texts do not always tell us how to resolve every relative time. Even a well-made timeline is an interpretation of evidence.

Faulkner proposed that different colors of ink might help a reader follow the temporal shifts of Benjy's section. The proposal demonstrates that he cared about how a reader could navigate his form. But a color key would not turn the work into a conventional novel. It could mark the year of a scene while leaving the emotional connection between scenes to be discovered. Formal difficulty in this book is not an ornament added to the plot; it is one of the means by which the plot becomes meaningful.

Try to recall the scene of the children by their grandmother's house from four perspectives. In chronological time it is a childhood day. In Benjy's consciousness it is a recurring site of Caddy's love and movement. In Quentin's emotional life Caddy's visible body becomes an object of anxious regulation. In Jason's later world the family's losses become debts. In the final movement Dilsey bears memories of the children as someone who has cared for them. The same event is not simply one event told four times. Its force changes with the lives built around it.

The achievement of The Sound and the Fury lies partly in forcing us to do two apparently opposed things: reconstruct a coherent past and refuse to let that past imprison us inside any one person's explanation. Faulkner's technique makes time palpable as sensation, obsession, accusation, and ritual. A reader may finish with a serviceable family chronology. The harder understanding is why each person can inhabit the same history and still live in a different world.

## Faulkner: Caddy, the Character Everyone Tries to Own

Candace Compson, called Caddy, is a principal force in The Sound and the Fury. She cares for Benjy when the adults fail to understand his needs. Her childhood courage and independence become signs of a freedom her brothers alternately cherish and fear. Her pregnancy and collapsed marriage reshape the household. Her exile leaves both an adult woman and a child in painful circumstances. Yet she does not receive one of the novel's four narrative sections. The book tells us an extraordinary amount about what she means to other people while denying us sustained direct access to her own interior account.

That artistic decision is sometimes described by saying Caddy is absent. But absence can be misleading. She appears in remembered scenes, she speaks in dialogue, she acts, and the reader can see how she comforts one brother or challenges another. The essential absence is more specific: she does not control the telling. We encounter her most frequently through the recollection, fear, desire, or accusation of men who claim a special stake in her body and choices. The gap between a woman who lived and a woman whose meaning others declare is the core of the form.

Benjy is the first to reveal this. He does not offer a theory of femininity or family honor. Caddy is a familiar smell, a voice, a source of touch and guidance. His distress when she changes or departs is real. So is the care she gave him. Yet the fact that Benjy needs stability does not mean Caddy could or should have remained permanently a child or a caregiver. A reader can be moved by his grief without turning it into a verdict that her independence was wrong. The novel's power depends on allowing both the need and the limit to exist.

The famous childhood image of Caddy climbing a tree supplies a vivid measure of the brothers' differences. She wants to see what the adults conceal in the house. She acts. The boys below see her clothes and respond from positions that already anticipate their adult relationships to her. In later readings of the novel, the muddy underclothes have often been turned into a sexual symbol. But the tree also marks a child's curiosity and willingness to do what the boys cannot do from where they stand. The symbolic pattern should not erase the literal agency of the child in the scene.

Quentin's attachment is explicitly moral and sexual in a way Benjy's is not. He identifies his sister's virginity with the legitimacy of the Compson name and, more deeply, with his own identity. When Caddy's choices contradict that ideal, he imagines the violation as something he has to own. His false confession of incest to his father is an effort to replace a history in which Caddy chose another man with a history in which Quentin is at the terrible center. The imagined offense is a grotesque method of recovering possession. It is not evidence that the offense occurred.

The father's response reveals another kind of failure. His skepticism toward the importance of virginity could, in principle, free Caddy from an oppressive standard. Instead, he often addresses Quentin with abstractions and weary contempt. He has little interest in helping either child find a workable language for care or responsibility. His cynicism is not equivalent to respect for a daughter's autonomy. It can leave the existing social arrangements untouched while denying suffering any seriousness.

Jason's version is the bluntest. He believes Caddy cost him a bank job promised through her husband. This belief becomes an inexhaustible claim against her daughter and the whole household. He steals Caddy's financial support and treats the niece as a dangerous investment. A woman who has been expelled for damaging the family's reputation remains financially indispensable to its survival, while the son considered responsible keeps her money. The hypocrisy is not accidental background. It is a concrete method by which control is maintained.

Caddy's daughter, Miss Quentin, intensifies this contradiction. She is another young woman whose sexuality and movements are monitored by men. Jason presents her resistance as proof of moral inheritance from her mother, a story that absolves him of considering what he has done to her. The daughter eventually steals the hidden cash and escapes. Her action is not guaranteed to produce a good life. It does, however, prevent Jason from ending the novel as the successful author of her future. She leaves a narrative that has tried to reduce her to debt and shame.

A scholarly account of Caddy should resist two tempting simplifications. One is to call her simply pure, as though her primary worth lay in the tenderness she gave Benjy before she grew up. That view reproduces the brothers' refusal to acknowledge her adulthood. The other is to call her simply liberated, as though social power and economic exclusion no longer affected her once she left home. Her choices occur in a world where a family's name can be used to deny her access to her own child, where marriage is an economic transaction, and where her support must pass through a hostile brother.

Nor can we pretend that the novel provides complete private testimony from Caddy by means of inference. We hear fragments, but some crucial matters remain unspoken or contested. Quentin's memories of her lovers are not objective transcripts. Jason's accusations are certainly not a neutral record. Even Benjy's affectionate perceptions cannot communicate her motives in full. Part of interpreting the novel well is keeping open the distinction between what someone says about Caddy and what the text actually establishes that Caddy says or does.

It is worth considering why a novel centered on a woman's life might refuse to enter her consciousness directly. The structure can expose the way a patriarchal family turns a person into a symbol of its own honor. It can make the reader feel the violence of not hearing the account that would most disturb the brothers' explanations. But a critic can also ask whether the author has repeated some of the very exclusion he illuminates. These readings are not mutually exclusive; the absence can be a powerful technique and a genuine limitation at the same time.

Faulkner later wrote a genealogy of the Compson family, usually called the Compson Appendix, for The Portable Faulkner in the nineteen forties. It supplies additional imagined history and revisions of emphasis. It should not be silently treated as a missing chapter of the original nineteen twenty-nine text. Readers can compare what that later document says about Caddy with what the original novel permits, but the comparison should preserve the chronology of Faulkner's own writing. An author's later framing does not erase a first edition's formal exclusions.

Caddy's absence asks us to consider a general rule of narrative. A character can be central to everyone's explanation and still not possess the power to explain herself. Sometimes the story most urgently needed is the one the text refuses to provide. In The Sound and the Fury, that refusal generates much of the book's emotional force. It also prevents us from treating the brothers' sorrow, jealousy, or grievance as a final judgment upon the woman around whom their memories circle.

## Faulkner: Quentin, Honor, Shame, and a Life Narrowing

Quentin Compson goes to Harvard with an inheritance that includes much more than his grandfather's watch. He carries the family name, his parents' expectations, the money raised by selling land, and an idea of feminine purity that cannot withstand his sister Caddy's independence. He also carries his own vulnerability. The second section of The Sound and the Fury records an intense psychological crisis. Its fractured language is often admired as an exercise in literary technique, but that technique matters because it allows us to feel how an idea can close off the future of the person who holds it.

Quentin is not simply a representative of an old Southern code. He is a young man whose love and identification with Caddy become so entangled that he cannot imagine her decisions as separate from his fate. He knows what his culture says about honor, marriage, and the reputation of a family. He has learned that a sister's sexual conduct is a measure of the men who are supposed to protect her. These rules distribute responsibility asymmetrically: they make a woman's body a sign of collective virtue while granting her too little power to define that virtue herself.

His false confession of incest is perhaps the most revealing attempt to make those rules work. Quentin tells his father that he and Caddy have committed an act they did not commit. Why invent a crime worse than the situation he fears? One possible answer is that he wants to substitute a shared, forbidden secret for the unbearable fact that Caddy has an independent sexual life. If he can turn her choices into his own catastrophic act, he might at least be part of the meaning. The invention is not a rescue of Caddy. It is an effort to control the story of her body by taking possession of its supposed guilt.

Quentin's father does not believe the confession, and he offers no adequate alternative. His language is bleakly knowing: honor is fragile, ideals are human inventions, time exposes their vanity. To a young person in crisis, this can sound like dismissal, even when it contains skepticism about oppressive conventions. An adult can reject the ideal of chastity without helping a frightened son learn how to love someone whose choices he cannot command. The father has an insight into the artificiality of a rule, but little ability to respond to the pain produced by that rule.

When Quentin breaks the hands off his grandfather's watch, we see what happens when an idea moves from social life into perception. He knows time is passing, and he resents it. His thought is filled with family scenes that no longer exist as he wants them to exist. His day is marked by errands and moments of ordinary social contact, but it is pulled toward the river and his suicide plan. He cannot stop the clock by breaking its display. His emotional world has turned an object that measures change into the symbol of an accusation that will not relent.

The fight with Gerald Bland is another symptom of a masculinity he feels unable to inhabit. Gerald's ease with social life and women seems to embody everything Quentin fears about desire without consequence. Quentin's attack is not an ethical victory over Gerald. It is a futile attempt to make an external opponent responsible for a conflict that cannot be settled through violence. His bodily exhaustion and embarrassment afterward bring the heroic fantasy down to the scale of a young man who is injured and confused.

The episode with the Italian girl is equally instructive for different reasons. Quentin tries to help a child and becomes entangled in public suspicion about his intentions. The encounter is not the same as his relationship with Caddy, but it shows a world in which gestures have meanings that do not obey his private account of them. He can insist that he did no harm and still be forced to confront other people's interpretations. This is an ironic counterpart to his treatment of his sister, whose own intentions he repeatedly attempts to subsume under his reading of her behavior.

In reading this section, distinguish pain from explanation. Quentin's suffering is not in doubt merely because the standards he holds are damaging. Nor does that suffering certify those standards as correct. He may think his life has been destroyed by Caddy's choices; the novel gives us abundant reasons to question that account. His mental crisis is produced through relationships, beliefs, experiences, and a mind whose full condition cannot be inferred from a few diagnostic categories. Calling him simply weak, diseased, or a martyr to an honorable cause makes the book smaller than it is.

The narration itself frustrates the desire for a clean diagnosis. The river, shadow, clocks, desire, the memory of Caddy's body, and his father's words appear in shifting sequence. Readers are given the experience of connection without an authoritative chart of causes. This does not mean the episode is beyond ethical analysis. It means we should state when we are interpreting rather than reporting a fact. The book makes clear that Quentin plans and carries out his suicide. It does not authorize a confident single-cause explanation for why a person ended his life.

One of Faulkner's achievements is that Quentin's education does not prevent him from narrowing his imagination. Harvard represents mobility and prestige, and the family has literally sold part of its ground to send him there. He can engage ideas, learn formal language, and move through a world larger than Jefferson. Yet his consciousness remains organized around a social code learned at home. Physical departure is not the same as intellectual liberation. Money has bought a journey, but not the capacity to think past his father's or his region's inherited categories.

Consider the time between his death and the novel's events in nineteen twenty-eight. Quentin's name returns in Caddy's daughter, a girl the household tries to contain. Jason uses the earlier family crisis as a justification to control her. The dead brother is made into part of the family's continuing script of injury and decline. If Quentin were only a private tragedy, his story might end at the river. Instead, the same standards reappear as living instruments of surveillance and punishment in the lives of women who survive him.

There is no benefit in romanticizing Quentin's suicide as the last honorable act of a decaying aristocracy. That would turn a death into an aesthetic certificate for a code the novel continually exposes as cruel and incoherent. Equally, reducing him to a case study would silence the peculiar intensity of his love and fear. Faulkner's form gives us intimate access without giving us the right to approve or simplify every emotion we encounter.

The broader lesson concerns the difference between love and possession. Quentin experiences his sister's life as though it were a question posed to him and requiring a permanent answer. The more he demands certainty, the less room he has for her humanity and his own future. That is why his section is an achievement of both psychology and narrative ethics. It lets us understand from within the grip of an idea while keeping alive the possibility that the idea is false.

## Faulkner: Dilsey, Labor, Race, and the Limits of Redemption

The final section of The Sound and the Fury opens with a woman who has been working through the whole book but has seldom been allowed to occupy its center. Dilsey Gibson is old, tired, and responsible for an extraordinary amount of the Compson household's daily survival. Her work is so continuous that the white people for whom she works can treat it as part of the weather. In the Easter section it becomes visible, and so does the problem of how the novel represents a Black woman whose life has been shaped by service to a white family.

The Compsons are a family in decline, but decline is relative to a position of privilege. They still expect meals, laundry, care of children, and assistance with Benjy to be supplied. Dilsey and her family make that expectation practical. When Jason curses and Mrs. Compson complains, somebody must put food on the table and respond to the person in distress. The conflict between supposed social standing and actual sustaining labor is one of the work's most effective moral revelations. A person who does little is treated as important because of ancestry; a person without whom the house would collapse is ordered about.

The Gibson family includes Dilsey's children Versh, Frony, and T. P., and her grandson Luster. They are not interchangeable attendants. Across the fragmented childhood scenes and the Easter weekend, different members of the family occupy different years and carry different duties. When Luster searches for a quarter to attend the traveling show, his wish is small but concrete. Benjy's needs can interrupt it. The novel's narrative arrangement allows us to notice both the boy's impatience and the dependence of a white adult upon his work. A reading that calls every such action pure selfless devotion would flatten the life of the worker.

Dilsey's tenderness toward Benjy is significant because it is practical. She helps him move, eat, and settle. She knows when other people have mishandled him. Her care is not necessarily gentle at every moment; it is the labor of a person who has to get things done while her body is tired and the household is disorganized. The novel makes the cost of that competence part of her dignity. Competence is not proof that the demands made upon her are just.

Consider the contrast with Mrs. Compson. The mother constantly describes herself as ill-used, but her version of suffering rarely acknowledges the labor she demands from Dilsey. A household can make a person invisible by treating their attention as automatically owed. Dilsey's presence gives the reader a way to measure the distance between a family's account of itself and its material conduct. This is not merely an argument that the Compsons are bad employers. It is a way of seeing how class and race determine whose needs count as private tragedy and whose work counts as an ordinary condition of life.

On Easter Sunday Dilsey attends a Black church with Benjy and members of her family. The change of setting reveals a congregation and a religious practice not defined solely by the Compsons' claims. Reverend Shegog's sermon moves from uncertain beginnings into a powerful collective encounter with the story of suffering and resurrection. The congregation responds, and Dilsey is profoundly affected. The service opens the novel's moral and temporal world beyond the repeated grievances of the white household. It also reminds us that Black communal life has institutions, speech, and spiritual knowledge of its own.

Yet a reader should ask why the novel centers this particular form of Black life. Dilsey is praised for endurance, loyalty, and care, qualities that can be genuine moral achievements. They are also qualities that white Southern literature has historically demanded of Black domestic workers, sometimes without asking what those workers would choose if they were free of the obligation to serve. The novel can critique the Compsons' selfishness while still limiting the space it gives Dilsey for autonomous ambition, anger, or departure. Admiration is not the same as complete representation.

The third-person technique matters. Benjy, Quentin, and Jason each receive a major section organized by their own mental life. Dilsey receives focus, but not that kind of extended interior monologue. We observe her actions and hear her speech. The narrator also moves elsewhere, including Jason's pursuit of Miss Quentin. We should therefore avoid calling the fourth section simply Dilsey's first-person account. Faulkner widens the perspective, but its access is asymmetrical. The formal difference has ethical consequences in a novel about the people whose stories are considered worthy of being told.

There is a recurrent temptation to interpret Dilsey as the book's complete religious solution. She hears a sermon about life after death; she has witnessed the Compsons' decline; she continues to care. On this account, the family has failed and Dilsey stands as redemptive endurance. There is real evidence for that reading, especially in the Easter scene. But the conclusion becomes too easy if it implies that her labor was rewarded, that segregation and racial subordination have somehow ceased, or that spiritual insight is a substitute for changed material relations. None of those consequences occurs in the narrative.

The Sunday service's force comes partly from the fact that the congregation acts together. Jason's language is solitary, suspicious, and acquisitive. Mrs. Compson's is self-absorbed. Quentin struggles within a painfully isolated mind. The church creates a collective response in which a person's voice is altered by other people's voices. Yet that sociality should not be romanticized as a realm outside history. The church exists in a segregated society, and its members still carry the consequences of economic and racial inequality. The novel lets them create meaning, not exemption from circumstance.

Faulkner's invented county draws upon the real history of Mississippi. Slavery and the post-emancipation racial order shape the background of the Compson family's property, the Gibson family's work, and the hierarchy of who commands whom. A narrative about the fall of a white family can easily make these structures disappear by framing slavery's legacy only as scenery for aristocratic loss. The attentive reader instead asks who sustained the estate, who owns the land, who receives the money, and whose years of work are treated as belonging to someone else's family story.

The strongest interpretation of Dilsey is neither that she is simply a saint nor that the novel has nothing of value to say about her. She is a person represented with memorable force under a framework that gives more narrative interiority to white men who have repeatedly failed. That tension is not something to resolve through praise or denunciation alone. It is part of what a serious reading of the novel must explain.

At the ending, Benjy's carriage follows the familiar route and his crying subsides. The Compsons' visible world appears to settle briefly into place. But Dilsey will still have a household to manage, and the social arrangement that makes her work indispensable remains intact. If the novel asks us to admire her survival, we must also ask what she has had to survive and why other people's peace depends so reliably upon her effort.
