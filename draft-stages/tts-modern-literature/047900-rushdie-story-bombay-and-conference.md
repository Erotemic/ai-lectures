# Midnight's Children — The Bombay Childhood and the Conference of Voices

Saleem Sinai grows up surrounded by people who want him to be a particular kind of child. His parents regard the circumstances of his birth as a sign of destiny. The adults living around Methwold's Estate inhabit a densely connected social world of quarrels, flirtations, medical emergencies, rumors, and neighborhood rivalries. A new nation has begun its political life, but for Saleem the immediate world is a household in Bombay, a school, a changing body, and the fear of disappointing those who expect an extraordinary future.

His younger sister becomes known as the Brass Monkey, an impetuous child drawn to disruption. Saleem is treated as the remarkable one, and she resists the arrangement. As they grow, their relationship shows how families distribute attention according to stories they have already told about their children. The privileged boy is often clumsy and frightened; the supposedly secondary girl finds ways to assert herself. Later she will become Jamila Singer, a famous voice in Pakistan, acquiring a public identity far beyond the childhood nickname.

The child Saleem learns to spy. He hides in a washing chest and hears things about his mother's emotional life that she has kept from the family. In the midst of this secrecy, he also discovers that the voices he hears are not merely people speaking near him. Thoughts enter his consciousness without traveling through ordinary sound. At first, he interprets his experience through religious and familial expectations. To be selected by a mysterious power might fulfill the story everyone has projected on him. But that same power makes privacy difficult and responsibility uncertain. Hearing someone's thought does not mean knowing what to do with it.

His extraordinary ability eventually expands. The first hour after Indian independence has produced a group of children with improbable gifts, and Saleem can communicate with them in his mind. Those born nearest midnight possess especially powerful capacities, though the children differ in temperament, region, wealth, language, and allegiance. Saleem gathers their voices into the Midnight Children's Conference. For a period they can assemble without occupying the same room, as though the size and variety of India could be present within one mind.

Some of the children are visionary, shy, bitter, playful, ambitious. Parvati is associated with magical concealment and the world of performers. Shiva, the other boy born at the exact stroke of midnight, joins the gathering with a fierce distrust of Saleem's hopeful rhetoric. Saleem imagines that a national parliament of children might transcend the divisions of the adults. Shiva asks, in effect, why the world of poverty and power should be erased by a boy who has had the easier life. The argument is not a small disturbance in a harmonious meeting. It is the central challenge to Saleem's belief that connection itself makes justice.

The fantasy of telepathy enables an extraordinary democratic image: people otherwise separated by geography, language, and status can be heard together. But the conference is organized through Saleem. He decides when it meets, controls the channel, and interprets its significance for his autobiography. A parliament convened inside one person's head still depends on that person's attention. His privilege and vanity do not disappear when he calls himself a representative of everyone else.

Meanwhile, a child's body is vulnerable to ordinary humiliations. Teachers injure his pride. Classmates mock his appearance. An accident damages a finger, and a hospital blood test gives the family a clue they cannot easily accept: Saleem is not the biological son of the parents who raised him. The discovery strikes at the very center of Ahmed's belief in lineage. Family tension increases. Saleem is sent for a period to stay with his uncle Hanif and aunt Pia, whose world of cinema promises a different kind of storytelling from the solemn national destiny imposed on him at home.

Mary Pereira, who has long remained close to the Sinai household as a nurse and caregiver, eventually confesses what she did in the delivery room. Saleem and Shiva were switched. The announcement does not retroactively cancel the years of care Saleem has received, but it changes the vocabulary available to everyone. Ahmed responds with anger and humiliation. Saleem must now ask whether his life belongs to the family that made him or the bloodline that made his body. Shiva's injuries and resentments cannot be corrected merely by a revelation, because he has already lived the childhood that the switch assigned him.

Saleem also discovers that his actions can inflict harm on others. He becomes entangled in a neighborhood scandal involving Commander Sabarmati, his wife, and her lover. An anonymous message contributes to the commander's discovery of the affair; the ensuing violence kills a man and injures a woman. Saleem's involvement reveals the gap between curiosity and the right to interfere. A narrator fascinated by the interconnectedness of lives may mistake his access to a secret for the authority to use it. The book does not let youthful recklessness remain merely charming.

The family begins to move again, and with the movement come changes in Saleem's capacities. Time in Pakistan places him among relatives connected to the military and to a political order of very different public tone. In these years a coup appears within the family story, with Saleem's naïve participation in the machinations around General Ayub Khan. The episode turns a child who imagined a free congress of minds into someone close to an authoritarian transfer of power. The contrast is deliberate, even if Saleem reports his role with the inflated scale characteristic of his storytelling.

Back in Bombay, the conference grows harder to sustain. Saleem cannot guarantee the goodwill of the children or keep Shiva's hostility from altering the assembly. At one stage he tries to bring the others together while excluding Shiva. The supposed union of the nation's children becomes subject to the private exclusions of its founder. An operation on Saleem's obstructed sinuses changes him again: he loses the telepathic faculty through which the conference operated, but gains an extraordinarily sensitive sense of smell. What had been an experience of shared voices becomes an ability to detect people and circumstances through their traces.

A reader may be tempted to say that the magic was a decorative allegory, but it performs work in the story. The voices make the nation's diversity immediate and overwhelming; their disappearance makes political unity fragile and contingent. Saleem's extraordinary nose can uncover things others miss, but it does not grant wisdom or protect him from the decisions of adults and governments. He can describe a crowd and still misjudge an individual. He can sense danger without stopping it.

By the end of these childhood years, the boy once celebrated as a nation's living emblem has become a disputed son, an unreliable witness, a failed convener, and a reluctant participant in family scandals. Yet he has also known genuine relationships that no laboratory test could reduce to blood. His story is not simply the loss of childhood innocence. It is the education of someone who believed that possessing many voices made him entitled to speak for them.
