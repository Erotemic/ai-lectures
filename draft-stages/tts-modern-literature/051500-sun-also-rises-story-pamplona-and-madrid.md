# The Sun Also Rises — The Story: Fiesta, Violence, and the Madrid Taxi

When the fiesta begins in Pamplona, the city changes tempo. There is music in the streets, food and wine at all hours, crowds watching the bulls run, and bullfights that concentrate danger into a highly formal performance. The visitors from Paris move through the celebrations with a freedom that feels exhilarating at first. But the group has carried unresolved attachments and resentments into this crowded setting. As the days proceed, the festival makes those conflicts public.

Jake Barnes watches the matadors with an expertise his companions only partly share. A young bullfighter, Pedro Romero, stands out. Romero is nineteen, remarkably composed, and willing to perform close to a dangerous animal without substituting cheap gestures for genuine technique. Jake recognizes the quality of his work. Montoya, the hotelkeeper, sees Romero as someone whose developing career requires protection from distractions. To the enthusiasts, this is not merely a handsome young celebrity. His practice is serious, fragile, and exacting.

Brett Ashley is fascinated by Romero, first through the performance and then through the young man himself. Her attraction intensifies during the festival. Jake knows how much her attention may disrupt Romero's life, and he knows Montoya would disapprove. Yet he introduces them and eventually helps Brett find the bullfighter. This is a consequential choice. Jake is willing to sacrifice something he values in Spain because he cannot refuse a request from the woman he loves. The act complicates his self-image as a detached observer of everyone else's faults.

Meanwhile Mike Campbell's drinking and financial troubles worsen his anger. He mocks Robert Cohn, repeatedly directing humiliating remarks at him. Cohn had traveled with Brett earlier and appears unable to accept that she does not intend to continue the attachment. His persistence makes him an object of annoyance, and the group's hostility toward him is also marked by ethnic prejudice. No one in this conflict occupies a simple moral category. Cohn's possessiveness can be troubling; the antisemitic ways he is mocked remain wrong; and his later resort to violence does not become admirable because he has been insulted.

As Brett and Romero grow closer, Cohn confronts the others, demanding to know where she is. In a series of altercations he strikes Mike and Jake. He has been a trained boxer, and the violence is overwhelming. Jake is knocked unconscious. Cohn later reaches Romero and beats him as well. Romero refuses to submit to the humiliation despite being badly hurt. Cohn's victory in physical terms leaves him emotionally shattered. His attempts at apology cannot restore the damaged relationships. The scene reverses the familiar hope that violence can settle questions of honor or love.

On the final day of the bullfights, Romero performs with remarkable composure despite the beating. The bullring has already been linked to an actual death during the running of the bulls. The festival is not harmless pageantry, and the novel does not erase the animal suffering and human danger involved. Still, Jake watches Romero's work as an example of directness, timing, and courage. The display has an aesthetic dimension distinct from the chaos of the spectators' personal lives. Romero's success does not protect his private relationship with Brett from further strain.

Brett leaves Pamplona with Romero. Cohn has already departed. The fiesta ends, and the friends who remain disperse. Bill returns toward ordinary life elsewhere; Mike moves on; Jake travels to San Sebastián. The structure relaxes after the crowded festival. Jake swims, reads, eats, and tries to enjoy quiet days by the sea. He finds a measure of order in simple routines. The contrast with Pamplona is deliberate: a chapter can contain almost nothing dramatic and still reveal a character's effort to recover equilibrium.

Then a telegram comes from Brett. She needs him in Madrid. Jake answers the call without much apparent deliberation. He travels to her hotel and finds her alone. The relationship with Romero has ended. Brett says she sent the young man away rather than distort his future. She also describes how his expectations for her conflicted with the sort of woman she is willing to be. She intends to return to Mike. Her account need not be accepted as the final, exhaustive explanation of what happened between them. We hear it through Jake, who has his own hopes and injuries.

Jake helps her arrange the practical matters of leaving Madrid. The book closes as they ride together in a taxi. Brett imagines a different life in which she and Jake might have been happy. Jake answers with the novel's famous short sentence: Yes, isn't it pretty to think so? The reply is tender and skeptical at once. It neither cancels their affection nor promises that affection can undo the conditions separating them. Jake knows the pleasure of an imagined future. He has also learned not to mistake the imagined future for one he can inhabit.

The title returns in the reader's mind at this point. Days end, journeys stop, friendships break, but the world continues its cycles. The characters have not been redeemed by Spain, and they have not been destroyed by a single disastrous fiesta. They will go on living with choices already made. Hemingway ends without a judgment explaining exactly what they should feel. The final conversation is brief enough to leave room for regret, affection, exhaustion, and an enduring capacity to wish.
