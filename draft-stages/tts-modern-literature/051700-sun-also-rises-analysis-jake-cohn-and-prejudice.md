# The Sun Also Rises: Jake Barnes, Robert Cohn, and the Unreliable Observer

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Jake Barnes rarely announces that he is offering a biased account. He sounds as though he is reporting people he knows: their clothes, jobs, drinks, jokes, and failures. This apparent coolness is central to The Sun Also Rises. Jake is simultaneously the character through whom we experience the story and a person implicated in its conflicts. The reader has to examine the distance between observation and interpretation, especially in his treatment of Robert Cohn.

The novel begins not with Jake's own life but with Cohn's. Jake tells us about Cohn's Princeton education, boxing, marriage, literary ambitions, and involvement with Frances. He includes the fact that Cohn is Jewish and recalls how antisemitic exclusion affected him in college. He also mocks Cohn's romantic disposition and his desire for adventure. Because Jake chooses the order and tone of these details, the reader begins with a portrait already saturated by irritation.

Some of Jake's criticisms have factual basis. Cohn can be possessive of Brett; he struggles to accept that her involvement with him does not create an exclusive claim. His anger turns into violence at Pamplona. The point of recognizing narrator bias is not to deny those events. It is to notice that Jake has his own stake in the rivalry. Jake is in love with Brett. He is wounded in ways that affect their relationship. He envies kinds of access to her that other men possess. His portrayal of Cohn may therefore mix accurate observation, jealousy, class judgments, and ethnic prejudice.

The antisemitic material should not be waved away as harmless period color. Mike taunts Cohn using stereotypes, and Jake's opening characterizations participate in a social world where Jewishness becomes a recurring mark of difference. A critic can distinguish a historically situated fictional prejudice from the author's own declared beliefs without pretending the effect on the page vanishes. We can examine when Jake distances himself from another speaker's abuse and when his report quietly reinforces it.

Cohn also occupies an awkward position in the group's moral order. He believes in romantic narratives and imagines that devotion should receive a response. His values can look out of date beside the others' ironic banter. Yet the supposedly sophisticated group is hardly successful at conducting relationships without cruelty. Their mockery of his idealism may expose something they do not wish to admit: they want love and loyalty too, but their social style punishes anyone who speaks plainly about wanting them.

Jake often gains authority by describing actions precisely. He is knowledgeable about fishing and bullfighting. His expertise gives credibility to his reports of physical practice. The danger is extending that credibility automatically into judgments about people. A person who can distinguish an excellent matador from a performer who fakes danger may still misread a friend, especially when desire and resentment are involved. Hemingway lets competence in one domain coexist with partiality in another.

Observe how Jake handles his own wrongdoing. He helps Brett meet Romero despite knowing the risk to Romero and the confidence Montoya has placed in him. This action is as revealing as Cohn's conduct, though Jake does not present it with the same relish for condemnation. If we believed Jake was simply the novel's moral judge, we would need to explain why he participates in the very pattern of interference he dislikes. A more convincing reading treats him as perceptive, wounded, often decent, sometimes unkind, and capable of betraying his standards.

The episode in which Cohn attacks Jake makes these questions harder. Violence is a concrete action that cannot be transformed into a virtue by correcting for narrator bias. Cohn hurts people. But Jake's earlier contempt also requires scrutiny. A critic should resist the false choice between declaring the narrator wholly trustworthy and declaring the facts of the assault unknowable. We have a fictional account with multiple kinds of evidence, and we can assign different degrees of confidence to different claims.

For a workshop, select Jake's opening portrait of Cohn and rewrite it as a neutral sequence of externally observable facts. Then list the evaluative statements and the evidence Jake supplies for each. Repeat with a passage in which Jake describes himself, especially his reaction to Brett or his conduct toward Montoya. Is the same standard applied to both subjects? What does the comparison reveal about the narration's blind spots?

Hemingway's first-person technique does not announce unreliability with sensational lies or a surprise revelation. It asks the reader to detect how social confidence becomes narrative authority. The prose is concise enough to feel transparent, while its narrator remains a person with memories, prejudices, and private needs. The result is a modernist lesson in the limits of witnessing: describing a world accurately in many details is not the same as seeing all the people in it justly.
