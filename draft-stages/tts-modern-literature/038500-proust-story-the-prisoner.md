# In Search of Lost Time — The Story, Part Five: The Prisoner

Albertine is living in the narrator's Paris apartment. He speaks of their arrangement with an air of domestic familiarity, as though it were a temporary solution to the anxieties that disturbed him at Balbec. But nearly every element of their day has acquired a second meaning. Who will accompany her? Where will she go? Will she see a woman the narrator suspects of having been a lover? What explanation will she give when she returns? In The Prisoner, also translated as The Captive, the question of whether two people love each other becomes entangled with a more oppressive question: can one person make the life of another person completely knowable?

The narrator is often ill or unwilling to go outside. Françoise helps sustain the household. Albertine leaves on errands or excursions, sometimes under an arrangement designed to reassure him, sometimes in ways that alarm him. He employs information and intermediaries to track her movements. He insists to himself that he is growing bored with her, that their intimacy has lost its freshness. Yet the possibility of her independence instantly restores the pain he calls love. He is not merely afraid that she may betray him. He is afraid of the part of her life that does not take place in his consciousness.

Albertine sometimes accepts his demands and sometimes resists them. She tells stories that may be evasions or defensive inventions; he tells himself stories that allow him to excuse his surveillance. The relationship includes tenderness, shared jokes, moments of peace, and even beauty. It also includes coercion. Treating it as a tragic romance without that word would obscure its central mechanism. The more he tries to remove uncertainty, the more suspicion he creates. Every explanation generates a new possible contradiction; every person who might know Albertine becomes a possible witness to be interrogated.

There is one scene of peculiar calm when he watches Albertine asleep. Her breathing and still body seem to give him relief from the inaccessible, independent life that torments him while she is awake. He can imagine that she belongs entirely to the room. The apparent tenderness of the description is inseparable from its danger. Sleep is the condition in which she cannot contradict his image of her. What comforts him is not necessarily greater understanding but the temporary absence of her freedom to act.

Outside the apartment the city continues to change. Street vendors call through Paris. Weather enters the room through sound and light. Those impressions trigger memories and reflections that sometimes carry the narrator away from jealousy. The novel remains attentive to sensory life even when its central relationship is strangling itself. The contrast matters. An entire world offers itself for perception, yet the narrator repeatedly narrows his attention to the possibility that Albertine has spoken to the wrong person.

Another life reaches its end in this volume. Bergotte, the writer whose books once seemed to the young narrator almost impossibly beautiful, goes to an exhibition to look at Vermeer's painting View of Delft. Ill and physically weak, he focuses on a small yellow area of the painting and thinks about the precision and luminous density of the artist's work. He regrets that his own later prose may have become too dry. Overcome, he collapses and dies. Proust gives the scene an extraordinary double force. It is a death in a public gallery, and it is a final artistic judgment in which a writer measures his achievement against a painted surface that appears more alive than anything he has written.

The death also recalls the limits of cultural prestige. Bergotte can be famous and still doubt that he has made good use of his talent. The narrator can devote years to admiring writers and still fail to write. The story does not suggest that correct aesthetic insight protects a person from mortality. On the contrary, Bergotte recognizes something essential at the instant when life can no longer be revised.

Music returns through Vinteuil, the fictional composer whose sonata once expressed Swann's feelings for Odette. At the Verdurins' gathering, the narrator hears a larger work, the septet, and finds that motifs and relationships once perceived separately now belong to a more intricate whole. The music seems to demonstrate how a work of art can retain, transform, and rediscover experience. It is especially painful that the person who preserved and helped make this music known is connected with the narrator's obsessive suspicions: Mademoiselle Vinteuil and her companion, whose relationship he regards through a fearful, distorted lens. Artistic creation can move through people whom social prejudice dismisses.

The Verdurin salon is also the setting for a cruel reversal in Charlus's affairs. The baron has tried to protect and advance Morel, the talented violinist to whom he is attached. Madame Verdurin, jealous of his influence and determined to control the social circle, works to turn Morel against him. Charlus, usually confident that birth and manner can impose obedience, is publicly humiliated and emotionally wounded. The spectacle is not merely revenge against a proud aristocrat. It exposes the violence a salon can perform while pretending to be a community of friends.

Social intrigues might seem remote from the narrator's apartment, but they echo its relationships. Charlus imagines that gifts, rank, and attention should secure Morel's affection. Madame Verdurin imagines that social dependence gives her ownership of her guests. The narrator imagines that keeping Albertine close can make her fidelity verifiable. In each case a person mistakes influence for possession, and discovers that the other person continues to have desires beyond the arrangement.

The narrator finds new grounds for suspicion everywhere. He learns about Albertine's outings after the fact; an innocent explanation can feel deliberate because it was not supplied earlier; a harmless acquaintance can suddenly become a rival. He tells himself he must leave her, and then becomes frightened by the prospect that she might actually go. He is drawn toward a freedom he cannot bear to grant her. The more uncertain he feels, the more elaborate his attempts at reassurance become. The relationship is no longer merely unhappy. It is structurally incapable of producing the certainty he requires.

Then, with remarkable abruptness, the domestic arrangement ends. Françoise informs the narrator that Albertine has left. She has gone while he was still expecting to find her in the house. The blow is almost administrative: an absence reported in an ordinary household message. Yet it makes all the narrator's elaborate plans collapse at once. He had arranged a life in which he could delay the question of separation indefinitely. Albertine has decided not to keep waiting for his decision.

The title The Prisoner is deliberately unstable. Albertine has been watched and confined in a relationship governed by another person's jealousy. The narrator has also imprisoned his own attention, surrendering the rest of the world to a problem he cannot solve by gathering information. But the symmetry must not erase their unequal positions. Albertine's freedom has been materially restricted; his suffering does not cancel his responsibility for the restriction. The volume ends not with a moral verdict delivered from above, but with the practical demonstration that possession cannot make another human being stay. Albertine is gone, and the narrator is forced to face what he has built his life around.
