# Midnight's Children — The Emergency, a Child, and the Pickle Factory

When Saleem returns to India, he has little of the prestige attached to the baby who was born at independence. Parvati, who helped restore his name, lives among magicians in Delhi. Picture Singh and the other performers have created a social world with its own expertise, loyalties, and vulnerabilities. Saleem comes and goes, at one point staying in the home of an uncle whose respectable position offers little comfort. He is still searching for an arrangement in which he might live without being made into a historical instrument.

Parvati hopes for a lasting relationship with him, but Saleem recoils from ordinary domestic responsibility. She turns to Shiva, the other child of midnight. Shiva has grown into a formidable soldier, with the powerful knees and aggressive confidence that distinguished him as a child. His military status gives him access to force and political influence. The two boys switched at birth have not simply returned to their supposed original social positions. Their lives have accumulated entirely different powers, injuries, and choices. Shiva's strength does not make him noble, and Saleem's facility with words does not make him reliable or generous.

Parvati becomes pregnant by Shiva, and he abandons her. Saleem eventually marries her, assuming the role of father to the child she carries. Parvati is not a passive symbolic mother awaiting a national hero. She has acted, desired, performed magic, sheltered Saleem, and made decisions he did not control. Yet she is also exposed to the consequences of a man's abandonment and a community's judgment. Her changing position helps show what can be lost when Saleem organizes a woman's life chiefly around his own destiny.

Her child, named Aadam Sinai, is born at the moment a state of Emergency descends on India in June 1975. The symmetry is deliberate. Saleem was born alongside independence; the next generation appears as constitutional protections are suspended and centralized power expands. In historical India, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's Emergency lasted from June 1975 until March 1977. Opposition figures were detained, press freedoms curtailed, and government programs included slum demolition and coercive sterilization. The novel connects these documented policies to a fantastic campaign against the miraculous children of midnight. The fantastical plot should not be mistaken for a literal historical program targeting telepaths and magicians.

Saleem's narrator calls the prime minister the Widow and enlarges her into a nearly mythic antagonist. That language records the imaginative pressure of authoritarian power, but it can also make political history look like a battle between chosen individuals. The reality the fiction invokes is broader: bureaucracy, police, political patronage, the vulnerability of poor residents, and the capacity of official orders to reshape bodies and neighborhoods. The distinction becomes decisive in the destruction of the magicians' settlement.

The magicians' ghetto is demolished during the Emergency. Parvati dies in the violence, and Saleem is captured by Shiva. One of the narrative's bitter reversals is complete. The boy who once stood outside the conference of children as a skeptic and rival now serves the force capable of dismantling it. Saleem, deprived of freedom, is compelled to disclose information about the other midnight-born children. In the novel they are rounded up and subjected to sterilization intended to end their extraordinary abilities and their future as a generational possibility.

This plot should not soften the real history of coercive population-control campaigns, nor should it imply that every historical victim was part of a secret magical caste. The fictional target is a population of gifted children whose diversity and reproductive future threaten the state's wish for uniform control. The real victims were human beings subject to political pressure, including poor and marginalized people whose consent could be overridden by official power. Rushdie's fantastic intensification directs attention toward the violence of control over reproduction and urban residence.

Saleem has often failed the conference through arrogance, indecision, and exclusion. Now the government destroys it through imprisonment and bodily force. His own betrayal under coercion cannot be explained away, but neither can it be equated with the state's responsibility for the apparatus of repression. The novel makes readers distinguish moral weakness under pressure from the organized political structures that create that pressure. A gifted child who promised to speak for others is now made to help deliver them into captivity.

After the Emergency ends and elections remove Indira Gandhi's government from power, Saleem is released. Political time has changed direction again, but no repeal can bring Parvati back or restore what the children have lost. He finds Aadam cared for by others in the community. The boy is biologically Shiva's child, but Saleem accepts a paternal place in his life. The novel therefore returns to the question with which the hospital switch began. Blood can describe one relation; it cannot fully define the obligations made by love, choice, and continuing care.

Picture Singh accompanies them toward Bombay. There Saleem encounters a familiar taste: a preserve that recalls the food made by Mary Pereira. He finds Mary again, now associated with a pickle factory, and a place for himself in its work. He also finds Padma, who becomes the addressee and challenger of the narrative we have been listening to. The book folds back on its own beginning. The man telling us about a nation from jars and memories is the grown child whose origin was rearranged by a woman who worked in a hospital.

Saleem proposes marriage to Padma. He approaches his thirty-first birthday, another anniversary of national independence, with an extraordinary prediction: that the cracks in his body will finally overcome him and that he will break into an almost numberless multitude. The prediction is not a sober medical diagnosis, and the book does not provide an external witness reporting that this literal dispersal has occurred. It is how Saleem imagines the end of a body burdened with more history than one person could contain.

A conventional family saga might close by clarifying the legitimate line of descent. A conventional nationalist fable might present a child who finally becomes a unified nation. Rushdie supplies neither resolution. Saleem remains a son whose biological and social origins differ, a father by care rather than blood, and a narrator whose stories have both preserved and distorted the past. His body is imagined as fragmenting while the pickles preserve chosen fragments. Padma remains there to question him, a reminder that the voices of other people cannot be absorbed without remainder into his voice.

The result is not a simple declaration that India is broken or that independence has failed. It is a warning about the cost of asking a single history to contain everyone. Saleem began as a child who seemed to coincide perfectly with a nation. He ends as a man who knows how many lives cannot be reduced to one body, one lineage, one state, or even one memoir. What survives, precariously, is the attempt to tell without possessing the whole.
