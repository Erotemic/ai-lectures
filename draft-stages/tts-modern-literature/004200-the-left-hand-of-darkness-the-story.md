# The Left Hand of Darkness — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Genly Ai has come to the planet Gethen as an envoy from the Ekumen, a community of worlds linked by travel, exchange, and a shared interest in peaceful relations. Gethen is also called Winter. Much of its inhabited land is cold, and the immense ice sheets elsewhere on the planet are a constant fact of life. Genly is alone in his mission, and he cannot make the people he meets accept the existence of an interstellar society merely by telling them it exists. They have to decide whether to believe him, trust him, and choose a future with people who come from elsewhere.

Gethenians differ from Genly's own people in one fundamental way. They do not live permanently as male or female. For most of the month they are sexually inactive; during a recurring period called kemmer they may become male or female and can conceive or bear children. Genly understands the biological facts, but he has not escaped his own assumptions about which voices, movements, and gestures seem masculine or feminine. He often reads a person through a category that does not fit the society before him. This becomes part of the trouble when he tries to understand his most important ally.

In Karhide, one of the two major rival nations, Genly has gained the support of Therem Harth rem ir Estraven, a powerful official who serves as the king's prime minister. Estraven has tried to move the diplomatic mission forward. Genly does not know whether to trust him. Estraven is guarded, indirect, and knowledgeable about dangers Genly does not perceive. When he advises caution, Genly hears evasion. When he offers help, Genly wonders what he wants in return.

Karhide's ruler, King Argaven, is anxious, suspicious, and unpredictable. He fears the possibility of losing control over his country and reacts badly to what the envoy represents. At a public celebration Genly observes Karhide's ceremonies, ambitions, and dense network of reputations. The king's court is filled with people watching for signs of favor and disgrace. A border dispute with the neighboring state of Orgoreyn has also become politically important, and the envoy's proposal cannot be separated from the rivalry between the two nations.

Estraven has enemies at court who believe the border quarrel with Orgoreyn can be used to strengthen national feeling. A powerful rival, Tibe, is willing to turn that fear into public authority. To Genly, these relationships are hard to read because so much of Karhidish politics is conveyed through indirection and obligations that do not translate neatly into the concepts he has brought from home. The envoy keeps asking for a clear yes or no to a question whose implications the people around him are still arguing about.

Estraven loses favor. The king banishes him from Karhide, and the official who had tried to help Genly must flee across the border. Genly, still unable to see how much Estraven has risked on his behalf, goes on trying to persuade the Karhidish government. He meets the king and explains that the Ekumen offers membership rather than conquest. Argaven rejects him. Genly's mission seems to have reached a dead end, and the man who might have helped him is gone.

Genly travels through Karhide and visits a community of Handdara practitioners at Otherhord. The Handdara is a religious and philosophical tradition whose practitioners value uncertainty, waiting, and the disciplined refusal to claim more knowledge than one possesses. A group of Foretellers gathers to answer a question posed by Genly: whether Gethen will become part of the Ekumen within five years. Their ritual is physically and mentally demanding. The answer is yes. It does not tell him how such a future will come about or whom he can safely trust along the way.

The envoy now looks toward Orgoreyn. Its political structure appears, at first, more rational and welcoming than Karhide's court. It has committees, coordinated institutions, and officials who seem prepared to discuss his mission openly. Genly crosses the border expecting that a society less dominated by one monarch may be easier to persuade. Estraven, now an exile, has already made a life there under precarious conditions. The two are moving toward the same country, but Genly still does not understand that the dangers waiting for him are greater than anything he encountered in Karhide.

What began as a diplomatic mission now becomes a personal danger for Genly and Estraven.

Orgoreyn initially seems like the answer to Genly Ai's diplomatic difficulties. Its officials receive him and listen to the idea of joining the Ekumen. Where King Argaven offered suspicion and volatile royal pride, the Orgota government presents committees and administrators who can discuss policy in an orderly fashion. Genly visits the capital and speaks with influential men. He begins to believe that the political opening he wanted has finally appeared.

But the government is not one thing. Different officials compete for advantage, and a secret security apparatus operates behind the apparent openness of its institutions. The Orgota treat their citizens through an elaborate system of registration and authority. Political enemies can disappear into the machinery of the state. Genly notices some of these features, but he has traveled a long way and wants to believe that negotiations are succeeding.

Estraven is in Orgoreyn too. Exiled from Karhide, he has tried to survive by taking work and forming uncertain alliances. He hears what the officials say about Genly and sees how their hospitality might be converted into a trap. Estraven warns the envoy that the people courting him are not necessarily the people who can protect him. Genly finds the warnings difficult to trust, partly because the circumstances make Estraven appear to be playing another political game. The distance between the two men continues, though Estraven has far more immediate knowledge of what is happening.

In Orgoreyn the official kindness has a particular rhythm. A visitor may be invited to a generous meal, congratulated on his status, and assured that a decision is nearly at hand, while security officers prepare quite a different future for him. Genly is not simply failing to persuade a hostile king. He is being led toward greater dependence on people who can withdraw their protection instantly. Estraven understands the danger because exile has forced him to live among citizens whose movements and words are closely watched. His warnings to Genly become more urgent as the room for escape narrows.

The Orgota authorities eventually arrest Genly. Their public hospitality has not made him free. He is transported to a remote labor camp, one of the places where inconvenient people are sent out of sight. There he is questioned, drugged, and subjected to conditions that make recovery difficult. Drugs interfere with the Gethenian sexual cycle and with Genly's own body. Prisoners are moved and worked under exhausting control. Genly's status as an envoy offers him no protection once the government has decided it prefers his disappearance to an honest negotiation.

Estraven learns where Genly has been taken. He might protect himself by remaining invisible, or by accepting that the envoy has been lost. Instead he undertakes a rescue. He uses contacts, careful planning, and dangerous measures to get Genly out of the camp. By the time they escape, Genly is severely weakened. Estraven must carry much of the practical burden of their flight, finding food, shelter, and a route that neither country's government will expect them to take.

The ordinary border crossings are too dangerous. Karhide's authorities still regard Estraven as an exile who may be killed if he returns. Orgoreyn is searching for its escaped prisoner. Between the nations lies an immense frozen region called the Gobrin Ice. Crossing it is possible but terrifying. It means traveling for weeks with a loaded sled over snow, crevasses, storms, and terrain where an error may be fatal. Estraven decides that the ice is their best chance. Genly will need to recover enough to share the work.

They prepare what they can, collecting supplies and calculating how long the journey might take. Genly has arrived on Winter expecting to speak for worlds he knows and a wider civilization he represents. Now he is dependent on one person, an exile whom he has distrusted almost from the moment they met. Estraven does not ask for a formal confession. He builds the possibility of survival one practical choice at a time. Genly begins to see that the person he had treated as a political puzzle is the person putting his own life in danger to save him.

The next part follows their journey over the glacier. Much of it is made of walking, hauling, sleeping, waking, and doing all of those things again. There is little space for the drama of court politics. Their lives depend on weather, food, stamina, and whether they can learn to speak honestly to one another before the ice takes away their remaining chances.

Their escape will require more than a political arrangement: it will require the two men to cross the ice together.

Genly Ai and Estraven set out onto the Gobrin Ice with a sled packed for a journey that may take months. They are escaping Orgoreyn, where Genly was imprisoned, and heading toward Karhide, where Estraven is still under sentence of exile. The route offers no comfortable refuge. It is a landscape of snowfields, hidden crevasses, fierce winds, and long distances that cannot be shortened by urgency.

At first their partnership is mostly practical. The sled must be loaded correctly; the path must be tested; food and strength must be measured against the number of days ahead. Estraven knows more of life on Winter, and Genly gradually becomes a better traveling companion. Both men get tired and irritated. They also become capable of helping one another without interpreting every gesture as a political sign. They are moving through danger that cares nothing for the categories Genly once used to understand the people of Gethen.

As the journey continues, they talk. Genly asks about Estraven's family and the relationships he has left behind. Estraven asks about other worlds and the reasons anyone would spend years inviting strangers into a wider human community. They exchange stories and explain the customs that have shaped them. Genly starts to recognize the limits of his assumptions about sex and gender. Estraven is neither the man nor the woman that Genly's habits keep trying to imagine, and friendship becomes easier once Genly stops expecting one of those familiar answers.

They also discuss mind-speech, a form of direct mental communication that Genly can teach. The ability is not simply another diplomatic technique. When Estraven hears Genly's mind-speech, the voice carries a disturbing resemblance to someone from Estraven's past. The encounter brings old grief into their growing intimacy. The two do not suddenly understand everything about one another. But they are able to share things they could not share at the beginning of the journey.

They discover a kind of domestic routine on the ice. They have to pack the tent, harness themselves to the sled, judge the day’s likely distance, and decide how much of their food can be eaten. Snow and fog can make the world look entirely without features. The same person who was hard to approach in a palace can be an intimate companion when both travelers have spent days hauling a load through cold that hurts their hands and faces. They begin to know each other's limits and habits as well as their opinions.

The crossing is long, physically punishing, and sometimes monotonous. Food runs low. They endure weather that could end their lives in a night. Each day brings a new calculation: whether to continue, whether to wait, whether the land ahead is stable enough to cross. In their isolation the political mission that brought Genly to Winter seems very far away, though it is the reason both men are here. Estraven's life has been uprooted twice for trying to help a visitor whose country cannot guarantee his safety.

Eventually they leave the ice and approach the Karhidish border. Genly has reached the country where he first began his mission, and there may finally be a way to contact his ship. Estraven's position is more perilous. The banishment order has not disappeared. He moves toward the border despite the danger and is shot by guards. Genly cannot save him. The man who guided him across the ice, freed him from imprisonment, and made his mission possible dies before he can see the result.

Genly brings the truth of their journey to Karhide. The circumstances have changed. The government's calculations change too, and King Argaven eventually accepts the possibility of contact with the Ekumen. Genly is able to summon the ship that has waited for him. Visitors arrive from other worlds, making real what had previously sounded like one stranger's improbable story. The planet's first steps toward joining the Ekumen are no longer only a promise in an envoy's report.

But Genly's final personal journey is not to a diplomatic reception. He goes to Estraven's home region and meets people who remember the exile as a family member rather than a political figure. He carries what he can of Estraven's story back to those who loved him. The mission has succeeded, but its success came through an act of trust that cost its most devoted advocate his life.

At the end, Gethen has not transformed overnight. Karhide and Orgoreyn still exist; their histories and differences cannot be erased by the arrival of a ship. Genly has learned what it means to speak with someone he once systematically misunderstood. He returns to the larger human community carrying the knowledge that the world did not open because he was right about the future. It opened because Estraven believed that a relationship between strangers was worth risking everything to make possible.
