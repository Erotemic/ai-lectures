# Ficciones: The Garden of Forking Paths — A Murder Hidden in a Theory of Time

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A spy is running out of time in wartime England. His name is Yu Tsun. He is Chinese, has taught English, and is working for German intelligence during the First World War. He has discovered the location of a British military concentration, but his contact has been discovered. Captain Richard Madden is closing in. Yu Tsun must send the secret to his German superior without access to an ordinary communication channel.

We first encounter this account through an editorial frame that relates it to a passage in a military history. The frame suggests that a puzzling interruption in a British offensive might have a hidden explanation. We then enter Yu Tsun's statement. Already the history of a battle and the memory of one individual seem to be competing forms of evidence.

Yu Tsun is not a simple patriot in reverse. He does not admire the German regime he serves. He resents the contempt of the intelligence chief who regards him through a racist hierarchy. He wants to prove that he can accomplish a task the chief believes beyond him. This motive does not excuse the acts that follow. It makes the acts harder to flatten into a story of national loyalty.

With Madden approaching, Yu Tsun takes a train to reach a man named Stephen Albert. We do not yet know why Albert matters. The journey becomes increasingly tense: timetables, platforms, trains, the uncertainty of pursuit. At Albert's house, something unexpected happens. The host is a scholar of Chinese culture who recognizes Yu Tsun's family name and begins speaking about an ancestor of his, Ts'ui Pên.

Years earlier this ancestor had abandoned official duties to write a vast novel and construct an enormous labyrinth. The surviving novel seemed chaotic, with mutually contradictory episodes and characters who died in one passage but lived in another. No physical labyrinth was found. Albert has come to believe the two tasks were one. The novel is the labyrinth. Its structure does not unfold a single history in which an event excludes every incompatible possibility. It presents time as branching into differing futures, some of which may later converge.

Albert's reconstruction is more than a conceptual lecture to Yu Tsun. It is a generous recognition of his ancestor's achievement. A project the family had regarded as failure acquires an intelligible design. For a moment the desperate fugitive finds himself listening to someone who has taken his inherited culture seriously. The two men imagine what it could mean for alternative outcomes to coexist. Different versions of their meeting might contain friendship, hostility, or no meeting at all.

Then the trap in the story's title turns. Madden approaches. Yu Tsun asks Albert to turn toward a document, draws his pistol, and shoots him. He is arrested. The reader finally learns why the innocent scholar was chosen. Albert is not merely a man's surname; it is the name of the French town that identifies the British military target. Yu Tsun counts on English newspapers to report the murder of Stephen Albert. His intelligence chief will see the name and understand. The plan works. He sends the message by causing a death that journalists cannot ignore.

The conclusion does not celebrate the strategy. Yu Tsun acknowledges his horror and exhaustion. This is not a story in which a philosophical picture of multiple futures dissolves the moral responsibility of a choice. Albert might have lived in another imagined history; in this one Yu Tsun killed him. The relation between all possible paths and the path actually taken is the story's deepest wound.

It is tempting to describe the tale as a modern scientific theory of branching universes. That is a comparison a later reader may make, but it is not an account of Borges deriving fiction from subsequent physics. The invention belongs first to a constructed novel inside an espionage narrative. Form and plot are inseparable: we are denied one piece of information, offered another kind of explanation, and finally made to see that the mystery of the murder and the mystery of the labyrinth have been wound around each other.

The garden has no single image we can safely hold as the definitive world. What remains certain is that a human being who has imagined many futures still has to answer for what he did in one.
