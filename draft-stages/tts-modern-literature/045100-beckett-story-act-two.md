# Waiting for Godot — Act Two: Tomorrow Repeats

We return to the same road and the same tree. The most conspicuous change is small: the tree now has leaves. Whether that is evidence of a day passing, a change of season, or an assault on the characters' sense of time is not decided for us. The stage presents it as a fact of appearance. Vladimir comes first, singing a repetitive song about a dog, its death, and its burial. The song folds back into itself. It is an appropriate way to begin the second act of a play in which returns are never perfect copies.

Estragon comes back without a settled memory of the evening we have seen. He says he has been beaten again. He and Vladimir greet and irritate one another. They try to identify the place and compare it with the previous day. Vladimir remembers the tree, the appearance of the travelers, the boy's message. Estragon is less sure of everything. He has the pain of a body that continues through time without the reassuring narrative of what that body has endured. Vladimir needs him to remember, not merely to settle an argument, but to protect the reality of the previous day's experience.

The two again consider separation. They can fight; they can embrace; they can insult one another and then invent a reconciliation. The proposals sound spontaneous yet belong to a repertoire that they seem to know already. Estragon's boots, left behind, come back into the action. A hat can be exchanged or tried on. The pair begin a sequence with hats that is funny because it is elaborately coordinated and pointless. These men possess no stable job, no visible home, and little dependable history. They have routines. Such routines become a temporary architecture in the space where a plot might have been.

There are flashes of danger. Estragon thinks people are approaching, and the two search for somewhere to hide. The stage provides few places for concealment. They consider the tree, move about, and perform fear as a kind of elaborate choreography. Waiting has sharpened their desire for a new event while leaving them helpless before the possibility that an event might really come.

Pozzo and Lucky return. But they are not the same public spectacle we watched in the first act. Pozzo is blind. Lucky cannot speak. The commanding voice has lost its eyes; the man made to think on command has lost his voice. Pozzo now depends on Lucky to guide him. Their entry results in a collapse. They fall and cannot easily recover. Pozzo cries for help. The other men debate whether they ought to assist and what the assistance might be worth. A person is on the ground suffering; the words produced around him are both ethical discussion and delay.

When Vladimir attempts to help, he falls himself. Estragon tries to help and falls too. The scene fills with men calling out and struggling to stand. It can be played as frightening, pitiful, and irresistibly comic; its physical logic is inseparable from its moral meaning. A simple charitable action has become another complicated system of bodies, leverage, payment, impatience, and failure. Eventually the men stand and help Pozzo. Lucky is roused. Pozzo, still proud and irritable, offers no reliable explanation of how his blindness occurred. He does not acknowledge the earlier encounter. His answer to questions about the moment of change is a refusal of the comforting idea that life can always be told as a sequence of causes and dates.

The audience knows it has seen these men before. Pozzo denies their shared history. Vladimir's memory and the audience's memory are placed under pressure by this contradiction. Has some great interval passed between the acts? Are the men's perceptions unreliable? Do we merely lack the kind of background that a realistic play would provide? We are not authorized to choose a neat explanation. We can know that the stage asks us to witness radical alteration while the characters cannot furnish an agreed record of it.

After Pozzo and Lucky go, Vladimir and Estragon return to their own unfinished business. Vladimir is especially shaken by the possibility that memory might be the only thing keeping yesterday from vanishing. Estragon, exhausted, sleeps and wakes. The need for companionship persists even while companionship brings annoyance. If two people repeat the same conversations, this may mean that they are trapped; it may also mean that each remains available for the other's next hour.

Then the messenger boy arrives. Godot will not come this evening, he says, but promises tomorrow. Vladimir questions him with increasing urgency. Is he the boy who came yesterday? The boy denies it. Is he sure he has seen Vladimir? Vladimir wants one fact to survive until tomorrow: that another person can testify to their encounter. We have no independent way to settle whether this is the same child, a different brother, or simply a report shaped by fear and uncertain memory. The boy exits with the message that arrival has once again been postponed.

Near the end, the men revisit the possibility of hanging themselves. They try to use Estragon's belt as a test, and it fails. The comic practical problem prevents the terrible plan from becoming an event. They talk about returning tomorrow with a better rope, unless Godot comes first. Even the option of ending their wait is now dependent on what tomorrow might bring. It is not clear whether they have chosen life, surrendered to inaction, or merely lacked the means to do what they propose.

They decide to go. They do not move. The second act concludes as the first did: a declared action withheld by visible bodies. But a spectator should not say that nothing has changed. A tree has leaves. A master is blind. A servant is mute. Men have been beaten, frightened, and exhausted. Their relation to memory has grown more desperate. What has not changed is the machinery of postponement. Each day ends by making the next day responsible for the answer.

The final silence does not prove that Godot will never arrive, although it denies us an arrival within the play. It does not establish that the two men will never leave, although we leave them motionless. Beckett ends precisely at the boundary between what the stage shows and what a spectator wants to supply. His play is complete; the appointment is not.
