# Ulysses III: city, empire, close reading, and literary possibility

## Ulysses: Dublin, Empire, Religion, and the Question of Belonging

James Joyce set Ulysses in Dublin on June sixteenth, nineteen hundred and four. Ireland was then part of the United Kingdom, and political arguments over its future were everywhere. The Catholic Church held considerable cultural authority; an Irish-language revival formed part of the effort to imagine a distinct national identity; British institutions governed many aspects of public life. The city's newspapers, streets, monuments, schools, and pubs did not speak with one voice about what being Irish meant. Joyce made these competing languages part of the novel's daily traffic.

The book was written years later, mainly in self-imposed exile, and completed as Ireland's political future was undergoing radical transformation. Its 1904 setting is not an accidental vintage costume. It makes us examine a society whose institutions tell different, sometimes incompatible stories about who belongs and who has the right to speak for the country.

## Who can say what Ireland is?

The young Stephen Dedalus is burdened by the claims of national identity. He knows Irish history and is alert to the humiliations of domination. He also resists being made into the representative artist of a nation, as though literary talent were public property. His quarrels with religion, family, and cultural expectations are not simply adolescent rejection. They test whether an individual can create anything without first accepting the roles that established institutions offer him.

In the tower an English visitor, Haines, is interested in the Irish language. The older milk woman, whose labor supports the men's breakfast, cannot answer him in Irish. It is tempting to use this scene to pronounce the language revival either noble or absurd. The encounter is more exact than either judgment. It exposes unequal access to a national ideal. A cultivated visitor may admire an endangered language while a woman with fewer resources has been denied the opportunity to acquire it. The irony is not that Irish has no value. It is that talking about a people is easier than listening to one.

In the school, Mr. Deasy claims the authority of history and practical common sense. He offers Stephen advice about money, political responsibility, and order, then voices antisemitic conspiracies. His prejudice demonstrates how a person can sound knowledgeable while repeating an explanation that excludes real people from the social world. Stephen does not need to produce a complete theory of history to recognize that Deasy's confidence is not evidence.

## What the city's official journey leaves outside

Wandering Rocks moves through nineteen city passages, opening with a Jesuit priest and ending with the viceroy's procession. Between those poles of spiritual and imperial rank, Dublin carries on with need, work, flirtation, worry, and poverty. The structure is political without behaving like a pamphlet. We see people who serve institutions, avoid them, laugh at them, and continue living despite them.

The viceroy is a representative of British rule, but the carriage cannot make the whole city belong to his point of view. Its progress is one itinerary among many, and the novel gives considerable attention to people whose journeys matter to no official record. That is an artistic challenge to the idea that only the people at the top of a political hierarchy constitute history.

The same city is full of imperial and religious symbols that the characters interpret differently. Monumental architecture may offer a meeting place, an occasion for a story, or a reminder of domination. A church service may be an expression of sincere belief to one worshipper and a fascinating arrangement of sensory rituals to Bloom. Joyce does not require every individual response to agree with a single authorial verdict about the institution.

## Bloom and the danger of a single definition

Leopold Bloom is a particularly revealing figure of national belonging because his identity is layered. His father, born Rudolf Virag, came from continental Europe and had Jewish roots. Bloom has a complex religious history and does not present himself as a simple representative of observant Judaism. He is also a Dublin inhabitant, a husband and father, a worker, a person who knows streets and social obligations, and a participant in the ordinary economy of the city.

The men who insult him are not interested in such complexity. In Cyclops the Citizen treats Jewishness as evidence that Bloom cannot belong to Ireland. The social attack works by turning one aspect of family history into the whole person. It also makes Bloom's response difficult. Should he defend himself as Irish? Should he explain the history of persecution? Should he refuse the aggressor's right to set the terms of the question?

Bloom does several things, imperfectly and under pressure. He insists that persecution and hatred are wrong. He rejects the idea that a nation must be held together by violence against outsiders. When he names Jesus as a Jew, he uses a figure central to Christian Ireland's public identity to expose the contradiction of antisemitic Christian nationalism. The Citizen does not become ashamed and repent. He grows angry enough to throw a biscuit tin.

A reader should distinguish the novel's depiction of prejudice from factual claims about Jews. Deasy's assertions about conspiracy and the Citizen's accusations are false and serve the novel as examples of exclusion. It is also important not to make Bloom stand in for every Jewish experience. His specific family background and his changing religious affiliations are part of Joyce's imagined person, not a survey of European Jewish life.

## Language can turn solidarity into an enemy

The Citizen's indignation about British rule has real political context. A colonized or subordinated population can reasonably resent a government that exercises authority over it. Yet the Citizen's account of loyalty becomes narrower than the country itself. The episode dramatizes a logical and ethical failure: because an outside power has injured Ireland, the nationalist concludes that the nation must define itself by excluding another person as alien.

The form of the chapter helps us hear how this happens. The unnamed narrator's hostility makes Bloom's mild habits seem provocative. Overblown rhetorical passages dress routine pub talk in epic and ceremonial clothing. A crowd becomes a historical assembly by the power of words alone. The result is not simply a bad opinion held by one character. It is a demonstration of the social machinery by which a group confirms its prejudices and presents them as common sense.

Joyce's alternative is not an easy cosmopolitan slogan in which all differences have disappeared. Bloom does make judgments about other people, and some of his own notions are ignorant or superficial. He lives in a society in which religious and national categories have practical consequences, and the effects cannot be wished away by declaring everyone human. But he offers a different standard of belonging when he attends funerals, worries about a widow, and protects a stranger. He acts as a member of a community even when some of its members deny him recognition.

## History exists inside a person's day

Ulysses has no need to show parliament passing a law in order to be a historical novel. It shows history as a collection of habits and pressures that structure life before anyone consciously chooses them. An English visitor can expect an Irish woman to speak Irish. A headmaster can repeat anti-Jewish propaganda as history. A nationalist can decide who qualifies as Irish in a pub. A viceroy can drive through streets that belong emotionally to people who cannot govern them.

The novel does not claim political institutions are imaginary. It asks how they acquire power over thought and conduct. The clearest evidence is often a moment in which a character uses public language to avoid seeing an individual. If someone has been called a foreigner, a heretic, or a drunk, how much of that person's experience becomes inaudible?

In its most compassionate moments, Ulysses refuses to let the category finish the story. Bloom's Jewish ancestry matters and is not erased, but it is not a full account of his citizenship. Stephen's Irishness matters, but it does not dictate the art he must make. Molly's role as a wife matters, but it does not exhaust her consciousness. A modern city is not one voice singing a national anthem. It is a place where multiple histories are spoken at once, and where a person's dignity depends partly on the willingness of others to hear more than the loudest of them.

## Reading Ulysses Closely: Seven Ways to Hear the Form

A reader can finish a plot synopsis of Ulysses and still be bewildered when opening the book. The difficulty is not that its plot has a secret solution withheld from beginners. The difficulty is that Joyce changes what counts as narrative information. A phrase may report an action, imitate a social convention, echo a remembered song, or stage a fantasy. A sentence may begin in a narrator's register and end close to the language of someone's thought. The reader's first useful skill is not decoding every allusion. It is recognizing what kind of attention a page is asking for.

We can practice that skill on seven situations from the novel. Each is a way of reading, rather than a claim that every episode can be reduced to one trick.

## One: track a concrete event beneath competing thoughts

Start in Calypso. Bloom is making breakfast. He feeds the cat, purchases a kidney, and brings Molly food. Keep those events in mind while his thought moves through prices, desire, advertisements, and household memory. It may feel as though the narrative has repeatedly walked away from its subject. In fact, its subject includes the relation between a completed action and all the private associations that accompany it.

Try reading one paragraph by separating three things: what Bloom physically does, what he notices, and what he remembers or imagines. Then allow the three streams to overlap again. The point of the exercise is not to produce an official answer key. It is to feel how a mind can be present in more than one time while a body completes a simple task.

This method also protects against a common mistake: treating every mental sentence as an assertion by the author. Bloom can misunderstand a stranger, exaggerate an opportunity, or fantasize about an outcome. A character's thought is evidence of that character's mind; it is not automatically evidence of the world's actual state.

## Two: ask what a form does before asking what it means

In Aeolus, the headlines are hard to ignore. Rather than looking for a symbolic meaning in every one, begin by asking what the headline form normally does. It extracts a phrase, gives it typographic emphasis, and claims that something important has happened. Does the event under the heading justify this excitement? Does the heading arrive before or after the relevant scene? Does it make an ordinary remark sound official, or a serious argument sound like filler?

The technique is a practical introduction to literary mediation. A headline is a machine for distributing attention. Joyce makes that machine part of his fiction, so the reader notices it operating. After recognizing the effect, we can connect it to the newspaper office's conversation about rhetoric, to Bloom's work arranging advertisements, and to the city's political language.

## Three: listen for repeated sounds, not only repeated ideas

In Sirens, it helps to approach the opening fragments as motifs rather than failed sentences. Later, when a related sound appears in the bar's music or in Bloom's reactions, it acquires emotional direction. You do not need to perform a complete musical analysis of Joyce's composition. You can mark two or three recurring sounds and ask what each gains from its new context.

This is similar to listening to a song whose refrain becomes painful after a verse changes what the words refer to. Bloom's thoughts about Molly and Rudy are not always introduced by a direct statement of grief. Sound can bring the memory into focus. The formal experiment makes a reader participate in recognition, rather than merely receive an announcement that the protagonist is sad.

## Four: do not trust the confidence of the storyteller

In Cyclops the unnamed narrator has a tone of camaraderie and contempt. His confidence can make us forget that we are listening to a character with interests of his own. When he mocks Bloom, ask what action Bloom has actually taken. When the narrative moves into heroic hyperbole, ask whether the scale of the description matches the scale of the event. When the Citizen attacks Bloom as an outsider, ask whose definition of belonging has been assumed rather than demonstrated.

The biscuit tin is an excellent test. A man throws a small object at a departing carriage and misses. The language converts the act into an epic catastrophe. One cannot deny the aggressor's hostility just because the weapon is comic, nor accept the inflated account as literal physics. The discrepancy is the substance of the satire. Style tells us about a community's way of justifying itself.

## Five: separate a staged fantasy from a physical consequence

Circe is written like a play. Stage directions tell us about a courtroom, a transformation, an apparition, or a grotesque procession. But some of these events are expressions of fear and desire, not shared facts of the street. The reader must distinguish what happens to Stephen's body from what takes place within the episode's theatrical imagination.

Take the appearance of Stephen's dead mother. The apparition expresses his guilt, his anger, and his relation to religion. It is followed by a real disturbance when he strikes a chandelier. It would be a mistake to say that a ghost literally entered the brothel and caused the property damage. It would also be a mistake to ignore the apparition because it is not literal. The form lets the private experience acquire a consequence in the world, and that transition is what we need to track.

## Six: treat apparent precision as a question, not a guarantee

In Ithaca, Joyce uses systematic questions and answers. The syntax implies control, as though every object, action, and relation could be described exactly. Begin with a question about a mundane task, such as drawing water or making cocoa. Notice how the answer can expand into categories far larger than the task requires. Then compare that exhaustive method with the much harder question of what Bloom and Stephen mean to each other.

The catechism is not simply false. It can report actual things. But its form continually reminds us that description and understanding are not identical. A list of objects in a room may be exact without telling us what made the room welcoming. A calculation concerning two men's ages may be correct without measuring the grief of a dead child. Precision can illuminate reality, but it becomes comic when mistaken for completeness.

## Seven: let an ending change the earlier story

Finally, listen to Molly in Penelope. Her monologue returns us to events that have been mediated by Bloom's worry or other men's conversation. Her account does not cancel every earlier fact, but it changes the balance of authority. Something that looked like a simple marital betrayal becomes a relation containing history, desire, dissatisfaction, anger, loss, and memory.

The nearly unpunctuated form resists the ordinary divisions into separate, officially ranked thoughts. Still, do not mistake the form for proof of perfect candor or timeless femininity. Molly remembers selectively, just as everyone does. She is a particular fictional person, not a transparent document about women. One useful question is how her recollection of the marriage proposal alters our sense of Bloom's day without guaranteeing a reconciliation.

## What about the allusions I do not know?

There are thousands of historical, local, literary, musical, theological, and linguistic references in Ulysses. A reader can feel compelled to explain them all before proceeding. That approach can turn reading into an endless series of interruptions. Try a different order. First determine the dramatic situation: where are we, who is present, what action or mental movement occurs? Then notice the dominant technique. Only then investigate the references that affect your understanding of the scene.

For instance, knowing the Homeric Cyclops helps identify the Citizen's one-sided nationalism and the comic thrown tin. You do not need to identify every allusion in the pub's inflated rhetoric to recognize its function. Knowing the relation of Telemachus to Odysseus helps illuminate Stephen and Bloom, but it does not require you to force their last meeting into an adoption. The best annotation improves the experience of reading; it does not replace the experience with a system of boxes to be checked.

You may also choose different reading speeds. A passage of dense interior association may repay slow attention to each shift. A parodic list may be better heard initially as an accumulation rather than analyzed item by item. A scene of two men talking over cocoa deserves time for the awkward pauses that its exacting syntax tries to conceal. One pace does not serve every passage because the novel itself is exploring different forms of time.

## What the experiments reveal

These seven techniques share a question: how does language determine what we are able to know about another person's day? An advertisement selects desires. A headline selects importance. A song carries private memory inside public sound. A hostile narrator selects a villain. A stage direction makes inward fear visible. A scientific question promises completeness. An intimate monologue reopens judgments made in the speaker's absence.

Reading Ulysses closely does not mean admiring difficulty as a badge of seriousness. It means noticing when the form puts pressure on our habits of attention. We become less willing to accept the first description of a person, more attentive to the difference between report and invention, and more capable of staying with a mind that changes direction. The novel can be funny, vulgar, tender, and frustrating because the city it makes audible contains all those registers. A useful reading method learns to hear the change rather than trying to silence it.

## Ulysses: How a Difficult Novel Changed Literary Possibility

On February second, nineteen twenty-two, James Joyce's Ulysses appeared in book form in Paris. The publisher was Sylvia Beach, an American bookseller whose shop, Shakespeare and Company, had become part of the city's literary life. Joyce was turning forty. The novel was set nearly eighteen years earlier, on a single day in Dublin, and had occupied him for years while he lived outside Ireland. Its first publication as a complete book was an achievement of literary patronage, personal commitment, and practical risk, not the routine launch of a novel that established publishers had been eager to handle.

The story of its publication matters because the book's experiments challenged more than literary taste. They challenged which parts of bodily and mental life a novel could describe, who had the authority to decide whether a description was obscene, and how the written text of an unusually complex work could survive printing and revision.

## The work grew during composition

Joyce began writing Ulysses in 1914 and developed it through the following years, largely in Trieste, Zurich, and Paris. The Morgan Library's exhibition of manuscripts, plans, and proofs shows a writer continually revising his methods. The Homeric architecture and broad plotlines offered a framework, but the surface of the prose changed as he wrote. Joyce added passages, expanded experiments, and altered the balance between interior thought and external literary form.

The manuscript history undermines a common myth of genius: that Joyce conceived a perfectly complete apparatus and then transcribed it. Surviving plans show deliberate structure, but surviving drafts show discovery during execution. The mature Ulysses is not one technical invention repeated eighteen times. It moves from interior monologue toward newspaper headlines, musical recurrence, parody, historical pastiche, theatrical hallucination, scientific catechism, and the final inward speech of Molly Bloom.

This is one reason it changed the perceived possibilities of fiction. The novel demonstrated that form need not remain stable merely because the characters and setting remain continuous. A day could be made to contain multiple literary traditions without turning into a collection of disconnected short stories. Joyce held the work together with recurring people, locations, losses, motifs, and a coherent movement toward home.

## The magazines that took the risk

Before the book appeared, installments were printed in the American magazine The Little Review, beginning in 1918. Its editors were Margaret Anderson and Jane Heap. Ezra Pound helped promote Joyce's work; Harriet Shaw Weaver was also a crucial supporter of his career, both as publisher and through financial assistance. The Little Review's decision to publish an experimental and sexually frank novel exposed the editors to legal pressure in the United States.

The magazine's publication of the episode later called Nausicaa became the focus of an obscenity proceeding. In that episode Bloom masturbates while watching Gerty MacDowell on a beach. The action is part of a complicated scene about desire and representation, not an isolated instruction to the reader. Nevertheless, the explicitness of the passage was central to the prosecution. Anderson and Heap were found guilty in 1921 and fined. Postal suppression had already obstructed the magazine's distribution.

The legal fact should be stated precisely. A prosecution of the magazine's editors for an installment was not the same thing as a single statutory ban passed against every future edition of the novel. Restrictions on importing and distributing Ulysses operated through several mechanisms over time. The consequences for the work were nonetheless severe: a prospective American publisher had to contend with censorship and the threat of seizure.

The case also demonstrates that the struggle was not fought by Joyce alone. The women editing The Little Review, a bookseller willing to publish the complete work in France, and other literary supporters took practical risks for a novel that critics later learned to describe as a canonical masterpiece. The history of literary freedom becomes distorted when the author is the only person remembered.

## Sylvia Beach and the completed book

Beach agreed to publish the novel in Paris after its prospects elsewhere appeared poor. On February second, 1922, the first book edition arrived. It was not a simple commercial transaction in which a large publishing firm purchased an already marketable property. Beach organized subscriptions and undertook an enterprise that other publishers had avoided. Joyce's dependence on her risk and labor is part of the record.

The first edition also inaugurated an editorial problem that has never been merely clerical. Joyce made extensive revisions, including during proof correction, while production introduced errors of its own. The novel's multilingual references, changing genres, unusual punctuation, and other experiments make it difficult to decide whether every apparent anomaly is intentional. Later editors attempted to establish more reliable texts; Hans Walter Gabler's 1984 critical edition became influential and controversial.

A reader should therefore be cautious about a claim that there is one unproblematic, final, universally agreed version of Ulysses. Editions differ. Page numbers can vary significantly. When making a detailed argument about a phrase or punctuation, identify which edition supplies the text. For plot and the broad formal architecture, these issues need not prevent a newcomer from reading. They matter greatly when an interpretation depends on the exact form of a line.

## The American legal turning point

In December 1933, United States District Judge John M. Woolsey ruled in favor of admitting Ulysses in the case commonly known as United States v. One Book Called Ulysses. He read the book as a whole rather than treating a sexually explicit passage as an isolated contaminant. The decision helped clear the way for the first authorized American edition, published by Random House in 1934. The case became a notable landmark in the legal treatment of literature and obscenity.

It would be misleading to say that the decision made all censorship vanish or instantly unified every country's law. The book's British publication and distribution history followed a different course. What the American case made visible was a change in judicial willingness to consider literary form and overall effect rather than extract a fragment from the novel and pronounce judgment on that fragment alone.

This concern with wholes is peculiarly appropriate to Ulysses. The beach scene is neither the entire novel nor an accidental detachable episode. It belongs to a larger inquiry into bodily life, loneliness, public language, sexual fantasy, and narrative perspective. Whether one finds its treatment humane or troubling, understanding its role requires reading beyond the sentence likely to cause offense.

## What influence can actually mean

Ulysses is often placed at or near the center of accounts of literary modernism. That claim can become empty when repeated as praise. We should specify the innovations subsequent writers and critics had to respond to. Joyce expanded the representation of ordinary consciousness, demonstrated that narrative style could change radically within a single novel, and made a particular city's everyday life the material of an epic architecture.

His achievement did not create modernist experimentation single-handedly. Other writers, among them Marcel Proust, Virginia Woolf, and Dorothy Richardson, were exploring memory, perception, and the boundaries of fictional consciousness in overlapping years. Nor should we assume that every important later novelist borrowed directly from Joyce. Influence may occur through reading, imitation, opposition, shared intellectual conditions, or later critics' accounts of the tradition.

What can be said more firmly is that Ulysses became a work that novelists and critics could no longer ignore when arguing about the possibilities of fiction. T. S. Eliot responded to its use of myth as a way of shaping modern experience. Later generations read its mixture of languages and literary forms as both an achievement to learn from and a difficult burden to get out from under. Its effect includes writers who loved it, writers who resisted it, and readers whose understanding of what a novel might do was changed simply by knowing that such a book existed.

We should also distinguish influence from universal accessibility. The novel's learned references and technical shifts can make it challenging. The appropriate response is not to decree that everyone must admire every page. It is to see why its methods were consequential and then ask what they enable, where they exclude, and when they place artistic ingenuity ahead of the human beings they represent.

## A hundred years after the first edition

One reason Ulysses remains taught is that its formal experiments refuse to become purely historical curiosities. News headlines still select what the public should notice. Political groups still magnify small quarrels through grand rhetoric. Advertising still mingles with private desire. People still try to define neighbors through one aspect of identity. And a mind still moves between bodily sensation, remembered grief, half-learned language, and attention to others without waiting for a narrator to organize it into respectable topics.

The novel does not offer a civic program or a doctrine of kindness. It offers a sustained demonstration of how limited any one social account of a person can be. Bloom is diminished by men who have decided what a Jew must be. Molly is misunderstood by men who suppose her sexuality is the whole story. Stephen is judged by relatives, employers, and drinking companions according to different scripts. Their day becomes an enormous book because Joyce refuses to decide that those scripts have already said enough.

The durable lesson of Ulysses is not that every writer should imitate its complexity. It is that the ordinary may demand formal invention if it is to be seen fully. A person cooking breakfast, grieving a child, arguing in a bar, or lying awake beside a spouse is already living within history and language. Joyce made a novel that required literature to take that fact seriously. Whether a reader feels delight or resistance toward its methods, its challenge remains: how much of a life can a form really hold?
