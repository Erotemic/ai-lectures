# The Sun Also Rises: Brett Ashley, Desire, and the Burden of Other People’s Stories

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Lady Brett Ashley is the center of attraction in The Sun Also Rises and one of the figures most likely to be reduced to a verdict. She is sometimes described as selfish, free, reckless, courageous, or destructive. Each word catches part of her situation and threatens to flatten the rest. Hemingway constructs Brett through an account controlled by Jake Barnes, a man who loves her and has been hurt by the fact that their lives do not fit a conventional romantic ending.

The novel gives Brett a wartime history. Her work as a nurse or volunteer brought her into contact with wounded men, including Jake. The war altered intimate life as well as political geography. Brett has suffered and has made choices about marriage, sex, travel, and friendship. Her bobbed hair and manner mark her, in the eyes of contemporaries, as a woman challenging older expectations. The narrative never gives us a complete autobiography in her own voice. We must therefore distinguish what Brett actually says and does from the theories her male companions develop about her.

Jake's injury is central to their relationship, but it is not an adequate explanation for every choice either makes. Brett loves Jake; she is unwilling to build an exclusive partnership under the restrictions they imagine. Her refusal can be read as painful honesty rather than simple cruelty. At the same time, she knows that Jake will help her, and she sometimes makes demands that injure him. The two are entangled in a pattern of mutual dependence. A fair account does not have to make her either villain or emblem of liberation.

Mike Campbell, her intended husband, has financial troubles and a serious drinking problem. Their prospective marriage is hardly a stable haven. Cohn becomes attached after a period with Brett in San Sebastián and expects more from the liaison than she wishes to give. Pedro Romero offers the attraction of youth, bodily discipline, and a place outside the familiar expatriate circle. Each man supplies a different story of what Brett is supposed to mean in his life. She resists some of those stories while helping create the situations in which they collide.

Her relationship with Romero especially clarifies the distinction between agency and perfect independence. She initiates the connection and leaves Pamplona with him. Later she says she has ended it partly because he wants a kind of femininity she is unwilling to perform, and partly because she does not want to damage his career. We do not have Romero's complete account, and we receive Brett's explanation through Jake. It is still significant that she chooses to leave rather than transform herself to satisfy him. The action complicates the picture of a woman who only follows desire wherever it leads.

The men often speak about Brett as though her decisions were pieces on a board over which they are entitled to compete. Mike mocks Cohn, Cohn fights, Jake suffers, and Montoya fears the consequences for Romero. All these reactions are real parts of the plot, but they can make Brett's own preferences seem secondary. A reading attentive to gender asks which obligations the men imagine they have purchased through attraction, friendship, financial generosity, or sacrifice. It also asks when Brett exercises power over other people and whether she recognizes the effects.

The social context matters. Women in the 1920s faced changed opportunities and constraints after the First World War, but the historical category of the independent modern woman is not a ready-made explanation for Brett's character. She is an individual fictional person living through privilege, economic dependence, loss, and desire. Her aristocratic title does not ensure financial security. Her mobility does not mean she is untouched by the judgments of others. We can analyze the novel's depiction of changing gender norms without treating a single character as the definitive representative of a decade.

A practical reading method is to create a scene ledger with four columns. In the first, record Brett's direct requests. In the second, record what she actually decides. In the third, record the men's responses. In the fourth, note Jake's judgments about what she wants or ought to do. The categories will not always align. Where they diverge, we can see how another person's story has attached itself to her actions. This method also makes it harder to treat every harmful consequence as solely her responsibility: Cohn is responsible for his assaults, Jake for his introduction to Romero, Mike for his insults.

The final taxi conversation does not resolve these tensions. Brett imagines a life with Jake. He replies without accepting that fantasy as a plan. We may hear love, irony, forgiveness, fatigue, or all of them. What we should not hear is a tidy moral lesson that one person's sexuality ruined a generation. Hemingway's novel is more precise than that. It portrays people who seek intimacy after war and repeatedly discover that neither desire nor modern freedom guarantees the ability to care for someone without hurting them.
