# Ulysses: Dublin, Empire, Religion, and the Question of Belonging

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

James Joyce set Ulysses in Dublin on June sixteenth, nineteen hundred and four. Ireland was then part of the United Kingdom, and political arguments over its future were everywhere. The Catholic Church held considerable cultural authority; an Irish-language revival formed part of the effort to imagine a distinct national identity; British institutions governed many aspects of public life. The city's newspapers, streets, monuments, schools, and pubs did not speak with one voice about what being Irish meant. Joyce made these competing languages part of the novel's daily traffic.

The book was written years later, mainly in self-imposed exile, and completed as Ireland's political future was undergoing radical transformation. Its 1904 setting is not an accidental vintage costume. It makes us examine a society whose institutions tell different, sometimes incompatible stories about who belongs and who has the right to speak for the country.

## Who can say what Ireland is?

The young Stephen Dedalus is burdened by the claims of national identity. He knows Irish history and is alert to the humiliations of domination. He also resists being made into the representative artist of a nation, as though literary talent were public property. His quarrels with religion, family, and cultural expectations are not simply adolescent rejection. They test whether an individual can create anything without first accepting the roles that established institutions offer him.

In the tower an English visitor, Haines, is interested in the Irish language. The older milk woman, whose labor supports the men's breakfast, cannot answer him in Irish. It is tempting to use this scene to pronounce the language revival either noble or absurd. The encounter is more exact than either judgment. It exposes unequal access to a national ideal. A cultivated visitor may admire an endangered language while a woman with fewer resources has been denied the opportunity to acquire it. The irony is not that Irish has no value. It is that talking about a people is easier than listening to one.

In the school, Mr. Deasy claims the authority of history and practical common sense. He offers Stephen advice about money, political responsibility, and order, then voices antisemitic conspiracies. His prejudice demonstrates how a person can sound knowledgeable while repeating an explanation that excludes real people from the social world. Stephen does not need to produce a complete theory of history to recognize that Deasy's confidence is not evidence.

## What the city's official journey leaves outside

Wandering Rocks moves through nineteen city passages, opening with a Jesuit priest and ending with the viceroy's procession. Between those poles of spiritual and imperial rank, Dublin carries on with need, work, flirtation, worry, and poverty. The structure is political without behaving like a pamphlet. We see people who serve institutions, avoid them, laugh at them, and continue living despite them.

The viceroy is a representative of British rule, but the carriage cannot make the whole city belong to his point of view. Its progress is one itinerary among many, and the novel gives considerable attention to people whose journeys matter to no official record. That is an artistic challenge to the idea that only the people at the top of a political hierarchy constitute history.

The same city is full of imperial and religious symbols that the characters interpret differently. Monumental architecture may offer a meeting place, an occasion for a story, or a reminder of domination. A church service may be an expression of sincere belief to one worshipper and a fascinating arrangement of sensory rituals to Bloom. Joyce does not require every individual response to agree with a single authorial verdict about the institution.

## Bloom and the danger of a single definition

Leopold Bloom is a particularly revealing figure of national belonging because his identity is layered. His father, born Rudolf Virag, came from continental Europe and had Jewish roots. Bloom has a complex religious history and does not present himself as a simple representative of observant Judaism. He is also a Dublin inhabitant, a husband and father, a worker, a person who knows streets and social obligations, and a participant in the ordinary economy of the city.

The men who insult him are not interested in such complexity. In Cyclops the Citizen treats Jewishness as evidence that Bloom cannot belong to Ireland. The social attack works by turning one aspect of family history into the whole person. It also makes Bloom's response difficult. Should he defend himself as Irish? Should he explain the history of persecution? Should he refuse the aggressor's right to set the terms of the question?

Bloom does several things, imperfectly and under pressure. He insists that persecution and hatred are wrong. He rejects the idea that a nation must be held together by violence against outsiders. When he names Jesus as a Jew, he uses a figure central to Christian Ireland's public identity to expose the contradiction of antisemitic Christian nationalism. The Citizen does not become ashamed and repent. He grows angry enough to throw a biscuit tin.

A reader should distinguish the novel's depiction of prejudice from factual claims about Jews. Deasy's assertions about conspiracy and the Citizen's accusations are false and serve the novel as examples of exclusion. It is also important not to make Bloom stand in for every Jewish experience. His specific family background and his changing religious affiliations are part of Joyce's imagined person, not a survey of European Jewish life.

## Language can turn solidarity into an enemy

The Citizen's indignation about British rule has real political context. A colonized or subordinated population can reasonably resent a government that exercises authority over it. Yet the Citizen's account of loyalty becomes narrower than the country itself. The episode dramatizes a logical and ethical failure: because an outside power has injured Ireland, the nationalist concludes that the nation must define itself by excluding another person as alien.

The form of the chapter helps us hear how this happens. The unnamed narrator's hostility makes Bloom's mild habits seem provocative. Overblown rhetorical passages dress routine pub talk in epic and ceremonial clothing. A crowd becomes a historical assembly by the power of words alone. The result is not simply a bad opinion held by one character. It is a demonstration of the social machinery by which a group confirms its prejudices and presents them as common sense.

Joyce's alternative is not an easy cosmopolitan slogan in which all differences have disappeared. Bloom does make judgments about other people, and some of his own notions are ignorant or superficial. He lives in a society in which religious and national categories have practical consequences, and the effects cannot be wished away by declaring everyone human. But he offers a different standard of belonging when he attends funerals, worries about a widow, and protects a stranger. He acts as a member of a community even when some of its members deny him recognition.

## History exists inside a person's day

Ulysses has no need to show parliament passing a law in order to be a historical novel. It shows history as a collection of habits and pressures that structure life before anyone consciously chooses them. An English visitor can expect an Irish woman to speak Irish. A headmaster can repeat anti-Jewish propaganda as history. A nationalist can decide who qualifies as Irish in a pub. A viceroy can drive through streets that belong emotionally to people who cannot govern them.

The novel does not claim political institutions are imaginary. It asks how they acquire power over thought and conduct. The clearest evidence is often a moment in which a character uses public language to avoid seeing an individual. If someone has been called a foreigner, a heretic, or a drunk, how much of that person's experience becomes inaudible?

In its most compassionate moments, Ulysses refuses to let the category finish the story. Bloom's Jewish ancestry matters and is not erased, but it is not a full account of his citizenship. Stephen's Irishness matters, but it does not dictate the art he must make. Molly's role as a wife matters, but it does not exhaust her consciousness. A modern city is not one voice singing a national anthem. It is a place where multiple histories are spoken at once, and where a person's dignity depends partly on the willingness of others to hear more than the loudest of them.

## Scope of historical claims

The 1904 political setting and 1922 publication are historical claims; Bloom's and the Citizen's actions are narrative events; the conclusion about multiple forms of belonging is an interpretive synthesis. This lecture does not treat the fictional pub speech as representative of all Irish nationalists or claim Irish-language revivalism and antisemitism are intrinsically linked.
