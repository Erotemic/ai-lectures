# The Handmaid's Tale — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A woman lives in a room that is almost bare. It has a bed, a chair, a window that cannot be opened far enough to climb through, and no object that could easily be used to end her life. She is dressed in red. Outside, she must wear a white covering around her face that limits what she can see and how easily other people can see her. The household calls her Offred. It is not the name she was born with. It identifies the man whose household has been assigned the use of her body.

The country is called the Republic of Gilead. It occupies territory that used to be the United States. Its rulers say a catastrophe of declining births demands order, discipline, and submission. Their own power has been established through violence. Women have been forbidden to read, to hold ordinary property, and to work for themselves. The regime divides them into prescribed classes. Wives of senior men wear blue. Women who perform domestic work, called Marthas, wear green. Women considered capable of bearing children, the Handmaids, wear red. The clothing announces a position before a person can speak.

Offred serves a high-ranking Commander and his wife, Serena Joy. She is meant to bear a child for them. That single purpose determines when she may leave the house, who may touch her, and how her body is observed. She remembers another world, so the rules never become entirely natural to her. Before the takeover, she had a job, a husband named Luke, and a little daughter. She could pay for something, walk on the street, read a magazine, and choose whether to become a mother again. She is now separated from both husband and child. She does not know whether Luke is alive. Her daughter was taken from her, and the absence of a child whose features she remembers is a pain that the uniform cannot conceal.

The novel does not give us this history all at once. Offred speaks from the life she has now and returns in memory to the life she lost. Often she changes the details of a recollection or admits that another version might be closer to the truth. The following account will keep the events connected so that a listener can follow what happened, but those uncertainties are part of her experience, not defects to be repaired.

## What happened before this room

The change came in stages, followed by a speed that made resistance difficult. Men in power killed the president and members of Congress, blamed enemies, declared emergency measures, and built a new state. At first it was possible to believe that restrictions might be temporary. Then women were dismissed from their jobs. Their bank accounts were made inaccessible, and their money passed into male hands. Friends began looking at one another with alarm. Offred's mother, who had spent her life protesting for women's freedoms, now seemed less like someone living in an argumentative past and more like someone who had understood a danger earlier than others.

Offred and Luke tried to flee to Canada with their daughter. On that journey they had to perform normality for strangers who might question them. The family was caught. Offred ran with the child, but armed men took the girl away. Later, in a training center called the Red Center, women were taught how to become Handmaids. Their instructors, known as Aunts, mixed religious language, enforced shame, and punishment. Aunt Lydia, the voice Offred remembers most, taught that restrictions would protect women from the hazards of the old world. In practice that protection meant the disappearance of consent.

A friend from Offred's former life, Moira, was also held at the Center. Moira was defiant, funny, and resourceful. She resisted attempts to turn her into a symbol of docility. For Offred, Moira's presence mattered because it proved that the two women had once been people whose relationships existed outside Gilead's categories. Moira eventually escaped from the Center by overpowering an Aunt and wearing her uniform. Offred learned of it as a triumph, but a triumph without the assurance of safety. Under this state, every escape creates a period in which the person left behind can only imagine what happened next.

Offred was assigned to her present household after another posting. The Commander is important enough to be sheltered from many of the rules imposed on others. His wife is not. Serena Joy is a former public advocate of traditional domestic roles. Now she sits in a garden and a drawing room that have become the boundaries of her own life. She resents the Handmaid in the house, not only because Offred occupies an intimate position but because the law forces both women to share a process neither can honestly call a marriage. Their bitterness is not the same as freedom from responsibility. Serena can harm Offred and frequently uses the authority she has.

Every month, at the time considered favorable for conception, there is a prescribed act called the Ceremony. In the room, Serena sits behind Offred, holding her, while the Commander has intercourse with Offred. The participants are expected to enact the official story that this is a religious duty and a necessary transfer of fertility. Offred experiences it as compulsory sex organized by the state. She has to remain physically present while attempting to withdraw elsewhere in her mind. Even the household's silence is controlled; speaking about the act as violence would risk punishment.

## A small city of rules

Offred is allowed out to shop for food. Her assigned shopping companion is another Handmaid, Ofglen. They walk in pairs, ostensibly for safety but also so each can report the other. The streets hold shops identified by pictures rather than written signs, because many of the women who shop may not legally read. Armed Guardians stand at checkpoints. At the Wall, the bodies of people executed by the regime are displayed with markings of the crimes for which they were condemned. Among them are doctors punished for performing abortions before the takeover. Gilead uses the bodies as announcements: the past can become criminal after the law changes.

At first the two shoppers repeat approved greetings, speaking almost entirely in slogans. Neither knows whether the other is an informer. Eventually Ofglen makes cautious openings. She mentions that some people oppose Gilead, and that there may be a network called Mayday. Offred is frightened by the possibility, because hope itself can be a trap. The state has made ordinary conversation into a kind of interrogation in which trust must be guessed rather than established.

There is also Nick, the Commander's driver and a household Guardian. He is often nearby at the edges of Offred's movement, washing a car or occupying a doorway. His glances suggest more than the law permits, but his position is ambiguous. He could be a man taking risks for personal reasons, a servant spying for the household, or a member of the secret police, the Eyes. Offred cannot tell. She is increasingly aware that choosing whom to trust may determine whether she survives.

One day the Handmaids attend the birth of another woman's child. The woman is Janine, now called Ofwarren. The gathering is highly ritualized. The Wives celebrate in a neighboring room while the Handmaids attend to the actual labor. When the child is born, the Commander's Wife is publicly presented as its mother. Janine is briefly praised for something her social status will not let her possess. Offred remembers how the Aunts used Janine's history of sexual assault to teach the other women to blame victims for their own injuries. Here the state that claims to honor motherhood confiscates both the language and the rights that usually accompany it.

Offred also sees the risk of public religious spectacles called Prayvaganzas and the state executions known as Salvagings. These ceremonies turn political hierarchy into theater. People are expected to look as though they believe what is taking place. A public performance of certainty can hide many private calculations, but it gives the authorities evidence that their command has been obeyed.

## The forbidden room

The Commander changes the pattern in a way Offred does not anticipate. He has Nick arrange a private meeting. She expects that she may be asked to break a rule, or that the meeting may be a trap. Instead the Commander invites her into his study to play Scrabble. The game is ordinary and frightening. Reading is forbidden to her. The pieces of a board game give her temporary access to words that the country has declared dangerous. It is also a privilege he can withdraw at will.

More meetings follow. The Commander offers small rewards and contraband luxuries. He speaks with the confidence of a man who thinks his own dissatisfaction makes him sympathetic. He wants a relationship with his Handmaid that feels spontaneous and affectionate, as though the coercive terms under which she lives can be suspended by a private joke. Offred takes the opportunities because they give her information and moments of self-possession. She cannot afford the luxury of treating the meetings as innocent. A man able to break the rules safely has not granted the same safety to the woman he invites to join him.

At one point he gives her an old fashion magazine. The paper reminds her not only of desire and entertainment but of an entire world of choices that now appear frivolous because they were once so readily available. The ordinary object is proof of the distance between present and past. It also reveals that Gilead has not destroyed all the forbidden materials; it distributes them according to rank.

Offred remembers her mother and her friend Moira. She remembers arguments about how women ought to live, and about whether political changes were arriving quickly enough. She begins to see how easy it was to take the unfinished freedoms of the old country as permanent. Yet her recollection does not turn that country into a paradise. Her former life contained fear, inequality, and disagreement. What she has lost is not a perfect social order but the ability to negotiate her own imperfect life.

The Commander eventually takes Offred to a place called Jezebel's, an illegal club used by privileged men. She dresses in clothes that would bring punishment elsewhere. Inside are women whose appearance and labor contradict Gilead's public morality. The authorities keep the club because men of influence want what their own laws deny. Offred unexpectedly finds Moira there. The woman she knew as an escapee has been captured and forced into the club's economy. Moira explains what happened after her flight from the Red Center. She has survived, but her situation is not freedom. Offred listens to a friend whom she had imagined as a continuing rebellion and realizes how much that image depended on her own need for hope.

The Commander takes Offred to a private room at Jezebel's. He expects her to play at desire. The law has made her compulsory availability seem like a marital function, but he still wants to be wanted. There is no mechanism by which she can safely tell him the difference.

## What Serena knows

Serena knows the household is not producing a child. She suspects the Commander may be infertile, although Gilead officially attributes reproductive failure to women. Her solution is to arrange for Offred to sleep with Nick. She offers Offred access to a photograph of her missing daughter. The picture reveals that the child is alive and older now, living in another family. It is a gift and a cruelty. Serena can obtain the image precisely because Offred cannot seek her daughter herself.

Offred goes to Nick. What begins in a network of threats and bargains becomes something more personally important to her. She returns to him voluntarily, despite the risks. They share physical intimacy and conversation. It is possible for their attachment to be real while the conditions surrounding it remain profoundly unequal and dangerous. Offred's need for Nick changes her relation to Ofglen's quiet resistance. She becomes less willing to accept the work Ofglen asks her to do. She has found a small future she can imagine from one day to the next, and she fears losing it.

At a state-organized execution, the Handmaids are ordered to attack a man accused of rape. The crowd is told that its violence is righteous punishment. Ofglen strikes him quickly. Later she tells Offred that the man was actually connected with the resistance, and that she acted to shorten his suffering. Offred sees how the state uses the anger of people it has already wounded. Even an apparent eruption of popular will can be designed in advance.

Soon Offred goes shopping and finds someone else wearing Ofglen's name. This new woman speaks in official phrases. When Offred cautiously inquires, she learns that the earlier Ofglen killed herself as the Eyes approached. The report may be true, but Offred has no way to verify it. She has lost the one person who might have connected her to a larger effort beyond the household. Her own meetings with the Commander and Nick may already be known.

Serena discovers evidence of Offred's visits to Jezebel's. She is furious, and the punishment she promises is frighteningly indefinite. Offred waits alone upstairs. Memories, guilt, and possibilities collide. She cannot tell whether a confession, flight, or silence would save her. Then a black van arrives, apparently from the secret police.

Nick tells Offred that the men have come on behalf of Mayday, and that she should go with them. She wants to trust him. She cannot know whether he is telling the truth. The Commander protests as she is taken away, not in a way that establishes a claim to her, but in a way that reveals how little his power can protect anyone once the machinery moves against his house. Offred steps into the van. We do not see what happens to her afterward. It may be a rescue. It may be an arrest. Her story stops at the moment when the choice can no longer be explained by the narrator who made it.

## The voice after the voice

Then the book moves far into the future. Gilead is no longer the present regime, and scholars are meeting at an academic conference in the year 2195. A researcher discusses an account assembled from cassette tapes discovered years later. The tapes appear to contain the testimony we have just heard, but the speaker we call Offred has not been identified with certainty. Her recorded narrative was transcribed and arranged for study. The scholars debate which powerful man might have been her Commander and what can be inferred from surviving records.

The conference is evidence that Gilead did not last forever. It does not tell us whether Offred escaped, was killed, survived to grow old, or ever saw Luke or her daughter again. The scholarly language often directs attention toward the identities and careers of men in power rather than toward the voice of the person who endured their actions. The listeners laugh at remarks that make the testimony feel like material for an elegant professional exercise. A woman's private account has reached the future, but she cannot correct the people who now explain it.

That is where the novel leaves us: not at the moment a state is overthrown, and not with a confirmed reunion, but with a voice whose survival on tape outlasted the institution that tried to silence it. We know enough to understand what was done to her. We do not know whether she was saved. The distinction is painful, and the book insists on keeping it.

## Reading notes and spoilers

This is an original expository retelling of Margaret Atwood's 1985 novel, not of later television adaptations or the sequel *The Testaments*. The novel's unresolved final van scene remains unresolved here. Gilead's *Historical Notes* are part of the fiction, not proof that Offred's fate is known.
