# The Left Hand of Darkness — The Story, Part 1: The Envoy and the Kingdom

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Genly Ai has come to the planet Gethen as an envoy from the Ekumen, a community of worlds linked by travel, exchange, and a shared interest in peaceful relations. Gethen is also called Winter. Much of its inhabited land is cold, and the immense ice sheets elsewhere on the planet are a constant fact of life. Genly is alone in his mission, and he cannot make the people he meets accept the existence of an interstellar society merely by telling them it exists. They have to decide whether to believe him, trust him, and choose a future with people who come from elsewhere.

Gethenians differ from Genly's own people in one fundamental way. They do not live permanently as male or female. For most of the month they are sexually inactive; during a recurring period called kemmer they may become male or female and can conceive or bear children. Genly understands the biological facts, but he has not escaped his own assumptions about which voices, movements, and gestures seem masculine or feminine. He often reads a person through a category that does not fit the society before him. This becomes part of the trouble when he tries to understand his most important ally.

In Karhide, one of the two major rival nations, Genly has gained the support of Therem Harth rem ir Estraven, a powerful official who serves as the king's prime minister. Estraven has tried to move the diplomatic mission forward. Genly does not know whether to trust him. Estraven is guarded, indirect, and knowledgeable about dangers Genly does not perceive. When he advises caution, Genly hears evasion. When he offers help, Genly wonders what he wants in return.

Karhide's ruler, King Argaven, is anxious, suspicious, and unpredictable. He fears the possibility of losing control over his country and reacts badly to what the envoy represents. At a public celebration Genly observes Karhide's ceremonies, ambitions, and dense network of reputations. The king's court is filled with people watching for signs of favor and disgrace. A border dispute with the neighboring state of Orgoreyn has also become politically important, and the envoy's proposal cannot be separated from the rivalry between the two nations.

Estraven has enemies at court who believe the border quarrel with Orgoreyn can be used to strengthen national feeling. A powerful rival, Tibe, is willing to turn that fear into public authority. To Genly, these relationships are hard to read because so much of Karhidish politics is conveyed through indirection and obligations that do not translate neatly into the concepts he has brought from home. The envoy keeps asking for a clear yes or no to a question whose implications the people around him are still arguing about.

Estraven loses favor. The king banishes him from Karhide, and the official who had tried to help Genly must flee across the border. Genly, still unable to see how much Estraven has risked on his behalf, goes on trying to persuade the Karhidish government. He meets the king and explains that the Ekumen offers membership rather than conquest. Argaven rejects him. Genly's mission seems to have reached a dead end, and the man who might have helped him is gone.

Genly travels through Karhide and visits a community of Handdara practitioners at Otherhord. The Handdara is a religious and philosophical tradition whose practitioners value uncertainty, waiting, and the disciplined refusal to claim more knowledge than one possesses. A group of Foretellers gathers to answer a question posed by Genly: whether Gethen will become part of the Ekumen within five years. Their ritual is physically and mentally demanding. The answer is yes. It does not tell him how such a future will come about or whom he can safely trust along the way.

The envoy now looks toward Orgoreyn. Its political structure appears, at first, more rational and welcoming than Karhide's court. It has committees, coordinated institutions, and officials who seem prepared to discuss his mission openly. Genly crosses the border expecting that a society less dominated by one monarch may be easier to persuade. Estraven, now an exile, has already made a life there under precarious conditions. The two are moving toward the same country, but Genly still does not understand that the dangers waiting for him are greater than anything he encountered in Karhide.
