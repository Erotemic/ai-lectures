# Contact - The Story

An original, spoiler-containing orientation to Carl Sagan's 1985 novel, not the 1997 film. It follows the complete narrative, including the five-person journey and the mathematical discovery at the end. Interpretation follows in `019000`.

## A child who wants to know

Long before anyone on Earth has heard a message from another star, a girl named Eleanor Arroway wants to know why the world is the way it is. Ellie is curious about radios, numbers, and the night sky. She discovers that a radio is not magic: invisible waves can be received, separated from noise, and made intelligible. She wants the same kind of explanation for everything else. When a teacher gives her an unsatisfying answer about the number pi, she goes looking for a better one herself. The number continues beyond any decimal place she could ever write down. It is not merely very long. It belongs to a kind of mathematics that resists the notion of a tidy ending.

Her father, Theodore, encourages her curiosity. She loves him, and when he dies while she is still young, the loss changes the shape of her life. Her mother eventually marries John Staughton, a physicist Ellie finds condescending and difficult to trust. She grows more determined to make her own way, and the most reliable path seems to be the study of things that can be checked: a calculation, an instrument, a measurement that another person could repeat.

Ellie studies physics, advances through a world in which talented women still have to fight to be treated as colleagues, and becomes a radio astronomer. She meets brilliant people who encourage her and accomplished people who underestimate her. The distinction does not always track scientific merit. Among those she must deal with is David Drumlin, a prominent scientist whose objections to her work are sometimes technical and sometimes entangled with professional power.

Her central project is the search for extraterrestrial intelligence: not looking through a telescope for a creature walking on another planet, but listening for a signal that nature alone would be hard pressed to produce. She knows the odds might be poor, the distances staggering, and the years of listening unrewarded. She also knows that if other intelligent beings have discovered radio technology, their transmissions might be detectable across interstellar distances.

By the time Ellie directs Project Argus, an effort based at the Very Large Array in New Mexico, the work has become both a scientific program and a political target. Other astronomers want the telescope time for questions with more immediate prospects of results. The critics are not all villains. They have legitimate competing projects. But Ellie has spent years preparing instruments and techniques to recognize a signal if one arrives, and she believes abandoning the search would be a choice about how little humanity is willing to imagine.

For a long time, they hear nothing they can distinguish from ordinary cosmic radio noise. Then the pattern changes.

## A message from Vega

The signal seems to come from the direction of Vega, a bright star about twenty-six light-years away. Before anybody declares the discovery of an alien civilization, the team must rule out the many ways a telescope can lie: a malfunction, a local transmission, a human satellite, a misread signal, somebody's deliberate hoax. The pattern is repeated and compared with observations made elsewhere. Scientists who would love to disprove the result are invited to try.

The first recognizable content is a sequence of prime numbers. A star can produce a complicated signal, but a deliberately ordered mathematical sequence is something else. Human beings use primes because they can be recognized independently of any particular spoken language. At last the world has a reason, stronger than excitement or wishful thinking, to suspect that a mind beyond Earth is speaking.

The signal does not end with primes. It contains layers of information, almost as if its sender has wrapped several messages inside the same transmission. The next part astonishes the investigators for another reason. They recover images of Adolf Hitler speaking at the 1936 Berlin Olympics. The alien civilization, or something relaying from the vicinity of Vega, has apparently returned an early powerful television broadcast from Earth. Humanity's first image in this exchange is not an inspiring declaration of planetary unity. It is a figure associated with one of the worst chapters of its history.

Some people find this a threat. Others see a clumsy greeting: we heard you; here is proof that the signal is meant for you. The mystery becomes immediate political business. Governments worry about national security, communications, economic advantage, and the possibility that advanced technology could make old power balances meaningless. The President of the United States, Helen Lasker, and her advisers must decide what can be kept secret, what can be shared, and whom to trust.

Ellie finds herself dealing with Kenneth der Heer, the president's science adviser, and Michael Kitz, a defense official whose professional instincts lead him toward secrecy and suspicion. She also works with Vaygay Lunacharsky, a Soviet scientist capable of becoming a partner even while the two governments still treat one another as rivals. The Message has arrived at a world divided by borders and weapons. It has not made those divisions disappear.

Another part of the transmission is longer, denser, and more puzzling. It resembles a vast set of diagrams, pages upon pages of technical instructions. The scientists suspect that somebody is teaching them how to build an object, but the diagrams are impossible to read fully without a key. A machine that can be inferred from incomprehensible plans is a frightening invitation. What would it do? Who would be allowed to operate it? Could its builders be setting a trap?

Experts gather from different countries. The international collaboration is sometimes idealistic and sometimes competitive, and the scientists do not agree about all the risks. Eventually they identify a remarkable shape: an apparatus with a central chamber arranged for five human occupants. The Message is not simply a mathematical greeting. It appears to be a set of instructions for a journey.

## Voices on Earth

Outside the scientific meetings, another conversation is happening. Religious leaders interpret the signal in different ways. Some proclaim the message an apocalyptic warning or the work of evil. Others think that life elsewhere would only enlarge the scope of creation. Ellie, who has always demanded evidence, must explain why a message from intelligent beings would not automatically settle questions about God, divine purpose, or what a human life is for.

Two prominent voices come into her life. Billy Jo Rankin is an influential religious broadcaster inclined toward certainty and confrontation. Palmer Joss is a religious thinker whose convictions are strong but whose conversations with Ellie can be genuinely curious. Ellie disagrees with him, sometimes sharply. Yet she discovers that being listened to seriously by a person who disagrees is different from being caricatured by a person who wants an enemy.

Their exchanges also touch her private life. She can spend years watching a sky that rarely answers, because she trusts the process of asking. Joss believes there are kinds of experience that people know without having been able to turn them into a repeatable experiment. Ellie does not grant that the two ways of knowing have identical authority. The disagreement stays alive rather than dissolving into a conversion scene.

The same uncertainty surrounds the Machine. The international effort has to decide whether to build an object whose purpose remains an inference. Powerful people want national control. Scientists want access to information. The public wants reassurance and, just as strongly, a part in history's largest discovery. Ellie wants them to avoid reducing an encounter on a planetary scale to a contest over which country gets to place its flag on the technology.

A brilliant and eccentric industrialist, S. R. Hadden, becomes important. Hadden has immense resources and an eccentric ambition to escape the ordinary limits of age and mortality. He sees technical possibilities others miss, and he also likes having enough power to change the outcome of a negotiation. He suggests that the missing key to the Message may be hidden in another property of the radio transmission: its phase. Scientists test the suggestion and find a way to decode the instructions. The Machine becomes, at least in principle, buildable.

What follows is not a single heroic laboratory breakthrough. It requires new industries, new materials, money on an almost impossible scale, and arguments that no committee can resolve with certainty. Construction proceeds in more than one country. There is a bitterly competitive selection process for the five people who might travel. Ellie, who helped make the discovery possible, is not guaranteed a place. For the American position, authorities favor Drumlin.

Then disaster interrupts the entire project. At the construction site in Wyoming, a bomb is detonated. Drumlin saves Ellie from the immediate danger and is killed himself. The Machine is destroyed, or damaged so badly that the attempt seems finished. The violence also exposes the danger from human beings who are sure their beliefs justify sabotaging everyone else's future.

Ellie survives and finds that grief has been complicated by shame. Drumlin was a rival. She resented the way he had acquired the place she thought should have been hers. At the moment of his death, among her many reactions is the horrible recognition that one obstacle to her own candidacy has vanished. She knows that such a thought is not the whole of her feeling; it still troubles her. The dead man cannot be reduced to a professional antagonist now that he has saved her life.

Meanwhile Ellie's mother becomes seriously ill. The family conflicts that Ellie has set aside cannot be solved by discovering a message from the stars. Staughton thinks she has neglected her responsibilities. Ellie feels misunderstood and trapped between worlds that each make absolute claims on her attention.

## An invitation hidden in plain sight

When the visible Machine project appears to be over, Hadden sends a new invitation. He is living on a private orbital station, pursuing ways to remain alive longer and preserve himself into the future. Ellie travels into orbit to meet him. From that distance Earth looks like one small world, without the borders that have absorbed so much human energy throughout the Message's reception.

Hadden reveals that a third Machine has been prepared secretly in Japan, on Hokkaido. It is nearly ready. A great public effort has failed, but private wealth and international technical collaboration have maintained a second possibility. Whatever Ellie thinks about Hadden's methods, she can no longer pretend that the invitation has vanished.

The five people selected for this attempt embody several countries and kinds of expertise. Ellie goes with Vaygay Lunacharsky, Devi Sukhavati, Abonnema Eda, and Xi Qiaomu. They do not all bring the same upbringing or assumptions about what intelligence elsewhere must be like. That is part of the point: if humanity is going to be represented by a handful of people, there is no reason to imagine those people should all come from the same place.

The Machine is activated at the end of 1999. The passengers enter a chamber built according to plans they received from light-years away. They have checked what they can, but there is no rehearsal that could prove what is about to happen. The apparatus begins its operation, and their familiar surroundings vanish.

They pass through a network of passages connecting distant regions of space. Stars and vast structures appear where no human has traveled. They begin to understand that the Machine is less a rocket than a way to enter a transportation system built into the galaxy itself. The journey takes them to an immense hub near the center of the Milky Way, a place where routes from many parts of space meet.

For Ellie, the overwhelming scale is not the only surprise. After all the technical apparatus and all the arguments over engineering, the travelers find a setting that resembles a peaceful beach. The environment seems arranged so human senses can bear it. It offers something like hospitality in an otherwise unimaginable place.

The companions are separated for individual encounters. Ellie is approached by a figure that looks and sounds like Theodore Arroway, the father whose death altered her childhood. She recognizes that this cannot simply be her father returning. An intelligence she has never met has adopted a form drawn from her own memories so that she can speak to it without terror.

The encounter is profoundly personal, but the being does not tell her that the universe is arranged for human comfort. It explains that the transport network was built by an even older civilization. The intelligences now maintaining it have inherited structures whose makers and purposes they do not fully understand. They, too, inhabit a universe full of unfinished questions. They have extraordinary powers compared with humanity, but they are not all-knowing.

The inhabitants of this greater community had heard Earth's broadcasts and had seen a species with the capacity for enormous achievement and enormous self-destruction. They chose to send an invitation. The Five have now answered it, and their hosts have chosen to make the meeting understandable rather than imposing an incomprehensible spectacle.

The being tells Ellie of a deeper puzzle. It may be possible to find patterns within the mathematical structure of the universe itself, not merely messages sent through space. The number pi, which fascinated her as a child, comes back into the story. Beyond all ordinary calculation, there may be a sign that whatever intelligence built this cosmos is not identical with the civilizations now traveling through it. Even these caretakers cannot say where such a sign would finally lead.

The travelers' time is limited. Each has had an encounter shaped by personal memory. They return to the chamber and are brought home.

## A journey nobody can prove

On Earth, only about twenty minutes have passed. For the travelers, the experience has lasted many hours. The instruments and recordings they expected would document the journey are blank, apparently erased by the fields involved in transport. They have testimony, memories, and the fact that all five have returned with a coherent account. They cannot present the sort of independent physical record that would settle the matter for a skeptical world.

The authorities are furious. Kitz argues that the entire episode might be a hoax. He points out a disturbing fact: the transmission from Vega stopped at the moment the Machine was activated. Since Vega is so far away, the timing raises an apparent causal contradiction. If someone on Earth had told the senders to stop, the command could not possibly have reached them instantly. Perhaps the signal was being produced closer to home than everyone believed. Perhaps the whole international project has been manipulated.

Ellie understands why the questions matter. She would have asked many of them herself if the story came from somebody else. But she was there. She remembers the journey. Her colleagues remember it. To accept the official explanation that nothing happened would require her to deny an experience she knows, though she cannot yet demonstrate, to be real.

The government announces that the Machine failed. The five are pressured into silence. Their testimony becomes political inconvenience rather than a triumph of knowledge. Ellie returns to scientific work, but now she inhabits a strange position. She is an experienced investigator who has made the greatest possible discovery and cannot give her colleagues the evidence they need to accept it.

Palmer Joss listens to her account. He believes her, not because she can produce the decisive instrument reading, but because he trusts her. Ellie has spent much of her life explaining that trust is not sufficient evidence for a scientific claim. She has not necessarily changed that conviction. She has learned what it feels like to stand on the side of a true account for which proof has not arrived.

Hadden's own story ends in another kind of silence. The world hears that he has died. In fact he has staged his departure and set off alone on a long private voyage, hoping that technology and time might permit him to escape mortality. The man who helped humanity reach another civilization decides, at the last, to chase the stars on his own terms.

## The circle

Ellie does not stop investigating. She uses computing power to search the digits of mathematical constants for the sort of pattern she was told might be hidden there. The project is extravagantly ambitious. Most of the digits of pi look like an endless succession with no obvious design. Any one short string might occur by chance. A meaningful discovery would have to be more than the occasional cluster of numerals that appeals to a human eye.

While that search continues, another message reaches her from much closer to home. After her mother's death, Ellie learns from a letter that the man she has regarded as an unsympathetic stepfather, John Staughton, is her biological father. The adored Theodore, who raised and encouraged her, was not her biological father. A truth she had built her childhood around has turned out to contain a hidden layer. The revelation does not erase Theodore's love or make him less her father in the way she experienced it. It forces her to recognize that the people closest to her have lived with knowledge she did not possess.

Then the mathematical search produces its answer. Far into the base-eleven expansion of pi, Ellie's program finds an extended, ordered sequence of zeros and ones. When arranged as an image, the digits form a circle. The possibility of a message inside mathematics has become, in the world of the novel, something she can inspect.

There is no final declaration from a godlike figure, no instruction for what humanity must do next. The discovery does not explain who arranged such a sign or how a mathematically defined universe could contain it. It opens a still larger question.

As a child, Ellie wanted a better answer than a teacher could give. As a scientist, she learned to distinguish a pattern from random noise and a hopeful belief from a reproducible observation. She has been carried farther than she imagined, then brought back to a planet where many people will not believe her. At the end, the small shape emerging from the numbers is her newest evidence and her oldest fascination joined together. Humanity has received a message, built a machine, met minds older than its own, and returned home without resolving the oldest mysteries. It can begin asking them more carefully.
