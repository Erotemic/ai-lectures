# Invisible Man — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man is narrated by a man who never gives us the name by which he was born. When he begins speaking, he is living underground in a city, drawing power illegally to keep an astonishing number of electric lights burning around him. He insists that he is invisible, although he has a body and people can see him. He means that people who encounter him repeatedly fail to recognize the person who is actually present. From this underground room he begins telling us how he arrived there.

As a young man in the segregated South, he is an ambitious and capable student. He believes that careful speech, good manners, and hard work will earn him the opportunity to improve his position. After giving a successful school speech, he is invited to repeat it for a gathering of prominent white men. The evening turns into something terrifying. He and several other young Black men are blindfolded and forced to fight one another for the amusement of the audience. Afterward they are told to scramble for money on an electrified rug. Humiliated and injured, the narrator is finally allowed to deliver his speech. He has learned something about the men who claim to reward him, but he also receives a briefcase and a scholarship to a Black college. He goes away believing that the scholarship may open the life he wants.

At college, the narrator respects the institution's president, Dr. Bledsoe, and imagines himself someday becoming a leader of similar standing. He is given the duty of driving a wealthy white trustee, Mr. Norton, around the area. Norton asks to see more than the carefully prepared scenes of the campus. Their drive takes them near the home of Jim Trueblood, a poor Black farmer whose account of an incestuous assault within his own family has drawn scandal. Norton becomes fascinated by Trueblood's story, even while the narrator is desperate to remove him from an encounter that could embarrass the college.

The day grows more difficult. Norton becomes ill and the narrator takes him to a place called the Golden Day, where Black veterans gather and where a visiting college trustee is not received with the deference the narrator expects. Some of the men are former professionals, soldiers, or intellectuals whose lives have been damaged by racism and psychiatric confinement. The narrator cannot control what is said in front of Norton. His drive has become a sequence of unexpected disclosures, and he returns to campus fearing punishment.

The narrator has also heard a powerful speech honoring the college's Founder, a figure whose image dominates the institution's ceremonial life. The speaker, Reverend Homer A. Barbee, is blind. He describes the Founder's achievements in grand, reverent language while the students are expected to listen as though a shared destiny has been settled for them. The narrator wants to participate in that future. The president's anger after the disastrous drive is therefore more than the loss of a job: it threatens the whole picture of himself he has been encouraged to cultivate.

Bledsoe is furious. The president tells the narrator that his first obligation was to protect the institution's image in the eyes of powerful supporters. Truth, in this account, is less important than managing what wealthy white men are allowed to see. The narrator protests that he followed Norton's wishes. Bledsoe does not accept the excuse. He expels him, but offers letters of introduction to important men in New York City, where the narrator may find summer work and perhaps earn the right to return to school. The narrator leaves with the letters and a new determination to prove his worth.

In New York he visits offices and waits for answers that never arrive. The letters do not produce jobs. At last, the son of one of the men addressed agrees to explain why: Bledsoe has written not recommendations but warnings that the narrator is troublesome and unworthy of help. The supposed route back to college was a method of sending him away without allowing him to understand what had happened. The young man's faith in Bledsoe is shattered. He has obeyed the college president's instructions and discovered that the president used his trust against him.

The young man takes the employment that is offered to him instead: a job at a paint factory called Liberty Paints. Its pride is a product advertised as brilliantly white, and the factory has its own complicated hierarchy of workers and supervisors. The narrator enters a new world of machinery, rules, and people whose power over him has little connection to his intelligence or education. His first hopes in the South have fallen apart. The next stage of his story takes place in a factory where, once again, he will be made to perform a role he scarcely understands.

His first work in New York carries him into another institution with its own hidden rules.

At Liberty Paints, the narrator discovers that a job described as simple can be filled with hidden rules. He is instructed to add black drops to the company's famous white paint, a product whose slogan promises purity and brightness. He is later sent to work with Lucius Brockway, a Black man who has spent years mastering the factory's boilers and processes. Brockway knows that the company depends upon his skill, though the organization gives him little public honor for it. He is jealous of threats to his position and deeply suspicious of union activity.

A quarrel develops. Brockway believes the narrator may be connected to union organizers, and the argument becomes a fight. While they struggle, equipment is left unattended. An explosion injures the narrator. He awakens in a medical ward, disoriented and unable at first to recover his sense of self. Doctors experiment with electric shocks and speak about his condition as if the man in the bed were an object. He eventually leaves, with his body functioning again but his trust in the surrounding world further damaged.

In Harlem, he is taken in by Mary Rambo, a woman whose kindness asks little in return. She offers him lodging and food when he has scarcely anything. He begins to feel connected to a neighborhood rather than to a college or factory management. Then he watches officers evict an elderly Black couple from their home. Their belongings are placed on the street, and neighbors gather in anger. The narrator speaks to the crowd, drawing on the public-speaking talent he developed as a student. His words help turn private distress into collective protest.

A political organization calling itself the Brotherhood notices his talent. Its leaders recruit him as a public speaker, offer him money and a new identity, and train him in their language of social change. Brother Jack, one of its central figures, presents the group as a movement guided by an understanding of history and collective justice. The narrator wants work that matters. The Brotherhood gives him a platform and an audience in Harlem, and for a while he feels that he has finally found people who value his abilities.

He comes to know Tod Clifton, a charismatic Black organizer whose energy and intelligence impress him. They work amid competing political groups and tensions within Harlem. One of their opponents is Ras the Exhorter, a militant nationalist who rejects the Brotherhood's interracial program and sees its leaders as exploiting Black members for their own purposes. Ras challenges the narrator in public and presses him to explain whom he really serves.

The Brotherhood then begins changing its priorities. The narrator is reassigned away from Harlem despite the relationships he has built there. He attempts to follow the organization's instructions and adapt to its internal discipline. When he returns, he finds that support in the neighborhood has weakened. People have noticed the Brotherhood's absence, and some doubt whether its promises were ever serious. Clifton disappears from the organization, and the narrator searches for an explanation.

Before that discovery, Harlem has become difficult for the narrator to navigate. People who once greeted him warmly ask what the Brotherhood is doing for them now. Ras and other opponents are quick to make use of the organization's retreat. The narrator still remembers how the old couple's eviction drew people into the street and how a speech could make strangers briefly feel that they stood together. Now his own speeches are being scheduled or forbidden by people who do not live with the consequences in the same way.

He finds Clifton on a street corner selling little dancing dolls that caricature Black people. The narrator is stunned that someone he respected has taken up this degrading trade. Before he can learn what led Clifton there, a police officer confronts the seller. Clifton resists, and the officer shoots him. His death takes place abruptly, in the street, far from the formal speeches and theories that once made his work seem important.

The narrator organizes a funeral procession for Clifton and speaks before the crowd. It becomes a large public event, and the people of Harlem respond to the dignity he brings to the fallen organizer. The Brotherhood is angry. Its leaders did not authorize the funeral, and they do not want a local death to determine the organization's wider strategy. The narrator begins to see that people he knew as friends may be expendable to those who claim to represent them. In the next part, he will try to confront the Brotherhood's leaders, discover more about the lives available to a person who can be mistaken for anyone, and descend toward the underground room from which he began his story.

Clifton’s death brings the narrator into direct conflict with the Brotherhood he once trusted.

After Tod Clifton's death, the narrator argues with Brother Jack and the other leaders of the Brotherhood. He believed that the organization would honor an organizer who worked in Harlem and died there. The leaders want him to think instead of the group's broader plans. Their explanations do not account for the grief of the people who marched in Clifton's funeral. During an angry exchange, Jack's artificial eye becomes visible, and the narrator sees another difference between the leader he imagined and the man sitting before him. Jack can speak of historical necessity while keeping himself at a distance from the costs borne by those he directs.

The narrator leaves the meeting and moves through the city in growing confusion. To avoid recognition, he wears dark glasses and a hat. Strangers immediately begin addressing him as someone called Rinehart. Different people know Rinehart as a gambler, a lover, a street operator, and a preacher. The narrator discovers that the same appearance can call forth entirely different identities depending on who is looking. He has no idea what Rinehart actually believes, or whether the man believes anything at all. For a short time he occupies the confusion of being mistaken for someone whose life appears to contain many incompatible roles.

He decides that the Brotherhood must be challenged on its own terms. The organization has taught him to use language strategically; now he plans to conceal his intentions while appearing obedient. He meets with Brother Hambro, who explains that Harlem is no longer a priority and that the movement may have to sacrifice particular people or communities to its theory of what comes next. The narrator is appalled. He considers gathering information by approaching a woman close to the leaders, but the encounter he arranges brings him instead to Sybil, a woman with fantasies about Black men and little useful knowledge of the Brotherhood's inner life. The scene leaves him more isolated than informed.

Then trouble erupts in Harlem. He is called toward the neighborhood and finds streets filled with anger, damage, and fighting. The immediate causes are tangled among housing conditions, police violence, the Brotherhood's withdrawals, and the movements of people like Ras who have challenged its legitimacy. Shops are attacked. Fires are set. People gather in groups whose purposes and loyalties shift as events accelerate. The narrator sees that no single leader can control what is happening.

Ras appears in extravagant costume, now calling himself a destroyer rather than an exhorter. He directs his anger against the narrator, whom he sees as a traitor to Black people. The narrator runs. He cannot call on the Brotherhood for protection, and the crowd around him is too chaotic to offer a safe place to stand. He takes part in setting fire to a building, then flees again as the night disintegrates into violence and pursuit.

Two policemen confront him near a manhole, suspecting that the briefcase he carries contains stolen goods. He escapes by falling into the opening. The police close the cover above him, leaving him underground. He discovers that he can survive in the hidden spaces below the city. The briefcase he carried through the story contains objects from earlier stages of his life, including the scholarship papers and other reminders of the institutions that tried to define him. In the darkness he burns what he can to make light and sees the accumulated fragments of his old identities together.

The narrator eventually makes the cellar his home. He draws electrical power into it and fills it with lights. He listens to music and writes down the journey we have just followed. He is no longer the boy who believed a scholarship guaranteed recognition or the organizer who thought a political movement could tell him who he was. He has also not reached a final set of answers about how to live among other people. He begins to imagine leaving his underground refuge. A person who has disappeared from public life may someday have to speak again.

That is the end of the events narrated in Invisible Man. The young man has passed from a Southern school through college, factory, Harlem organizing, betrayal, political violence, and hiding. His name remains unstated. The story he tells is the record of how he became a person who could no longer accept the identities offered to him, and how he began looking for a way back into the world without handing his life over to someone else's description.
