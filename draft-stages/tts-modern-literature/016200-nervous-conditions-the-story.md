# Nervous Conditions — The Story

A continuous, spoiler-containing narrative account of Tsitsi Dangarembga's 1988 novel, written for a listener who has not read it. Analysis follows at `017000`.

## An unexpected beginning

Tambudzai, called Tambu, is telling us how she grew up in Rhodesia, the country now called Zimbabwe. She begins with a fact that is uncomfortable to hear: when her brother Nhamo died, she did not grieve in the way people expected. She does not present herself as a girl incapable of affection. She asks us to understand why, in the family and society she knew, her brother's death could change her life so completely.

To explain, she takes us to a rural homestead where work is necessary every day. Fields must be cultivated, food prepared, children looked after, and money found for school fees. Tambu lives with her father, Jeremiah, her mother, Ma'Shingayi, whom she also calls Mainini, and her siblings. Her father's brother, Babamukuru, has become a respected school administrator. The brothers belong to the same extended family, but they inhabit very different material worlds.

Babamukuru's education has brought him professional standing and a relatively comfortable household near a Christian mission. He has studied abroad with his wife, Maiguru, and their children, Chido and Nyasha. He is expected to support relatives who have not been as fortunate. The family regards him with reverence; a request or a decision from him can determine which child goes to school and which one stays home.

For Tambu, education represents a possibility of escaping the limits that have already been drawn around her. She likes learning. She wants to continue. But her father sees little reason to spend scarce money educating a daughter. A son will carry the family forward, as the adults define it; a daughter is expected to marry and perform work for others. Tambu cannot simply argue that she deserves the same opportunities as Nhamo and expect the household to reorganize itself.

So she tries to create her own opportunity. She asks for land to grow maize, hoping to sell the crop and pay her fees. Growing it requires real labor. The project is a child's practical answer to an adult refusal: if there is no money for school, she will attempt to earn it. But her brother takes some of the maize. When she realizes he is undermining the one plan she has, she fights with him. The quarrel exposes an injustice more serious than ordinary sibling rivalry. Nhamo has a route into an education that she is expected to sacrifice for him, yet he will not even leave her small independent effort alone.

A teacher named Mr. Matimba intervenes. He recognizes Tambu's determination and helps her take maize to town to sell. Through an encounter there, he obtains money that can pay for her schooling. The transaction is imperfect: the sympathy of white strangers is part of how he secures the help. Still, for Tambu the result is immediate. She can continue studying. Her effort has produced something the family had declared impossible.

The reprieve does not end the family hierarchy. Nhamo is sent to the mission where Babamukuru works. He begins to enjoy its food, clothing, and status. As his visits home become less welcome to him, he grows disdainful of the rural life in which he was raised. He seems to forget the language and habits that once linked him to his family. Tambu sees how quickly new privilege can become contempt for the people who have not received it.

Then Nhamo becomes ill and dies. The news comes through Babamukuru and Maiguru. His death is a genuine loss to the family, but it is also the reason Tambu is chosen to take his place at the mission. The schooling she had fought for will become available through an accident that no amount of merit could have produced. The narrator does not disguise her relief. She allows that contradiction to stand.

## Babamukuru's house

When Tambu arrives at the mission, she discovers an order of living almost unimaginable from her homestead. There is abundant food, a large house, indoor conveniences, books, separate rooms, and a disciplined schedule. The family welcomes her, particularly Maiguru, who helps her prepare for school and treats her with warmth.

Tambu is grateful to Babamukuru. She sees in him the person who has opened a gate to the life she wants. He expects respect, hard work, good grades, and proper behavior. Tambu intends to satisfy him. She learns quickly. Academic success brings her pleasure, and the cleanliness and security of the house make the daily burdens of her earlier life seem far away.

She also comes to know Nyasha, Babamukuru and Maiguru's daughter, who is near her age. Nyasha and her brother Chido spent formative years in England while their parents pursued higher education. Returning to Rhodesia has not been easy for Nyasha. She is uncomfortable with the expectation that she should seamlessly take up the role of a properly obedient daughter in a society she no longer completely understands. Her father is disturbed by her clothes, speech, reading, and willingness to dispute him. Nyasha speaks English with ease and struggles with aspects of the Shona-speaking life around her. But she is not simply trying to become English. She is trying to understand who she can be without having someone else answer for her.

At first Tambu is startled by Nyasha's manner. She has grown up believing that the proper response to Babamukuru is obedience. Nyasha asks questions. She reads books her mother dislikes, argues about rules, and challenges her father's authority even when she knows he can punish her. Yet she also shares her room and conversation with Tambu. Their friendship becomes one of the novel's strongest relationships because they can recognize things in one another that the adults do not fully see.

Nyasha encourages Tambu to read more widely and to question the apparent neutrality of things that seem like common sense at school. Tambu is interested, but cautious. She has fought hard to enter an institution that Nyasha can already see as limiting. For Tambu, the mission is an extraordinary opportunity. For Nyasha, whose family background has given her access to both English and Rhodesian worlds, the same institution is filled with contradictions. Neither girl can simply take over the other's experience.

Maiguru presents a quieter contradiction. She is educated to a high level, yet she is expected to serve her husband's extended family and has little practical independence over the money she earns. Tambu is surprised to learn how much Maiguru has accomplished academically, and how little that accomplishment frees her from the demands of being a wife and senior woman in the family. Education has enlarged her possibilities without dissolving all the rules around her.

## Nyasha's struggle at home

The tensions sharpen after a school dance. Nyasha has been with other young people and stays out longer than Babamukuru approves. He sees her behavior as a challenge to family honor. When she returns, an argument escalates into physical violence. Nyasha resists him, and the struggle becomes frightening for everyone present. Tambu witnesses a side of the revered headmaster that does not fit the image of a benevolent guardian.

For Babamukuru, discipline, parental duty, and public reputation seem to justify his rage. For Nyasha, the rule she is being ordered to obey seems both unfair and impossible. Her father has helped create the international life that changed her; now he wants her to behave as though none of that change has happened. The family house is a place of comfort and achievement, but it is also a place where a daughter's growing independence can provoke fear and punishment.

Tambu does not suddenly abandon her admiration for Babamukuru. That would be too simple. She owes him opportunities she values and sees his hard work. But the scene makes it harder to believe that gratitude requires agreeing with everything he does. She begins to notice that the very system giving her a future can also damage the people within it.

Nyasha studies intensely. Her eating becomes a site of conflict and control. Sometimes she does not want food. Sometimes her father insists she finish a meal. She worries about her body, her performance, and the demands that are constantly being made of her. Tambu can see that her friend is suffering but cannot yet understand how serious the danger will become.

## The family gathers at the homestead

During a holiday, the mission household returns to Tambu's home village. Their arrival brings goods and food, but also exposes the unequal distribution of work. The men meet to discuss the affairs of the extended family, while women cook, serve, and do the labor required to support the gathering. Maiguru, despite her degree and professional role, is expected to shoulder much of this work because of her position as Babamukuru's wife.

New trouble has arisen around Jeremiah's household. Lucia, Tambu's outspoken maternal aunt, is staying near the homestead. She is involved with a man named Takesure, and her pregnancy and living arrangements become matters of family controversy. The men prepare to discuss what should happen to her. Lucia refuses to accept that she must remain outside while men decide her future. She enters the meeting and insists on speaking for herself.

Lucia can be forceful, comic, and provocative. She is not presented as a saint or a model of formal education. What she possesses is the ability to make herself heard when the structure of a gathering is designed to exclude her. Tambu watches a woman with little institutional prestige interrupt the proceedings of men who have appointed themselves judges.

Babamukuru decides that the family has another moral problem: Jeremiah and Ma'Shingayi were not married in a church ceremony. He proposes arranging a Christian wedding for them. The plan is expensive and humiliating to Tambu, who knows her parents have lived together for many years and have raised children. To her the ceremony does not look like a joyful beginning. It looks like a public judgment that her existing family has been illegitimate until a respected man chooses to correct it.

Nobody asks Tambu whether she consents to be displayed in the ceremony as a dutiful daughter. Babamukuru expects her participation. Her mother is not in a position to overrule him. Tambu finds herself caught between gratitude toward the person paying for her education and resistance to the way he has imposed his authority on her parents.

She is given clothes for the wedding and instructed to attend. On the day itself she refuses. Her refusal is not a grand public speech. It is a stubborn, frightened act by a girl who has finally reached a point where obedience feels more intolerable than the punishment that will follow.

Babamukuru is furious. He beats her and assigns her domestic labor as further punishment. Tambu must confront the fact that the man who has enabled her schooling can also humiliate and hurt her for saying no. He does not become a cartoon villain; he continues to help members of the family. Yet his generosity does not erase the violence with which he demands authority.

Lucia speaks up in Tambu's defense. Maiguru also reaches a limit. Frustrated by the expectations placed on her as wage-earner, hostess, and dutiful wife, she leaves Babamukuru for a time. Her departure unsettles a family accustomed to relying on her without fully acknowledging her independence. She returns, but the episode shows that even a woman who appears compliant may have been keeping a careful account of what she is asked to surrender.

## The larger school

At the mission, Tambu's academic success brings an unexpected opportunity. Visiting nuns administer an examination, and she is offered a place at a prestigious Catholic girls' school, Sacred Heart. The education available there carries even greater status. For Tambu, who once had to grow maize to continue classes, it seems almost beyond belief.

Not everyone agrees that she should go. Babamukuru worries about what such an institution will do to her, while Nyasha questions the costs of being educated into a system run chiefly for white students. Maiguru helps argue for the opportunity. Tambu wants to seize it. Her mother is afraid that the Englishness associated with elite schools will take her child away, as she fears it has harmed other children of the family.

Tambu is eventually allowed to attend. She travels to Sacred Heart and sees a new scale of privilege. The school is rigorous and impressive, but its racial hierarchy is visible even in its living arrangements. A small number of African students are admitted into an institution built around white girls' education. Tambu must work within rules she did not invent and cannot simply challenge. Yet she is thrilled by the education itself. She loves study and is determined not to waste what she has gained.

She begins to drift away from Nyasha. Letters arrive, some affectionate, some troubling. Tambu is busy with the demands of her new school, surrounded by the promise of a future she has long imagined. She does not always answer. Friendship, once central to her daily life, becomes something she assumes will be there when she returns.

When she does see Nyasha again, the change is startling. Nyasha has lost a great deal of weight. She eats little, or vomits after meals. Her concentration on study has become punishing, and her conflicts with her father have not ended. The girl who once argued vigorously about books and social life now seems to be fighting for control of a world that has become unbearable.

One evening Nyasha suffers a severe breakdown. She speaks in distress about what has been done to her, about the forces that have shaped her life and denied her a secure place in either of the cultures she has been expected to inhabit. She is exhausted, physically endangered, and in need of care. The family seeks medical attention. One professional dismisses the possibility of such an illness in an African girl; another recognizes that Nyasha needs treatment, and she is admitted to a clinic.

The dismissal by the first professional is an injury in itself. A person's suffering is being filtered through someone else's assumption about who can have certain kinds of distress. Nyasha's illness does not become less real because it is difficult for an authority figure to classify. Nor does her suffering become a simple allegory that permits the rest of the family to forget the person enduring it.

Tambu sees the crisis but cannot solve it. She is still a schoolgirl. She can love Nyasha without being able to make her safe. Her mother warns again that the values imported through colonial schooling are dangerous. Tambu must live with the competing truths that education has opened the world for her and that its institutions have also inflicted damage.

## Where Tambu leaves us

*Nervous Conditions* does not end with Tambu abandoning school or triumphantly escaping every constraint. She goes on. The story is told by an older Tambu who has come to question more of what she once took for granted. She remembers herself as a girl who was determined, grateful, sometimes perceptive, sometimes complacent, and sometimes unwilling to recognize another person's pain while she pursued her own future.

Her brother's death did not magically resolve the injustice that made her schooling dependent on a boy's absence. Babamukuru's sponsorship did not make him incapable of violence. Maiguru's education did not free her from domestic obligations. Nyasha's intelligence did not protect her from a crisis that exposed the limits of her family's and her society's understanding. Lucia's defiance did not give her control over every event. Each woman's life contains both resources and constraints that no single explanation can erase.

There is no final scene in which everyone has learned the correct lesson and the problem has vanished. Tambu has begun to see that her cherished progress comes with questions she was once eager to avoid. The older narrator can tell the story because, at some point after these events, the process of questioning grew strong enough for her to examine the terms of her own success.

What remains is the feeling of a girl standing between two worlds she cannot simply choose between. The homestead contains labor, kinship, poverty, and belonging. The mission and Sacred Heart contain opportunity, power, learning, and danger. She has traveled from one to the others, but she carries them all. Her education has not ended her story. It has made her story harder, and more necessary, to tell.
