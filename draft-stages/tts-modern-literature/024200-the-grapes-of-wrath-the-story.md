# The Grapes of Wrath - The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The Joad family has been driven off the land it worked in Oklahoma. When we meet one of its sons, Tom, he does not yet know the full extent of the loss. He has just left prison on parole after serving time for killing a man in a fight. He is walking home, and the road carries him through a country where banks and machines have changed what it means to be a farmer. The farmhouses are empty. Neighbors have gone. Men who once dealt with a particular landlord now face decisions made by people far away, and tractors that can clear a field without caring who has lived on it.

Tom meets Jim Casy, a former preacher who has lost confidence in the kind of religious certainty he once supplied. Casy has not stopped thinking about goodness. He has come to suspect that people and their common lives matter more than the private sins and individual salvation he used to talk about. They walk together toward the Joad place. When they arrive, the house is deserted. Muley Graves, a neighbor who has refused to abandon the country, tells them what happened. The Joads have been forced out and are staying with Tom's uncle John while they prepare to go west.

For the family, California is not a dream invented out of nothing. Handbills have advertised work picking fruit, and rumors promise fertile fields and wages. They have almost no alternative. So they load their possessions onto an aging truck. What cannot be carried must be sold, left behind, or given up. The family believes it is traveling toward another chance to work and live together.

John Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath alternates this family story with chapters that widen the view to fields, highways, money, machines, and thousands of other displaced people. We will follow the Joads, but we should keep in mind that their truck is only one of many on the road.

## The people on the truck

Pa Joad has the habits of a man who expects to be able to provide for his family, but the loss of the farm has left him without his familiar authority. Ma Joad is practical, fierce about keeping everyone together, and often steadier than any of the men. Grampa and Granma carry memories of the old home that cannot be packed into a vehicle. Uncle John has spent years punishing himself over the death of his wife. The children Ruthie and Winfield are young enough to turn almost anything into a game until hunger and fear make the game impossible.

Tom's younger brother Al knows automobiles and takes pride in keeping the truck moving. His sister Rose of Sharon is pregnant and planning a future with her husband, Connie. She imagines a settled household and an easier life after the child is born. Noah, another brother, is quieter and less easily understood by the rest of the family. The former preacher Casy joins the trip, not because he has a guaranteed place in the family, but because the Joads make room for one more person.

Even before they leave, there is disagreement about the destination. Some have heard that California is rich. Others suspect that thousands of people answering the same advertisement cannot all find work. Ma is less interested in forecasts than in the immediate problem of getting the whole family there alive.

On the road they discover what travel means without spare money. Tires fail. The truck needs repairs. Food must last. People sleep beside highways, exchange news at camps, and study strangers to decide whether they can be trusted. At a roadside stop the family sees how contempt for poor migrants can harden before anyone learns their names. An insult about where they come from becomes a way of telling them they do not belong anywhere.

The journey breaks the family almost from the start. Grampa does not want to leave his land. He is persuaded into the truck, but the departure is a wound he cannot survive for long. He dies soon after they set out. The family buries him by the road, improvising what dignity it can while fearing official attention and the cost of stopping. They travel with another migrant couple, the Wilsons, for a while, each family helping the other because none has the resources to move safely alone.

At the Colorado River, Noah decides he will stay rather than continue. There is no grand confrontation. He is simply going somewhere else, toward a life of his own along the water. The Joads must accept that keeping everyone together is already becoming impossible. The Wilsons can no longer continue with them either. Along the desert crossing Granma grows terribly ill. Ma stays beside her through the night, concealing from the family that she has died because she fears that stopping will endanger the others. They reach California with two older generations gone and others left behind.

## The promised land

California looks green and abundant, but its abundance does not belong to the people arriving hungry. The Joads find families crowded into rough camps made of scrap materials. They hear the word Okie used as an insult. They learn that landowners can advertise more jobs than exist, bringing in more workers than they need and using the surplus to force wages down. Men are blamed for refusing bad pay even when the bad pay cannot feed their children. The family's destination is a system that knows how to use their desperation.

Connie, who had promised Rose of Sharon a comfortable future, becomes overwhelmed by the actual conditions and leaves her. His departure is another loss. Rose of Sharon cannot make herself stop imagining the life they had discussed, and Ma must care for a daughter whose pregnancy is increasingly difficult without adequate shelter or food.

At a rough camp often called a Hooverville, Tom and Casy try to help when labor recruiters arrive. The terms of the work are vague and the threats of police intervention are immediate. A confrontation breaks out. Casy takes responsibility for an assault in order to protect Tom, whose parole makes arrest especially dangerous. Casy goes to jail, and the family moves on.

The Joads then reach a government-run migrant camp, modeled in part on real camps that existed in California. For the first time in a long while they find a place with clean facilities, a measure of privacy, and rules made through the residents' own committees. The people there arrange dances, solve disputes, and prevent intimidation without allowing local police to dominate their lives. Ma sees how much dignity changes the atmosphere of a place. The family's situation is still poor, but they are not treated as animals.

Tom learns that people hostile to the camp intend to provoke a fight during a dance and give deputies an excuse to shut it down. Residents organize to prevent the disturbance. For one night the effort works. Yet the camp cannot manufacture jobs. A humane place to sleep cannot replace the income the Joads need. After some time, they leave again.

This is one of the novel's most painful rhythms. A place can be good and still be impossible to stay in. A family can work hard and still lose ground. The arrival of help does not automatically defeat the economic forces surrounding it.

## Work at the peach orchard

The family is led into an orchard where they can pick peaches. A fence, guards, and an apparently available wage make the job look like a chance to recover. The Joads work and discover that buying food from the company store quickly eats what they earn. Outside the guarded property, workers have been striking for higher wages. The family has crossed the picket line without fully understanding it.

Tom slips out and finds Casy, released from jail and now organizing among the farmworkers. Casy has changed while Tom was away. He has listened to workers and come to believe that the struggle for decent conditions must be shared. He does not tell Tom that every problem can be solved by individual moral purity or a return to an older religion. He speaks in the language of people trying to act together. The former preacher has become a labor organizer.

Men seeking to break the strike come upon them. In the confrontation, one of them kills Casy. Tom lashes out and kills the man who struck him. His response is immediate, violent, and dangerous. He is already on parole, and the consequences of being caught would likely destroy him. He returns to the family wounded and eventually hides while they leave the orchard.

The Joads move to cotton picking and manage for a time to live in a railroad boxcar. It is not the independent farm that Pa had imagined, but there is work, a roof, and a fragile chance to eat regularly. Al forms a relationship with Agnes Wainwright, the daughter of another family in the camp. His hopes for marriage do not include keeping the Joads together in their original form. Each new attachment may be a new home for one person and another separation for the rest.

Tom stays hidden near the family. Ruthie, provoked during a quarrel with another child, reveals that her brother has killed a man. Ma realizes that the concealment is no longer safe. She goes to Tom and tells him he must leave. He has to go because staying may bring punishment on everyone. Their parting is intimate and terrible: Ma is losing another son, and Tom knows he cannot come back to the old role he occupied in the family.

Tom has been listening to Casy. He now speaks about continuing the work that Casy began, about being present wherever people struggle to eat, live, and resist being crushed. This does not assure us that Tom will become a successful organizer. It tells us that he is leaving with a purpose that is larger than saving himself. He disappears from the family's immediate story.

## Rain and a decision without a guarantee

The cotton season ends and work is scarce again. Rain comes, at first as bad weather and then as flooding that threatens to overwhelm the low places where migrants are living. The Joads try to protect their shelter. Other families try to build barriers and move possessions. Water enters the boxcars. What a few days earlier looked like a temporary home becomes dangerous.

Rose of Sharon goes into labor while the family is trapped by the rain. Her baby is stillborn. No hopeful speech can change that fact, and the loss is particularly severe because the child had become the center of her imagined future. Uncle John takes the dead infant and sends it down the floodwater in an act of grief and defiance. There is no practical solution left for the family in the flooded camp. Ma leads them to higher ground, where they find a barn and shelter from the storm.

Inside are two other people, a boy and his father. The father is starving. He has not been eating, preserving what little food there was for his son. He is too weak to help himself. The Joads have almost nothing to give him. Rose of Sharon, grieving and exhausted after giving birth, has begun to produce milk. Ma recognizes that her daughter has one thing the starving man may be able to take when he cannot swallow ordinary food.

The other family members leave them privacy. Rose of Sharon offers her breast to the dying stranger and feeds him.

The novel ends there. It does not tell us whether the man recovers. It does not locate Tom, find jobs for the Joads, restore the farm, or reveal a social policy that suddenly saves every migrant. The last action is a gift of nourishment made possible by the very body that has just endured a terrible loss. A family that set out determined to hold its own members together finishes by caring for someone who has no kinship claim on it.

There is a story about an Oklahoma family crossing a continent in search of work, and another story about what happens when the conditions for one family's survival cannot be separated from the conditions of everyone else's. Steinbeck tells both. The journey is over, but the moral and political questions are not. To examine them, we need to listen to the parts of the novel that look beyond the truck and ask why a land of full orchards could contain so many hungry people.
