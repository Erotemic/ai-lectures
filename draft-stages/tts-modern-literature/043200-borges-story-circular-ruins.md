# Ficciones: The Circular Ruins — The Dreamer Who Is Dreamed

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A man arrives by water at an abandoned circular temple. He has barely any past that matters to him. His intention is clear and immense: he wants to dream another person into existence. Not imagine a character in the familiar sense, and not merely see a face in sleep. He wants a being who can walk and be mistaken for flesh and blood.

The temple is ruined, remote, and associated with an old divinity. Local people bring the stranger food. He spends his days sleeping and trying to control his dreams. The first dreams take the form of a classroom. He lectures to a multitude of students, searching for someone sufficiently independent to become the center of his work. He favors those who answer with intelligence rather than merely repeating the teacher's thoughts. At last he chooses one figure, dismisses the rest, and tries to make the selected dreamer grow into a complete person.

The effort fails. A period of insomnia ruins his project. He must begin again, this time working slowly and physically. He dreams a heart, then the rest of a body, attending to detail until the young man seems fully composed. Yet the body cannot quite be awakened by the dreamer's power alone. In a dream the old deity of the ruined temple appears and identifies himself with fire. The god promises to animate the created person so that the people of the world will take him for a real man. Only the fire god and the creator will know the secret.

There is a condition: the new human being must be instructed in the god's rites and eventually sent to a distant temple. The dreamer obeys. For years he teaches the youth, privately grieving the separation he must one day arrange. Before sending him away he removes his awareness of the origin of his life. This looks at first like an act of kindness. To discover that one's father had made one in a dream might be a terrible humiliation. But the erasure also gives the creator control over what his creation can know.

Time passes. The man is told of a remarkable person at another temple, someone who can walk through flames without injury. He understands that the report concerns his imagined son. He is afraid that this immunity will reveal the truth. The son could learn not merely that he is protected by fire but that his place in reality differs from everyone else's. The secret the creator most fears is the one he has built his entire life around keeping.

Then fire reaches the creator's own ruins. He expects destruction. Instead, the flames leave him untouched. He understands, in a moment of relief and terror, that he too exists as another being's dream. The revelation reverses the relationship that organized the story. His power over a dream was real within his experience, but his understanding of its source was incomplete. What he thought made him different from his son has proven to be a difference of position, not of kind.

The ending offers a temptation: imagine an infinite chain of dreamers, one created by another without a final beginning. But the story itself need not prove that the regress is infinite. It tells us the creator has discovered a predecessor and leaves the rest open. Borges makes an ontological problem personal. To know oneself as made is not only a logical embarrassment. It changes the meaning of memory, pride, the wish to protect another, and the fear of being exposed.

There is also a literary mirror. Every fictional person exists in a story imagined by someone else. But this observation alone does not exhaust the plot. If we treat the young man's distress as an empty joke because he is fictional, we would have to treat the dreamer's distress in the same way, and perhaps our own feelings for them. The story's force depends on our granting a character enough reality to care whether his reality can be taken away.

The circular shape becomes apt at last: a maker and a made being turn out to occupy the same structure. A god who can dream a person is also a person in someone else's dream. Borges makes the final sentence change the meaning of everything preceding it, including the reader's confidence in the voice that told the story.
