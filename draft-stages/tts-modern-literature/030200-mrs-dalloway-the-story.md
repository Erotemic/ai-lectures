# Mrs Dalloway — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On a June morning in London, Clarissa Dalloway decides to buy the flowers for her party herself. The errand is small. Her household is already busy preparing for the evening, and someone else could perfectly well buy flowers. But Clarissa wants to go into the city. She steps out into the morning and feels the air, the traffic, the movement of people, and the extraordinary fact that a familiar place is alive again after a devastating war.

It is 1923. Clarissa is in her early fifties. Her husband, Richard, is a Member of Parliament. They have a daughter, Elizabeth, nearly grown. Clarissa knows how to arrange a room, put guests at ease, and draw people together. She also knows that being a capable hostess has given other people a convenient way to define her. On this day, while she organizes a public gathering, her mind moves through years of private life that no guest will be able to see.

The novel takes place over the course of a single day, but it does not feel like a story confined to twenty-four hours. A passing stranger can recall a friendship from childhood. A sound in a street can lead into a memory of the war. One person's gaze can draw the account into another person's consciousness. The story repeatedly follows these connections. To hear it clearly, we can trace the day's events while remembering that its most important movements often happen in someone's mind.

## The morning in the streets

Clarissa walks through Westminster and toward the flower shop. She meets Hugh Whitbread, an old acquaintance whose good manners and standing in society are nearly a profession. He has come to London with his wife, who has been ill. Clarissa enjoys seeing him, although other friends have long found Hugh rather self-satisfied. She notices a motorcar, its blinds drawn, that causes bystanders to speculate about the important person inside. Somewhere above the city, an airplane writes an advertisement in smoke. The same sights gather together people who do not know one another and do not interpret what they see in the same way.

Clarissa's thoughts return to Bourton, the country house where she spent summers as a young woman. There she had friends who seemed capable of making life more intense than the respectable adulthood expected of them. Peter Walsh was passionate, clever, excitable, and determined to argue with her. He wanted to marry her. She refused him and eventually chose Richard, who offered a gentler steadiness and more room to be alone. The decision has been made for decades, yet she still tests it in memory. She can think Peter absurd and still wonder about the life she did not choose.

Then there was Sally Seton, unpredictable and daring. Sally questioned conventions that Clarissa had been taught not even to notice. Clarissa remembers the extraordinary intensity of her feeling for Sally, and especially a kiss they shared at Bourton. The memory is not a minor decorative detail of their youth. For Clarissa it remains one of the moments when being alive felt most complete. It does not fit easily into the social vocabulary by which people now know Mrs Dalloway.

When Clarissa returns home, she learns that Lady Bruton has invited Richard to lunch but has not invited her. She is more hurt than she wishes to admit. The house is full of preparations, but its mistress feels at moments almost unnecessary. She goes to her room, where she sometimes sleeps alone because she has been unwell, and begins mending the green dress she will wear that evening. She thinks about mortality, her own body's fragility, and the distance that can remain between people who have lived together for years.

## Peter returns

Without warning, Peter Walsh arrives. He has returned from India. The two old friends face each other in Clarissa's sitting room with a mixture of affection, irritation, and the embarrassment of realizing how much has remained unresolved. Peter plays with his pocketknife, a familiar habit. Clarissa sees how little his restless manner has changed. He sees the dignified wife of a politician and wonders whether she has settled for a life too narrow for her.

Peter explains that he is in love with a married woman in India and hopes to marry her. His account is passionate and somewhat impractical. He and Clarissa talk as if they have years to catch up on and no time to do it. He nearly breaks down. He asks whether she is happy. Before the conversation can become fully honest, Elizabeth comes in. The visit ends. Clarissa calls after Peter, reminding him of the party. The past has entered the house for a few minutes, and nothing in the house is quite as settled as it appeared.

Peter walks through London, judging Clarissa, defending himself against her imagined judgment, and remembering Bourton. He recalls the humiliation of realizing that she preferred Richard, the anguish of their final quarrel, and the certainty he once felt that she was wasting her gifts on conventional society. Yet as he wanders, his observations also reveal how hungry he is for drama in his own life. He watches people he will never meet and invents stories about them. He dreams while resting in Regent's Park. Even when he is thinking about someone else, the person most exposed is often Peter himself.

## Another marriage, another London

In the park another couple is struggling through the same morning. Septimus Warren Smith is a young veteran of the First World War. His wife, Lucrezia, whom he calls Rezia, is Italian. Before the war Septimus worked in an office and loved poetry. He volunteered for service believing in an ideal of England made of literature, patriotism, and noble feeling. During the fighting he became close to an officer named Evans. Evans was killed near the end of the war. Septimus had been able to carry on when his friend died, but afterward his world seemed to lose its capacity to mean anything.

Now ordinary sights can become unbearable or terrifying. Septimus hears and sees things other people do not. He believes Evans may be present. He detects immense messages in trees, birds, and the shifting air. At other times he feels utterly empty, without even the grief that would prove to him that he remains human. His thoughts move rapidly from the magnificent to the dreadful. He is afraid that he has committed some moral crime by failing to feel what he believes he ought to feel.

Rezia sees the suffering without being able to enter it. She misses Italy, the work and company of her old life, and the cheerful husband she thought she married. London can seem a city of strangers who notice her husband's distress only to judge it. She must watch over him and interpret his behavior to doctors while having nowhere to set down her own fear. Her marriage is profoundly lonely even as she sits beside him.

Dr Holmes, a general practitioner, has advised Septimus to take an interest in ordinary things. His reassurance that nothing is seriously wrong does not help. The couple is referred to Sir William Bradshaw, a prestigious specialist. Bradshaw listens, pronounces the condition serious, and proposes separation from Rezia and a period in one of his country establishments. To Bradshaw this is a rational program of treatment. To Rezia it feels like the loss of her husband to strangers. To Septimus, who already fears that his thoughts are no longer his own, it threatens the last refuge of his personhood.

The novel does not ask us to believe Septimus's visions are public facts. It lets us know what they are like from within his experience, while also showing how terrifying and isolating they are for him and for Rezia. The contrast with Clarissa's day matters. London's clocks strike for both households. Cars, parks, and passersby belong to one city. Yet the safety that one person assumes may be unimaginable to someone standing almost beside them.

## The people Clarissa loves, and the people she cannot reach

While these encounters unfold, Richard lunches with Hugh Whitbread and Lady Bruton. Lady Bruton is an imposing member of their circle, preoccupied with public causes. She needs help writing to a newspaper. Her interest in imperial schemes and public campaigns sits comfortably in a society that has not entirely reckoned with the sacrifices demanded by its institutions. Richard helps her, enjoys the occasion, and then thinks warmly of his wife.

On his way home he buys Clarissa roses. He means to say plainly that he loves her, but when he gives them to her the words will not come. Their conversation is affectionate and ordinary. It is also full of what each knows and cannot quite express. Clarissa appreciates his kindness. She does not wish she had married Peter. Nonetheless there are parts of her life that even Richard cannot enter, just as she cannot completely know what moves inside him.

Elizabeth is approaching a life of her own. Her tutor and history teacher, Miss Doris Kilman, has become important to her. Miss Kilman is poor by Clarissa's standards, embittered by exclusions she believes she has suffered, and intensely religious. She finds Clarissa frivolous and privileged. Clarissa, for her part, resents Miss Kilman's possessive attention to Elizabeth and the moral pressure she feels in her presence. The two women see defects in each other and sometimes see real ones. Neither sees the whole person. Elizabeth goes shopping with Miss Kilman, then escapes their uncomfortable outing and rides through London on a bus. She considers what kind of work she might one day do. Her future seems more open than the paths that shaped her mother's generation.

Clarissa remains at home while the city moves around her. The party is getting closer. She is absorbed in seating, flowers, guests, and the fear that the gathering may fail. But there is nothing trivial about the feeling that a room full of people might, for an evening, become something more than separate lives moving past one another.

## A hat, a visit, a window

Back in the Smiths' rooms, the afternoon briefly becomes gentler. Rezia is working on a hat, and Septimus joins her. Together they alter it, laugh, and experience a few minutes of companionship that seem to promise a return to ordinary marriage. The episode is precious because the reader has been shown how hard such moments have become. Septimus is not only an emblem of war trauma, and Rezia is not only an attendant to it. They are two people who know how to make something together.

Then word comes that Dr Holmes is arriving. Septimus understands that the doctor's visit may lead to his being taken away. His response is driven by terror and a conviction that the doctors intend to seize his life from him. He considers the objects around him and the ways he might escape. When Holmes forces his way toward him, Septimus jumps from a window and dies on the railings below.

The event is terrible for Rezia, whose attempts to keep him safe have not saved him. For the doctors, it can be fitted into their explanation of his illness. For passersby and strangers, it is an ambulance in a city already moving on. Peter Walsh hears the ambulance and reflects on the efficiency of London. He does not know the person inside, or that the death he is almost casually passing will enter Clarissa's party later that night.

## The party

By evening, guests begin arriving at the Dalloways' house. Clarissa receives them, moves between conversations, and worries over the success of the evening. Public life appears at every turn. Important people come, including the Prime Minister for a short visit. Guests notice who has arrived, who has changed, who is still attractive, and who has become respectable. There is vanity and stiffness, but also pleasure at seeing old faces. Clarissa's talent for hospitality is real. She wants to make a gathering in which people who ordinarily remain apart can briefly occupy the same space.

Peter comes. He still judges her and is still drawn to her. Sally Seton arrives too, now a married woman with children, a settled life, and a different manner from the girl Clarissa loved. Meeting again lets the old friends test how much of a younger self survives in the adult one. Their reunion does not restore Bourton. It does not need to. A past life can remain meaningful without returning unchanged.

Sir William Bradshaw and his wife arrive late. In conversation, Lady Bradshaw mentions that a young man, one of the doctor's patients, has killed himself. Clarissa is disturbed. It seems an intrusion of death into the social occasion she has worked so hard to create. She goes away from the guests to be alone.

She has never met Septimus. She knows almost nothing about him. But she imagines the dreadful final act, and she feels an affinity she cannot explain to anyone at the party. She has feared that living might sometimes become unbearable; she also loves life's fleeting, extravagant sensations. Septimus's death makes her think of people whose authority can crowd another life until there is no room left. She senses a kind of refusal in his act, though that is her interpretation and not a reliable account of what he intended. The scene does not make her an expert on the dead man. It shows how a stranger's death can shock a comfortable person into seeing the limits of her own comfort.

Through a window she notices an old woman in a nearby room, going about her own private evening. The separation between the women does not prevent Clarissa from recognizing that another life is continuing alongside hers, unseen. She remains with her thoughts for a little while. Then she chooses to return to her guests.

The book closes not with a speech Clarissa makes, and not with a resolution of her doubts, but with her reappearance. Peter, who has spent so much of the day questioning her choices, feels an overwhelming surge of emotion when he sees her. Nothing spectacular has happened in the room. The hostess has come back.

A single day's movement has placed a marriage, a war, a death, several past loves, a daughter's future, and the demands of a city into contact. Clarissa's party has neither cured suffering nor justified the divisions of English society. Septimus will not return. Rezia's grief remains. Yet the novel ends with a person rejoining the imperfect assembly she has made. The story has shown us how many distances lie between people. It leaves us with the possibility that trying to cross some of those distances is itself a meaningful act.

## Reader's companion: orientation and limits

Original explanatory narration of Virginia Woolf's *Mrs Dalloway* (1925), whose London action takes place over one June day in 1923. The story moves among consciousnesses; the nearly chronological orientation above does not imply Woolf tells it in linear, single-viewpoint form. Clarissa and Septimus do **not** meet. Clarissa's reflections about his death are her own conjecture, not an authoritative report of his intentions. No words from the copyrighted screen adaptations are used.
