# To Live — The Story

One continuous, spoiler-containing account of Yu Hua's novel *To Live* (*Huozhe*), first published as a book in 1993. It follows the novel rather than Zhang Yimou's substantially different 1994 film.

## An old man calls to his ox

A young man traveling through the Chinese countryside is collecting folk songs and the stories of the people he meets. His work takes him through fields and villages, among people who have lived through decades he mostly knows as history. One day he comes upon an old farmer plowing with an ox. The farmer talks to the animal as though it were an old friend. He even calls out the names of several animals, although there is only one ox in front of him. The collector is curious. The old man seems pleased to have someone willing to listen.

His name is Xu Fugui. The ox is also called Fugui. If we met him only at this moment, we might assume that he had always been poor, that his life had followed the land without interruption, and that he had nothing very dramatic to tell. But when Fugui begins speaking, the quiet field opens into a life of wealth squandered, wars endured, political changes survived, and family members lost. The old man is not telling a tale because he knows the moral that will explain everything. He likes remembering the people with whom he lived. The stories bring them back into the afternoon in which he is speaking.

Fugui was not born poor. His family owned land and a substantial house. His father expected him to inherit the responsibility that came with their position. He had a wife, Jiazhen, and a daughter, Fengxia. But young Fugui spent his days in gambling houses and brothels, taking pleasure in humiliating the people closest to him and acting as though the family's resources were bottomless. Jiazhen was patient, but her patience was not rewarded with gratitude. She could go to the gambling house and plead with him to return home, and he might respond by embarrassing her in front of strangers. He wanted the freedoms of a rich man's son without the obligations of a husband or father.

The habit that finally destroyed his old life was gambling. He played against a man named Long Er and lost, then lost again, until the wagers consumed the family's land and house. This was not a sudden disaster imposed by distant history. Fugui made it happen. He had the chance to stop. He did not. His father's anger was terrible because the debt represented more than the loss of comfort. A family inheritance, built across generations and tied to the standing of the household, had passed into someone else's hands. Fugui's father died soon afterward, and the son had to live with his own part in the ruin.

Jiazhen's father, who belonged to a wealthier and more respectable household, took her away for a time. She had every reason not to trust Fugui with her future. But she eventually returned, bringing their young son Youqing into the family. Fugui, now a poor man, was forced to experience life from a position he had barely considered while spending money he had not earned. There was work to do and insufficient food. He began to learn the cost of things that had previously been invisible to him.

There is an irony that will grow more painful as the decades pass. Long Er, who has taken possession of the land Fugui gambled away, now holds the status of a landlord. At the time, Fugui thinks of this chiefly as his own humiliation. He cannot know how the political meaning of that ownership will change.

## A conscription that breaks the calendar

When Fugui goes out to obtain medicine for his sick mother, he is caught up in the civil war. Soldiers of the Nationalist army force him into service. He has no effective way to return home or send reliable news to his family. A journey that should have been brief becomes years of separation. He is no longer the young man who can gamble away a fortune. He is a conscript trying to remain alive in a war whose strategic purposes are far beyond his control.

He meets other soldiers, including a man called Chunsheng. The army moves, waits, freezes, suffers, and fights. There are stretches in which the men are more concerned about hunger and cold than grand political principles. They do not have the information that commanders possess. They do not know which town will fall or when their families might hear from them again. Companionship forms under pressure because men who may die tomorrow still need to talk, complain, and help one another through the day.

Eventually Fugui is captured as the Communist forces defeat the Nationalists. He is released and makes his way home. The country has changed. So has his family. His mother died while he was away. Jiazhen has kept going without knowing whether he was alive. Fengxia, once a lively little girl, had a serious fever that left her deaf and unable to speak. Fugui had not been there to protect her or comfort her. The loss is not only the disability itself; it is the years of family life he can never recover. He has missed changes that cannot be retold to him with the completeness of living through them.

His return is still a gift. Jiazhen and the children have a father and husband again. Fugui has learned that the world does not owe him the pleasures he once demanded. He is capable of tenderness that would have embarrassed his younger self. He works the fields and begins to find meaning in ordinary responsibilities. The family's poverty remains severe, but for a while there is a home in which the people who love one another are present together.

The new government's land reforms transform the countryside. The landlord class becomes the target of political campaigns, and Long Er is condemned and executed. Fugui hears what happened and understands the terrible reversal. If he had not lost the family property through his own recklessness, he might have been classified in the same way. What he had understood as his life's great disgrace may have helped keep him alive. The conclusion is hard to accept because it does not turn gambling into a good act or make the execution just. It shows that the meaning of an event can change when public power changes around it. The same land that had been a symbol of inheritance becomes grounds for danger.

## The hard arithmetic of hunger

Fugui's household lives through the collectivization of agriculture and the campaigns that promise a transformed country. Village life is reorganized. New demands are placed on labor and production; communal expectations reach into ordinary meals and work. People are asked to produce more than their fields can reliably supply. The Great Leap Forward's inflated ambitions and disastrous policies contribute to severe shortages. Across the countryside, hunger becomes the condition under which every decision must be made.

In a family already living close to the edge, food cannot be treated as a small domestic matter. A child can be hungry and still have to work. A parent can know that a meal is insufficient and still divide it as carefully as possible. People negotiate, conceal, share, quarrel, and sometimes become cruel under pressures that are not of their own making. Fugui tries to care for the family while also facing the limits of what one household can control.

Jiazhen grows ill. Her strength begins to fail, yet she remains one of the main sources of steadiness in the family. The change in Fugui becomes visible in the way he sees her. The younger husband treated her love as an entitlement. The older man understands that she has carried burdens he could not or would not carry. When she is too weak to work as before, the family must find ways to continue without turning her into another person whose suffering can be ignored.

Youqing, their son, is energetic, caring, and sometimes difficult for Fugui to understand. He has his own concerns and loyalties. He loves the family's animals and responds to small injustices with an intensity his father can mistake for childishness. Fugui may discipline him harshly and regret it. Their relationship is full of the unfinished work of a father learning to love a child as a separate person rather than as someone who must obey him.

Then a crisis at the hospital destroys the family. The wife of an important local official needs blood during an emergency. Youqing is brought in as a donor because his blood type matches. The adults responsible for taking the blood draw too much. He dies. The event is grotesque in its avoidability. A child's life is treated as available for a person with higher status, and the system charged with protecting patients fails him.

The official whose wife was being treated turns out to be Chunsheng, Fugui's former companion from the civil war. Recognition brings no comfort. Fugui remembers the man who once struggled beside him to survive. Now the same man is connected to the death of his son through a system that gives official lives priority. The friendship of soldiers and the hierarchy of civil authority cannot be made to fit together. Fugui and Jiazhen have to confront a person whose private grief and public position are entangled with their own loss.

There is no explanation that can turn Youqing's death into a necessary payment for national progress. Fugui buries his son. The family continues, but it is permanently changed. Jiazhen's grief is not a small variation on her husband's. She has loved a child who is no longer there, and the fact that Fugui is now more tender cannot rescue her from that absence.

## Fengxia makes a life

Fengxia has grown up with communication limited by the fever she suffered during her father's absence. The family worries about how she will be treated and whether she can find a secure life in a society that often dismisses people with disabilities. She is not merely an embodiment of sorrow. She is affectionate, perceptive, and capable of forming relationships on her own terms.

A man called Wan Erxi comes into her life. He is a construction worker, not a figure of high prestige. He is also kind, steady, and sincerely devoted to Fengxia. Their courtship offers the family something that has been in short supply: the possibility that an ordinary future might unfold without catastrophe. They marry. Erxi's care is expressed through work and practical attention rather than grand language. Fugui and Jiazhen can see that their daughter is cherished.

For a while there is a new household within the larger family story. Fengxia is pregnant. Fugui watches the younger couple and sees a kind of happiness he once treated carelessly. There is nothing naive about the family's joy. They already know how much can be lost. The joy is a decision to welcome what is present anyway.

The Cultural Revolution transforms official and communal life. Political accusations multiply. People who had power can suddenly be condemned. Chunsheng, once an official, becomes a target of persecution. The changed conditions do not undo his connection to Youqing's death. Nor do they make his own suffering imaginary. He turns to the family he has injured. Jiazhen, who has endured terrible loss, is able to tell him that his life still matters. Her response does not erase her son's death. It is a refusal to make another death the means of settling it. Chunsheng is eventually driven to suicide. The family survives another turning of the political wheel without being allowed to stand outside its consequences.

When Fengxia goes into labor, hope becomes fear. There are complications and a severe hemorrhage. In the revolutionary atmosphere, medical authority and training have been disrupted, and the care available to her fails. Her son is born, but Fengxia dies. The child will be called Kugen. The name evokes a bitter root, a life beginning inside grief.

The birth and death occur together. Erxi is now a father without his wife. Fugui and Jiazhen have a grandson and have lost their daughter. A happy future has arrived in a form nobody wanted. The family takes the infant into its circle, because the child is real and needs care. This does not mean that Fengxia's death has been compensated. Kugen is not a replacement for his mother. He is another person to love.

## The narrowing household

Jiazhen's illness continues, and eventually she dies. Her death is different from the sudden catastrophe at the hospital. It comes after years of shared endurance and after Fugui has finally become the husband she once had reason to distrust. Their marriage began with humiliation and inequality. It ends with a knowledge of one another that could not have been achieved by pretending the earlier cruelty never happened.

Erxi continues raising Kugen with Fugui's help. He works in construction, where the need to earn a living places him among risks that a family with more resources might more easily avoid. He is killed in an accident involving heavy concrete. Once again an ordinary activity becomes the means by which the family loses someone it loves. The tragedy does not come from a villain with a single clear motive. Work, machinery, chance, and vulnerability combine in an event whose consequences are final.

Fugui is now responsible for his grandson. Kugen is a child who wants food, play, and affection. The old man cares for him in the best way he knows. He has little to offer materially, but he knows the value of staying. He has become someone who understands a child's needs because he has lost so many opportunities to answer them.

Yet even this companionship ends. Kugen dies after eating beans that Fugui has given him. The particular circumstance is almost unbearable because the food was an act of care, not an intended danger. For Fugui there is no political explanation, no identifiable enemy, and no reversal that could bring the boy back. The last child in his care is gone.

At this point a listener might expect the novel to close with Fugui's death. He has lost the people who once made his household, one by one. It would make a conventional tragic pattern: the man who squandered his family fortune begins to love, suffers the consequences of history and chance, and finally falls. But Yu Hua refuses to end there.

Fugui is still alive. He continues working. He grows older. He saves an ox from slaughter and gives it his own name. The two move through the fields together. He speaks to it, sometimes speaking as if all the lives that have passed through his memory are still near. What has been lost is not restored, and the novel does not pretend that a farm animal replaces a wife, children, and grandson. Yet the animal is living, depends on him, and shares his days. Fugui can still care for something. He can still tell a story to a stranger.

## The man who keeps talking

The folk-song collector has now heard the life behind the old man in the field. The world he encountered as a picturesque rural scene has widened to contain landlord wealth, the civil war, land reform, famine, the Cultural Revolution, and the deaths of an entire family. But those large historical names were not the way Fugui first introduced his life. He began with people, with the things they did, and with the particular pleasure of remembering them aloud.

We should not rush to supply a moral that the story declines to settle. Fugui's early gambling was destructive. Some later losses arose from political decisions, institutional failures, or dangerous work. Some were bound to illness and chance. It would be wrong to flatten them into a single lesson about good conduct, or to declare that everyone who suffers has somehow earned what happens. Fugui changes morally, but improvement does not make him safe from tragedy. History changes politically, but each new order can generate its own forms of danger.

The ending is not a triumphal promise that the future will be better. It is an old man and an old ox moving across a field, with day still remaining. The title asks us to consider living as an action rather than a reward. Fugui does not survive because he can explain each death. He survives, and in surviving he carries those lives in language. When the young collector leaves, the old man's story does not become less true because its teller remains among the ordinary sounds of the countryside.

That is the full movement of Yu Hua's *To Live*: a man loses his inheritance through his own choices, is swept into war, returns to a family damaged in his absence, learns to love them with care, loses them through intertwined histories of politics and misfortune, and finally continues to work and speak beside an animal he has rescued. To hear the ending is to understand why the book's title is not simply another word for happiness. It names what remains when explanations and rewards have ceased to be dependable.

## Source and listening note (not spoken)

The Chinese novel is distinct from Zhang Yimou's 1994 film. In particular, the novel's Youqing dies after an excessive blood draw, and the novel's Fugui survives all his family members; the film changes these outcomes. The story's original frame is a folk-song collector listening to an elderly farmer. This is original retelling prose, not an English translation of Yu Hua's text.
