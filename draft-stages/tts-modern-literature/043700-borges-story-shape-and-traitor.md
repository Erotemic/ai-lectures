# Ficciones: The Shape of the Sword and The Traitor and the Hero — Two Manufactured Histories

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two Borges stories use an old political injury to expose the arrangements by which a person or a nation comes to possess an official story. They are set around Irish independence, but neither is a straightforward historical account of that movement. One is a confession hidden in a false perspective. The other is a patriotic biography hiding a staged execution.

In The Shape of the Sword, a narrator encounters a man in South America who bears a conspicuous scar across his face. The man, known by an English identity, tells of his involvement in the Irish struggle. In the story he presents, he once sheltered a younger revolutionary named John Vincent Moon. Moon was educated and spoke with convictions, but he was frightened by physical danger. The older fighter saved him during an encounter with British troops and took him to safety.

As the fighting intensified, the fighter continued to act while Moon found reasons to remain hidden. The older man grew contemptuous. One day he overheard Moon betraying him to the authorities in exchange for money. Furious, he chased Moon through the house. In a violent confrontation he marked the traitor's face with a sword. We seem to know, at last, why the storyteller associates the scar with betrayal.

But the listener notices a missing conclusion. What happened to Moon? The scarred man points to his own face and admits that he is Moon. He has told the history from the position of the man whom he betrayed. The image of a courageous fighter was a disguise that allowed him to approach his shame without naming himself. We must now revise every earlier judgment, not simply add a surprise ending. A witness's words may have been selected by a person whose relation to the event differs from the one we imagined.

Theme of the Traitor and the Hero begins with another writer, Ryan, preparing a biography of his ancestor Fergus Kilpatrick, an Irish revolutionary whose death became a national legend. The story is openly presented as a possible plot; its premise involves Ireland and an imagined nineteenth-century conspiracy, not an independently documented historical murder. Ryan is troubled when details of the assassination resemble scenes from Shakespeare. A speech or circumstance seems to have been borrowed from literature rather than recorded from spontaneous life.

Research leads Ryan toward the organizer of Kilpatrick's movement, James Alexander Nolan. He discovers that Kilpatrick had been exposed as an informer. To reveal the betrayal openly might have destroyed a revolutionary cause. Nolan arranged a theatrical killing in which the compromised leader would die as a publicly mourned martyr. The city itself became part of the stage. The speeches, portents, and memorable gestures were planted in advance so that later generations would remember not the betrayal but a heroic sacrifice.

The historian faces a choice. He can publish what his inquiry suggests, or he can preserve the history his community is prepared to honor. Borges has Ryan withhold the discovery and write the reverent account. The reader is left to ask whether Nolan foresaw even this future: a historian who would uncover the truth and consent to conceal it. The project has turned the acts of one generation into a script for the memory of another.

These are different forms of misrepresentation. Moon's account is a wounded individual's evasion of his own responsibility. The revolutionary myth is a collective project, designed to make a death productive for a political cause. In both cases the telling matters as much as the event. We learn what a narrator is after discovering how he has made us assign the roles of hero and traitor.

There is a danger in praising such tricks only as brilliant construction. In the first story a man was actually betrayed. In the second a community has surrendered evidence for solidarity. A carefully made narrative may be an artistic achievement while remaining morally costly to the people who have to live under it.

Borges's lesson is not that all heroes are traitors or that all history is propaganda. It is that names like hero and traitor depend on whose account survives, and that a historian may discover a deliberate literary design inside what everyone else accepts as a national memory. A story about the past can be true in its facts and manipulated in its meaning; it can also become popular because the manipulation is exactly what its audience wants.
