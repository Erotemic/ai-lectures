# Kokoro — The Story

This is a complete plot narration of Natsume Sōseki's *Kokoro*, including its ending. It precedes the interpretive lecture at `010000`. Spellings and some forms of address differ among English translations.

## The man on the beach

A young university student has gone to Kamakura for the summer. He is away from Tokyo, away from lectures and examinations, with time to swim and watch the people around him. On the beach he notices an older Japanese man in the company of a foreign visitor. The older man draws his attention for reasons he cannot quite explain. Later, when he sees the man alone, he approaches him. The beginning of a friendship is almost embarrassingly slight: a curious young man decides that a stranger may know something worth knowing.

We never learn the young man's name. The older man becomes known to us by the name the student gives him: Sensei, a word meaning teacher or master. He is not, so far as the student can tell, presently teaching anyone. He is educated and financially comfortable enough not to work for his living, but he seems to have withdrawn from the ordinary ambitions of educated men. He does not seek students, fame, or a career. His politeness does not quite conceal an aversion to being known.

The student continues the acquaintance when both have returned to Tokyo. There are evenings at Sensei's house, conversations that begin and stop without reaching the thing the student most wants to understand. Sensei lives with his wife, Shizu. They are affectionate in ways that appear real, yet there is a silence between them which the visitor cannot explain. Shizu is warm to their young guest, and he comes to see the couple as a possible picture of adult life. Why does a man with an apparently kind wife and no material urgency seem so unhappy?

One detail begins to bother him. Sensei regularly visits a particular grave in Zōshigaya Cemetery. He does not tell the student whose grave it is. Questions about the visits produce evasions rather than answers. There are remarks about loneliness, love, and the danger of trusting people. Some of what Sensei says sounds like advice. Some sounds like an accusation directed at himself. The student thinks he is being offered fragments of a philosophy, when in fact he is hearing fragments of an event.

The student is young enough to believe that a mentor's reserve might conceal deeper wisdom. Sensei seems to understand something essential about the world, and his refusal to describe it makes it more compelling. Yet the older man's warnings are not abstract observations made from a safe distance. There is a specific human cost behind them. The person he visits in the cemetery is not an ancestor whose loss he has merely failed to recover from. It is a friend whose death is bound to Sensei's own decisions.

## A household with a secret

Shizu has her own incomplete account of her husband's past. She knows that he became darker and more withdrawn after the death of a friend. She does not know why the death altered him so deeply. The student sometimes wonders whether Sensei's unhappiness could involve his marriage. He also discovers that his questions can embarrass Shizu, who cannot answer them and may fear the answer herself.

When the student and Sensei talk privately, Sensei does not simply advise him to avoid making mistakes. He suggests that selfishness can hide inside conduct that looks respectable. A person may sincerely believe himself decent and still injure another person when something he wants is threatened. These assertions are severe, and the student initially hears them as general judgments about human nature. He asks for the story that would make them intelligible. Sensei occasionally suggests that he might someday give it, but not yet.

It matters that the student keeps coming back. Sensei has almost given up the expectation of being understood. This young man's attachment is inconvenient, but it is also the possibility of an audience for a truth that has remained unspoken. There are hints that Sensei recognizes himself in the student, or wishes the student to avoid becoming like him. The relationship is not a simple exchange between a young ignorant person and an older knowledgeable person. One has a future still open before him. The other is trapped in the consequences of a past decision. Each is drawn toward what the other lacks.

The student does not know that Sensei's reserve has been maintained for many years. What appears at first as a settled temperament is the long aftermath of a crisis. Sensei lives in a house in which his wife has not been told the central fact that organizes his days. She may feel that her kindness ought to have rescued him. Instead, she is asked to live beside an injury she cannot see. Their marriage is neither simply false nor wholly transparent. The man who loves Shizu also fears that revealing his history would destroy her trust and happiness. His silence is presented as protection, though it is also a way of avoiding the consequences of speaking.

## Home, illness, and another obligation

The student completes his studies and returns to his family in the countryside. His parents have very different expectations of adulthood. A university education, in their understanding, should lead to a useful position and a respectable career. They are proud of their son. They do not fully understand his attachment to a man who seems to do nothing, and they have little patience with an intellectual life that refuses the usual signs of success.

His father is ill. The condition grows serious enough that daily household life becomes organized around the possibility of his death. The student's mother, brother, and other relatives discuss doctors, expectations, and practical responsibilities. The son discovers that a death anticipated in the family is not the same as a death imagined privately. People enter and leave rooms, make plans, grow tired, speak of employment, hold to routines. Illness pulls a whole household into a different experience of time.

At the same time, a larger public transition takes place. Emperor Meiji dies in 1912. Newspapers report national mourning. The emperor had ruled during an era of immense political, social, and cultural transformation in Japan. In the student's family, concern for the sovereign exists alongside concern for a much more immediate dying man. The father asks about events in the country; his own condition gradually occupies more of everyone's attention.

The student corresponds with Sensei. He wants advice about his future, perhaps help finding a position. But their letters do not settle his questions. At a crucial moment he receives a message from Tokyo urging him to come. He cannot easily leave his father's bedside. He replies that he is unable to travel. The tension might have remained ordinary—an older friend demands attention while a family needs its son—but soon a much larger letter arrives.

It is not a routine letter. It is long enough to resemble a manuscript. The student begins to look through it and discovers that Sensei is finally going to tell the story he had promised. He also discovers that Sensei intends to die. The letter is not an invitation to discuss the past when convenient. It is a testament that may reach its reader too late for any reply.

The student leaves the sickroom. He boards a train toward Tokyo, torn between a father whose death is near and a mentor who appears to have chosen his own. He reads as he travels. The final movement of the novel is the content of that letter. We never return to a conventional present-tense scene in which the student reaches Sensei's door and tells us what happens next. We enter the older man's account of his life, and the account becomes the ending.

## The inheritance

When Sensei was a young man, both his parents died. He was left with property and money, and an uncle took responsibility for helping him manage his affairs. At first the uncle seemed a natural figure to trust. Over time, however, Sensei discovered that the man had taken advantage of him and depleted a substantial part of the inheritance. Family ties and polite appearances had concealed a financial betrayal.

The loss altered Sensei's understanding of other people. He became suspicious of motives that could not be examined directly. He learned how easily a trusted person could explain away an action that benefited himself at someone else's expense. The experience also produced a dangerous simplification: if he had been deceived by his uncle, perhaps every apparent kindness concealed self-interest. He removed himself from the arrangement, recovered what he could, and continued his education in Tokyo with resentment he did not know how to discharge.

Eventually he found lodgings with a widow and her daughter. The mother is often referred to as Okusan, a form of address. The daughter, whom the narrative calls Ojosan and who will later be known as Shizu, brought warmth to the household. Sensei had begun to distrust ordinary arrangements of family life. In this house he slowly discovered pleasure in conversation, domestic routine, and being welcome. He grew attached to the daughter. His attachment remained difficult for him to admit, even to himself.

He wanted to marry her. He was also convinced, partly because of the uncle's deception, that announcing a desire left a person vulnerable. The widow might have understood his intentions, but Sensei approached every possible sign as if a trap could be hidden beneath it. Love was becoming another place where he expected a rival or a dishonest bargain.

## K enters the house

Sensei had a friend, a fellow student called K. K lived under the pressure of unusually severe ideals. He believed in disciplining himself, subordinating ordinary desire to spiritual and intellectual aspiration, and enduring hardship rather than pursuing an easy life. His family circumstances were complicated; he had disappointed the people who hoped his education would lead him along a more conventional path. He lived austerely and suffered for it.

Sensei wanted to help. He brought K into the widow's house, reasoning that companionship and a stable home might restore him. The decision seems generous. It also places the person Sensei trusts most in daily contact with the young woman he loves. At first he does not understand the danger. K is so disciplined, so intent on higher purposes, that Sensei assumes romantic feeling will not concern him. The assumption reveals how little Sensei understands his friend's inner life.

The two men share meals, go about their studies, and move through the same rooms. As K begins to recover, his manner toward the daughter changes. There are conversations and signs of comfort that Sensei begins watching with growing alarm. He has never clearly declared his own intentions. He therefore has no claim K would recognize, and he resents the fact that his position is invisible. The jealousy is heightened by shame: he wants to think of himself as an honorable friend, but he is afraid of losing something he has not yet asked for.

Then K confides in him. He has fallen in love with the young woman. K regards this feeling as a failure of the discipline he has tried to practice. He is not boasting of a conquest. He is asking a friend to listen to a private conflict. Sensei has a chance to respond honestly. He could reveal that he loves her too. He could acknowledge that neither man knows what she wants. He could ask for time. Instead, he conceals his own position.

He speaks to K in the language of the ideals K holds against himself. He reminds K of the path of spiritual effort that has been so important to him and implies that surrendering to passion would betray it. Sensei knows how much those ideals mean to his friend. He uses their weight precisely because he wants K to hesitate. What sounds like a moral argument is also a tactical move in a rivalry Sensei has refused to admit exists.

And while K is struggling, Sensei goes privately to the widow. He asks for her daughter's hand in marriage. The answer is favorable. There is no dramatic contest. Sensei wins the consent he sought, and only then does he fully confront the fact that he has acted behind his closest friend's back.

## A door, a death, and the note

K learns of the engagement from the widow. Sensei has not had the courage to tell him. The household continues for a short time under the pressure of what each person knows and does not know. Then K dies by suicide in his room.

The death devastates Sensei. He discovers the body and the note K has left. The note does not deliver a clean accusation against him. That absence matters. Sensei is left with his knowledge of what he did, and without a message that would settle the question of what K intended or believed at the end. He recognizes that he had treated a trusted friend as an obstacle, that he had borrowed the language of K's highest values to weaken him, and that he had secured his own happiness by hiding the very truth his friend had entrusted to him.

Yet Sensei cannot prove that his betrayal alone caused K's death. K was troubled before their rivalry; his ideals and personal history were burdens in their own right. The uncertainty does not relieve Sensei. It makes the guilt impossible to complete. He can neither state a simple verdict nor dismiss his own role. His mind circles the episode for years.

He marries Shizu. The marriage is real; he loves her. But happiness does not arrive in the form he had imagined. Every ordinary domestic pleasure is shadowed by what happened in the house where they met. He visits K's grave. He continues to refuse his wife the full account. The friend is dead, but the life Sensei thought he was protecting is now dominated by the death.

He grows older without finding a way to take responsibility that would allow others to respond. The privacy of his guilt protects him from confrontation and makes genuine reconciliation impossible. Shizu senses the sorrow, but the central fact remains withheld from her. Sensei describes himself as a person whose existence has been divided against itself. He once believed his uncle's selfishness had taught him to recognize the bad faith of others. His own betrayal of K has taught him that he belongs among the people he condemned.

## The testament and the unanswered future

Years pass. When Sensei meets the younger student at Kamakura, much of his adult life has already been spent in this condition. The grave visits, the comments about selfishness, the distrust of other people and his own motives, the distance even within marriage: all now have a history. The student had assumed there was a secret behind Sensei's reserve. There was, but it was not a single fact that could simply be disclosed and forgotten. It was a way of living that had formed around an action he could not undo.

The death of Emperor Meiji and General Nogi's subsequent suicide bring Sensei's private crisis into contact with a public idea of the end of an era. Nogi dies after the emperor he served. Sensei finds in that event a language for his own departure, the language of dying with an age. It is important not to mistake this for an instruction the novel gives its readers. This is how Sensei understands and explains his decision. His reasons are mixed with remorse, loneliness, historical symbolism, and the wish to bring a life of self-accusation to a close.

He writes the testament for the student who kept asking to know him. At last he gives the story in detail. He asks the young man to learn from it, and asks that Shizu not be told while she is alive. Even in the gesture of confession, he continues to govern what another person may know. His last effort to tell the truth is therefore also an act of concealment.

Think about the young man on the train. He is leaving a dying father to respond to an older friend whose letter may already be too late. He has received the complete explanation he desired, but it arrives as an obligation rather than a solution. What will he do with it? Will he return home before his father dies? Will he keep the secret from Shizu? Will he imitate Sensei's habits of silence, or understand that admiration for a teacher need not mean repeating the teacher's choices?

Sōseki leaves those questions unanswered. There is no scene in which the student rescues Sensei. There is no final conversation in which Shizu receives a reassuring explanation. The story ends with Sensei's words, and the young reader within the story must decide how to live after reading them. At the beginning, a student wanted to know why a stranger seemed wise. By the end, he has learned the history of that stranger's failure, and the consequences of knowing are now his own.

That is the complete movement of *Kokoro*: from a beach encounter to a household mystery, from a student's sick father to a testament, and from an inheritance betrayal to a betrayal of friendship that outlives the friend. We have followed what happened. The question of what the form and the historical setting do to that history belongs to the analysis that follows.

This narration is an original account rather than a translation or excerpt.
