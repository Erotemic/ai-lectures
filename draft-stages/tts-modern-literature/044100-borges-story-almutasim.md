# Ficciones: The Approach to Al-Mu’tasim — A Review of a Novel That Never Existed

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

An ambitious novelist writes a detective story set in India. Its hero, a young student, enters a world of violence and slowly becomes convinced that the traces of goodness he encounters must come from a hidden person of surpassing spiritual power. The critic who reports the novel praises its imaginative architecture, worries about the way it ends, and compares two supposed editions. That is the apparent situation. The novelist is invented. The book being reviewed does not exist.

Borges's The Approach to Al-Mu'tasim presents itself as the criticism of a work by an imagined writer, Mir Bahadur Ali. The reviewer knows its literary history: an earlier edition with a certain kind of mystery, and a later illustrated version whose more explicit allegory may have damaged the invention. We receive the lost novel not by reading it, but by hearing a critic describe what such a novel would contain and why it might succeed or fail.

The reported plot begins with a student in Bombay. He has grown skeptical of the religion of his family, and he enters a confrontation between Hindus and Muslims during a religious procession. Violence carries him along. He kills, or thinks he kills, another person, then flees the police into a city whose streets and marginal lives he hardly understands. What begins as an effort to escape punishment becomes a long search through human encounters.

One person seems to possess a small quantity of extraordinary peace or kindness. The student infers that this quality has come from another person, who possesses more. In turn, that source points toward another. Rather than pursuing a thief or a murderer, he follows the diminishing reflections of an unseen spiritual center. The title names the figure toward whom the chain appears to lead: Al-Mu'tasim. The detective story becomes a search for the source of virtue.

The promised destination does not arrive as the ordinary solution to a case. The reviewer can describe the door, the voices, and the way the hero approaches a presence, but the imaginative force of the novel depends on how much remains withheld. The invented critic is wary of a revised version that too plainly reduces the mystery to a theological equation. The first account's indirection lets the reader experience longing rather than merely assent to a doctrine.

This is an early instance of Borges's technique of building fiction inside the form of literary criticism. It was first published in the 1930s, before Ficciones itself. Its location within the collection is a genuine bibliographical issue: different editions and anthologies arrange or omit it differently. A listener should not treat its placement as a timeless rule about the book. The creative point, however, does not depend on one table of contents.

There is an interesting reversal between writer and reader here. Normally the author invents a story and a critic comes afterward to interpret it. Borges invents the critic's interpretation, and from that we imagine the story. The reviewer seems to know more about the fictional novel than we could possibly verify. He is our only access to its supposed facts. Yet his reservations about excessive allegory make him sound judicious, and that apparent fairness helps make the absent book feel real.

The search inside the reported novel can be read as spiritual aspiration, but its setting is not a neutral backdrop. The invented India is mediated through an Argentine writer's literary sources, orientalist conventions, and philosophical interests. It should not be taken as documentary testimony about an Indian community. The sectarian riot, the wandering student, and the chain of human encounters are the story's invented means of organizing a problem about goodness and distance.

The final question is what we hope to find from a chain of testimony. Each person who appears kind seems to refer us to another. If we could reach the source, would the search end, or would we only have found another reflection? A reviewer may demand a definitive ending, while the reader who has entered the fiction through a nonexistent book discovers that absence is the engine of the work. The book we cannot read invites us to keep imagining it.
