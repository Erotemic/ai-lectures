# Ficciones: The Lottery in Babylon and Herbert Quain — Chance and Invented Books

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two stories in the first half of Ficciones begin with forms that are not supposed to behave like ordinary fiction. One is a report about the social history of a lottery. The other is a critical survey of a dead novelist's works. In both, the real plot is hidden in the logic of the system being described.

In The Lottery in Babylon, a speaker describes a city where almost every status and event may be assigned by chance. It was not always so. The lottery began as a familiar gamble, offering prizes in exchange for tickets. That seemed too weak to compel the attention of an entire society. Penalties entered the drawing: not every ticket would bring money; some might bring loss, disgrace, or punishment. At first the punishments were enforceable in recognizable ways. Later the machinery became more elaborate. A mysterious body called the Company gained authority over outcomes and began to determine, or appear to determine, more and more of what happened.

The narrator describes lotteries nested inside other lotteries. One drawing might select the sort of consequence that a later drawing would specify. An unfavorable result could itself be modified by another random procedure. At some point the difference between an extraordinary coincidence and an officially planned event becomes impossible to prove. Citizens no longer know whether a promotion, an arrest, a romantic encounter, or an accident is part of the lottery. The Company grows more secret as its alleged reach grows more complete.

Does the Company exist? The narrator does not offer evidence strong enough to settle the question. It might be an administrative power whose decisions shape every life. It might be a name people use to give senseless events a secret author. There may be no intelligible center at all. The story is disturbing because random outcomes and conspiratorial explanations begin to do the same work. Both can relieve a person of the burden of asking who made a decision, and both can make responsibility disappear.

The next object is a book review, or rather a review of several books. An Examination of the Work of Herbert Quain describes a novelist whose formally adventurous works appear to exist only inside Borges's invented criticism. One novel is a detective puzzle whose solution seems to embarrass the reader, since the apparent answer may conceal a better one. Another experiments with branching narrative in reverse. The reader begins at a consequence and moves backward into mutually incompatible pasts. A theatrical experiment uses a layered structure in which what counts as drama or reality changes. A book of short pieces offers material that another writer might borrow.

The fictional critic takes the works seriously enough to explain their structures and judge their shortcomings. Yet the reader cannot go to a shelf and check whether Quain's achievement has been represented fairly. We must reconstruct the nonexistent books from the critic's report. What at first seems like a shortcut around writing a novel becomes a demanding exercise for the imagination. We are asked to make books out of the descriptions of books.

The two pieces are especially revealing when paired. Babylon imagines a social order in which every event can be attributed to a hidden system. Quain imagines books in which the reader's search for form becomes part of the construction of the work. One turns chance into authority; the other turns literary authority into a possibility that never becomes a finished object. Both ask what happens when the institutions that supposedly explain our experience become opaque.

Borges also refuses to confuse sophistication with benevolence. A labyrinthine system can be elegant and cruel. A branching story can be ingenious and emotionally empty. When the narrator of the Quain survey gives a verdict, the verdict remains an opinion, not a law of aesthetics. We can admire a fictional novelist's inventions and still ask whether their designs make us see any person more clearly.

These are not plot summaries of books Borges secretly wrote and later hid. The books are fictional artifacts. Their absence is part of the technique. By offering what looks like reference material, Borges makes the reader an involuntary collaborator in a library that does not exist. And by describing a lottery that seems to allocate all reality, he asks us to consider whether the desire for a complete explanation can be more oppressive than mere chance.
