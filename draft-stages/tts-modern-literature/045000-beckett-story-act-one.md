# Waiting for Godot — Act One: The Appointment

A country road. A tree. Evening is approaching. A man is trying to remove a boot. Nothing about the setting explains where the road leads or who owns the land. Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot begins not with a catastrophe or a mystery to be solved, but with an ordinary physical problem. Estragon, the man with the boot, is tired. Another man, Vladimir, arrives. He knows Estragon; Estragon knows him. They have nicknames for one another, Gogo and Didi. Their friendship is real enough to survive irritation, separation, and the small cruelties of each other's habits. But its history is not supplied as an orderly biography.

Estragon has spent the night in a ditch and says he has been beaten. Vladimir asks questions, worries about the future, talks about his own bodily discomforts. The two men move between complaint and concern so rapidly that the audience scarcely has time to classify a remark as comic or grave. They might separate. Estragon repeatedly proposes going away. Vladimir reminds him that they cannot. They are waiting for Godot. They believe there is an appointment, though their recollection of exactly what was promised, where they were told to wait, and whether this is even the right day is far from secure.

That uncertainty is the play's first important action. A promise gives the men a reason to stay, but the promise itself is uncertain. They try to reconstruct the encounter: Was Godot specific? Did he answer? Did he say he would think it over? The men have made a life-changing commitment to something they cannot report with confidence. Even the small matter of identifying the place is difficult. A tree can be a signpost, but a tree is not an address. They try to fill their time with talk, argument, embraces, insults, the removal of boots, and proposals that they give up altogether.

Early in their conversation Vladimir remembers the Gospel account of the two thieves crucified alongside Jesus. The evangelists, he observes, do not all tell the same story about who was saved. Estragon does not share his interest in the textual problem. He remembers pictures and colors more readily than scriptural argument. In a more conventional play this discussion might provide the religious explanation that everything afterward confirms. Here it simply opens another possibility: even accounts that promise the highest kind of rescue disagree about what happened and who receives it.

The pair consider hanging themselves from the tree. Their speculation moves rapidly between fear, desire, comic calculation, and practical difficulty. The stage is not a philosophical seminar where despair has been lifted out of the body. To contemplate death while failing to find a workable means is both terribly exposed and awkwardly funny. They return to waiting. Whatever might arrive later becomes a reason not to decide now.

Then the stage suddenly acquires visitors. A commanding man named Pozzo enters, driving another man, Lucky, on a rope. Lucky carries luggage. Pozzo speaks as if the world is organized for his convenience; Lucky is burdened with the physical work of preserving that convenience. Pozzo's entrance breaks the repetitive intimacy of the friends' exchange with a public spectacle of rank and power. He gives orders, arranges his possessions, eats, boasts, and treats the two waiting men as an audience before which to perform his superiority. Vladimir and Estragon watch with shifting feelings: curiosity, disgust, hunger, amusement, and a hesitant wish to intervene.

Pozzo says he is taking Lucky to market to sell him. Lucky has served him for years, but Pozzo presents the sale as if it required no explanation beyond his own displeasure. Lucky is called upon to dance. His dance, cramped and depleted, is offered like an entertainment and evaluated like a deficient service. Then Pozzo commands him to think. The performance that follows is one of the play's most startling passages. Lucky, who has spoken virtually nothing, erupts into an extended torrent of words. It resembles an academic lecture coming apart: theology, scientific terminology, and fragments of abstract argument tumble into one another, repeated and interrupted without settling into a conclusion. The others cannot let it continue. His hat is removed, and the torrent stops. Thought itself seems to have become a job one performs on command, and then loses on command.

Pozzo and Lucky depart. Their encounter has created many sensations and no solution. It is tempting to ask whether their route supplies a clue about Godot, but the two situations are never joined into one trustworthy map. Vladimir and Estragon continue to wait. They try to recall what just happened, and what kind of people the travelers were. Estragon's practical needs return. They are hungry, tired, and uncertain. No one has explained how the appointment will improve their lives.

At the end of the act a boy appears. He carries a message. Mr. Godot will not come this evening, he says, but will surely come tomorrow. Vladimir questions him. The boy tends Godot's goats; his brother tends the sheep. Godot is said to beat the brother. Vladimir tries to learn more about a man whose power over them has been exercised almost entirely through absence. The boy can offer little that would make the promise dependable. He is frightened of the men. His message comes wrapped in another uncertain request to be recognized and remembered.

Darkness comes. The men say they ought to leave. Yet the last stage direction denies the movement their words have promised. They stay where they are. This is the first act's conclusion, and it is an ending in the theatrical sense: the curtain falls, the audience must leave the scene behind. But it is not the conclusion of the men's problem. An absence has generated a day of talk, comedy, conflict, and hope. Tomorrow has been promised again. The road and the tree are waiting with them.

To hear this act as a story, follow not the clue that will eventually disclose Godot, but the series of decisions deferred by his expected arrival. Each interruption changes the temperature of the evening; none cancels the appointment. The play has given us a very precise account of how a day can be filled without arriving anywhere.
