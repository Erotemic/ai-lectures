# The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part One: Benjy’s Saturday

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Begin beside a fence. Someone on the other side is hitting a little white ball. A man calls to a caddie, and the sound of that word reaches a listener for whom it is not a job title. It is a name. It sounds like Caddy, his sister, and the sound releases a remembered world. This is how William Faulkner begins The Sound and the Fury. He gives us neither the family's pedigree nor a convenient explanation of who is speaking. He gives us Benjy Compson, walking by the fence on a Saturday in Mississippi, and he allows a word to draw thirty years of family life into the present.

The date at the head of the section is April seventh, nineteen twenty-eight. It is the day before Easter and Benjy's thirty-third birthday. Benjy cannot speak in conventional language. He cries, moans, moves toward things he recognizes, and recoils from changes that disturb him. His consciousness, however, is presented to us in sentences. The writing is not a transcript of speech he could deliver. It is Faulkner's effort to invent an experience of perception before the explanatory story most narrators would supply. Benjy is not having the past recalled as a clearly labeled historical account. He encounters a sight or a sound, and suddenly another time is present.

Luster, a younger member of the Black Gibson family who work in the Compson household, is taking Benjy around the grounds. Luster is searching for a lost quarter. He wants money to attend the traveling show that has arrived in town. Benjy wants no such thing. He wants familiar routes, familiar faces, the security of things that once stayed where they belonged. Luster's practical irritation and Benjy's grief share a single landscape, but they do not amount to the same problem. The family for which the Gibsons labor is not simply unhappy; its money and household authority have been deteriorating for decades. The golf course beyond the fence lies on land that used to belong to the Compsons.

The name Caddy sends Benjy to the childhood he shared with four Compson children: Quentin, the eldest; Caddy, his sister; Jason, another brother; and Benjy himself. For part of that childhood he was called Maury, after his mother's brother. When the family decided that he did not meet their expectations of what a son should be, his mother insisted on changing his name to Benjamin. His body and his needs did not change with the name. What changed was the family's language for its embarrassment. They wanted a family name rescued from a child whom they judged incapable of carrying it.

In one of the novel's earliest remembered scenes, the children play outside while their grandmother is dying. The adults withhold what is happening in the house. Caddy climbs a tree to look into a window. From below, the boys see her muddy underclothes as she climbs. Quentin is disturbed, Jason reports, and Benjy sees a movement that matters because it is Caddy's. The scene will acquire meanings the children cannot yet comprehend. There is death hidden inside the house, and there is a young girl's freedom to climb and see what the adults will not tell her. The boys' different reactions anticipate the possessiveness, resentment, and fear that will shape the adult household.

Caddy is the child who most consistently cares for Benjy. She leads him, comforts him, and notices his distress. He recognizes her through her smell, sometimes associated with trees and open air. The bond is intimate and ordinary. For a child denied the explanations other people receive, a scent, a path, a touch, or a voice can give the world stability. But Caddy is a person growing older, not a permanent piece of the garden. As she begins relationships beyond the home, Benjy registers unfamiliar scents and departures. He cries when her familiar presence changes. He cannot tell us that she has a right to a life of her own; neither can the rest of the household easily accept it, though they possess words with which to justify their demands.

Around the house stand a father and mother who do not reliably shelter the children. Mr. Compson is intelligent, worn down by drinking and resignation. He offers aphorisms instead of an adequate response to pain. Mrs. Compson is absorbed in sickness, family reputation, and her grievances. She imagines her own suffering as the center of the family's story. The actual work of feeding, tending, escorting, and protecting is done largely by Dilsey Gibson and her relatives. When Benjy's memories move among childhood rooms, outdoors, and the kitchen, it is often a Gibson family member who physically makes a day possible.

We learn what happens to Caddy by fragments. She grows up, becomes sexually active, and becomes pregnant. A marriage is arranged with Herbert Head, a man whose wealth seems to offer escape from disgrace. He discovers that the child is not his and abandons the marriage. Caddy is excluded from the Compson home, but her daughter is raised there. The child is also named Quentin, after Caddy's brother. Thus there are two people named Quentin in this novel: the brother who dies in nineteen ten, and the daughter whose turbulent teenage life is unfolding in nineteen twenty-eight. Benjy's sensory memory does not sort their names for us. The listener must.

Caddy's wedding produces one of the most piercing absences in Benjy's section. She is dressed for departure; he is dressed and moved through a ceremony whose social meaning he cannot grasp. To him, it is enough that the person who steadied his world is going away. The novel does not give Benjy a clear declaration about abandonment. It lets the ceremony, the voices, and his distress reveal it. He also remembers that the family sold its pasture, the very space linked to his ordinary pleasures, in order to fund Quentin's education at Harvard and to support Caddy's wedding. A sacrifice described by others as family advancement becomes, for Benjy, a loss of ground.

Other memories carry a harsher story. At a gate near passing schoolgirls, Benjy runs toward one of them. Frightened adults interpret the encounter as a sexual danger, and the family responds with a devastating bodily intervention: he is castrated. Faulkner does not invite us to treat the family's judgment as a medical diagnosis or to imagine that Benjy has consented. The scene belongs to a world quick to exert control over someone who has little recognized power to explain himself. The possibilities of institutional confinement hover over his life as well.

There are also fragments of Quentin, the older brother, who was sent north to Harvard and never returned. The young man's suicide in nineteen ten reaches Benjy not as an orderly cause-and-effect report but through conversations and changes in the household. Mr. Compson eventually dies; the mother withdraws further into complaint; Jason becomes the adult son who holds the household's money and weaponizes its humiliations. Benjy continues to inhabit the house where the family he knew has disappeared.

Listen to the present-day day beneath the memories. Luster wants his quarter. Golfers call across the fence. Benjy has moments of contentment, then cries. He follows a route around a property no longer wholly his family's. He has a candle-lit birthday cake with candles; his mother handles his presence as both a worry and an affront to her vanity. The Gibson family supplies the attention and patience the household's supposed owners largely fail to offer. Each ordinary task can bring a sensory return to Caddy, and every return contains the renewed fact that she is gone.

A conventional narrator might say: this family has suffered a tragedy, and here is its sequence. Benjy cannot do that. Yet his account discloses something that a polished family history might conceal. The people who speak loudly about honor can be negligent; the sister branded a disgrace was a source of tenderness; and the land called a family possession was also a place where a vulnerable person lived in bodily safety. Benjy's world is more than an elaborate puzzle to decode. Its disorienting form places a demand on us: before judging him or solving him, attend to what he senses, what he loves, and what has been taken away.

By the time his Saturday ends, almost nothing in Benjy's situation is resolved. He has not recovered Caddy, and the family's fortunes have not returned. Faulkner gives us a first account of loss that cannot become a tidy explanation, because the person experiencing it has no stable distance from the losses. The past does not sit behind him. It keeps arriving with the word Caddy, the smell of trees, a place in the yard, and the movement of a body that remembers a sister's care.
