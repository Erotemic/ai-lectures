# Proust: Desire, Secrecy, and the Stories Society Permits

In the opening of Sodom and Gomorrah, the narrator watches Baron de Charlus and Jupien recognize an attraction between them. The scene is presented as a revelation. But a revelation for whom? Not for the two men, who already know what they desire. It is a revelation for the observer, whose assumptions about masculine status and public behavior have kept him from seeing what was happening. That distinction matters whenever we study sexuality in Marcel Proust's In Search of Lost Time.

Proust's characters live in a society that frequently stigmatizes same-sex desire. The danger of exposure differs according to class, wealth, gender, and personal circumstances, but it is a material fact of the fictional world. People meet through coded signals, disguise relationships under socially acceptable names, or rely on companions who understand what outsiders fail to see. Proust builds intricate narratives from these conditions. He also draws on concepts about sexuality common in his historical setting, including classifications and metaphors that modern readers have good reason to question. To explain his fiction responsibly, we must distinguish what a character desires, what society permits that character to say, and what the narrator claims to know about the desire.

Charlus is an especially revealing example because he possesses enormous social authority. He can intimidate a room through rank and confidence. Yet his private life is vulnerable to exposure and rumor. His relationship with Jupien crosses a class boundary while remaining dependent upon concealment. His later attachment to Morel, the violinist, places emotional need in conflict with his habitual expectation that others will defer to him. Rank does not protect him from humiliation, and humiliation does not make him innocent of abusing power. Proust's characterization is effective because the baron is never only one thing.

At a Verdurin gathering, Charlus's effort to patronize and elevate Morel becomes an opportunity for Madame Verdurin to attack him. Morel can be tempted by the social advantages of repudiating the baron. The result is cruel. A powerful man discovers that a group he cannot command can organize his exclusion. Yet it would be too simple to conclude that Charlus is punished because his desire is wrong. The central causes are rivalry, dependency, deliberate manipulation, the baron's pride, and a society in which private sexuality can be weaponized.

The narrator's observations contain several levels of evidence. He may directly witness a gesture or sexual encounter. He may hear gossip about someone's habits. He may infer a person's identity from a voice, posture, or manner of looking. These levels are not equivalent. Proust sometimes seems to invite broad generalization from a very small set of observations, and the narrator's explanations can resemble the pseudo-scientific certainty of his age. We should resist treating those passages as contemporary empirical claims about gay people. The literary interest often lies in the narrator's desire to convert uncertainty into a taxonomy.

The novel's account of women loving women is still more compromised by the narrator's limited access. As a boy at Combray he spies on Mademoiselle Vinteuil with her female lover. He later becomes obsessed with Albertine's possible relations with women. But Albertine is never offered a comparably extended, independent narrative voice in which to explain her experience. The narrator interrogates, imagines, and recruits witnesses. Her silence or evasiveness may have many causes, including the danger posed by his demands. A hidden life is not necessarily a guilty life, and secrecy under coercion does not validate the coercer's suspicions.

The importance of Mademoiselle Vinteuil and her companion is not exhausted by the childhood scene. They are also connected to the preservation and performance of Vinteuil's musical work. The narrator's moral and sexual judgments are therefore placed in tension with an aesthetic fact. People whom his society disparages participate in making an extraordinary work of art audible. That detail cannot by itself resolve every question about Proust's treatment of women. It does prevent the fiction from identifying artistic value with conventional respectability.

Robert de Saint-Loup complicates the picture in another direction. He first appears as a seemingly straightforward young aristocrat, affectionate friend, soldier, and lover of Rachel. Later the narrator learns of his relationships with men, including Morel. His marriage to Gilberte carries the social promise of continuity while concealing private attachments. Readers must be careful here. A late revelation does not turn everything we learned about Robert into an elaborate fraud. His generosity and friendship need not have been false because his sexual life was more complex than the narrator knew. The problem lies partly in the narrator's expectation that a person should present one stable, easily classified identity.

The brothel scene in Time Regained again returns Charlus to the center. The narrator watches men performing brutality for payment in a setting organized by Jupien. The scene is not a neutral report on an entire sexual community. It is a particular fictional scenario involving fantasy, class, power, theatricality, and an observer who is himself fascinated by what he witnesses. The men acting roles are not identical to the selves they present. Charlus's preferences do not authorize general conclusions about all same-sex desire, just as Swann's jealousy does not define all heterosexual love.

A further complication concerns Proust himself. Scholars and biographers have studied his relationships with men and their relevance to the novel's construction. But the presence of autobiographical material does not make the narrator's claims identical with the author's beliefs, and a fictional character is not simply an encrypted historical acquaintance waiting to be identified. The novel's power comes partly from transformation. Proust makes desire travel among male and female figures, changes positions and relationships, and places a narrator with limited knowledge in charge of telling the story.

What, then, should the reader do with passages that speak in the language of moral or medical classification? First, locate the voice. Is this a person's direct speech, a social prejudice the narrator reports, a generalization the narrator makes, or a scene whose action undercuts the generalization? Second, examine who controls information. Who can admit a relationship publicly, who risks exposure, who can buy privacy, and who can force someone else into concealment? Third, distinguish the harm created by unequal power from the mere existence of a desire outside conventional norms.

These questions change the reading of Albertine. The narrator fears that she desires women. He also regulates her movements, sends people to accompany her, and treats ordinary independence as evidence of betrayal. We can recognize the fear without endorsing it. We can recognize that Albertine may conceal things without concluding that the narrator is entitled to the truth of every private encounter. The novel's tragedy is not simply that desire is mysterious. It is that a person who cannot tolerate mystery may attempt to abolish another person's freedom.

Proust's portrayal remains historically situated. Some passages are sympathetic to lives forced into secrecy; others repeat suspicious or caricaturing habits. It is neither necessary to excuse the latter because the work is canonical nor to erase the former because the work is old. A careful reader can see the contradictory materials operating together. In Search of Lost Time becomes most illuminating when its analysis of social stigma is allowed to stand beside its narrator's unreliable acts of classification. That makes it an account not only of sexual desire, but of the limits of the stories a society permits people to tell about themselves.
