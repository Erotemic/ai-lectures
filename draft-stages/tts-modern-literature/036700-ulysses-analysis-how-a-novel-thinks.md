# Ulysses: How a Novel Can Think in More Than One Language

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine telling the story of a man who goes to work, attends a funeral, eats lunch, visits friends, and finally returns to his wife. You could describe what he does. You could explain what he thinks. You could alternate dialogue and description and hope your readers came to know him. James Joyce does all this in Ulysses, but he discovers another possibility. Instead of using a stable narrative voice to report a whole day, he allows the forms of language encountered in that day to alter the form of the novel itself.

The distinction matters. Ulysses is often introduced as the great novel of stream of consciousness. That phrase names a genuine part of its technique, but it is not a sufficient description of the entire work. As Pericles Lewis observes in Yale's Modernism Lab, Joyce's initial exploration of interior monologue develops into a far wider array of styles. The novel is not one unbroken transcript from inside a skull. It is a changing experiment in how inner life, public discourse, literary tradition, and bodily experience interact.

## The mind is not a polished speech

At the beginning, Stephen Dedalus's thoughts move through quotation, memory, perception, philosophy, and resentment. When he walks on Sandymount Strand, the boundary between what he sees and what he thinks is sometimes unmistakable and sometimes difficult to locate. To read the passage well, we need not translate every line into a neat proposition. We can ask what causes a thought to change direction. A sound recalls a word. A sight recalls an argument. An argument reopens a grievance.

Bloom's thoughts are different. He notices how things work. A shop front suggests an improvement in advertising. A passing face suggests a story about someone's family. A smell becomes appetite or nausea. He can think about a kitchen and a dead child in close succession without explaining the movement to himself. A traditional narrator might announce that the domestic detail symbolizes mourning. Joyce makes us experience the adjacency before asking us to interpret it.

This technique can be called interior monologue, but even that term requires care. The words on the page are not a tape recording of private thought. They are selected and arranged by Joyce. Sometimes a narrator's sentence slips toward a character's diction without introducing a quotation. Sometimes a bodily sensation breaks into a passage of formal commentary. Sometimes the prose is deliberately ambiguous about whose vocabulary we hear. Literary form does not vanish when the narrator stops explaining; it becomes more difficult to recognize.

## The newspaper and the trap of a headline

Consider Aeolus, the episode in which Bloom negotiates advertising and Stephen discusses rhetoric at a newspaper office. The text is interrupted by headlines. A headline promises to identify the news, give it importance, and package it for circulation. By imposing headline form on scenes that can scarcely bear the weight, Joyce demonstrates how public language can inflate, redirect, or trivialize what happened.

A newspaper can make anything seem official by printing it in a certain way. Yet headlines are not always lies. They are conventions that compress events for a reader's attention. Joyce's satire works because this useful convention can also substitute a ready-made emphasis for understanding. The chapter invites us to distinguish the event from the terms in which institutions make it consumable.

In Wandering Rocks, by contrast, the problem is not compression but distribution. Nineteen street movements give us pieces of overlapping life. The reader becomes a tentative citywide observer but never acquires total omniscience. Snippets of one itinerary appear in another. A reader who notices the interference can begin to reconstruct chronology, but the effect is not simply a crossword. It is the experience of a society in which each person's important story is background to another person's afternoon.

## Music without a score

Sirens starts with fragments that sound detached from meaningful narrative. Later their sounds recur in a bar full of songs and listeners. Reading the chapter is like hearing motifs introduced before one knows where they belong. A sound can correspond to a word, a voice, an action, or a memory. It can be funny until its return makes it painful.

The technique is often compared with musical fugue. The comparison is illuminating because of repetition and counterpoint, but we should not pretend the novel is a fully notated fugue whose formal rules can be checked like a conservatory exercise. The musical analogy guides attention to recurrence and variation. Joyce is exploring how a novel might make us listen to language as time rather than only extract propositions from it.

The result suits Bloom's situation. He hears songs that other customers enjoy together, but he also hears the private history of Molly, Rudy, and betrayal. Public music becomes the medium of solitude. No additional paragraph announcing his sadness would reproduce quite the same effect.

## A narrator who makes hostility sound ordinary

Cyclops offers a more political demonstration. An unnamed, sarcastic narrator tells us about the Citizen and Bloom in a Dublin pub. His language comes already loaded with judgments about who is annoying, who is generous, who belongs, and who deserves to be mocked. Readers who accept the narrator's tone without examination risk accepting his contempt. Meanwhile the episode erupts into inflated imitations of legal, biblical, heroic, and journalistic registers.

The little biscuit tin thrown at Bloom is treated with the verbal magnificence suitable to an enormous epic battle. We know the scale is ridiculous. Yet the question of who gets to control the telling is serious. Satirical language can expose a nationalist's prejudice; prejudiced language can also disguise violence as a public joke. The chapter makes us experience both processes at once.

It is helpful here to separate narratorial reliability from the moral views of a character. The unnamed speaker's account is partial and hostile. The hyperbolic passages are not neutral footnotes correcting him. They are further performances of public discourse. Our task is not to find a single plain narrator hiding behind them but to measure the distance between the actions and the tones used to describe them.

## What happens when the genre changes altogether?

In Oxen of the Sun the language moves through historical literary styles while a woman gives birth and young men debate and drink. We hear English prose aging toward the present. Why use linguistic history for a maternity scene? Because a style, like a language community, does not emerge fully formed. Every voice has been born from prior voices. The analogy is productive but also ironic. The male speakers' virtuosity does not mean they understand the laboring woman whose body supplies the occasion.

Circe goes further. Its form is a drama script with fantastically elaborate directions. A courtroom materializes out of Bloom's anxiety. Figures transform. Stephen's dead mother appears. The style of theatre allows events normally hidden within consciousness to be shown as if they occupied the same stage as actual policemen and brothel furniture. There is no need to decide that every hallucination is a supernatural fact. The visible convention of stage direction lets the work dramatize inward forces while keeping practical consequences in play.

Ithaca does something apparently opposite. It adopts the language of systematic questions and answers. After Nighttown's uncontrolled transformations, the catechism promises exhaustive knowledge. Exactly what happened? What was present? How can the relations of two people be classified? Yet its precision becomes absurdly excessive. The more it lists, the more visible the gap between factual inventory and mutual understanding. Knowing the motion of stars cannot tell us what Stephen means to Bloom.

Finally, Penelope allows Molly Bloom a long, lightly punctuated inward monologue. The ending is often celebrated as the restoration of an authentic female voice after so many male interpretations. It does give Molly a kind of authority the novel has long withheld from her. But nothing about a lack of periods makes words unmediated nature. Joyce has constructed this voice carefully, and Molly's memories, conventions, prejudices, and wishes remain her own partial world.

## Style is an ethical problem

The succession of styles is not simply a display of James Joyce's verbal genius. It repeatedly asks who gets described by whom, what language makes visible, and what it prevents others from seeing. The newspaper makes events saleable. The pub makes violence heroic. The hospital makes birth a literary performance. The catechism makes domestic intimacy measurable. Molly's monologue answers some of the representations imposed upon her, but it also reveals another consciousness that cannot be exhausted by a label.

The structural risk is obvious. An elaborate technique might become a game that leaves people behind. Joyce repeatedly returns from that risk to the bodies and losses underneath it: a dead child, a funeral, the desire to be welcomed, a stranger struck by a soldier, a woman lying awake in bed. His novel is experimental because he suspects a single narrative language would falsify the amount of life one day contains.

To understand Ulysses is not to identify every technique by name. It is to recognize what each technique does to our relation with the people and events it presents. That is why the style keeps changing. No voice, not even an extraordinarily intelligent one, can claim a city to itself.
