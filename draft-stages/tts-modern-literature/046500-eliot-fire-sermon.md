# The Waste Land, Part Three: The Thames and the Fire Sermon

The Fire Sermon begins beside the Thames, but not the river of ceremonial spectacle. Summer visitors have departed. The banks are wet and shabby, and the passing season has left traces of intimacy that no longer have owners. The river itself is a familiar English poetic subject. Eliot invokes an old refrain calling on the Thames to flow gently, then sets that music among bottles, paper, and human absence. The refrain does not cease being beautiful. Its beauty now has to coexist with the waste left by ordinary recreation.

A speaker fishes near a canal behind a gasworks. The setting is specific, even ugly by the standards of conventional pastoral poetry. Meanwhile Shakespeare's The Tempest enters in the speaker's imagination. A son has lost a father; an image of wrecked kings rises among bones and a rat moving along the shore. A classical or Renaissance scene of loss has been relocated to industrial water. The fishing figure is suggestive, and readers often connect it to the wounded Fisher King of Grail legend, but the poem does not pause to declare that the fisherman is one stable character with a complete mythology. Its activity is the more local one of watching water that will not restore a broken world.

Sound proceeds by collision. A popular song, a horn, an automobile, and a bit of French suggest a world in which ancient songs and contemporary music-hall material occupy the same few lines. A reference to Philomela returns as a birdlike sound with a history of violation behind it. As in the second part, an apparently charming sound can carry the memory of an injured woman. The poem can sound bawdy, comic, erotic, and bleak almost in a single breath. We should not try to elevate every popular reference into something solemn merely because the poem also knows classical literature.

We enter the city again under the winter fog. A merchant named Mr. Eugenides appears, associated with Smyrna and the international business of currants. Trade vocabulary enters the poem with conspicuous abruptness. He makes a social invitation that has a sexual implication, though the poem does not supply a later scene showing exactly what happened. Notice the way the language of commerce, foreignness, desire, and hospitality is compressed into a few lines. This is a person glimpsed through a speaker's response, not an entire ethnic or commercial group represented by one individual's behavior.

Then the poem reaches one of its most uncomfortable modern episodes. The day is winding down, and a typist returns home to her rooms. Her domestic life is made of small practical objects: food laid out, a stove, the end of work. A young man arrives. The meeting becomes sexual, but not through a reciprocal exchange of desire. He presses ahead despite her indifference. The narration makes the encounter look chillingly routine, in part because the figures seem to perform scripts whose emotional meaning they have ceased to examine. The young man leaves satisfied with himself; the woman remains and turns back toward her ordinary routine. We must be careful about the claim here. The poem does not give us a case record with witnesses, and it does not explicitly name the encounter as a legal offense. But neither does the text establish mutual consent merely because the woman does not offer resistance in a form the man recognizes. The scene requires attention to asymmetry and to the narrow range of feelings its narration allows her.

Who observes them? Tiresias, the blind seer of Greek myth, appears as a witnessing consciousness. In one of the traditions Eliot invokes, Tiresias has experienced life in both male and female form; this makes the figure a charged link among perspectives that would otherwise seem socially and bodily incompatible. In Eliot's own notes, Tiresias is described as a crucial unifying presence, yet a note is not a command to rewrite every passage as an event literally seen by one supernatural individual. Tiresias observes rather than intervenes. The authority of this witness is also a source of trouble. What does an observer understand of a woman's private response? The poem gives us a way to ask that question but does not grant complete access to her.

The scene is often described as an emblem of mechanical sex. That description captures the act's routinized gestures, but it can too easily make both participants equally implicated in a generalized modern emptiness. Their positions are not equal. One is pressing forward, the other is enduring. If a reading blames the woman for a culture's loss of passion, it has ignored how the narration distributes power. This distinction matters especially because the typist has a social situation, workday fatigue, and limited privacy, not merely an allegorical function.

The river returns, but its music becomes even stranger. We hear the Thames as a space of oil and tar, barges and turning tide. A scene of Elizabeth I and the Earl of Leicester passes through a kind of song, bringing courtly political power and erotic implication onto the same water as the modern city. Other voices speak of Richmond, Moorgate, and Margate; the geography is metropolitan and specific. Their accounts of sexual experience are damaged or attenuated. One woman reports a promise of renewal after an encounter, while she herself makes almost no answer. It is not necessary to decide that every female speaker is literally one person. The poem's form makes their experiences resonate by placing them in a common auditory field.

The voices of the river daughters recall figures from Wagner's Ring cycle, but their lament has been carried into London transit routes and streets. It is a striking transformation. In the opera, river figures live at the edge of a great myth of theft, power, and catastrophe. Eliot's river voices are exposed to the ordinary weather of class, exhaustion, and sexual politics. The mythic register does not make the modern people more grand. It makes the distance between music and what they endure more apparent.

The movement finishes with two traditions speaking into one another. A reference to Augustine's arrival in Carthage evokes a world of overpowering desire. A sequence of burning sounds calls up the Buddha's Fire Sermon, where the senses and the passions are described as aflame. In the poem's own soundscape, burning is not a lecture heading. It is a rising pressure in a voice that asks to be delivered. The allusions are not identical. Christian confession and Buddhist teaching differ profoundly in their conceptions of the self and liberation. Their encounter here makes a shared urgency audible without proving that the two religions say exactly the same thing.

What kind of damage has this movement portrayed? Not just the river's physical pollution. We have heard the remnants of romance, the violent memory carried by song, the shallow invitation of a merchant, a sexual encounter whose apparent normality makes it more disturbing, and multiple women trying to understand what has happened to them. Water can carry trade and waste; it can preserve a city and take away its intimacy. The fire in the title evokes craving and a wish to escape its cycle. Neither element supplies a simple cure.

The formal achievement is to make the reader move among bodily, social, and spiritual scales without settling comfortably at any one of them. Industrial London is not an illustration pasted onto a preexisting religious lesson. A woman's room is not merely an abstract symbol of a broken civilization. It is a room in which a person has to return to living after someone else has gone. Those ordinary details are what keep the poem's large claims answerable to actual human experience.
