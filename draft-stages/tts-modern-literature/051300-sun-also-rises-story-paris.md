# The Sun Also Rises — The Story: Paris and an Unspoken Wound

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The Sun Also Rises begins in Paris in the 1920s with a narrator who seems more interested in explaining his friend than in explaining himself. Jake Barnes describes Robert Cohn: an American from a Jewish family, educated at Princeton, where boxing helped him contend with antisemitism, later a husband, editor, and novelist. Cohn is now living with Frances Clyne, whose attachment to him has grown tense. He wants travel, romance, and proof that life can begin again. Jake, a newspaper journalist, is impatient with these dreams. The reader is almost immediately inside a friendship marked by envy and contempt, though Jake's own reasons for these feelings will emerge only gradually.

Jake lives as an American expatriate after the First World War. He works, drinks, visits cafés, and knows how the city operates. His voice is concise, practical, and observant. But a great deal is missing from what he initially says. The war has wounded him in a way that has changed his capacity for sexual intimacy. He is deeply in love with Lady Brett Ashley, an Englishwoman whose wartime service brought her into his life. They are affectionate and emotionally attached, but their relationship has no straightforward future. The novel never presents the injury as a case report. It allows the difficulty to surface through dialogue, embarrassment, and small gestures.

Cohn asks Jake to join him on an adventure in South America. He has been inspired partly by a romantic book and wishes to escape the dullness of Paris. Jake turns him down. The exchange tells us something about both men. Cohn imagines that travel will answer the question of how to live; Jake believes people carry their problems with them. Yet Jake himself will soon leave Paris for Spain, hoping that fishing and bullfighting will provide an ordered alternative to social confusion. His skepticism is not immunity to the desire for escape.

One evening Jake meets Georgette, a woman who sells sex, and takes her to dinner and dancing. Their interaction involves money and playacting, but it also makes visible Jake's complicated relation to sexuality. At a dance club Brett appears. She is striking, direct, and accustomed to moving through a world of male attention. Cohn is immediately fascinated. Jake's knowledge of Brett is much older and more painful. They leave together, speaking with the ease of people who have tried more than once to solve a problem that has no convenient solution. They love one another, yet they do not settle into a life together. Brett refuses to pretend that her erotic desires can simply be dismissed.

The group forms a network of encounters in restaurants, taxis, bars, and apartments. Brett is connected with Count Mippipopolous, a wealthy man who has seen a great deal of life. He treats pleasure as something to be taken deliberately. Jake measures people through how they spend money, how they conduct themselves when drunk, and how much they know of particular arts. His assessments can be sharply funny. They can also be cruel, especially where Cohn is concerned.

Frances and Cohn's relationship deteriorates. Frances, anxious about her future and frustrated by Cohn's wish to leave her, humiliates him in conversation. The scenes are uncomfortable because people who know one another well are using that knowledge to cause pain. Jake watches and reports. He rarely intervenes in a way that changes the outcome. His restraint gives him an air of neutrality, but as the novel continues we must ask whether a reporter who decides which embarrassments to record is ever truly neutral.

Brett goes away for a time to San Sebastián. Cohn too disappears from Paris. Later Brett returns with Mike Campbell, a Scottish man she plans to marry. Mike is genial, financially unstable, and prone to drinking. Cohn has become enamored of Brett and has traveled with her while she was away. This creates a situation in which several men have different claims, real or imagined, upon a woman who has made no promise to keep them all satisfied. Jake knows more than he says. The friction is not yet open conflict, but the ingredients are present.

Jake's friend Bill Gorton arrives, bringing humor and the prospect of a journey. Together they plan to go to Spain: first for fishing in the countryside, then to Pamplona for the festival of San Fermín and the bullfights. Cohn will join them. Brett and Mike decide to come too. The journey seems like an opportunity to exchange a fatigued urban social circuit for landscape, sport, and a place where skill can still be recognized. But the group carries its quarrels onto the train.

Notice how much the opening has done without a formal declaration of theme. We have learned about a damaged body, a desired but impossible relationship, the privileges and costs of expatriate life, a friendship corroded by prejudice and envy, and a culture of drinking and money. None has been explained in an essay by the narrator. The facts are distributed through meals, chance meetings, and conversations whose most consequential parts are sometimes omitted.

The story will now test whether a new landscape can change any of these relationships. Jake hopes to find an intelligible world in Spain, where he believes there are clear standards of craft and courage. Whether he can enter that world without bringing the drama of Paris along is the question that the journey will answer.
