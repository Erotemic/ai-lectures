# In Search of Lost Time — The Story, Part Six: The Fugitive

Albertine has left. The narrator wakes into an apartment whose routines were built around a person he believed he could keep near him indefinitely. His first response is not a noble acceptance of her independence. It is alarm, bargaining, fury, humiliation, and the conviction that he cannot survive the loss. The Fugitive, also published under the title Albertine Gone, studies what happens when the person who filled another's imagination can no longer be questioned, followed, or held in place.

The narrator had repeatedly said that he was tired of living with Albertine. Now that she has departed, he experiences the separation as a catastrophe. He tries to disguise the intensity of his wish for her return. He calculates what letters will make him seem indifferent and what gestures might persuade her without making him appear weak. He asks his friend Robert de Saint-Loup to intervene with the family she has gone to join. He is willing to spend money to recover the arrangement he had been trying to escape. He wants a result that proves Albertine freely chooses him, but his methods still lean toward persuasion and pressure rather than genuine freedom of choice.

He reads every message for hidden intentions. An account that Albertine is safe and content becomes intolerable because he cannot verify it. A plan that might win her back also threatens to reveal how little control he has. Proust's treatment of jealousy becomes more disturbing precisely because the narrator is highly intelligent. He can expose his own motives, describe their contradictions, and then act on them again. Knowledge of an obsession does not itself make the obsession cease.

Then the news arrives that Albertine has died after a riding accident. Her death is sudden, and the narrator's mind refuses to absorb it in a single act. He can repeat the information, but the fact does not make his mental image of her inert. In memory she still has a future: she can return from an errand, defend herself against an accusation, tell a different story, or change her mind. The living Albertine had escaped his control; the dead Albertine escapes his ability to finish the argument. There will be no conclusive interview in which he can ask her what she desired or what she intended.

He does not stop investigating. On the contrary, he finds new ways to interrogate her past. He seeks testimony through people who knew her, including Aimé, a hotel employee familiar with Balbec. Reports concerning Albertine's relations with women seem to confirm old suspicions, but each report is filtered through the witness's motives, memory, and interpretation. The narrator treats fragments as possible keys to a total truth. Nothing gives him that truth. Even if a report is accurate, it cannot reconstruct an entire person. We are watching an intelligence use its abilities to deepen uncertainty by refusing the limits of available evidence.

The narrative makes the effect of grief startlingly concrete. A garment, a route through the apartment, an ordinary pleasure he had imagined sharing with Albertine can produce sudden pain. He realizes that he has loved not one stable image but many Albertines: the woman who laughed at Balbec, the woman who slept in his room, the woman whose errands he monitored, the woman he hoped might return. A particular memory hurts because a particular version of the narrator had depended on her. The selves formed during the relationship do not vanish simply because a message announces her death.

Time, however, acts even where the will cannot. The narrator does not decide to cease loving Albertine and thereby accomplish it. He changes by living through other days. Old reflexes weaken. New attachments, distractions, and objects of attention acquire force. He sometimes notices this change with relief and sometimes with horror. If forgetting is possible, does that mean the love was not real? Proust resists that conclusion. A feeling can have governed years of life and still not remain present forever. The transformation of feeling is not proof that the earlier experience was a lie.

Other stories return. The narrator renews contact with Gilberte, the daughter of Swann and Odette, whom he once adored as a boy. She now belongs to a transformed social world. Her name and prospects show the fluidity of the ranks everyone once treated as fixed. She eventually marries Robert de Saint-Loup. That marriage appears to unite families and social traditions that, in Combray, had belonged to separate paths and separate worlds. But the social symbolism does not guarantee domestic happiness. Robert has private erotic attachments that Gilberte does not fully understand, and the marriage will carry its own injuries.

The narrator also travels to Venice with his mother. The city has occupied his imagination for years through its name, buildings, paintings, and travelers' descriptions. Now he walks among churches and canals, looking at art, feeling the texture of a place that seemed almost impossible when it was only an idea. The journey contains pleasure, renewed companionship with his mother, and an uneasy recognition that even this long-desired city does not release him forever from old patterns of need.

Venice also becomes the setting for a miniature drama of mistaken reading. A telegram appears to bring a message from Albertine, and the narrator is thrown into an emotional turmoil that should have been impossible if she is dead. Later he discovers that a message from Gilberte was misread or transcribed in a way that produced the false appearance of Albertine's name. It is one of the novel's sharpest demonstrations that interpretation depends on small material facts. A misplaced letter can revive an entire personal world because the reader is already prepared to see what he longs to see. The episode has a complicated textual history in surviving editions, and its place in the narrative should not be taken as evidence that Proust had finalized every detail of the novel before his death.

The narrator also must face the impossibility of holding Venice itself in place. At the point of departure, the thought that he might meet a woman he desires nearly persuades him to remain after his mother has left for the station. He is pulled between an immediate imagined pleasure and loyalty to someone who actually loves him. He finally joins her. The tension between the life he constructs in anticipation and the life that proceeds while he is choosing has not disappeared.

The social epilogue of this volume becomes increasingly intricate. Relationships that once seemed to be settled acquire hidden histories. The narrator learns more about Robert's attachments to men, including Morel, the violinist associated with Charlus. Gilberte discovers that her marriage contains forms of secrecy that echo the earlier generation. An inherited name can join estates and fortunes without revealing the truth of the people who carry it.

Near the end of this stretch of the story, the narrator spends time with Gilberte at Tansonville, around the landscapes of his childhood. The two walks he had treated as wholly separate can, he discovers, be connected. What had seemed an absolute division was partly an artifact of his limited perspective. Gilberte also revises his memory of the feelings she once had for him. The young narrator who thought he knew her indifference had made an error. Even a cherished account of one's own suffering is open to correction.

The Fugitive is a story of death, but also of the end of one form of self-certainty. The narrator loses Albertine and cannot know her completely in death any more than in life. He loses the pain of that loss gradually, without being able to command forgetting. He encounters old names reshuffled into new marriages, and old paths discovered to meet. His imagined world has been contradicted, not once but repeatedly. He now possesses a greater archive of memories, misunderstandings, and desires. What he still lacks is a way to turn this accumulation into the work of art he has always wanted to write.
