# Invisible Man — The Story, Part 2: Paint, Harlem, and the Brotherhood

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At Liberty Paints, the narrator discovers that a job described as simple can be filled with hidden rules. He is instructed to add black drops to the company's famous white paint, a product whose slogan promises purity and brightness. He is later sent to work with Lucius Brockway, a Black man who has spent years mastering the factory's boilers and processes. Brockway knows that the company depends upon his skill, though the organization gives him little public honor for it. He is jealous of threats to his position and deeply suspicious of union activity.

A quarrel develops. Brockway believes the narrator may be connected to union organizers, and the argument becomes a fight. While they struggle, equipment is left unattended. An explosion injures the narrator. He awakens in a medical ward, disoriented and unable at first to recover his sense of self. Doctors experiment with electric shocks and speak about his condition as if the man in the bed were an object. He eventually leaves, with his body functioning again but his trust in the surrounding world further damaged.

In Harlem, he is taken in by Mary Rambo, a woman whose kindness asks little in return. She offers him lodging and food when he has scarcely anything. He begins to feel connected to a neighborhood rather than to a college or factory management. Then he watches officers evict an elderly Black couple from their home. Their belongings are placed on the street, and neighbors gather in anger. The narrator speaks to the crowd, drawing on the public-speaking talent he developed as a student. His words help turn private distress into collective protest.

A political organization calling itself the Brotherhood notices his talent. Its leaders recruit him as a public speaker, offer him money and a new identity, and train him in their language of social change. Brother Jack, one of its central figures, presents the group as a movement guided by an understanding of history and collective justice. The narrator wants work that matters. The Brotherhood gives him a platform and an audience in Harlem, and for a while he feels that he has finally found people who value his abilities.

He comes to know Tod Clifton, a charismatic Black organizer whose energy and intelligence impress him. They work amid competing political groups and tensions within Harlem. One of their opponents is Ras the Exhorter, a militant nationalist who rejects the Brotherhood's interracial program and sees its leaders as exploiting Black members for their own purposes. Ras challenges the narrator in public and presses him to explain whom he really serves.

The Brotherhood then begins changing its priorities. The narrator is reassigned away from Harlem despite the relationships he has built there. He attempts to follow the organization's instructions and adapt to its internal discipline. When he returns, he finds that support in the neighborhood has weakened. People have noticed the Brotherhood's absence, and some doubt whether its promises were ever serious. Clifton disappears from the organization, and the narrator searches for an explanation.

Before that discovery, Harlem has become difficult for the narrator to navigate. People who once greeted him warmly ask what the Brotherhood is doing for them now. Ras and other opponents are quick to make use of the organization's retreat. The narrator still remembers how the old couple's eviction drew people into the street and how a speech could make strangers briefly feel that they stood together. Now his own speeches are being scheduled or forbidden by people who do not live with the consequences in the same way.

He finds Clifton on a street corner selling little dancing dolls that caricature Black people. The narrator is stunned that someone he respected has taken up this degrading trade. Before he can learn what led Clifton there, a police officer confronts the seller. Clifton resists, and the officer shoots him. His death takes place abruptly, in the street, far from the formal speeches and theories that once made his work seem important.

The narrator organizes a funeral procession for Clifton and speaks before the crowd. It becomes a large public event, and the people of Harlem respond to the dignity he brings to the fallen organizer. The Brotherhood is angry. Its leaders did not authorize the funeral, and they do not want a local death to determine the organization's wider strategy. The narrator begins to see that people he knew as friends may be expendable to those who claim to represent them. In the next part, he will try to confront the Brotherhood's leaders, discover more about the lives available to a person who can be mistaken for anyone, and descend toward the underground room from which he began his story.
