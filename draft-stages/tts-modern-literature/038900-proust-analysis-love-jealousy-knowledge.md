# Proust: Love, Jealousy, and the Limits of Knowing Another Person

Suppose someone loves another person but cannot bear being unable to account for that person's movements. They ask a question. The answer is incomplete. They ask a second question, then a third. Each answer generates more possibilities. At a certain point they are no longer trying to build a relationship; they are conducting an inquiry that can never end. Marcel Proust returns to this structure first in Charles Swann's attachment to Odette and later in the narrator's destructive relationship with Albertine. It is one of the most penetrating and morally unsettling parts of In Search of Lost Time.

Begin with Swann, who does not initially find Odette irresistible. He visits a salon where she is often present; a piece of music becomes associated with the feeling of seeing her. Repetition provides intimacy, and intimacy produces dependence. When she is absent one evening, he searches Paris for her. A person who had not seemed indispensable has become the condition of his peace of mind. The pursuit is important because its immediate object is not conversation with Odette. It is relief from not knowing where she is.

Swann has immense cultural knowledge and remarkable social tact. Yet he can spend hours contemplating one gesture of Odette's as though it possessed a uniquely decipherable meaning. His intelligence becomes a liability when applied to evidence too thin to support the conclusions he wants. He considers whom she has seen, when she spoke, what she meant by a pause. A sentence remembered from weeks earlier becomes a possible confession. A courtesy offered to another man becomes a threat. Proust asks us to notice that clever inference is not the same thing as justified inference.

His relationship with the Verdurin circle adds a second layer. Swann does not observe Odette in isolation. He sees her through a group that defines membership by loyalty, creates rivalries for entertainment, and turns gossip into social power. A remark supplied by a third person can seem to confirm a fear precisely because the third person appears to know more. The salon is an engine that manufactures signals, but those signals are not necessarily reliable. People lie for reasons of ambition, embarrassment, jealousy, or sheer habit. Swann's desire selects from the available information the details he most fears.

The narrator repeats this structure with Albertine on a grander and darker scale. He is not simply injured by what she does. He is wounded by the idea that she might have a life whose private events remain unknown to him. Her possible attraction to women becomes the center of his anxiety. He asks questions whose apparent subject is her past but whose desired result is total future reassurance. Albertine can never provide that result. Even an absolutely sincere answer could not prevent him from inventing a second interpretation of it.

This is why Proust's treatment of jealousy should not be read as an endorsement of surveillance. The narrator's distress is real. His attempts to restrict Albertine are also real. The first does not excuse the second. He appoints escorts, monitors arrangements, questions witnesses, and treats her independence as a threat to his own existence. He tells himself that this is evidence of love. The text simultaneously supplies the reasons to doubt him. His peace when she sleeps arises partly because she cannot move or speak. The very condition he finds most reassuring is the one in which she can exercise the least agency.

The language of jealousy often claims the authority of investigation. That claim is worth resisting. Investigation works best when the question is answerable, the evidence can be checked, and the investigator can identify what would count against a hypothesis. The narrator's suspicion fails all three tests. Any reassuring report can be dismissed as collusion; any delay can be interpreted as concealment; an ordinary social connection can become proof of an affair. A theory that converts every possible observation into confirmation is not a method of discovering truth.

This is also where Proust complicates the reader's role. We are granted lengthy access to the narrator's thoughts, not equivalent access to Albertine's. It is easy to mistake depth of access for authority. Because we know how much he suffers, we may begin to assume that his account of her behavior is accurate. But consciousness is not the same as a courtroom record. Proust's prose can make the reader feel an inference developing from within while still exposing the inference's faults.

Consider the reported connection between Albertine and Mademoiselle Vinteuil's circle. The narrator treats acquaintance as almost conclusive evidence of sexual history. We can understand why the association terrifies him, given what he once saw at Combray. We still need to ask what it actually establishes. A person can know another person without having shared that person's intimacies. A person can conceal part of their life because disclosure would be dangerous, not because a particular allegation is true. Proust creates the possibility of jealousy without supplying a simple authoritative transcript of Albertine's desires.

After Albertine leaves, the narrator appears to be offered a final test of his attachment: he wants her back. Then she dies. If the novel were merely an investigation, death would stop the evidence and leave the verdict unresolved. Proust goes further. The narrator continues to interview people, study reports, and revise the many versions of Albertine he carries within himself. An answer from one informant opens another suspicion. The dead person cannot correct the record, and the living person's memory changes as the search proceeds.

Eventually the narrator forgets, not completely but enough to live. This is the most disturbing fact for an imagination that equates intensity with permanence. Love once seemed the defining truth of his existence. Now it has become part of his history rather than a command governing every day. Proust does not therefore announce that he never loved Albertine. He asks whether feeling ever tells us the whole truth about its object, and whether sincerity at one moment predicts sincerity at every future moment.

The same pattern appears beyond sexual love. Robert de Saint-Loup, Charlus, Madame Verdurin, and the narrator himself struggle to turn affection, admiration, rank, gifts, or patronage into guarantees. The novel distinguishes between attachment and possession by showing what happens when the first is treated as permission for the second. When Charlus believes his patronage gives him authority over Morel, an apparently different relationship reproduces the same error.

One useful way to read these chapters is to classify the narrator's statements into four categories: what he directly witnesses, what another person reports, what he infers, and what he fears. The categories are not interchangeable. His sensations may be vivid even when his inference is weak. A rumor may be accurately reported as a rumor while remaining false in fact. A frightened imagination may produce a detailed story that no one observed. The exercise is valuable outside literature because it shows how certainty can grow without new evidence.

A second question concerns reciprocity. Which demands does the narrator place upon Albertine that he does not accept for himself? He fantasizes about other women while treating her possible relationships as intolerable. He claims the freedom to change his mind while structuring her days to prevent changes he cannot control. This asymmetry is not an incidental imperfection to overlook in a romantic hero. It is a central part of the book's study of power.

Proust's lovers are not laboratories for timeless propositions about all men and all women. Their conduct takes place within particular class structures, legal relations, sexual conventions, and stigmas. But the analytic problem they reveal travels beyond that society: the mind can become attached not only to another person, but to the fantasy of eliminating uncertainty about that person. The cure is not better espionage. The novel offers no single practical cure. It does show, with devastating precision, why more information cannot solve a demand that no human relationship could ever satisfy.
