# The Waste Land: Voices, Montage, and the Problem of Who Speaks

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine listening to a radio while walking through several rooms. The radio is not tuned to the same station in each. A weather report turns into a singer, then a private call, then someone reading an old speech. You might recognize each kind of utterance, but you would not assume that all the words belong to one continuous conversation. The Waste Land produces something of that experience with the materials of poetry. It is not literally a radio transcription. It is a deliberately composed sequence that makes changes of voice part of its subject.

Start with a simple distinction: speaker, author, and quoted voice. T. S. Eliot wrote the poem. That biographical fact does not make him the person reporting every event. A person within the poem may say I; that person may be imagined, remembered, or borrowed from an older work. An older poem or drama can supply words that briefly seem to belong to the present scene. The author is responsible for placing those words, but their literary speakers have their own histories. Treating every first person as Eliot's private confession makes the poem narrower and often less intelligible.

Consider the opening. A voice speaks about spring and winter with a confident, almost general authority. Soon a different voice remembers travel and childhood. The transition does not announce itself with a character introduction. Are these entirely different speakers? Could the first be a frame around the second? A reader may debate the precise boundary, but the formal effect is clear: a statement that sounded universal gives way to a particular life, with a particular class background and memory. The poem asks us to revise our assumption about who can speak for everyone.

In the first movement, the apparently prophetic passage about dry stones shifts again into a remembered garden where intimacy brings the failure of speech. Then an advertised fortune-teller supplies a theatrical form of knowing the future. If we force these into one psychology, we lose what distinguishes them. The fortune-teller is selling or performing certainty; the person in the garden cannot make sense of feeling; the prophetic register can speak with authority even when the land shows none of the conditions for life. Each voice has a different relation to knowledge.

The same issue appears in the two rooms of A Game of Chess. The decorated room is introduced in a sumptuous, controlled literary language. The urgent questions that follow are shorter, anxious, nearly abrasive. In the pub, a narrator tells us what she said to Lil and what Lil replied, while a public call interrupts. There are already multiple levels of speaking. The poem is not only switching characters; it is switching forms of social mediation: ornament, private demand, gossip, and public regulation.

A helpful term is montage, but it should not become an excuse to stop analyzing particular words. Montage means that adjoining pieces change each other's force. A high literary allusion may sound different after pub slang than it would in a classical anthology. Conversely, a local phrase about work or sex can acquire tragic resonance by being placed next to Shakespeare. Neither part is merely illustrating the other. Their adjacency produces new meaning, often without offering a final hierarchy.

This is particularly important in The Fire Sermon, where Tiresias is presented as a witnessing consciousness. In Eliot's note, the seer has an exceptional role in connecting figures across the poem. But a theological or mythic explanation is not the same thing as a single film camera whose point of view can be mapped in every line. Tiresias is a device for bringing different sexual histories into contact. The fact that a figure can witness a scene does not settle what the woman in it inwardly experiences. An older myth can authorize perspective while exposing the limits of perspective.

Try a concrete listening exercise. Take the scene of the typist and the young man. On one pass, mark only physical action: arrival, encounter, departure, return to routine. On another, ask which words seem to evaluate the action. Are they the woman's own judgments, the seer's, the poem's narrator's, or a kind of social cliché that has been allowed into the narration? If a word makes an incident seem ordinary, ordinary to whom? By separating the positions, you can distinguish the poem's capacity to portray moral evasion from the assumption that it endorses that evasion.

A second exercise concerns the crowd on London Bridge. The visual description makes people seem numerous and anonymous; an allusion to Dante places them in a world of the dead. The commuters themselves do not speak. Their silence is a formal fact. It is one thing for a poem to accuse a crowd of spiritual death and another for it to allow the crowd to recount its varied lives. Eliot chooses the former distance here. That choice is artistically powerful, but it is also a limited kind of knowledge. Literary interpretation can acknowledge both.

Does the poem have a controlling voice at all? Yes, in the compositional sense. Its recurrence of water, death, speech, and failed intimacy is too deliberate to be accidental. But control of arrangement should not be confused with an omniscient speaker whose feelings the reader must endorse. The poem achieves coherence through patterns across differences. That is a distinctive modernist solution to a familiar artistic problem: how to represent a shared world when its inhabitants do not experience it in the same way.

One could place this experiment beside Joyce and Woolf without making the three writers interchangeable. In Ulysses, changing styles occur across an intensely particular day and city. In To the Lighthouse, an almost continuous act of attention moves among consciousnesses and transforms the reader's sense of time. Eliot's poem can shift without providing the physical continuity of a Dublin day or the household continuity of the Ramsays. Its unit is often the utterance itself, set loose from the secure story that once housed it.

A final caution. To identify the origin of a quotation is not yet to interpret its function. A line from a Renaissance play may arrive in London as a social rebuke, a death song, a taunt, or a rhythm. Ask why this voice appears at this moment, how it sounds against its neighbors, and what cannot be known from it. The poem is not a chorus in which everybody finally agrees. It is a constructed encounter between voices whose incompatibility remains audible. Its achievement is to make the fracture a form.
