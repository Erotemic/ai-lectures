# The Remains of the Day — The Story, Part 2: The Drive and the Meeting

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

In 1956, Stevens receives a letter from Miss Kenton, who has been Mrs. Benn for many years. Her marriage has had difficulties, and Stevens reads her words as a possible wish to return to Darlington Hall. The house now employs a reduced staff under its American owner, Mr. Farraday, who has encouraged Stevens to take a holiday and offered him the use of a car. Stevens decides to drive toward Cornwall, where his former colleague lives. He describes the journey as a practical expedition to recruit an experienced housekeeper. The days on the road bring him face to face with people who have no reason to accept the old assumptions governing his life.

He stops to admire the landscape, stays in inns, and takes advice from strangers. Some people mistake him for a gentleman rather than a servant. Others want to draw him into conversations about public life, politics, and dignity. In one village he finds himself among people who believe that dignity belongs to those who can take part in a democracy, not merely to people who perform their assigned roles impeccably. Stevens tries to keep the conversation polite without exposing the history of his own employment. When people ask whether he served Lord Darlington, he sometimes becomes evasive. This is striking for someone who has spent decades describing that service as the center of his identity.

He thinks repeatedly of his new employer's habit of joking with him. Mr. Farraday expects Stevens to answer teasing remarks with jokes of his own. Stevens studies the problem almost as if it were a new technical discipline. In practice, spontaneous banter defeats him. The new household no longer functions under the social code by which he learned to measure himself, and he is trying to adapt without admitting how much has changed.

The country roads also provoke memories of Miss Kenton. He recalls the times she attempted to speak with him privately, the arguments over dismissals and staffing, and the long stretches when they worked side by side. One remembered evening she learned that her aunt had died, and Stevens did not respond with the consolation she had needed. Another memory concerns her announcement that she had a suitor and was considering marriage. Stevens never asked her to stay, never offered an alternative future, and eventually watched her leave. He now rereads these incidents through a vocabulary of work and professionalism. The reader can hear how often their private meanings escaped him when they first occurred.

During his travels he also confronts the reputation of Lord Darlington. The name no longer inspires only reverence. People know of the man's association with British sympathizers of Nazi Germany and the failed attempts to influence European politics before the war. Stevens has long believed that a loyal servant should not judge his employer's decisions. On the road, however, other people are plainly willing to judge them. The further he drives from the Hall, the less secure his old explanation becomes.

At last Stevens meets Mrs. Benn. Time has passed since they worked together. They are no longer a butler and housekeeper arguing over arrangements in a great house. She speaks about her marriage, the difficulties she and her husband have experienced, and the life she has made away from Darlington. She has thought about leaving her husband, but they have reconciled. Their daughter is expecting a child, and she is looking toward becoming a grandmother. The prospect of returning to the Hall is not the future she now wants.

In the course of their conversation Mrs. Benn admits that she has sometimes wondered what life might have been if she and Stevens had chosen differently. She speaks with care, neither declaring an old romance nor pretending there was nothing between them. Stevens feels a profound private loss. In his account he does not suddenly become a man capable of saying everything that has gone unsaid. Their meeting ends. They part, and there is no invitation to begin again together.

Stevens reaches the seaside at Weymouth and sits near the pier as evening approaches. He speaks with a stranger who has also worked in service. The other man talks about taking pleasure in the later part of the day rather than spending it mourning what has passed. Stevens listens. He tries to think about what remains of his own life: the job at Darlington Hall, the new employer, and the small possibility of learning to behave differently with the people around him.

He resolves to improve at banter for Mr. Farraday. This resolution may sound trivial after so many lost opportunities, but it is the sort of action Stevens can imagine taking. He has not reclaimed his father, corrected the dismissal of the Jewish servants, rescued Lord Darlington from his politics, or found a life with Miss Kenton. The long-anticipated meeting has not brought him a new housekeeper or a partner. The story ends with an aging man preparing to return to his work, aware, perhaps more than before, of what his careful life has cost.
