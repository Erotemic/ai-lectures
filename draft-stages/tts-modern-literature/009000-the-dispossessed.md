# The Dispossessed: Freedom on Both Sides of the Wall

## A wall that faces both ways

At a spaceport on a harsh moon, people have constructed a wall. It marks the boundary between their society and a landing field from which travelers may depart to the neighboring planet. The wall is physically modest, but it carries immense political force. On one side are people who understand themselves as free from property, states, and masters. On the other lies a world whose wealth and institutions they regard as forms of domination. When a physicist named Shevek decides to cross, some of his neighbors treat his journey as betrayal.

The wall opens Ursula K. Le Guin's *The Dispossessed*, published in 1974. Its political and literary project is announced in a subtitle: *An Ambiguous Utopia*. The story gives us an anarchist society that has achieved a great deal and developed its own habits of conformity, and a wealthy, technologically advanced society whose beauty depends upon inequality. Neither can be understood by translating it into the other's propaganda. Both contain people capable of care, selfishness, courage, compromise, and violence.

Shevek was born on Anarres, the moon settled by followers of a revolutionary thinker called Odo. He is a gifted theoretical physicist working on the nature of time. His desire to exchange ideas with scholars on Urras, the planet from which his people departed, brings him into conflict with institutions that claim not to exercise coercive authority. On Urras, where he hopes to find openness, he discovers other means of controlling knowledge. His problem is not simply to choose the better planet. He must learn how an institution can betray its own principles and what one person can do without pretending to live outside society.

The novel is carefully built around two histories. One follows Shevek's life on Anarres, from childhood toward his departure. The other follows his visit to Urras after the departure has happened. These sequences alternate, so that the reader knows he will cross the wall before understanding the experiences that made the crossing necessary. The structure is an argument about time, political change, and the possibility of return.

We will examine the societies as imagined institutions, the personal relationships that make their costs visible, the novel's temporal design, and the question of what scientific knowledge belongs to. We will also consider the author's own documented interest in anarchism, and why a work sympathetic to an ideal can be most convincing when it refuses to make its adherents flawless.

## Reading toward an anarchist novel

Ursula K. Le Guin had studied literature and grown up in a family deeply engaged with anthropology and storytelling. Her fiction often asks readers to inhabit a society whose assumptions do not align with their own. In the early 1970s, as she developed *The Dispossessed*, she researched anarchist political traditions and utopian literature. In an interview published by *Fifth Estate*, Le Guin recalled reading the anarchist books she could find in Portland and named Peter Kropotkin and the Goodmans among important influences.

She also explained the sequence of her imaginative problem. She had begun with an idea of a physicist who seemed to be at odds with the society around him. Reading anarchist thought helped her discover what sort of society that might be and why his conflict with it would be serious. The political philosophy was therefore not merely attached to a completed adventure. It helped her recognize the shape of her protagonist's predicament.

Her account contains another valuable detail. Le Guin spoke without pretending that she herself lived a perfectly anarchist life. She was raising children and writing within an ordinary network of family and professional commitments. The distance between a philosophy and the conditions under which a person lives it became part of the novel's imaginative honesty.

Anarchism here does not mean chaos or the absence of all organization. The Odonians of Anarres coordinate production, assign tasks, educate children, respond to shortage, and maintain collective institutions. Their ideal is to avoid domination by property owners or a coercive state. They understand work as mutual obligation rather than service to a master. The question is whether an organized society can preserve that aspiration as it becomes older, more complex, and more accustomed to its own arrangements.

The book won major science-fiction awards, including the Nebula and Hugo for best novel. Its achievement, however, is not a political prize announcement disguised as fiction. It is an examination of how ideals become habits, how habits become pressures, and how the pressure to belong can restrict precisely the freedom a community was founded to protect.

## Two worlds, two kinds of possession

Anarres is ecologically severe. Scarcity is present in ordinary decisions: where one works, what can be stored, how food is distributed, and how households are organized. Its society has inherited Odo's critique of private property, profit, and institutional domination. People share resources through systems of collective distribution and use a language, Pravic, whose habits of expression reflect their social ideals.

These arrangements have consequences beyond economics. A person does not simply assume that a child belongs to parents as a possession or that a job belongs to a worker as a permanent entitlement. Work and care are shared through arrangements meant to prevent private command over others. These ideals can produce genuine solidarity. They can also expose people to demands made in the name of everyone.

Urras, by contrast, is environmentally abundant and economically divided. A-Io, the state in which Shevek spends much of his visit, offers impressive institutions, universities, luxury, and a formal hierarchy of wealth and status. Other states and conflicts complicate the landscape. It would be misleading to treat the planet as a single uniform capitalist society or to imagine that all its inhabitants enjoy the prosperity Shevek is shown.

Shevek initially encounters the polished surfaces: comfort, scientific recognition, rooms and meals that seem almost unlimited compared with Anarres. The very abundance disorients him. Items that are not needed for survival are treated as possessions, symbols of rank, and reasons for competition. Those habits can feel natural to their owners and strange to a visitor raised to distinguish use from ownership.

The contrast is more complex than the assertion that scarcity produces virtue and abundance produces corruption. Anarres's scarcity can justify harsh collective demands. Urras's wealth can support genuinely valuable scholarship and beautiful material achievements. The question is who controls the use of each society's resources and whether people can challenge the arrangements without becoming enemies of the social order.

The wall is an image of this mutual exclusion. From Anarres, it seems to keep exploiting outsiders away. From the spaceport, it seems to confine the community it claims to protect. The physical object has no natural political meaning by itself. It acquires meaning through what people are prevented from doing across it.

## The child's prison game

The novel's backward-moving strand begins in Shevek's youth and shows how a person learns to inhabit a society before learning to criticize it. Children devise games, test rules, form friendships, and discover the emotional power of being included or excluded. One early game involving confinement becomes a disturbing model of coercion among people raised to reject prisons. They have no official prison system to imitate in daily life, yet they can produce domination through play.

The episode should not be flattened into a claim that children are naturally cruel and therefore anarchism cannot work. Its more precise function is to separate institutions from impulses and habits. A society may abolish a formal coercive structure while its members retain the ability to frighten, shame, and control one another. The absence of a prison building does not guarantee an absence of social confinement.

Shevek grows through educational arrangements intended to encourage curiosity and cooperation. His intellectual abilities become apparent, and so do tensions about his manner of thinking. Scientific inquiry often requires sustained solitude and attention to a problem whose value is not yet obvious to a group. A system organized around shared work must decide how to accommodate that kind of activity without turning intellectual prestige into a new hierarchy.

This is not a contrived issue. A mathematician may need time to follow an idea before knowing whether it will help anyone. A society that demands immediate utility can suppress discoveries. A society that grants unlimited privileges to scholars can reproduce the class divisions it rejects. Shevek's work exposes a genuine problem of institutional design, not merely the villainy of individual bureaucrats.

The alternating narrative makes us watch those tensions develop. We have already seen the adult Shevek leave. Childhood scenes become evidence explaining why a departure that looks traitorous to neighbors might be an attempt to defend the community's founding commitment to freedom.

## When a drought reorganizes a life

Anarres's environment makes collective labor an ongoing necessity rather than an abstract statement of solidarity. During periods of scarcity, people are asked to take difficult assignments, travel far from familiar relationships, and help sustain systems whose benefits are shared unevenly in time. Shevek's own life is affected by such work, and the resulting separations are not best understood as malicious acts by a secret ruler.

The problem is more difficult. A society dedicated to mutual aid needs ways to respond to emergencies. It cannot guarantee that everyone will perform the task they most enjoy. Yet the same vocabulary of necessity can be used to discourage questions about whether a burden has been fairly distributed, how long it must last, and whether a particular person's capacities are being ignored. Coordination has a justified purpose; it also needs accountability.

A lecture about utopia should take the hardship seriously. If Anarres were abundant, the politics of voluntary work would be easier to idealize. Le Guin chooses a place where survival requires collective action, so the philosophical question becomes concrete. How much personal freedom can an individual responsibly claim when others need help? Who judges the urgency, and who can challenge that judgment without being treated as selfish?

This tension makes the later disagreements about scholarship more intelligible. Shevek's mathematical work may serve a distant public good, but it does not immediately distribute food or repair infrastructure. It is possible for a community to value science in principle while repeatedly treating the time required to do it as an indulgence. Protecting inquiry under scarcity demands an institutional imagination as serious as protecting labor under emergency conditions.

## Sabul and the bureaucracy without rulers

Shevek's scientific career brings him into contact with Sabul, an older physicist who controls access to established channels of publication and exchange. Sabul's authority is complicated because Anarres officially lacks the kind of office through which a state might command a scholar. Nevertheless, reputation, access to translations, control of correspondence, and institutional positions allow a person to obstruct another's work.

Sabul can appear as a small-minded rival, but the institutional issue extends beyond his character. People who monopolize a practical bottleneck may exercise power without holding a title that admits the power exists. The society is vulnerable because it has difficulty naming domination when domination no longer resembles a boss giving orders or a police officer making an arrest.

The Production and Distribution Coordination system, often associated with the PDC, and the Division of Labour arrange tasks and resources. These structures are necessary to collective life on a resource-poor moon. The novel's criticism concerns the difference between coordination and unquestioned authority. The more a procedure becomes treated as natural and final, the harder it becomes to argue that it should change.

Shevek's friend Bedap helps him understand these tendencies. Their conversations are important because Bedap is neither a visitor preaching against Anarres nor someone who has forgotten its ideals. He sees real accomplishments worth defending. His dissatisfaction arises from discovering that the society's customary forms of approval can punish independent inquiry and personal difference. The strongest criticism in the book comes from within the tradition being criticized.

A society without legal ownership can still possess people through expectations about acceptable work, relationships, and speech. The metaphor of possession moves from property to social pressure. Anarresti are taught that no person should own another, yet they may claim authority over a neighbor by appealing to the community's needs. That claim is sometimes justified. It becomes dangerous when the community cannot be questioned about whose needs count and who interprets them.

## Takver, care, and the work of living

Takver is not simply the love interest placed beside a solitary scientific hero. Her work, convictions, and practical commitments allow the novel to examine the relationship between intellectual freedom and mutual responsibility. Shevek and Takver form a partnership within a society whose ideals make possession suspect and fixed personal arrangements open to scrutiny. Their relationship requires them to decide what lasting commitment means when neither wants to own the other.

The story gives care a material dimension. People must cook, work, travel for assignments, rear children, endure separation, and respond to shortages. Freedom may include the possibility of refusing domination, but it also depends upon reliable forms of attention and support. Takver and Shevek's relationship is not free because they are unaffected by social demands. It is meaningful because they continue negotiating those demands in ways that attempt to preserve each other's capacity to act.

Periods of separation are painful. Work assignments and personal choices pull them into different places. These events show that institutions can burden a household without acting through explicit prohibitions on love. There may be no law against partnership, yet arrangements meant to serve the public good can make a particular family's life difficult. The personal and the political are connected through ordinary time.

Takver also recognizes that Shevek's work could matter beyond his private career. When he considers the dangerous journey to Urras, her response cannot be reduced to self-sacrificing support for a genius. She has reasons to fear the consequences, reasons to value the work, and a life that will continue with real responsibilities if he goes. Her judgment helps turn the journey into a choice made within a relationship rather than an escape from one.

This is important for the book's account of intellectual creativity. Scientific discovery does not emerge from a mind untouched by obligation. Shevek is formed by teachers, colleagues, partners, friends, and critics. His theory may be original, but his capacity to continue pursuing it depends on a social world capable of supporting inquiry and enduring disagreement.

## Crossing to Urras

When Shevek reaches Urras, he is received as a celebrated visitor. The university and its patrons provide comfortable rooms, access to scientists, and public honor. To someone from Anarres, the abundance can seem both seductive and disorienting. The welcome has conditions he does not immediately understand. His hosts have interests in his work, and their hospitality can become a form of control.

Urrasti scholars are not interchangeable conspirators. Some show real curiosity, possess important expertise, and value scientific conversation. The danger is that the institutions governing them may claim the results. The distinction between an honest collaborator and the system within which the collaborator works is as important here as it was in the Anarresti conflict with Sabul.

As Shevek learns more about the surrounding society, he discovers the exclusions concealed by the official tour. Poverty, class hierarchy, gender restrictions, and state violence are present in places his hosts have little reason to show him. Urras's apparent freedom of consumption depends on people's unequal control over resources and choices. The visitor is asked to admire a prosperous society through selected evidence.

The contrast with Anarres gains a second dimension. On the moon, people sometimes restrict free inquiry in the name of solidarity. On the wealthy planet, people sometimes restrict free inquiry in the name of national advantage and property. The motives and institutions differ; the danger to the circulation of knowledge can resemble itself across the wall.

It is therefore misleading to describe Shevek's trip as the discovery that one world is simply worse than the other. He learns to see both societies more clearly because each reveals what the other's self-description conceals. His commitment to the Anarresti ideal can deepen precisely as he becomes less willing to defend every Anarresti practice.

## Language as a form of political memory

Pravic, the language of Anarres, carries habits shaped by Odonian ideals. The people who use it have been taught to be wary of language that presents another person as an owned object. Their ordinary expressions can make different assumptions about possession and social status feel natural. A language in a fictional society is more than a list of unfamiliar nouns; it is one of the media through which people learn what their neighbors expect.

But the relation between language and freedom is not mechanical. Speaking without a conventional vocabulary of ownership cannot guarantee that no one will dominate others. Sabul can obstruct Shevek through professional networks even while participating in a culture that officially rejects domination. A person can use language shaped by mutual aid while shaming a neighbor for an independent choice. The novel's invented vocabulary therefore invites inquiry without supporting the conclusion that grammar alone determines political life.

The distinction has consequences for reading the novel's political comparisons. Anarres's ideals exist in education, work, speech, and the ordinary organization of care. They are real achievements. The same arrangements can become so familiar that members cease to notice where practices diverge from principles. A critical tradition needs the language not only to express its ideals but to describe how its own people may violate them.

Shevek's work of communication across worlds is thus connected to the smaller work of finding words inside his own community. If criticism is immediately dismissed as disloyalty, even a society devoted to free speech can become difficult to address. The most important test of a language of freedom is whether people can use it to name the new forms of power that freedom has made possible.

## Gender, prosperity, and the price of comfort

Le Guin's depiction of Urras includes gender roles that Shevek finds restrictive and unfamiliar. Elite women may be confined to social display and private influence rather than recognized as independent participants in scientific and public life. His encounters with them expose the mismatch between the promise of civilization and the distribution of meaningful freedom.

One must also examine the limits of the novel's own perspective. A visitor's judgment can reveal injustice, yet the women he meets are not always granted the same sustained interiority as the male physicist whose journey organizes the book. A critique of patriarchy can still represent women through the perceptions of a man whose own assumptions require scrutiny. Treating Le Guin as a serious writer includes allowing readers to notice where the thought experiment does less than it intends.

Scenes of fashionable hospitality become politically revealing when Shevek realizes how much work and hierarchy are hidden beneath surface ease. A wealthy household can look welcoming while depending upon servants and restrictions the host need not explain. Comfort offers its own kinds of blindness. People can inhabit a world organized around their desires and mistake its arrangements for the natural order of things.

Urras also contains people who contest those arrangements. A visitor who sees only an oppressive state may miss the resistance of the people who live within it. Shevek's growing involvement with dissent brings him beyond the university's protected world. The novel does not reserve ethical agency for the politically idealized moon. Urrasti people also organize, risk their safety, and imagine alternatives.

The danger is real because the state can respond with force. Public demonstrations make visible a conflict the university could not resolve through polite scholarly exchange. Shevek must decide how to act when hospitality and professional advancement depend upon keeping distance from suffering that has become impossible to ignore.

## A theory of time and a theory of freedom

Shevek's research concerns the nature of time. Within the fictional science, two accounts—often called Sequency and Simultaneity—seem difficult to reconcile. One emphasizes ordered succession; the other points toward relationships that hold across temporal separation. Shevek seeks a general theory capable of accounting for both. The novel does not provide a usable physical theory that a reader could derive and test against our universe. Its invented physics has a structural role in the story.

The book's alternating chronology enacts a related problem. One set of chapters moves forward through Shevek's visit to Urras. Another moves forward through the earlier life that made the visit possible. As the strands alternate, the reader holds two temporal perspectives in mind. Causes and consequences are encountered out of ordinary order, but the eventual structure gives them a coherence neither strand would provide alone.

This is more than a decorative analogy between science and fiction. Shevek's struggle to connect the separated accounts of time resembles his struggle to create communication across divided societies. He wants knowledge to move between people who have inherited reasons not to trust one another. The formal structure lets us experience the difficulty and necessity of making connections across a gap.

The scientific result has technological implications, especially for an ansible: a device allowing effectively instantaneous communication across interstellar distances. Urrasti institutions also hope for forms of rapid transport that the theory does not straightforwardly guarantee. Shevek understands that an equation can be valuable in more than one political project. If one state owns the result, it may gain power over the others. If the result can be shared, it may make communication possible across formerly isolating distances.

The novel therefore asks a serious question about scientific ownership. Does discovery belong to the person who produced it, the community that sustained the researcher, the institution that provided instruments, or the state that can exploit it? None of those claims can be dismissed without argument. Shevek's decision develops from his refusal to let any one authority make exclusive possession the final answer.

## Sharing a discovery

On Urras, Shevek's work attracts military and political interest. He begins to understand that his hosts' admiration has not made his research free. It has placed the work near people with the power to decide where it will go. The possibility of a communication technology is especially consequential because controlling communication can reshape relations among worlds.

Shevek takes steps to prevent a single state from monopolizing the theory. His decision to share it more widely is a practical expression of the political ideal he has spent the novel struggling to understand. The act is not a simplistic rejection of scientific credit. The discovery still has an author and a history of effort. It is a rejection of the idea that the author or a powerful patron must thereby acquire the right to exclude all others from its potential benefits.

The choice also resists the fantasy that an ingenious technology will solve political conflict by itself. Faster communication may make understanding possible. It can also transmit propaganda, commands, or threats more efficiently. The novel's final hope lies in the possibility of exchange, not in a machine that eliminates the work of living together.

Shevek turns to representatives of other worlds, including a Terran ambassador whose knowledge of Earth's damaged history complicates his account of progress. The presence of these outsiders prevents Anarres and Urras from remaining a closed moral universe. Both societies are part of a larger world, and neither has the authority to define what civilization must become.

The story's scientific conclusion is therefore ethical without becoming a technological fable. Shevek must decide to whom he will speak and what power he will allow others to exercise over his work. The equations do not make that decision for him. The responsibility belongs to the person and the communities affected by the choice.

## The demonstration and the limits of innocence

Shevek becomes involved in protest against Urrasti power, and the consequences reveal how quickly a wealthy society can abandon its image of refined order. The state responds to dissent with violence. The demonstration provides a severe contrast with the sheltered conditions of his university appointment. The institutions that welcomed a famous scholar are connected to institutions capable of suppressing the people he has begun to meet.

Yet the scene should not be reduced to a simple revelation that all Urrasti are oppressive or that Shevek has at last discovered the virtue of Anarres. Protesters have their own commitments and disagreements. The risks they take are not an instructional display arranged for the visitor. Their lives continue beyond his limited encounter with them, and his understanding remains incomplete.

The novel's sympathy for resistance comes alongside a critique of self-righteousness. A person can correctly identify injustice and still misunderstand the choices of those living within it. Shevek's political education is partly an education in listening rather than treating others as instances of his preferred moral categories.

This is especially relevant when the book is discussed as a utopia. A utopian novel may present a set of institutions as an answer. Le Guin asks what it would take for a society to remain capable of criticizing its own answers. The decisive quality is not permanent innocence. It is the ability to recognize new forms of domination and to revise institutions that have become self-protective.

The demonstration also clarifies the cost of public action. On Anarres, Shevek risks disapproval and exclusion when he challenges norms. On Urras, state violence can place dissenters in immediate mortal danger. These pressures differ in scale and mechanism. Recognizing a pattern across the worlds must not make us erase that distinction.

## Return without a guaranteed welcome

The novel's final movement returns Shevek toward Anarres. His journey has not made him a prophet returning with a completed social blueprint. He has learned more about the strengths and failures of both worlds, acted on his convictions, and accepted uncertainty about his reception at home. People who resented his departure may still resent him. The structures he criticized have not dissolved in his absence.

The alternating chapters make the return especially moving. We know, from the opening, that he left. The chapters of childhood and adulthood have shown why he had to. Now the question is whether he can return to a society without pretending that its ideals have already been achieved. Returning becomes part of revolution understood as an ongoing practice, rather than a final event after which criticism is no longer needed.

The novel refuses the comfort of a perfect reconciliation. It asks whether people can remain committed to a community while confronting its limitations. Shevek's answer involves risk, relationships, and continuing work. He returns because freedom is something to be made with others, not a private territory obtained once and for all.

The wall is still there. What changes is the meaning of crossing it. At the beginning, departure appears as separation from one's people. After the parallel histories have converged, the same crossing can be understood as an effort to prevent inherited boundaries from becoming permanent truths. A structure built to protect freedom may need to be opened if it has begun to confine the people it serves.

## Ambiguous utopia as a method of thinking

The subtitle *An Ambiguous Utopia* is a reading instruction, though not one that demands equal praise or blame for every institution. It asks us to hold political ideals open to empirical and moral examination. Anarres demonstrates real possibilities of collective provision and resistance to private domination. It also demonstrates social pressure, bureaucracy, and the capacity to deny those failures. Urras demonstrates abundance, scientific resources, and human creativity, while placing them inside severe inequalities and state violence.

The novel is also an invitation to think about institutions as changing systems. A successful arrangement may create new interests in preserving itself. A language of liberation may become a language of conformity. A university's commitment to inquiry may be compromised when a government wants exclusive control of results. These are dynamics one can study without deciding in advance that all political forms are equally oppressive.

Its characters keep the investigation human. Bedap's friendship offers a voice of internal criticism. Takver's commitments give freedom a domestic and temporal dimension. Shevek's intellectual frustration tests a system that must coordinate labor. Urrasti protesters resist in circumstances more dangerous than an elite visitor first understands. None of these lives can be replaced by a comparison of constitutions on a chalkboard.

Le Guin's form gives the reader something the characters rarely possess: simultaneous access to the life that prepared a decision and the consequences that followed it. The two temporal sequences teach us to revise easy judgments. A visitor who looks disloyal at the spaceport becomes intelligible through a childhood of cooperation, disappointment, discovery, and love. The story's time structure makes possible a richer form of political understanding.

## The freedom of an unfinished revolution

Shevek's most important insight may be that a just society needs the capacity to change its own arrangements. Freedom is endangered when a state claims total ownership of resources, but also when a community treats its customs as incapable of error. The danger is greatest when a principle originally intended to resist domination becomes a reason to silence the people who identify new forms of it.

The novel's closing question is therefore practical. How can a community coordinate work without making coordinators unanswerable? How can it support scientific inquiry without reproducing a privileged caste? How can a relationship create lasting trust without turning one partner into the other's possession? How can knowledge be shared without making every discovery an instrument for those with the most power?

None of these questions has a final solution in the book. That is not a failure of its imagination. The uncertainty is precisely what makes its utopian project credible. An ideal can guide experiments, criticism, and reform without pretending to settle every future conflict. Shevek crosses the wall and returns because he wants a society capable of living with that openness.

Le Guin began with a physicist who did not quite fit his world. She gave him two worlds to examine and a form that requires us to understand both past and present. The lesson is neither that we must choose solitude over society nor that society must always overrule the individual. It is that people can build institutions worth defending while retaining responsibility for what those institutions become. The wall can protect. It can imprison. The question is who gets to open it, and what they are prepared to learn on the other side.

### Structure and comparison

- **Narrative device:** alternating chapters follow Shevek's visit to Urras and his earlier Anarresti life. Each sequence advances forward within itself; their convergence explains the departure.
- **Anarres:** resource scarcity, Odonian mutualism, labor coordination, anti-property ideals, informal conformity, and gatekeeping in science.
- **Urras:** contrasting states, wealth, class divisions, university resources, gender roles, military power, and dissent.
- **Scientific axis:** fictional General Temporal Theory and ansible; communicate potential applications while distinguishing communications from unproven instantaneous transport.
- **Ethical axis:** sharing research without monopoly, free inquiry within social obligations, return to imperfect community.

### Institutional questions

| Institution or relationship | Legitimate role | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Work assignment coordination | Match labor and scarcity | Informal coercion and unreviewable customs |
| Scientific publication network | Distribute and validate knowledge | Gatekeepers controlling credit and correspondence |
| University hospitality on Urras | Support research and exchange | Exclusive appropriation of discoveries |
| Partnership of Shevek and Takver | Long-term care and mutual help | Pressure from collective needs and distance |
| Interstellar communication | Connect separated communities | Concentration of technological advantage |

### Discussion exercises

1. Explain why removing private property cannot by itself remove social power; use a specific Anarresti scene.
2. Compare Sabul's leverage with the Urrasti state's interests in Shevek's research. Where do their mechanisms differ?
3. Analyze why the alternating chronology changes the interpretation of the first chapter.
4. Choose a scene with Takver and explain how it gives mutual obligation a cost as well as value.
5. Distinguish the ansible as a fictional communication device from the more speculative project of instantaneous material transport.
6. Explain why making Shevek's theory public is an ethical act without assuming that communication technologies eliminate conflict.
7. Analyze the final wall as an image with two politically different meanings.
