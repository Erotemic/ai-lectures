# Contact: What Would Count as Evidence for Another World?

A critical lecture about Carl Sagan's novel, focusing on scientific knowledge, testimony, power, religious belief, mathematical wonder, and the novel's distinctive scientific-process narrative.

## The experiment and the witness

Imagine the scientific team at the end of Carl Sagan's *Contact*. Ellie Arroway and four colleagues say they have traveled through an immense network connecting distant parts of the galaxy. They describe a place no Earthbound observer has visited. Each offers an account of meeting an intelligence vastly more capable than humanity. But the machine that carried them seems to have accomplished nothing visible, only a short interval has elapsed, and every recording that might have settled the matter has been erased.

The first half of the book taught the reader how hard Ellie works to reject mistaken inferences. She knows a radio signal might be an accident, a malfunction, a natural phenomenon, or a prank. She relies on independent observatories, mathematical patterns, and arguments that opponents can examine. And then the novel deliberately places her in the position of somebody whose most important knowledge is a memory she cannot independently verify.

That reversal is the central engine of *Contact*. It is often described as a story about science and religion, and it certainly is. But that formula can be too quick. The deeper subject is what happens to knowledge when a discovery passes from a private experience into institutions that determine whether a claim will be believed. The novel asks what counts as evidence, who gets to interpret it, and what a person owes to the truth when public agreement is unavailable.

The distinction matters because the book does not ask us to choose between a clever scientist and foolish believers. Ellie is frequently right, but other scientists are also capable of good-faith doubts. The religious characters disagree with one another. Government officials sometimes behave appallingly but must still confront genuine security risks. No single position provides a completely adequate response to contact with a civilization beyond Earth.

## Why the story spends so long on methods

Sagan was an astronomer, a public communicator of science, and a prominent advocate of the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. *Contact* appeared in 1985, after years in which radio astronomy, planetary exploration, and speculation about other civilizations had been part of both serious research and popular imagination. Sagan did not merely borrow a spaceship from science fiction and place an adventure inside it. He constructed a novel around the institutions and practices through which a discovery might become scientifically credible.

That is why Project Argus matters. Ellie does not discover a readable telegram simply by pointing a telescope at a convenient star. A possible signal has to pass through instrumentation, interference checks, repeated observations, interpretation, and argument. The people doing this work may be brilliant and still be wrong. They have careers and rivalries. They may distrust each other's conclusions for sound reasons and for less admirable ones. The whole project is embedded in decisions about funding, telescope time, and what inquiries society considers worth pursuing.

A reader accustomed to adventure plots might regard the long meetings and technical discussions as delays before the real action. But the procedures are much of the action. A scene in which scientists independently confirm a sequence from Vega is a dramatic scene about humanity becoming entitled to say something it had never been entitled to say before. The discovery is not merely that Ellie believes she has heard a voice. It is that several observers, applying different checks, are unable to explain the structure as natural noise or local interference.

Consider the first simple numerical pattern. Prime numbers carry no specifically human language. They are not secret passwords supplied by Sagan's imagination. They belong to a mathematical structure that two independently developed intelligences might share. But even here the conclusion is not automatic. An apparently ordered sequence might have a natural explanation, and a scientific account must ask whether the pattern remains after one corrects for instruments and statistical expectations. In the fiction, the evidence becomes convincing through its layered structure and independent confirmation, not by the narrator announcing that the protagonist's intuition cannot fail.

The transmission then reveals the image of Hitler at the 1936 Olympics. This is an ingenious storytelling choice. Instead of introducing distant beings through a neutral symbol or an idealized portrait of humankind, Sagan stages an encounter mediated by one of Earth's own political catastrophes. A civilization sending back an early television broadcast might be returning an identifying token. Yet what that token means to its senders could differ radically from what it means to its recipients. They may have no idea what a human knows about Hitler. Communication can succeed at one level and fail at another.

The scene also refuses to let humanity imagine itself innocent before the new audience. The first question is not simply whether other beings will look like us. It is whether they will understand what we have done, and whether we understand what we are revealing.

## A global project with unequal power

When the transmission becomes a blueprint for the Machine, the book's scale shifts from observatory to planet. Sagan imagines international cooperation, but he refuses to make it frictionless. There are negotiations over access, national prestige, military implications, commercial development, and selection of the travelers. The message is cosmic; the decisions about it are thoroughly human.

This produces one of the novel's more interesting tensions. A machine designed for human passengers seems like an invitation. Yet an invitation from an unknown power can also be an instrument of control. The characters cannot establish the builders' intentions before they respond. A technologically superior civilization would not need to ask permission to harm Earth, some argue; others answer that inferiority makes every confident guess dangerous. Both are reasoning under uncertainty.

The five-seat design is especially important because it prevents us from treating Ellie as the only possible representative of humankind. The final travelers include Vaygay Lunacharsky, Devi Sukhavati, Abonnema Eda, and Xi Qiaomu. They carry different histories and kinds of expertise. Sagan's multinational cast is an aspiration toward planetary representation, but a five-person crew is still a very small selection. The question of whose voices stand for Earth has not been solved by putting flags from different nations beside the same vehicle.

It is also telling that the Machine cannot be assembled from the plans alone without an industrial revolution in miniature. Technical knowledge is a social achievement. Engineering depends on institutions, labor, materials, supply chains, and money. A blueprint is not the finished artifact; a design can be readable and still unattainable. Sagan makes the gap between receiving knowledge and possessing its capabilities into an event that changes the world.

Hadden complicates the triumph of cooperation. His private wealth and technical influence help unlock the Message and make a hidden Japanese Machine possible. He achieves what public institutions may have lost after the bombing in Wyoming. Yet it would be hard to call this a straightforward defense of private enterprise. Hadden is ingenious and indispensable in the plot, but his interests are not the same as everybody else's. He can make unilateral decisions on a scale that other people cannot even examine. His personal obsession with longevity also sets him apart from scientists who seek knowledge that can outlive them without preserving their own bodies.

A civilization can decide to build a grand machine for common understanding and still be forced to confront who owns its parts. That is not an obsolete concern of the Cold War. It is a recurring question about every large technical system, and Sagan lets it be felt through a concrete story.

## Science and religion are not symmetrical contestants

Palmer Joss and Billy Jo Rankin are both religious figures, but they are not interchangeable. Rankin approaches the extraterrestrial message with a politics of alarm and doctrinal defense. Joss is more interested in what happens when different convictions encounter evidence neither can ignore. His conversations with Ellie are arguments between people, not cardboard signs reading Faith and Reason.

Still, the novel does not claim that religious testimony and scientific measurement have the same function. Joss can describe his transformative experience and say that he is certain of its meaning. Ellie can listen respectfully and still ask what would permit somebody else, who was not there, to distinguish it from a mistaken interpretation. She is right to make that distinction. A sincerity that deserves respect does not therefore supply a general procedure for establishing public facts.

Then the ending reverses their places. Ellie has had an experience she cannot reproduce; Joss accepts her testimony. It would be easy to summarize this as Sagan forcing his atheist scientist to admit that science and faith are identical. But the story is more complicated. Ellie has not forgotten the difference between first-person conviction and publicly testable evidence. She continues to want evidence. She begins a computational search precisely because she knows memory and trust alone will not persuade other investigators.

The fictional reversal asks readers to feel the human cost of a principle they may still accept. If our procedures for knowledge are sound, they may require us to suspend public judgment about a person telling the truth. The answer is not to abandon the procedures. The challenge is to inhabit the interval between knowledge as someone experiences it and knowledge as a community can warrant it.

The book also makes room for a less comfortable possibility. Perhaps the universe is not organized to answer our most personal religious questions, even if intelligence is abundant. The beings Ellie meets are not gods. They maintain an inherited galactic network and admit that they do not know who made it. Their technological superiority has not eliminated uncertainty. This detail prevents the encounter from becoming a final revelation that closes inquiry.

## Ellie, her father, and the danger of reducing a person to an argument

Ellie's childhood and family life are often treated as emotional material surrounding the scientific plot. But they are bound into the book's structure. Her love for Theodore makes the later encounter with a simulacrum of him bearable; her conflict with John Staughton makes the final letter transformative. The two fathers in her story force her to reconsider what she believes she knows about the people closest to her.

The alien's decision to appear as Theodore might initially seem a cheap way to guarantee that Ellie will trust it. It is also an act of interpretation. A species able to construct a transportation system across the galaxy nevertheless chooses to make itself understandable through one human being's memory. It cannot simply offer Ellie the universe in an abstract, neutral form. Communication requires accommodation to the listener.

The human cost is apparent in Ellie's response. The encounter feels like the return of someone she lost, but she is not foolish enough to believe that the being is literally the father who died years earlier. The scene permits wonder and loss to coexist without pretending that technical knowledge can repair every broken relationship.

The later revelation about Staughton pushes harder. Ellie discovers that the man she has treated as an intrusive stepfather is biologically her father, whereas Theodore, whose care shaped her identity, was not. This is not proof that all perceptions are wrong or that blood matters more than lived affection. It is a reminder that a person's account of family can be sincere, deeply consequential, and incomplete. The emotional revelation mirrors the layers of the cosmic Message without being reducible to an equation about them.

Sagan deserves credit for giving Ellie intellectual agency in a professional environment that often withholds it from women. Yet the novel also exposes limitations in its own characterization. At times, personal relationships appear arranged to illustrate philosophical stances: the skeptical scientist, the thoughtful believer, the combative preacher, the powerful patron. Contemporary reviewers differed over whether the scientific argument was more vivid than the people carrying it. One can admire the ambition of a scientifically detailed first-contact novel while acknowledging that not every relationship has the density of its ideas.

This is a critical question worth asking rather than a verdict we must accept. Can a novel about planetary institutions create characters whose interior lives feel greater than their designated positions in a debate? Sagan sometimes does, especially when private grief interrupts Ellie's professional certainties. At other moments we can see the explanatory design underneath the scene.

## Why the ending uses pi

The final circle hidden among the digits of pi is among the novel's boldest inventions. We should be careful about what sort of claim it makes. Pi is a mathematical constant defined independently of any civilization. Its digits depend on the base in which we write it; the same number looks different in base ten and base eleven. The book imagines that at an enormously remote position in the base-eleven expansion, an extended arrangement of zeros and ones can be laid out into a recognizable circle. In the novel, this is a fictional finding, not a real result about pi.

We also have to distinguish an astonishing pattern from proof of a designer. In a long enough sequence, many apparently meaningful patterns may appear, and deciding whether one is unexpected requires care about what we searched for and how many possible patterns we would have accepted. Nothing about the actual mathematical definition of pi supplies evidence that a cosmic intelligence inserted a symbol there. Sagan knows the appeal of that idea is speculative. His fiction asks us to imagine the consequences of such a sign becoming compelling.

The ending also alters the scale of the investigation. A radio transmission from Vega might have been sent by a local civilization. A wormhole network near the galactic center might have been assembled by older civilizations. But pi is not a structure belonging to one star system. If a genuine intentional mark could be found in the mathematics all observers share, the question would no longer be who sent the Message or who built the Machine. It would be who, if anyone, is responsible for the deeper architecture within which all machines and messages exist.

Notice the circle. Sagan does not end with a block of divine instruction. He gives us a shape simple enough for a child to draw and a mathematical distance so large that almost no ordinary human effort can approach it. This returns Ellie to her earliest curiosity without rewarding her with a final answer. The story ends with the possibility of a new question that can be investigated.

The premise has a philosophical vulnerability. A number like pi is not something we ordinarily think of as a physical object that a technologically skilled being can edit. The idea of encoding a message inside its digits invites questions about what it would mean for a universe to have been chosen or constructed differently at the level of mathematical laws. The novel does not solve that problem. It uses the problem to widen the imaginative horizon. That is legitimate science fiction, not established cosmology.

## What kind of contact has occurred?

At one level, *Contact* ends with humanity having achieved almost nothing it can publicly demonstrate. The most consequential trip in its history has been hidden behind an official account of failure. At another, the world is transformed: the received signal has already passed independent scientific checks, industries have been built, geopolitical coalitions shifted, and the possibility of companionship in the cosmos has moved from speculation to documented communication.

Those are different kinds of contact. There is contact between distant civilizations, contact between scientific communities, contact between antagonistic governments, contact between Ellie's convictions and Joss's, and a painful new contact between Ellie and the truth of her family. Some are successful. Others remain partial or misinterpreted. The book is not a celebration of frictionless connection. It portrays the labor needed to cross a distance that is physical, cultural, political, or emotional.

One of its most demanding questions remains with us. What would we do if the most important discovery of our lives arrived in a form that did not fit our existing procedures for being certain? If we gave up all demands for evidence, we would become vulnerable to deception. If we refused every claim that begins in an experience we cannot yet replicate, we might confuse intellectual discipline with a refusal to learn.

Sagan's story does not hand us a perfect balance. It gives Ellie the harder task of continuing to investigate. She neither abandons the cosmos for the comfort of a private vision nor betrays what she has experienced to obtain official approval. She returns to work, now aware that the community of knowers may lag behind the reach of an individual life.

In that sense, the ending is not a victory for faith over science, or science over faith. It is a defense of inquiry as a practice large enough to include wonder without declaring wonder to be evidence. The universe may contain companions, inheritances, and mysteries far older than ourselves. The response worth carrying home from *Contact* is not a finished creed. It is a commitment to keep asking, checking, and communicating, even when the answer changes who we thought we were.
