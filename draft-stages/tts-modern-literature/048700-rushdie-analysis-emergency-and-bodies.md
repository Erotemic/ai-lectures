# Midnight's Children — The Emergency: When a State Claims the Future

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

At the end of Midnight's Children, the political state discovers a terrible way to answer an imagined threat. The children born with independence have different powers, divergent opinions, and no single political organization capable of ruling the country. Nevertheless, their existence troubles an authoritarian government in the novel, which imprisons and sterilizes them. The attack is directed not only at what they can do now, but at what they might create later. A government acts as though the possible future must be subdued before it arrives.

To understand this climax, distinguish three layers. The first is documented history. India was placed under a state of Emergency from June 1975 to March 1977 under Indira Gandhi's government. Civil liberties and press freedoms were restricted, political opponents detained, and programs of demolition and sterilization enforced with coercion. Scholars identify the political circle around Sanjay Gandhi as particularly important in several of these campaigns. People faced concrete threats to homes, legal protections, and bodily autonomy. This history does not depend on the supernatural premises of Rushdie's fiction.

The second layer is the novel's invented action. Parvati and Saleem live among Delhi's magicians; their settlement is destroyed; Parvati is killed; Shiva captures Saleem; and the network of midnight children is broken through forced procedures. The targeted magical generation does not exist in documentary records. It is a fictional concentration of real fears about state power over reproduction, neighborhood life, and dissent. Failing to separate these layers can lead to two different mistakes: treating fantasy as history, or dismissing the history because the narrator is fantastical.

The third layer is interpretation. Why would a novel about independence culminate in a campaign against the generation born at independence? One answer is that a republic can turn against the promise of its own founding when power becomes more important than plural citizenship. The midnight children are not all virtuous or wise, but their abilities make difference visible. The Emergency's fantasy of control treats difference itself as a problem. That is an argument we can derive from the arrangement of events; it is not a verbatim statement of the government's actual motives.

The body is crucial. Earlier in the book, Saleem's physical peculiarities seem to mark him out for grandeur. His nose links him to his grandfather and later becomes a tool of sensing. His illnesses, accidents, and surgery transform what he can know. In the Emergency scenes, bodily vulnerability ceases to be a private embarrassment or miraculous gift. The state claims the authority to decide whose body may reproduce and whose home may continue to exist. The move from comic bodily detail to coercive bodily policy is one of the book's deepest changes in tone.

Shiva's role also matters. Once the resentful counterpart to Saleem, Shiva has become a man able to exercise organized force. He is not simply an opposite symbol: force against language, poverty against wealth. He makes choices and accepts forms of power that turn his personal grievances into other people's suffering. We should therefore resist a crude destiny story in which the boy raised poor had no possible future except brutality. A critique of inequality does not require the removal of personal responsibility.

Saleem's coerced disclosure of the other children's identities raises a more painful issue. What is betrayal when someone is threatened by an overwhelming institution? The novel gives no clean heroism to Saleem. He has failed his peers before, through ambition and exclusion. But when he is imprisoned, the balance of moral agency changes. A critic should be able to name his actions without treating the captive as though he possessed the same power as the state that imprisoned him. The distinction makes moral judgment harder, not weaker.

The destruction of the magicians' settlement connects bodily regulation to urban space. Poor people often live where governments and property systems render them insecure. Demolition is not just the loss of buildings; it disperses work, care, community, and the memory of shared daily life. In the novel, Parvati dies amid that destruction. Her death should not be converted immediately into an elegant symbol. It is a personal calamity, experienced by people whose lives the state can treat as obstacles to an administrative plan.

The Emergency ends through political change. The 1977 election and the end of emergency rule matter because they show that institutions and collective action have a history beyond the reign of a single leader. Yet the novel does not offer restoration as full compensation. Those sterilized do not simply regain what was taken. The dead do not return. This gap between a change of government and a repaired life is necessary to the book's moral scale.

A reader might test this analysis by comparing two kinds of intervention in Saleem's story. Mary Pereira switches babies to overturn class injustice and produces damage she could not control. The Emergency government claims a public purpose for far more systematic intrusions into people's lives. The acts are not equal in scale or moral gravity. But their contrast reveals the danger of assigning a person a role in a social design without obtaining that person's consent. Rushdie's novel repeatedly asks what happens when an abstract future is treated as worth more than the lives through which it must be made.

The final image of a cracking body makes the problem visible once more. Saleem cannot contain his country's history, and the state cannot legitimately force the nation's future into one shape. The most consequential political lesson of this fantastic ending may be simple to state, even if the novel reaches it by extravagant means: a nation is not preserved by controlling the bodies through which its people live.
