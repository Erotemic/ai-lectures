# The Lord of the Rings — The Story, Part One: The Fellowship of the Ring

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

The story begins with a celebration, not a prophecy. Bilbo Baggins is turning one hundred and eleven, and the Shire expects the sort of extravagant party for which its wealthiest eccentric has become famous. Bilbo has a comfortable home, a great deal of money, and a tendency to speak as though the roads beyond the Shire are more interesting than the neighbors. His younger cousin and adopted heir, Frodo, is turning thirty-three. The guests eat, drink, and speculate about what Bilbo intends to do next.

Bilbo has already decided. Before the evening ends, he uses a little gold ring to disappear in front of the assembled company. His joke is also a farewell. He wants to leave Bag End, visit old friends, and perhaps finish the book he has been writing about his journeys. Gandalf, a traveling wizard who knows more about the Ring than Bilbo does, insists that he leave it behind for Frodo. For a moment that request turns the affectionate old hobbit angry and possessive. Then he recovers himself and goes. Frodo inherits the house and the mysterious ring, and life in the Shire resumes.

Years pass. Frodo lives quietly, though he is less satisfied with quiet than most of his neighbors. Gandalf comes and goes, and finally returns with troubling news. He has learned that the Ring is not merely a device for invisibility. Long ago, the dark ruler Sauron made it as an instrument of domination. Other great rings were given to powerful people, but this one was designed to rule them. Sauron lost it in an earlier war. A creature named Gollum kept it for centuries beneath the mountains, until Bilbo found it. In the meantime, Sauron's power has been growing again in Mordor. He knows the Ring survived. His servants are searching for the place and family associated with Bilbo's name.

Gandalf's discoveries have taken years. He has consulted records concerning the last great defeat of Sauron and learned how Isildur, one of the victors, took the Ring rather than surrender it for destruction. Isildur later died when the Ring was lost in the waters of the Anduin. The object vanished from recorded history for a long interval before reaching Gollum. Gandalf has also tracked the changing story that Bilbo told about finding it. Bilbo first treated the Ring as a prize Gollum willingly gave him, then gradually admitted a less comfortable truth. The small inaccuracies matter because the Ring can make the possessor want not merely to keep it, but to feel justified in keeping it.

Gandalf tests the Ring in Frodo's fireplace, where writing appears that neither ordinary heat nor ordinary sight could have revealed. Its identity is beyond doubt. If Sauron recovers it, the free peoples of Middle-earth may not survive the balance of forces he can bring against them. But taking it and using it to oppose him would be dangerous even in noble hands. Gandalf refuses to possess it. So does Frodo's first impulse to ask him to take responsibility. The Ring must leave the Shire, and Frodo must carry it until wiser people can decide what to do.

The decision is frightening partly because the Shire is worth protecting. Frodo knows the lanes and gardens, the habits of old families, and the pleasures of being overlooked. He is not a prince preparing to regain a throne. He is a property owner trying to arrange his departure without alarming friends and servants. He sells Bag End to unpleasant relatives, buys a small home farther east, and makes the journey look like an ordinary change of address. His gardener, Sam Gamgee, has been caught listening to Gandalf's conversations and is instructed to accompany him. Sam knows little of the politics of far kingdoms. He does know that he will not let Frodo go alone.

## The road out of the Shire

Frodo, Sam, and Pippin Took set off through familiar countryside whose safety is suddenly uncertain. A rider dressed in black approaches. His movements are controlled, searching, and unnervingly quiet. Frodo feels an almost physical pressure to put on the Ring and disappear. They hide while the rider searches, then continue by fields and paths that had seemed harmless before. Another meeting with the riders nearly traps them, until traveling Elves appear and offer temporary shelter. Frodo begins to understand that places can still look gentle when danger is already moving through them.

The hobbits reach the farm of Farmer Maggot, who has once frightened Frodo for stealing mushrooms as a child. Age has changed the balance between them. Maggot now helps them rather than scolding. At the river they meet Merry Brandybuck, who has made preparations for the planned move and knows considerably more about Frodo's actual intentions than Frodo suspects. Merry and Pippin have watched his secret planning. They choose to come with him, despite the dangers. Their companionship ceases to be a secret before the travelers have left the familiar world.

For a while the plan depends on people staying behind as well as people leaving. At Crickhollow, the little house purchased for Frodo's supposed retirement becomes a decoy. Fredegar Bolger, a friend who knows enough about the danger to be frightened, remains there while the others slip away. Later, riders searching for Frodo break into the area and drive him from his hiding place. The effort to leave without alarming the Shire has not kept every friend safe. The travelers are beginning to discover that secrecy is a burden distributed to those who protect them, too.

Instead of crossing open country where the riders may be waiting, the hobbits enter the Old Forest. This is not a harmless preserve. Its trees seem hostile to paths and visitors. One ancient willow traps Merry and Pippin in its roots and trunk. A strange figure called Tom Bombadil arrives, singing, and releases them. He lives in a house with Goldberry and seems to belong to a different order of reality from the anxious, organized contest over power. The Ring does not command him as it does others. But he does not become the solution to the problem: he is careless in ways that make him a poor guardian of an object that must be kept from Sauron.

Leaving Tom's house, the hobbits become lost among burial mounds. A malicious spirit traps them underground in a cold, ceremonial darkness. Frodo finds the courage to call for help instead of abandoning his friends. Tom returns and frees them. From the barrow's treasure they take small swords of old manufacture. These weapons carry a history they cannot yet appreciate. One will matter in a battle much later, and far from the Shire.

At Bree, a small town where hobbits and larger folk live near one another, the travelers enter an inn called the Prancing Pony. Frodo has been told to wait for news from Gandalf, but Gandalf is absent. Frodo makes himself conspicuous in the common room and, in an alarming accident, puts on the Ring. To the patrons he seems to vanish. A weathered stranger called Strider has been watching him. Strider warns that the Black Riders, servants of Sauron, are close, and that the travelers will not survive without a guide. He knows details that persuade Frodo to trust him, despite his appearance and the natural fear of accepting a stranger's protection.

The innkeeper has delayed a letter from Gandalf that would have explained Strider's identity. The stranger's real name is Aragorn. He is one of the Rangers who have long protected northern lands without asking the ordinary inhabitants to recognize their work. He takes responsibility for guiding the hobbits east toward Rivendell, a refuge where Elrond may know what can be done with the Ring.

Their departure is miserable. Their lodging is attacked during the night, their ponies are lost, and they must travel on foot through land where every hill may expose them. At the ruined watchtower of Weathertop, riders overtake them. Frodo, overwhelmed by terror, puts on the Ring. The action makes him visible in a different way to his pursuers. A rider strikes him with a cursed blade. Aragorn drives off the attackers, but a fragment of the weapon remains in Frodo's wound. If it completes its work, Frodo will be drawn into the wraithlike condition of the riders themselves.

For days he becomes weaker. His companions can only move him onward. They meet the Elf-lord Glorfindel, who puts Frodo on a swift horse for the final rush toward the river guarding Rivendell. The riders pursue. Frodo crosses the Ford of Bruinen, scarcely conscious. The river rises in a flood called against the pursuers, scattering their horses and temporarily breaking their attack. Frodo loses consciousness before he can know whether he is safe.

## The house of memory

He wakes in Rivendell. Elrond has tended his wound and removed the fragment, though the hurt may leave consequences. Gandalf is there. So is Bilbo, older and changed, living among Elves and books. The reunion is tender. Yet the Ring already makes tenderness precarious. When Bilbo asks to look at it, Frodo suddenly sees his beloved relative as threatening, as though an old person asking for a keepsake could become a ravenous stranger. The impression passes. Frodo is ashamed. It is one of the first signs that the Ring does not merely endanger those who may one day possess it; it also changes the bearer from within.

Rivendell also changes the nature of Aragorn's story. The Ranger who looked like a hard-traveled stranger in Bree belongs to a family whose history reaches back to the kingdoms of Men and Elves. He has known Elrond's household for years, and his love for Arwen, Elrond's daughter, is bound up with difficult questions of duty, mortality and the future of a kingship. None of this makes Aragorn's immediate job easier. He can claim an ancestry, but he still has to persuade companions to trust him on an unfamiliar road. The story lets Frodo's eyes uncover a human being beneath the traveler, not a ready-made king simply waiting to be announced.

Elrond calls a council. Representatives have arrived from many places because the same threat is reaching them in different forms. The Dwarf Gimli brings news of pressure upon his people. Legolas speaks for woodland Elves. Boromir, heir to the ruling Steward of Gondor, has traveled from the South, where his people have long held the military line against Mordor. Aragorn is revealed as the heir of an older royal line. The council hears how the Ring was lost, how it came to Gollum, how Bilbo found it, and why it is now in the Shire. Gandalf reveals that the wizard Saruman, once regarded as the leader of their order, has been corrupted by his desire to control the conflict. Saruman detained Gandalf in his tower at Isengard, and Gandalf escaped only with outside help.

For Boromir, the Ring seems to offer a terrible possibility. His city is defending people who can no longer afford defeat. Why should they throw away the enemy's greatest weapon? Others know that no lasting victory can be built on that foundation. The Ring cannot be made into a clean, obedient tool. Its maker's will and the desire to command are involved in its very use. The council determines that it must be destroyed in the fire where it was forged, inside Mount Doom in Mordor. This is almost an impossible destination. There is no army that can simply march there carrying it openly.

In the quiet after long disagreement, Frodo offers to take it. His willingness is not based on military skill or a confidence that he will succeed. The task has come to him, and he will try to carry it. Sam insists on going too. A small company is formed: Frodo, Sam, Merry, Pippin, Gandalf, Aragorn, Boromir, Gimli, and Legolas. Nine travelers will oppose the nine chief wraiths hunting the Ring. Their task is to accompany and protect the bearer, not to become a conquering army.

The company leaves Rivendell in winter. They travel by night and keep their purpose hidden. Already the decision to move through wild country exacts a cost. Their first attempt to cross the mountains by a high pass fails when snow and wind close the way. Boromir and Aragorn struggle to keep the hobbits from freezing. They must turn back. Their alternatives narrow to a dangerous underground route through the ancient Dwarven realm of Moria.

## Under the mountains

Even reaching Moria has a cost. A dark shape rises in the water beside the western entrance and attacks the company. It pulls down the door after them, closing the route by which they might have retreated. Sam has had to release Bill, the pony who helped carry their burdens from Bree. For Sam the parting matters almost as much as the loss of their equipment. A companion need not speak to be loved, and the journey keeps asking its members to leave behind living creatures they cannot protect. In the dark they know neither whether the pony can find home nor whether any of them will see daylight again.

Moria was once a place of magnificence. Its halls are still immense, but the civilization that built them has vanished from sight. At the western gate the travelers must discover how to open doors framed by Elven letters. Inside, their torchlight falls on empty chambers and abandoned workmanship. There is no welcome. Even Gimli, who hoped to find signs of his kinsman Balin's colony, can read the silence only with increasing dread.

In an old chamber they find Balin's tomb and the shattered record of the colony's final days. The book describes being surrounded, losing corridors, and fighting enemies who moved closer as hope disappeared. The travelers are scarcely finished reading when their own attackers appear. Orcs pour through the doors, and a huge creature of fire and shadow pursues them across the halls. Gandalf recognizes an ancient enemy whose power belongs to a depth of history the younger companions scarcely understand. On a narrow bridge he holds the creature long enough for the others to pass. The bridge breaks. The monster falls, drawing Gandalf with it into darkness.

The assault that drives them out of Moria is not a contest the companions can win by being individually skillful. Aragorn's blade, Legolas's arrows and Gimli's courage have limits. They can hold a doorway or make a passage safe for a few moments, but they cannot reverse the return of an ancient power to a ruined kingdom. Gandalf's sacrifice is terrifying precisely because his companions had counted on him to understand what others could not. Now the world exceeds the guide's safety as well as theirs.

For the surviving company, there is no time even to know what has happened. They rush from the eastern gate into the daylight, shocked and bereaved. Gandalf was their guide, their interpreter of events, and often their practical hope. Aragorn must lead them now. The company enters the woods of Lothlórien, where the laws of ordinary time seem suspended among trees of great age. The forest's guardians do not readily welcome strangers, particularly a Dwarf, because old quarrels still shape relations between peoples who claim to oppose the same enemy. Gimli and Legolas must learn to cross that history without pretending it never existed.

The rulers of the wood, Galadriel and Celeborn, receive them. Galadriel understands something of what the Ring demands and of what its destruction will cost even those who do not serve Sauron. She offers Frodo a chance to see things in a mirror: not firm predictions, but images of danger, possibility, and places he loves. Sam sees damage in the Shire and wants to return. Frodo fears for his friends and his home, yet still has to carry the Ring forward. Galadriel herself is offered the Ring by Frodo. She could imagine ordering the world for good, replacing a dark tyrant with an overwhelming queen whose beauty and power would be impossible to refuse. She turns away from the offer. The Elven order she has helped preserve may fade, but it must not survive by becoming what it opposes.

The company rests and grieves. They receive cloaks, food, boats, and gifts suited to their differing needs. Galadriel gives Frodo a small light held in a crystal vessel, Sam earth for a garden and the seed of a tree, and the others things that will become useful in ways their hosts cannot fully explain. When the travelers leave, the forest recedes behind them with the sorrow of something that cannot be kept.

They move down the river Anduin in small boats. The landscape widens, then becomes more threatening. The company feels watched. Far upstream they saw a strange shape following them through the dark water; Gollum, drawn to the Ring, has not abandoned the trail. On the river, Boromir argues more urgently that they should turn toward Gondor. His people need help, and their city stands on the enemy's threshold. Aragorn must decide whether to take the great road to his own inheritance or remain with Frodo as the Ring-bearer. Frodo asks for time to think.

## The breaking

On a wooded hill near the river, Boromir follows Frodo. His need is sincere. He believes that the defenders of Gondor are being asked to die while an astonishing means of resistance is carried away toward destruction. He tells himself that a strong and honorable man could wield the Ring and save thousands. His reasoning becomes force. He tries to take it. Frightened, Frodo puts it on and escapes. Boromir, suddenly aware of what he has done, is crushed by remorse, but the harm cannot be taken back.

Wearing the Ring, Frodo experiences the scale of the struggle. His mind is drawn toward a distant searching will, and he nearly exposes himself to Sauron. He takes it off and makes his decision. If the Ring can divide the company simply by being carried among brave and decent people, it is too dangerous to expose more of them to its influence. He will continue alone to Mordor.

Sam understands Frodo well enough to guess this plan. He reaches the river as Frodo tries to leave in one of the boats and nearly drowns in his haste to follow. Frodo cannot persuade him to stay. The two hobbits cross to the eastern shore together, heading toward a land for which they have neither a safe road nor a reasonable expectation of survival.

Behind them, the remaining members of the company are scattered. Merry and Pippin have gone looking for Frodo. Aragorn searches for the others. Boromir has confessed his wrongdoing and will soon have to decide what he can still do for his companions. Armed enemies are closing in, sent from competing powers whose ambitions have not always been properly understood even by the travelers.

The Fellowship of the Ring ends without the reassuring victory of a completed mission. There is no longer one fellowship walking together. Frodo and Sam are on the wrong side of the river, two unarmed-seeming figures heading toward the enemy's country. Their friends are separated, exposed, and uncertain who is alive. What has been broken is the belief that courage and common purpose automatically preserve a group from its own fears.

Yet something survives the breaking. Boromir's temptation has not made everyone suspect one another forever. Frodo's decision is partly a recognition that friendship cannot be reduced to possession or control. Sam's stubborn refusal to abandon him is both exasperating and indispensable. Somewhere ahead are Gollum, the marshes, and Mordor. The second movement of the story will follow separate roads and show how deeply the fates of those travelers remain connected.
