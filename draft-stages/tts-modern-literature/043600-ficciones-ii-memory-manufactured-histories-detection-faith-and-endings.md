# Ficciones II: memory, manufactured histories, detection, faith, and endings

## Ficciones: Funes the Memorious — A Mind Without Forgetting

The person who remembers everything should be the perfect witness. Ask when an event occurred, what someone wore, what the weather looked like, and he could recover the answer without distortion. But an experience contains more than an event label. It contains countless changing qualities, and a mind that retained each one exactly might have very little room left for thought. Borges turns this possibility into the story of Ireneo Funes.

A narrator resembling Borges remembers a young man from Fray Bentos, in Uruguay. Early in their acquaintance, Funes impresses him by knowing the time without consulting a watch. He is sharp, reserved, and a little intimidating. There is nothing at first to suggest that this young man will become the center of a philosophical nightmare. Later, an accident changes his life. A fall from a horse leaves him unable to walk, and it seems to give him a memory of almost unbearable resolution.

Funes does not simply remember things other people would forget. He can recall the exact arrangement of clouds on an afternoon and distinguish it from another afternoon with a precision the narrator can scarcely imagine. Every perception seems available to him, complete with color, shape, sound, and temporal location. A room in his memory is not a schematic room whose furniture remains approximately where he last saw it. It is a sequence of countless appearances, each one as particular as a different object. The flood of particulars makes the present nearly intolerable.

The narrator comes to visit him at night. They converse in the dark while Funes reveals an extraordinary command of details and language. Funes has worked on systems of numbering and on attempts to replace general terms with exhaustive labels for specific things. A conventional number system reuses a manageable collection of signs to represent indefinitely many quantities. Funes imagines systems in which every number has its own designation. His inventions may be feats of memory, but they sacrifice the very generality that makes numbers useful to someone who has to calculate.

Then the narrator notices a deeper difficulty. Funes appears almost incapable of thinking in the way that requires abstraction. An ordinary person says that the animal seen from two angles at two moments is the same dog. For Funes, the distinct appearances are overwhelmingly particular. Grouping them under one identity can seem a careless erasure of difference. Yet without the ability to disregard some variations we cannot compare, classify, reason, or even sustain a stable concept of a familiar thing.

There is a trap here for the reader. Funes has suffered a physical injury and lives with an acquired disability; the story does not entitle us to infer that actual people with memory differences lack thought. Borges is inventing an extreme case to illuminate a relation between cognitive functions. The narrator himself is not an infallible clinician. He recalls a conversation from years earlier and openly arranges his recollection in language. We receive a representation of perfect recall from a witness whose own memory is ordinary and necessarily selective.

The scene is also social. Funes lives in a provincial environment, and the educated visitor has access to books and a language of intellectual prestige. The young man's capabilities disturb the hierarchy between them. Funes learns rapidly what the visitor has worked hard to acquire, but he does not gain the visitor's power to move freely through the world. Their encounter has admiration, condescension, discomfort, and curiosity, not simply a neat diagram of memory systems.

The story's central question is not whether remembering is bad. It is what forgetting makes possible. To speak of a tree, a friend, a nation, or a number is to omit almost everything about the individual instance before us. Those omissions are not necessarily mistakes; they are the conditions under which we can recognize a pattern and apply knowledge somewhere else. But abstraction is dangerous too. The visitor's neat categories can conceal aspects of Funes's experience he is unwilling to see.

Funes's strange capacity makes visible a bargain ordinary minds accept constantly: some detail is lost so that some relation may be retained. Borges refuses to treat that bargain as either a simple victory or a simple tragedy. The young man's perfect recollection gives him an incomparable intimacy with the immediate world and may prevent him from sharing much of it. A person who cannot forget anything has not necessarily understood everything.

## Ficciones: The Shape of the Sword and The Traitor and the Hero — Two Manufactured Histories

Two Borges stories use an old political injury to expose the arrangements by which a person or a nation comes to possess an official story. They are set around Irish independence, but neither is a straightforward historical account of that movement. One is a confession hidden in a false perspective. The other is a patriotic biography hiding a staged execution.

In The Shape of the Sword, a narrator encounters a man in South America who bears a conspicuous scar across his face. The man, known by an English identity, tells of his involvement in the Irish struggle. In the story he presents, he once sheltered a younger revolutionary named John Vincent Moon. Moon was educated and spoke with convictions, but he was frightened by physical danger. The older fighter saved him during an encounter with British troops and took him to safety.

As the fighting intensified, the fighter continued to act while Moon found reasons to remain hidden. The older man grew contemptuous. One day he overheard Moon betraying him to the authorities in exchange for money. Furious, he chased Moon through the house. In a violent confrontation he marked the traitor's face with a sword. We seem to know, at last, why the storyteller associates the scar with betrayal.

But the listener notices a missing conclusion. What happened to Moon? The scarred man points to his own face and admits that he is Moon. He has told the history from the position of the man whom he betrayed. The image of a courageous fighter was a disguise that allowed him to approach his shame without naming himself. We must now revise every earlier judgment, not simply add a surprise ending. A witness's words may have been selected by a person whose relation to the event differs from the one we imagined.

Theme of the Traitor and the Hero begins with another writer, Ryan, preparing a biography of his ancestor Fergus Kilpatrick, an Irish revolutionary whose death became a national legend. The story is openly presented as a possible plot; its premise involves Ireland and an imagined nineteenth-century conspiracy, not an independently documented historical murder. Ryan is troubled when details of the assassination resemble scenes from Shakespeare. A speech or circumstance seems to have been borrowed from literature rather than recorded from spontaneous life.

Research leads Ryan toward the organizer of Kilpatrick's movement, James Alexander Nolan. He discovers that Kilpatrick had been exposed as an informer. To reveal the betrayal openly might have destroyed a revolutionary cause. Nolan arranged a theatrical killing in which the compromised leader would die as a publicly mourned martyr. The city itself became part of the stage. The speeches, portents, and memorable gestures were planted in advance so that later generations would remember not the betrayal but a heroic sacrifice.

The historian faces a choice. He can publish what his inquiry suggests, or he can preserve the history his community is prepared to honor. Borges has Ryan withhold the discovery and write the reverent account. The reader is left to ask whether Nolan foresaw even this future: a historian who would uncover the truth and consent to conceal it. The project has turned the acts of one generation into a script for the memory of another.

These are different forms of misrepresentation. Moon's account is a wounded individual's evasion of his own responsibility. The revolutionary myth is a collective project, designed to make a death productive for a political cause. In both cases the telling matters as much as the event. We learn what a narrator is after discovering how he has made us assign the roles of hero and traitor.

There is a danger in praising such tricks only as brilliant construction. In the first story a man was actually betrayed. In the second a community has surrendered evidence for solidarity. A carefully made narrative may be an artistic achievement while remaining morally costly to the people who have to live under it.

Borges's lesson is not that all heroes are traitors or that all history is propaganda. It is that names like hero and traitor depend on whose account survives, and that a historian may discover a deliberate literary design inside what everyone else accepts as a national memory. A story about the past can be true in its facts and manipulated in its meaning; it can also become popular because the manipulation is exactly what its audience wants.

## Ficciones: Death and the Compass — The Detective Walks into His Solution

A rabbi is found murdered in a hotel. His name is Marcelo Yarmolinsky. He was attending a gathering of scholars, and the circumstances admit an ordinary explanation: a criminal has entered the wrong room and killed him. A police inspector, Franz Treviranus, is willing to consider a mundane robbery gone wrong. The detective Erik Lönnrot prefers a deeper pattern.

Lönnrot is not merely intelligent. He is committed to the belief that a sequence of murders can be reconstructed as a formal design. A line left near the first body seems to refer to the secret name of God. He searches works on Jewish mysticism and the letters of the divine name, convinced that the murderer is composing a series in which geography, number, and theology fit together. The more evidence can be interpreted symbolically, the more alluring the investigation becomes.

A second death takes place in another part of the city. Another inscription suggests a continuing sequence. Then comes an apparent third crime, with circumstances theatrical enough to invite interpretation. Witnesses describe figures in disguise, and another message is found. To the ordinary inspector, the case has become baffling. To Lönnrot, its geometry is nearly solved. He imagines the crime scenes as points in a pattern whose last position has not yet been occupied.

A letter and a map seem to indicate that three crimes complete an equilateral triangle. Lönnrot sees instead the possibility of a fourth point, one that will form a symmetric figure. He believes the final event must occur in the south, at the abandoned estate called Triste-le-Roy. Against the habits of a detective who coordinates with other people, he goes to the site alone. The house itself seems to reward his intellectual appetite, its passages, rooms, and repeated shapes reflecting the puzzle in which he is already enclosed.

There he finds the criminal Red Scharlach. The true design of the murders is personal, not liturgical. Scharlach bears a grievance against the detective connected with his brother and has decided to lure him into an isolated place where revenge will be possible. The first killing was an accident Scharlach exploited. Subsequent crimes and signs were staged to flatter the detective's taste for learned patterns. The symbolic labyrinth was not an explanatory key to the murderer's original intention. It was bait made specifically for the man who tried to solve it.

The detective's failure has been prepared by his own success at noticing associations. Lönnrot does find the correct fourth location. His inference is accurate about where the killer wants him to go; it is catastrophically wrong about why. A model can predict the next observation and still give a false account of the mechanism generating the data. The story separates a pattern that fits from an explanation that deserves trust.

At the end Lönnrot imagines a different labyrinth, a simpler one made from a line rather than elaborate geometry, for a possible future encounter with his enemy. He is still thinking in formal designs. Scharlach answers with the practical instrument of revenge. The detective has brought an abstract problem all the way to its lethal consequence.

One should also notice what is at stake in the use of Jewish sacred learning. The dead scholar is not a mere symbol, and antisemitic suspicions circulate as part of the public response. The killer manipulates the detective by taking advantage of fragments of a tradition the detective finds intellectually exotic. An actual death is obscured when a victim's scholarship is converted into a cryptographic prop. Borges's narrative exposes the danger of detached erudition while also exploiting the machinery of the detective tale for aesthetic pleasure.

A common detective story promises that clever attention to clues will restore order. This one fulfills and reverses the promise. Clever attention to clues gives Lönnrot a coherent route, and the route belongs to the villain. His mind is not defeated by its inability to find a pattern. It is defeated by its confidence that the pattern tells him whom to trust.

## Ficciones: The Secret Miracle and Three Versions of Judas — Time, Faith, and Interpretation

In The Secret Miracle, a writer is arrested in Prague during the Nazi occupation. His name is Jaromir Hladík, and he is Jewish. He has written and translated books, and he has an unfinished play that matters to him more than any reputation he has won. German authorities have condemned him to death. Nothing in the public machinery of the occupation seems open to appeal.

Hladík thinks about his work while awaiting execution. The play, The Enemies, exists partly in his mind. He has not completed it, and he is ashamed of what remains unfinished. In a dream or a prayer he asks for a year in which to finish the composition. The request is not simply for survival. It is for the completion of an artistic act he imagines may justify the time he has lived.

At the firing squad, a drop of rain hangs in the air and the outward world stops. The men remain motionless. The body's time has been suspended, but Hladík continues to think. He discovers that he has been granted the subjective year he sought. Without paper, movement, or an audience, he works through his play in memory. He corrects, reconsiders, and finally completes it. Then the halted instant resumes and the bullets kill him.

No one present can witness the miracle in the ordinary sense. The rescue happens neither to the body nor to the career. The finished drama will not be performed. The miracle has given its recipient the capacity to finish something, not the power to display the result. Borges invites two responses at once: awe at the private perfection of the gift, and grief at a world in which such a gift is imaginable only because history has made the writer so helpless.

Three Versions of Judas uses an entirely different form. It reads like a scholarly account of an imagined theologian, Nils Runeberg, whose publications reexamine the betrayal of Jesus by Judas Iscariot. Runeberg offers successive interpretations that become more extreme as he attempts to defend a theological proposition against familiar objections. In one approach Judas is conceived as a necessary counterpart to the divine sacrifice; in another, Judas's voluntary acceptance of infamy becomes a terrible kind of renunciation.

In Runeberg's final, scandalous hypothesis, the divine abasement has been misunderstood from the beginning. What if the figure of absolute humiliation is not the publicly honored savior but the condemned betrayer? His argument reverses an established framework, and each attempt to save the system from contradiction makes it stranger. Other theologians reject or ignore him. He dies with his discovery unaccepted, or perhaps with the conviction that he has uttered something the world cannot endure.

It matters that Runeberg is a fictional theologian. This is not a report that an actual scholar settled Christian theology. Nor is it evidence that Borges personally endorsed the final hypothesis. The story is an experiment in what happens when a thinker insists that an interpretive system must be consistent, and then follows its implications into territory its community will call heretical.

The pairing brings a paradox into focus. Hladík receives a private miracle that no one can verify from the outside, while Runeberg produces public arguments that no one will believe. One story places completed art inside an instant inaccessible to observers. The other places an internally coherent theological construction inside a community that refuses to regard it as revelation. Both ask how a person could know that a form of meaning is real when recognition is denied.

There is no need to rank belief above skepticism to understand the force of these inventions. The stories derive much of their power from refusing easy proof. The occupation of Prague and the violence against Hladík belong to recognizable human history, even though his stopped time is fictional. Runeberg's interpretations belong to an invented scholarly world, even though they engage a genuine religious tradition. Borges moves between historical horror and metaphysical possibility without asking the reader to mistake one for the other.

A year may fit inside one instant; an apparently familiar religious figure may occupy the opposite role from the one tradition assigns him. What joins the stories is the suspicion that meaning depends on a frame the world cannot readily see. Borges explores that suspicion in forms that make the desire for certainty both intelligible and dangerous.

## Ficciones: The End, The Sect of the Phoenix, and The South — Three Later Stories

Three stories became part of the familiar expanded Ficciones in 1956, after the original 1944 publication. They belong to very different kinds of narrative: a continuation of an Argentine epic, a riddle written like ethnography, and a journey to the pampas whose last moments may be experienced in more than one way. Their shared presence at the end of Artifices gives the collection a different final weight.

The End revisits the world of Martín Fierro, José Hernández's celebrated nineteenth-century poem about a gaucho and his conflicts with authority. Borges imagines an episode the epic does not supply in this form. A man confined by illness observes, or partly perceives, a guitar player waiting for someone in a rural store. The waiting man is a Black guitarist whose family has a history with Fierro. Eventually the famous figure arrives. The guitarist confronts him over his brother, who was killed by Fierro. A duel follows. In Borges's continuation, Fierro is killed by the man who sought revenge.

The title appears to close the life of a legendary character, but the ending is also an opening onto a moral aftermath. The victor now bears the burden of an act of killing. A character who has been cherished as a national icon can become, in another person's story, the murderer of a family member. By writing an ending to an admired text, Borges brings the omitted life of a secondary figure into the center of a new work. There is no innocent vantage point from which the national epic can simply declare itself complete.

The Sect of the Phoenix presents itself as an account of a secret association. Its members are said to be spread among many peoples and social categories. Their unity does not depend on shared doctrine or a recognized institution; it depends on a ritual referred to as the Secret. The narrative gives clues about its ordinary recurrence and transmission without plainly naming the practice. The learned voice promises an ethnographic explanation while making the object of explanation harder to pin down.

Many critics identify the Secret with sexual intercourse, and Borges himself later spoke about the story in terms that support that reading. But an interpretation is not the same thing as a missing sentence the author accidentally forgot to print. The tale studies the mechanisms through which a commonplace human act can be wrapped in the language of ancient mysteries, forbidden knowledge, and initiation. The method makes recognition possible while forcing a reader to ask why recognition feels like solving a conspiracy.

The South follows Juan Dahlmann, a librarian in Buenos Aires with divided European and Argentine family inheritances. After injuring himself in an ordinary domestic accident, he suffers a serious infection and endures a terrifying hospitalization. Eventually he seems to be released and takes a train toward a family property in the south. The journey feels restorative. Buildings, light, animals, and railway scenery seem to offer the world with a clarity that the hospital denied him.

He stops at a rural establishment, where a group of men begin provoking him. A fight threatens. An old gaucho throws a knife to Dahlmann, who knows little of such fighting. Accepting the weapon commits him to a struggle he is unlikely to survive. The last image leads him outside into the open plain, as if he were going toward the kind of death that belongs to his imagination of the Argentine past.

What happened? One reading treats the journey and duel as events in the story's represented world. Another reads the later journey as a dream or delirious alternative to an obscure death in the hospital. The fiction supplies materials for both, and its author explicitly invited a double reading in the later collection's paratext. The important point is not to declare one version a solved secret. It is to understand why Dahlmann's chosen image of death might seem more bearable than the helplessness of medical suffering.

These three pieces show Borges writing toward Argentina as well as away from it. The ending of an epic, a secret shared across all cultures, and a solitary journey into a stylized past all challenge the division between local material and universal invention. In the final story, a librarian leaves the city and approaches a knife duel. The library and the pampas are not opposed domains. The stories are inventions about how people read the lives they have inherited and how little control they possess over their endings.

## Ficciones: The Approach to Al-Mu’tasim — A Review of a Novel That Never Existed

An ambitious novelist writes a detective story set in India. Its hero, a young student, enters a world of violence and slowly becomes convinced that the traces of goodness he encounters must come from a hidden person of surpassing spiritual power. The critic who reports the novel praises its imaginative architecture, worries about the way it ends, and compares two supposed editions. That is the apparent situation. The novelist is invented. The book being reviewed does not exist.

Borges's The Approach to Al-Mu'tasim presents itself as the criticism of a work by an imagined writer, Mir Bahadur Ali. The reviewer knows its literary history: an earlier edition with a certain kind of mystery, and a later illustrated version whose more explicit allegory may have damaged the invention. We receive the lost novel not by reading it, but by hearing a critic describe what such a novel would contain and why it might succeed or fail.

The reported plot begins with a student in Bombay. He has grown skeptical of the religion of his family, and he enters a confrontation between Hindus and Muslims during a religious procession. Violence carries him along. He kills, or thinks he kills, another person, then flees the police into a city whose streets and marginal lives he hardly understands. What begins as an effort to escape punishment becomes a long search through human encounters.

One person seems to possess a small quantity of extraordinary peace or kindness. The student infers that this quality has come from another person, who possesses more. In turn, that source points toward another. Rather than pursuing a thief or a murderer, he follows the diminishing reflections of an unseen spiritual center. The title names the figure toward whom the chain appears to lead: Al-Mu'tasim. The detective story becomes a search for the source of virtue.

The promised destination does not arrive as the ordinary solution to a case. The reviewer can describe the door, the voices, and the way the hero approaches a presence, but the imaginative force of the novel depends on how much remains withheld. The invented critic is wary of a revised version that too plainly reduces the mystery to a theological equation. The first account's indirection lets the reader experience longing rather than merely assent to a doctrine.

This is an early instance of Borges's technique of building fiction inside the form of literary criticism. It was first published in the 1930s, before Ficciones itself. Its location within the collection is a genuine bibliographical issue: different editions and anthologies arrange or omit it differently. A listener should not treat its placement as a timeless rule about the book. The creative point, however, does not depend on one table of contents.

There is an interesting reversal between writer and reader here. Normally the author invents a story and a critic comes afterward to interpret it. Borges invents the critic's interpretation, and from that we imagine the story. The reviewer seems to know more about the fictional novel than we could possibly verify. He is our only access to its supposed facts. Yet his reservations about excessive allegory make him sound judicious, and that apparent fairness helps make the absent book feel real.

The search inside the reported novel can be read as spiritual aspiration, but its setting is not a neutral backdrop. The invented India is mediated through an Argentine writer's literary sources, orientalist conventions, and philosophical interests. It should not be taken as documentary testimony about an Indian community. The sectarian riot, the wandering student, and the chain of human encounters are the story's invented means of organizing a problem about goodness and distance.

The final question is what we hope to find from a chain of testimony. Each person who appears kind seems to refer us to another. If we could reach the source, would the search end, or would we only have found another reflection? A reviewer may demand a definitive ending, while the reader who has entered the fiction through a nonexistent book discovers that absence is the engine of the work. The book we cannot read invites us to keep imagining it.
