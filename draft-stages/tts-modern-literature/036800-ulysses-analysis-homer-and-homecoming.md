# Ulysses: An Epic Hidden in an Ordinary Day

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Why does a novel about a Dublin advertising salesman bear the title Ulysses? Ulysses is the Latin name of Odysseus, the Greek traveler who spends ten years struggling to reach home after the Trojan War. Homer's Odyssey describes monsters, gods, seafaring, hospitality, fatherhood, and the long-awaited return to Ithaca. Joyce offers a man carrying an advertisement through newspaper offices, a young teacher with nowhere to sleep, and a singer lying awake in a bed. The comparison is neither a joke that can be dismissed nor a mechanical key that unlocks every sentence.

Understanding the relationship requires us to ask what is gained and lost when an epic travels from a heroic age into a modern city.

## The correspondence is real, but flexible

Joyce planned Ulysses with an elaborate system of correspondences. His surviving schemes assign episodes Homeric counterparts, particular times, organs, arts, colors, symbols, and techniques. The Morgan Library's exhibition of the author's schemes establishes that these were part of the work's development and its explanation to selected readers. But Joyce's Homeric plans became more elaborate during composition, and he later had doubts about the way public circulation of the schemes could distort reading.

That history matters. The correspondence is not a pattern invented wholly by later professors, yet the chart is not a substitute for the novel. If every scene must perfectly reproduce an episode from Homer, we will be tempted to force the text into an answer sheet. Joyce is exploring what happens when a familiar cultural structure is stretched across material that resists it.

Stephen's morning forms a Telemachiad, recalling Odysseus's son Telemachus searching for news of his father. Stephen has a living father, Simon, but the relationship is difficult. He also struggles with a dead mother and the question of spiritual lineage. To call Stephen a fatherless son is therefore a useful but incomplete metaphor. We should not erase Simon or confuse symbolic deprivation with literal orphanhood.

Bloom resembles Odysseus in wandering through the city and returning home. Molly has a Penelope-like position as the woman at home, but the analogy is deliberately unsettled. Homer's Penelope is famous for resisting suitors during the wanderer's absence. Molly has sex with Boylan during Bloom's day away. This is not proof that she is simply the anti-Penelope, either. Her final recollection of Bloom's proposal complicates fidelity, memory, and the possibility of choosing someone while failing them.

## Hospitality matters more than monsters

Many correspondences are visible in comic miniatures. Homeric giants who devour sailors become a lunchtime chapter filled with appetite. The alluring Sirens become the singers and sounds of a Dublin bar. The one-eyed Cyclops becomes the Citizen, who can see his nation through only one idea of belonging and throws a biscuit tin after Bloom's carriage. Circe's dangerous transformations become a red-light district staged as extravagant hallucination.

These substitutions do not merely make ancient stories smaller. They test the moral vocabulary of epic. What counts as courage when there is no monster large enough to strike with a sword? Bloom argues against persecution in a room whose people can make him afraid. He refuses to abandon Stephen after the young man is knocked down. He visits a dying acquaintance's funeral with attention to the survivors' practical needs. These actions are not grand military achievements, but they resist the daily indifference that can harm people as effectively as an openly cruel act.

Notice that the Odyssey is also a story about hospitality. Odysseus survives because strangers offer shelter, and he also meets hosts who violate the rules of welcome. In Ulysses, a late-night cabman's shelter and Bloom's kitchen become sites where hospitality can occur without ceremony. Bloom does not have to be Stephen's father to offer cocoa. Stephen does not have to accept permanent residence to acknowledge the offer.

The names Eumaeus and Ithaca invite us to evaluate the quality of a homecoming rather than congratulate ourselves for recognizing a parallel. What has Bloom gained at the end of his travels? A chance to care for a young man and to return to his own domestic life. What has he not gained? Certainty about Molly, restoration of Rudy, or a marriage unscarred by loss. The epic ending has been broken into a smaller and more difficult form of persistence.

## Home is not the same as ownership

Stephen starts the day in a tower, but does not feel entitled to stay. Bloom has a house, but its emotional arrangement is uncertain. Molly has remained in the house, yet the novel's long silence about her inward life shows how easily a woman at home can become an object of other people's interpretation. Joyce repeatedly separates the idea of home from possession of property.

In Homer's poem, the returner's authority may be restored through the defeat of enemies and the reordering of the household. Joyce offers no such cleansing violence. Boylan is not killed or driven from Ireland. The marriage is not legally judged. Stephen is not ceremonially adopted. The house is entered, a cup of cocoa prepared, and a man goes to bed. A home is made, if at all, through acts that remain open to failure.

This difference has political force. Dublin in 1904 belonged to a country under British rule. The right to claim a home, a nation, a language, or a lineage was not straightforward. The Citizen's violent possessiveness of Ireland shows the danger of treating belonging as an exclusive title. Bloom's family history makes him suspect to some people who have never asked what his acts of citizenship and solidarity look like. In this setting, epic ownership must be examined rather than automatically admired.

## An epic of things usually left out

Traditional heroic poetry often records kings, battle, divine intervention, and the destinies of communities. Ulysses records digestion, advertisements, desire, female labor, bodily functions, misread notes, and unpaid bills. These are not presented to mock ordinary people for failing to be warriors. They are material from which the substance of any life is made.

Joyce's parallel with Homer can give a lunch immense resonance. But the lunch is not dignified only because it secretly resembles a myth. It also insists on its own importance: the body must be fed, the appetite is subject to taste and revulsion, and a person thinks while chewing. If we see the epic correspondence as the only source of value, we recreate the very hierarchy Joyce's novel is working to overturn.

The changing prose styles reinforce this argument. The epic has become not one polished song but a contest of voices: the journalist's headline, the nationalist's insult, the medical student's joke, the catechist's question, the singer's refrain. Heroism cannot simply belong to whoever has the most commanding language. One of the grandest-speaking men, the Citizen, is also one of the most dangerous.

## What the Homeric frame cannot settle

Some literary interpretations assign an almost sacred father-son reunion to Bloom and Stephen. It is tempting because Bloom's son died and Stephen's family relationships are troubled. But the two men's meeting ends with separation, not a new family contract. The point is not that the Homeric analogy has failed. It is that the analogy enables us to see the pathos of an incomplete encounter. The novel's characters remain more particular than the roles we borrow to describe them.

It is equally tempting to identify Molly with female earth, nature, or eternal sexuality and stop listening to her. The Penelope parallel can become another form of silencing if the reader treats her final monologue as a symbol rather than the speech of a specific woman with personal memories, grievances, and choices. The final voice is full of particulars that refuse a clean diagram.

A fruitful use of the Homeric system is therefore comparative. Ask what the named correspondence makes visible. Ask which differences are deliberate. Then return to the prose and to the needs of the character. The schema is a reading instrument, not a verdict.

Ulysses makes one of modern literature's strongest arguments about scale. If the ancient world could recognize a human life through the adventures of a returning warrior, a modern reader might learn to recognize it in a man trying to remain kind while the city makes him lonely. The epic has not vanished. It has been redistributed among people whom epic poetry might once have left in the background.

## Non-spoken interpretive map

- Stephen / Telemachus: spiritual and paternal estrangement, not a claim of literal fatherlessness
- Bloom / Odysseus: ordinary wandering, hospitality, and homecoming without restored sovereignty
- Molly / Penelope: analogy destabilized by her affair and her independent final voice
- Citizen / Cyclops: partial vision and exaggerated violence, in the biscuit-tin episode
- Eumaeus / Ithaca: shelter, conversation, hospitality, and unresolved departure
