# Rebecca — The Story

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A woman dreams of a house she can never visit again. The road is overgrown, its gate impossible to pass, and the great estate called Manderley has been taken back by wilderness. She wakes in the diminished present of her life abroad. She and her husband live quietly, moving among hotels and small routines. They are together, but something irrevocable has happened to the place that once seemed to define their world.

The woman tells us the story of how they came to lose it. We never learn her first name. In the memory she is young, inexperienced, and easily embarrassed. By the time she begins the account she is married to Maxim de Winter, but at the beginning of the story she is an employee, not a woman of property. Her job is to be the companion of Mrs. Van Hopper, a wealthy and overbearing American tourist staying in Monte Carlo.

Mrs. Van Hopper enjoys meeting important people, especially when those people would prefer not to be approached. A man named Maxim de Winter is staying at their hotel. He is older than the narrator, wealthy, reserved, and recently widowed. His first wife, Rebecca, was famous for her beauty, her charm, and her ability to make a grand country house come alive. She died in what was believed to be a sailing accident.

The young companion finds Maxim intimidating and attractive. When her employer is ill or otherwise occupied, Maxim spends time with her. They drive into the countryside, talk, and become accustomed to each other's presence. Yet the narrator is so unsure of her own worth that she can barely believe the attention is real. The difference between them in age, money, experience, and social position is enormous. Maxim offers very little explanation of his interior life. She fills the gaps with a romance of his dignity and grief.

When Mrs. Van Hopper announces that they must leave Monte Carlo, the arrangement is threatened. Maxim proposes marriage. The proposal is abrupt, and the narrator accepts. What might have been the ending of a conventional Cinderella story is instead the beginning of a long experience of fear. She will become the second Mrs. de Winter and move to the beautiful Cornish estate where Rebecca once ruled.

## The house that remembers someone else

Manderley appears as a place of astonishing beauty. Its gardens, halls, rooms, and carefully maintained routines are the expression of inherited wealth. But a large estate is not simply a building. It is a hierarchy of servants, guests, social expectations, and habits established long before the new mistress arrived. The young woman knows none of these rules. Her clothes seem wrong. She is uncertain when to give orders. She cannot imagine how she should greet the visitors who expect a confident hostess.

The housekeeper, Mrs. Danvers, receives her coldly. Danvers had been devoted to Rebecca, whose influence seems to fill the house even after death. Rebecca's flowers, letters, furnishings, and personal rooms survive. Her monogram appears on household objects. Her taste defines what seems graceful and correct. The narrator compares herself constantly to a woman who is not there to contradict any of the stories told about her.

Maxim's sister Beatrice is warmer than the narrator expects, though her frankness is intimidating. Frank Crawley, who helps manage the estate, is considerate and tries to make the young bride comfortable. But their kindness cannot resolve the principal difficulty: she does not know what Maxim felt for Rebecca, and she does not know what he expects of her. Maxim can be affectionate and then suddenly curt. She interprets his mood as evidence that he regrets the marriage and remains devoted to his dead wife.

On walks and visits she learns that Rebecca was an extraordinary hostess, accomplished in every social art. People loved Manderley during her lifetime. The new wife begins to imagine herself as a temporary occupant of a role that belongs to someone else. She is married to Maxim but has not acquired the identity of a mistress of Manderley. Even her name in the story is the name of a position: the second Mrs. de Winter.

Mrs. Danvers deepens the insecurity. She keeps Rebecca's west-wing rooms almost as a shrine, with clothes and personal objects still arranged as if their owner might return. She shows these rooms to the narrator with an intimacy that feels like a threat. The housekeeper's devotion has its own emotional intensity, but she directs it against the young woman who occupies Rebecca's place. The narrator becomes frightened of a dead woman she has never known.

There are warnings and hints that Rebecca's perfect public image concealed something different. Frank Crawley is reluctant to discuss the first marriage. Maxim avoids the subject. A local man named Ben, who frequents the shore, is afraid of having seen or said something he should not. The estate's boathouse and the cove where Rebecca sailed begin to matter. But the narrator has been taught by her fear to read every silence in Rebecca's favor: if no one will speak, perhaps the memory is too beloved to disturb.

## A costume, a window, a shipwreck

Manderley is to host a costume ball, reviving the grand social occasions for which the house was once known. The narrator sees an opportunity to perform successfully as its new mistress. She wants to surprise Maxim with an elegant costume and finally experience his pride in her.

Mrs. Danvers suggests copying the dress of a woman in a portrait displayed at the house. The narrator does not realize that Rebecca wore the same costume at an earlier ball. She appears on the staircase expecting admiration. Instead Maxim reacts with fury and orders her to change. The pleasure she imagined becomes a public humiliation. She discovers that Danvers has deliberately arranged the resemblance to Rebecca.

After the disaster the narrator is devastated. Mrs. Danvers presses her advantage, suggesting that the young wife is unwanted and can never belong at Manderley. In a scene near a high window, the housekeeper urges her toward an act that might end her life. The narrator's uncertainty about herself has become so severe that another person's malignant certainty can almost guide her movements.

The moment is interrupted by a disturbance from the coast. A vessel has run into trouble near Manderley. The emergency draws attention away from the house, and the narrator escapes Danvers's immediate control. But the rescue and investigation bring a still greater revelation. Divers discover Rebecca's sunken sailing boat. Inside the cabin is a body.

A corpse had been found and identified as Rebecca after her disappearance. Maxim had confirmed that identification. But the newly discovered body is Rebecca's. Her death did not occur in the straightforward way everyone has been told.

The narrator confronts Maxim. At last he explains the first marriage, and the story she has constructed around his grief collapses. According to Maxim, Rebecca was not the loving wife people imagined. She was manipulative, took lovers, and used her charm to maintain an impeccable public appearance while privately making him miserable. He says she mocked his attachment to Manderley and his desire to preserve its reputation. Their marriage had become an arrangement for appearances.

On the night of her death, he went to the boathouse to confront her. Rebecca told him that she was pregnant and that he would be forced to bring up another man's child as his heir. Maxim shot and killed her. He put her body in the boat, sank it, and later identified a different corpse as hers to protect his secret.

This confession is the novel's great reversal. For the narrator, it means that Maxim never loved Rebecca in the way she feared. She is overwhelmed with relief, even as she has just learned that her husband is a killer. Her deepest anxiety had been whether she was loved. Her response now makes preserving the marriage more urgent than reckoning with the crime. A new intimacy grows between husband and wife, founded on knowledge that could destroy them both.

## The inquiry

The discovery of Rebecca's body leads to an inquest. Evidence that the boat was deliberately damaged raises questions about whether Rebecca's death was an accident. Maxim must answer without revealing what he told his wife. The narrator begins to act more confidently at Manderley, offering instructions and assuming the privileges that once intimidated her. Yet her new authority rests beside the ever-present threat that an investigation might expose Maxim.

The inquest produces a verdict of suicide. It seems to resolve the danger, but the result is unstable. Rebecca's cousin Jack Favell, who had been her lover, does not accept that she killed herself. He possesses a note she sent him near the end of her life, and he tries to use it against Maxim. He demands money, then accuses Maxim more openly when the blackmail fails.

The local magistrate, Colonel Julyan, becomes involved. So do Mrs. Danvers and other witnesses. Rebecca's diary reveals that she visited a London doctor shortly before her death. To the narrator, the visit is frightening evidence: if the doctor confirms a pregnancy, the story of Maxim's violent motive may become impossible to hide.

Maxim and the others visit the physician, Dr. Baker. What they hear changes the apparent meaning of Rebecca's last encounter with Maxim. She was not pregnant. She had learned that she was seriously ill with cancer and could not have borne a child. The doctor had told her that her future was likely to be short and painful.

Maxim now believes that Rebecca deliberately provoked him into killing her so that she would not have to face her illness. This is his interpretation of what she intended, not testimony Rebecca is alive to give. The medical news supplies an explanation for suicide, and Favell's accusation loses force. The investigation does not reveal Maxim's crime. Once again he escapes the consequences that have seemed certain.

The couple prepares to return to Manderley. They have survived the public threat, and they imagine that they may finally live without Rebecca standing between them. Yet Maxim grows uneasy. Mrs. Danvers has disappeared from the house, and something about that absence alarms him.

They drive back through the night. As they approach Cornwall they see a strange light in the distance. It is not sunrise. The sky is colored by fire. Manderley is burning.

## The place they cannot return to

The novel does not give us a courtroom verdict on who lit the fire. Mrs. Danvers's disappearance and her devotion to Rebecca give us powerful reasons to suspect her, but the fire's origin is not conclusively established on the page. What matters unmistakably is that the house is lost. Whatever marriage the narrator and Maxim will make afterward, it will not be made in the place they once tried to possess.

We now understand the framing chapters. The narrator and Maxim survive and live abroad. They have a life together, though it is smaller and more repetitive than Manderley. The wife is no longer the terrified companion from Monte Carlo. She has helped protect her husband from discovery, learned to keep silent, and lost the grand estate that made their marriage seem desirable in the first place. The dream of Manderley is a dream of a past that cannot be restored.

A conventional romance might end when the heroine discovers that she is truly loved. A conventional crime story might end when a murderer is identified and punished. *Rebecca* gives us neither closure. Maxim has confessed his crime to his wife but not to the law. Rebecca remains known through testimony given by people who loved or hated her. The narrator has gained confidence and intimacy, but the cost of her new position cannot be separated from the secret she carries.

In the end the house is gone. The marriage continues. The woman who tells the story remains unnamed. And the first Mrs. de Winter, whose name is the title of the book, has shaped the narrative from the beginning without ever speaking for herself.

## Editorial notes

This retells Daphne du Maurier's 1938 **novel**, not Hitchcock's 1940 film or later adaptations. Maxim's account of Rebecca and his explanation of her intent are reported as his account, not established omniscient fact. The fire's instigator and Mrs. Danvers's eventual fate are not decisively resolved by the novel and are not asserted as facts. The prose is an original plot orientation.
