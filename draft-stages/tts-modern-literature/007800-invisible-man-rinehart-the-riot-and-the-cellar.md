# Invisible Man — The Story, Part 3: Rinehart, the Riot, and the Cellar

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

After Tod Clifton's death, the narrator argues with Brother Jack and the other leaders of the Brotherhood. He believed that the organization would honor an organizer who worked in Harlem and died there. The leaders want him to think instead of the group's broader plans. Their explanations do not account for the grief of the people who marched in Clifton's funeral. During an angry exchange, Jack's artificial eye becomes visible, and the narrator sees another difference between the leader he imagined and the man sitting before him. Jack can speak of historical necessity while keeping himself at a distance from the costs borne by those he directs.

The narrator leaves the meeting and moves through the city in growing confusion. To avoid recognition, he wears dark glasses and a hat. Strangers immediately begin addressing him as someone called Rinehart. Different people know Rinehart as a gambler, a lover, a street operator, and a preacher. The narrator discovers that the same appearance can call forth entirely different identities depending on who is looking. He has no idea what Rinehart actually believes, or whether the man believes anything at all. For a short time he occupies the confusion of being mistaken for someone whose life appears to contain many incompatible roles.

He decides that the Brotherhood must be challenged on its own terms. The organization has taught him to use language strategically; now he plans to conceal his intentions while appearing obedient. He meets with Brother Hambro, who explains that Harlem is no longer a priority and that the movement may have to sacrifice particular people or communities to its theory of what comes next. The narrator is appalled. He considers gathering information by approaching a woman close to the leaders, but the encounter he arranges brings him instead to Sybil, a woman with fantasies about Black men and little useful knowledge of the Brotherhood's inner life. The scene leaves him more isolated than informed.

Then trouble erupts in Harlem. He is called toward the neighborhood and finds streets filled with anger, damage, and fighting. The immediate causes are tangled among housing conditions, police violence, the Brotherhood's withdrawals, and the movements of people like Ras who have challenged its legitimacy. Shops are attacked. Fires are set. People gather in groups whose purposes and loyalties shift as events accelerate. The narrator sees that no single leader can control what is happening.

Ras appears in extravagant costume, now calling himself a destroyer rather than an exhorter. He directs his anger against the narrator, whom he sees as a traitor to Black people. The narrator runs. He cannot call on the Brotherhood for protection, and the crowd around him is too chaotic to offer a safe place to stand. He takes part in setting fire to a building, then flees again as the night disintegrates into violence and pursuit.

Two policemen confront him near a manhole, suspecting that the briefcase he carries contains stolen goods. He escapes by falling into the opening. The police close the cover above him, leaving him underground. He discovers that he can survive in the hidden spaces below the city. The briefcase he carried through the story contains objects from earlier stages of his life, including the scholarship papers and other reminders of the institutions that tried to define him. In the darkness he burns what he can to make light and sees the accumulated fragments of his old identities together.

The narrator eventually makes the cellar his home. He draws electrical power into it and fills it with lights. He listens to music and writes down the journey we have just followed. He is no longer the boy who believed a scholarship guaranteed recognition or the organizer who thought a political movement could tell him who he was. He has also not reached a final set of answers about how to live among other people. He begins to imagine leaving his underground refuge. A person who has disappeared from public life may someday have to speak again.

That is the end of the events narrated in Invisible Man. The young man has passed from a Southern school through college, factory, Harlem organizing, betrayal, political violence, and hiding. His name remains unstated. The story he tells is the record of how he became a person who could no longer accept the identities offered to him, and how he began looking for a way back into the world without handing his life over to someone else's description.
