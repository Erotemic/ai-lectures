# The Sound and the Fury — The Story, Part Four: Dilsey and Easter Sunday

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

On Easter morning, April eighth, nineteen twenty-eight, The Sound and the Fury changes its narrative voice. The three Compson brothers have each spoken through a version of their own consciousness. Now the story moves into third person. The first sustained attention falls not on the family that owns the house but on Dilsey Gibson, the Black woman who has spent much of her life caring for the family. She wakes into a cold, difficult morning, a house full of uncooperative people, and work that cannot be left undone because the people who depend on her would suffer.

Dilsey is aging. Her body does not move without cost. Her daughter Frony and grandson Luster belong to the household's extended world of labor, and each has a life whose concerns the Compsons often treat as secondary. The kitchen, meals, clothes, and Benjy's care require immediate attention. Mrs. Compson complains and makes demands; the authority she displays is disproportionate to what she contributes. Jason's movements are tense and impatient. He is about to discover that the money he has been keeping has gone missing. Dilsey does not receive a day off from other people's emergencies simply because it is Easter.

In the household there are signs that Miss Quentin has escaped. She has climbed down from her room and left. Jason's hidden cash is gone. The girl has found the money he has diverted from her mother's support, and she leaves with a man connected to the traveling show. To Jason, the theft is proof of her wickedness. To the reader, who knows how he obtained much of the money, the situation is more complicated. The girl is taking money he has concealed from its intended use, though her method and destination carry risks. Her escape has a consequence for Jason that he cannot publicly explain without exposing his own dishonesty.

Jason's anger is enormous because he has lost more than a hoard. He has lost the appearance of total control. He seeks help from the local sheriff, who has little reason to assist him in the way he demands. Jason cannot provide a simple account of rightful ownership without questions about why he was holding so much cash and where it came from. He then drives off in pursuit of the traveling performers. His fury takes him beyond the household, but the pursuit does not restore his power. He fails to bring Miss Quentin back. His attempts to force an encounter culminate in humiliation and a physical rebuff rather than a clean triumph.

Meanwhile Dilsey takes Benjy to church with members of her family. This is one of the novel's most important changes in setting. The Compson house has been full of claim and counterclaim, illness, shame, profanity, and resentment. The church is not untouched by the surrounding society's segregation and hardship. It is a Black congregation whose collective ritual gives voices and bodies a place that the white family's household rarely allows them. The movement from kitchen to church brings us into a community with its own relationships, customs, forms of authority, and account of suffering.

At the service a visiting preacher, Reverend Shegog, begins in a way that does not at once impress his listeners. As his sermon develops, his voice and language change. He draws the congregation into the story of Christ's suffering and resurrection. Easter becomes not merely a date in the calendar but a form of time that reaches beyond a family's ruin. The preacher invokes grief, endurance, death, and a promise that death is not the only word. People respond bodily and aloud. Their participation is not a decorative spectacle inserted to make the Compsons' trouble seem smaller. It is an expression of a community's worship and of the resources its members have made within a world that denies them equal standing.

Dilsey is moved to tears. The sermon connects with her own long knowledge of the Compsons, of children who grew up, of those who died, and of a house that seems to be ending while daily duties continue. Her sense of having witnessed both beginnings and endings has special force because she has spent years bearing other people's burdens. She sees the house's long decline more clearly than the household's owners. Yet she is not freed from her work by an epiphany. When the service is over, there will still be food to prepare and Benjy to attend. The spiritual dignity of the episode coexists with an economic and racial structure that remains unchanged.

The third-person narration matters here. Dilsey does not speak a full first-person monologue like the brothers. We come close to her through movements, conversations, the attention she gives to others, and the narrative's description of her labor. This is a real widening of the book's perspective; it is not the same as allowing her to control the whole story. Faulkner still mediates her voice. A reader should not take the section's reverence for her endurance as a complete account of what such a person might desire outside the household that depends on her.

When Dilsey returns to the Compson home, Jason is absent and angry, the niece is gone, and the familiar routines must be resumed. Benjy continues to be cared for, but his presence generates another sharp ending. Luster eventually takes him out in a carriage, driving through the town square. He chooses a route that differs from the one Benjy expects. Benjy becomes intensely distressed. It is a small change of direction to another person and a disorienting disruption to him. Jason appears, reacts angrily, and takes control of the reins. When the carriage resumes the familiar way around the monument, Benjy becomes calm.

The final carriage passage has often been read as a restoration of order. It does restore a local sensory order for Benjy: the horse, the square, and the expected direction align once more. But the household has not recovered its dead, Caddy has not returned, the lost money is not found, and the system of domination under which Dilsey works is not repaired. The novel ends with Benjy's experience of a familiar pattern, not with a political settlement or a family's moral cure. The precision of this distinction keeps the ending from becoming sentimental.

What has happened to Caddy's daughter? She has escaped the house and Jason's control; the novel does not provide a trustworthy report of her long future. Jason fears she is leaving with a man from the show, and the narrative supports that immediate departure, but we are not given permission to declare her freedom secure or to condemn her to a fate the text does not narrate. Her disappearance is a boundary for the family's power and for the reader's knowledge. The mother, Caddy, remains excluded. Her daughter's flight repeats an exit but does not repeat the exact life.

The Easter setting offers competing meanings of endurance. In the Compson family, endurance can mean stubborn preservation of a reputation, repeated accusation, or a refusal to release an old injury. Dilsey's endurance is different: she repeatedly helps people meet material needs even when they scarcely recognize the cost. The sermon describes survival in yet another register, through the language of death and resurrection. Neither the family's ruin nor Dilsey's labor is magically undone, but the novel suggests that the world contains forms of community and time unavailable to Jason's angry accounting.

The last section also makes us think again about who has been speaking. The family possesses a rich account of its own grievances because the three sons have been granted the interior stage. Caddy has never had a section to herself. Dilsey, although central to the last movement, has not received the same kind of first-person access. At the end we know more, but we have not heard every life on its own terms. That limitation belongs to the work's ethical and artistic design, not merely to a missing page.

A carriage turns. A man who has been crying grows quiet. It is not a grand conclusion, but it gathers the book's question into a physical act. How much of the world must remain stable for someone to live in it? Who keeps that stability in place? Who has the authority to change a route, a name, a marriage, a body, a life? The final Sunday makes those questions more acute than a simple story about a once-great family's fall could ever have done.
