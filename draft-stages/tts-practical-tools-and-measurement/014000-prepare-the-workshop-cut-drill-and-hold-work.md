# Prepare the workshop, cut, drill, and hold work

## Workshop Lighting, Layout, and Work Sequence Design

A workbench is sometimes described as a flat place to put tools. In practice it is part of the machine. The height determines which muscles carry the load. Its stiffness influences measurement repeatability. Its lighting controls what defects can be seen. The locations of tools, power, clamps, and offcuts determine how many chances there are to lose a reference, contaminate a bond, or move a hand into the path of a cutter.

A workshop that works well is designed around the sequence of operations rather than around a photograph of equipment arranged neatly against the wall.

### Follow the material through the job

Imagine producing six identical brackets from sheet material. First you need to inspect stock, establish dimensions, mark or set a stop, cut the blanks, remove burrs, drill holes, verify the first article, and repeat. Where does material enter the space? How long are the pieces? What must be supported on both sides of the cut? Where do you put parts that are finished versus parts awaiting inspection? If the only clear surface is also the assembly surface, an unfinished part can be mistaken for a finished one.

Try drawing a flow diagram before moving furniture. Use boxes labeled material arrival, rough cutting, accurate layout, drilling, deburring, inspection, assembly, and storage. An operation that generates abrasive dust should not sit immediately next to an open precision instrument or a glue-up that depends on clean surfaces. A spray or finishing operation has different ventilation and ignition concerns from dry assembly. Separating processes by contamination risk is often more valuable than separating them by the brand of tool involved.

### Light lets you observe geometry

Two lights with equal electrical power can create very different working conditions. A single bright overhead source can leave the inside of a drilled hole in shadow and throw the edge of a ruler into darkness. A diffuse ambient light helps orientation; an adjustable task light reveals a particular surface without forcing the worker to reposition the entire part. Grazing, low-angle light is effective for seeing ridges, scratches, uneven finish, and small dents. Light aimed across a surface reveals slope changes that direct frontal light may conceal.

The goal is not simply maximum brightness. Glare on metal, glossy paint, or a transparent measuring scale can make marks harder to read. Shadows moving with the operator can hide hazards. Position the light so hands and tools do not obscure the reference. Neutral color rendering can help when selecting finishes or examining heat discoloration, though color alone is not a reliable measure of material condition.

A repeatable inspection should specify viewing angle, distance, illumination, and the distinction between cosmetic and functional defects. A burr that catches a glove may be visible in raking light but invisible in an overhead photograph. A joint gap measured with a feeler gauge is more defensible than a claim that the gap 'looked fine.'

### The bench is a mechanical reference

A bench that rocks on three feet may still support a heavy object. But it cannot provide reliable layout if it shifts each time pressure is applied. Bench height also changes task demands. Fine assembly generally benefits from bringing the work closer to the eyes, while pushing a plane or filing a heavy part benefits from a surface and stance that permit controlled body force. One fixed height rarely serves every operation equally well.

A vise at the corner of a light bench can twist the top. A clamp may hold a part against vertical movement but allow rotation under a drill's torque. Workholding should resist the actual force and moment directions of the operation. Good benches provide clamping edges, clearance below them, and accessible locations for stops or fixtures. They also allow safe support of long stock without creating tipping moments.

Small measurement stations deserve protection from impact and dirty operations. A caliper laid next to grinding swarf invites contamination. A clean, stable reference surface and a known location for measuring instruments reduce both error and lost time. Record the ambient conditions if expansion or humidity materially affects the dimension being checked.

### Design the order of operations

Some mistakes are not problems of care; they are problems of sequence. If a hole must locate a later assembly, drill or establish that reference before trimming away the edge used to locate it. If a glued joint needs clean surfaces, complete dusty shaping before final cleaning and assembly. If a decorative finish hides fasteners, inspect the underlying structure before covering it.

A useful rule is to identify irreversible operations. Cutting, drilling, bending, gluing, painting, and fastening with destructive anchors often cannot be completely undone. Insert an inspection gate immediately before such a step when the cost of rework is high. For the six brackets, verify the first finished blank against the drawing before cutting the rest. Once the drilling jig is set, verify the first drilled part, then monitor for chips or loosened stops that could gradually shift the location.

Also distinguish batch work from one-piece flow. Cutting every piece first may be efficient if setup is expensive and the cut is proven correct. But it multiplies the consequences of a bad setup. Making one complete article before committing the batch can reveal a problem much earlier. There is no universal answer; the right sequence balances setup time against the risk of replicated errors.

### Space for failure and recovery

A workshop needs places for offcuts, rejected parts, cords, hoses, and ongoing jobs. A clear walkway is not wasted space; it is room to carry a long piece safely and room to step back when a tool binds. Loose cords should not cross a path where people carry sharp or heavy objects. Lighting and outlet positions should let an operator see the work without pulling a cable across a cutting surface.

Ventilation and dust capture belong in the layout plan from the beginning. A portable fan that moves dust off the bench but through the worker's breathing zone does not solve the underlying exposure. Source capture, suitable filtration, and cleaning methods should match the material and process. For wood dust, metal fumes, and silica-bearing masonry, the hazards and suitable controls differ. Plan these stations around the hazard, not around available floor space alone.

Finally, observe a real job. Count the number of times you turn around for a ruler, move stock to reach a clamp, or walk across the room with a hot or sharp object. Those observations are a better basis for rearranging tools than an idealized diagram.

A good workshop makes the intended action easy, the unintended action visible, and the result testable. That is the central connection between lighting, layout, and measurement: all three determine whether we can see and control what we are doing.

## Dust Collection, Respiratory Exposure, and Workshop Ventilation

Workspace layout also has an air-and-particle dimension that is less visible than the bench or tool position.

A woodworking shop can appear clean while exposing its occupants to a cloud of fine particles. Conversely, a chip-covered bench may look worse than a work area with invisible airborne dust, even though the exposure mechanisms differ. Dust control begins by distinguishing what a tool produces, what a collector captures, what remains suspended, and what a person can inhale.

When a saw tooth removes a piece of wood, it creates chips across a distribution of sizes. Large chips fall quickly. Smaller particles remain in air longer and follow air currents around the workbench, through a room, and into breathing zones. A sander tends to generate finer material than a typical rough cut. Some woods contain substances that can irritate skin and airways or contribute to sensitization. Cutting brick or concrete introduces a different concern, respirable crystalline silica, which has distinct exposure limits and controls. A single category called "workshop dust" hides important material differences.

### Capture at the moment of creation

The most effective place to intercept particles is close to their source. Local exhaust ventilation positions a hood or shroud so air moving into it transports chips and dust before they disperse. This requires a path through the collection system: inlet, duct or hose, separator, filter, and fan. A bag attached to a tool is not automatically good capture; performance depends on suction, airflow, seals, shroud geometry, and whether the tool throws debris outside the capture zone.

Airflow and suction are related but different. Static pressure is the potential that helps overcome resistance in a hose and filter. Volumetric flow is how much air actually moves per unit time. A small vacuum may develop high suction and work well through a narrow port, but not move the volume needed to surround a large saw opening. A high-volume dust collector may struggle with a long, small-diameter hose because resistance increases sharply. Matching a fan's operating curve to the duct and hood matters more than buying equipment on the basis of a single advertised number.

Imagine a collector that works well until its filter accumulates fine dust. As filter resistance rises, the fan may deliver less airflow. The bin might still collect large chips while fine dust escapes around the cut, precisely when the operator thinks the system is working. An airflow indicator, pressure-drop measurement, inspection of hoses, and regular filter maintenance offer more information than observing the pile of chips alone.

### Separate the layers of protection

Source capture, room ventilation, filtration, cleaning practices, and respiratory protection solve different parts of the problem. A room air cleaner can reduce some particles that have already escaped into the room, but does not replace capture at the tool. General ventilation dilutes what remains airborne but also moves contaminated air through the room on the way to exhaust. Opening a window may help under some conditions; wind and replacement-air flow can also draw dust across the operator's face.

Cleaning is a dust-control operation in its own right. Sweeping or compressed-air blowing can resuspend settled fines. Suitable vacuum extraction and dust-compatible cleaning methods generally control this better. A household vacuum not designed for the dust, especially certain fine mineral dusts, may leak contaminants through its filtration or be damaged. Filters and collectors must be selected for the material and task; combustible dust and ignition hazards create additional design constraints that ordinary vacuum selection does not address.

Respiratory protection is not a decorative backup. A respirator's effectiveness depends on the hazard, filter selection, fit, use, and maintenance. A poorly fitting facepiece may leak around the edge regardless of its advertised filter efficiency. In occupational settings, respiratory protection programs and exposure assessment have legal requirements. For hobbies, the same physics applies even when the regulatory framework differs. A respirator does not make an uncontrolled explosive dust atmosphere acceptable, nor does it eliminate the need to reduce exposure at the source.

### A workshop is an airflow network

Trace air from where it enters to where it leaves. A fan exhausting outside requires replacement air. If that air arrives through a combustion-appliance space, an unconditioned attic, or a dusty adjacent room, the pressure effects may create a new problem. Short, smooth duct routes and gentle bends can reduce pressure loss, but the design must also handle chip transport, electrical grounding considerations, filter maintenance, and fire risk. Large installations need knowledgeable design, not an improvised collection of adapters.

There is also a distinction between collecting large debris and controlling airborne exposure. A chip cyclone can protect a filter from bulk debris without necessarily filtering the finest exhaust particles. If the collector discharges back indoors, the filtration efficiency and leakage of its entire enclosure become central. If it exhausts outdoors, makeup air and heating or cooling losses become important. Neither choice is universally best.

### Test the actual process

Before sanding a panel, observe the tool's dust port, collector hose, and likely escape direction. With the tool operating under appropriate conditions, check whether visible plumes emerge at edges, whether the hose kinks under movement, and whether capture deteriorates as work continues. Use measurements and exposure assessment where warranted rather than using a lack of visible dust as a guarantee. Document material type, process, tool speed, extraction setup, and maintenance state.

The practical hierarchy is to produce less hazardous dust when possible, capture it as it is generated, control what escapes, and protect the person from residual exposure. A workshop becomes more capable when the operator understands air and particles as well as cutting geometry. Better dust control is not simply tidier housekeeping; it makes work more repeatable and protects people who may enter the space long after the tool stops.

## Cutting, Kerf, and Workholding

With the work area and exposure controls considered, the next question is how the work is held and what a cut removes.

Suppose you have a board exactly one meter long and want two pieces each half a meter long. Mark the midpoint, put the saw blade directly on the line, and cut. The two pieces cannot both be exactly half a meter. The blade turns some wood into sawdust. That missing band is the *kerf*, and it matters whenever the finished dimensions must add up accurately.

A cut has at least three geometries: the intended finished edge, the material removed by the tool, and the direction in which the tool actually moves. A pencil line is not infinitely thin. A saw blade may wander, deflect, or tilt. A workpiece may move under load. The better approach begins by identifying which side of the line belongs to the finished piece. Place the kerf on the waste side, and keep the measured dimension in the material you intend to retain.

### Marking is not the same as locating

A tape measure gives a number; a square transfers a reference; a knife or pencil marks a location. But the tool that makes the cut needs its own reference. You can guide a hand saw from a marked line, run a circular saw against a straightedge, or use a miter saw fence and stop. These strategies produce different kinds of error. Following a drawn line depends on eyesight and hand control. Running against a reliable mechanical guide reduces some of that variation, but introduces the need to position the guide correctly relative to the blade.

Suppose the saw's cut removes three millimeters. If you cut a long board into several precisely measured pieces and place each new mark from the previous cut, errors and kerf losses accumulate. A cut list and a fixed origin reduce this uncertainty. In repetitive work, a physical stop can make part lengths consistent, provided the stop does not trap the offcut against a moving blade or create another mechanical hazard. Precision is not a property of the tape alone. It is a property of the entire referencing process.

### The blade works against the material

Different cuts ask the tool to break material in different directions. Wood has grain: ripping parallel to fibers is not the same as crosscutting across them. Metal, plastic, and composite materials have their own chip formation, heat, and edge behaviors. A saw with the wrong tooth geometry can tear fibers, generate excess heat, or demand excessive feed force. A dull edge may create more friction and unpredictable forces even if it still seems capable of separating the material.

Think about what happens at the cutting edge. The tool applies force over a small region, deforming and fracturing or shearing the material. That produces a reaction force against the tool. With a hand saw, your arm stabilizes the response. With a powered circular saw, blade speed, tooth contact, and the way the workpiece closes around the blade can create binding or kickback. The operator's job is not just to guide the blade; it is to arrange the support so the material does not close onto it.

### Holding the work is part of the operation

Imagine cutting a board resting across two sawhorses with a gap in the middle. As you finish the cut, the board's own weight may cause the opening to close or the offcut to pivot. A cut supported in the wrong places can pinch the blade. For a different cut, a poorly supported offcut may tear fibers from the finished edge as it falls. You need to predict which piece stays stable, where the waste moves, and whether the tool's path remains clear.

Clamps, vises, bench hooks, fences, sacrificial backing, and supports are ways of constraining degrees of freedom. A clamp doesn't automatically make an operation sound. It must resist the actual forces without obstructing the tool, guard, or escape path for the waste. A long piece may require support at both its free ends. Thin material may need a backing board to prevent chatter or breakout. A part that is secure under downward force may still twist when a blade enters from the side.

Workholding is also a way to protect your hands from becoming the only fixture. If your grip must be close to the cutting path to keep the piece still, redesign the setup. A machine's guarding, riving knife, and anti-kickback provisions are not optional performance accessories; they are part of the intended operating system. The manufacturer's tool-specific method matters because a table saw, miter saw, jigsaw, and portable circular saw handle the interaction of blade and work in different ways.

### A good cut can still be the wrong cut

Accuracy is more than a clean edge. If a frame requires two rails to be the same length, equal lengths may matter more than either rail matching a rounded nominal number. If an opening must receive a fitted part, clearance, out-of-square conditions, and thermal or moisture movement may matter more than a perfectly straight isolated cut.

Before cutting, ask: Which face is the reference? Which portion is the keeper? Where does the kerf go? What holds both the keeper and waste as the cut separates them? What tolerances actually affect assembly? And how will I check the piece after cutting? Those questions sound deliberate because they are. But once practiced, they become faster than trying to rescue an inaccurately cut part.

An accurate cut is a controlled transformation of a known piece of stock into a known shape, with a predictable loss of material. The saw performs the removal. Layout and workholding make that removal repeatable.

## Drilling and Hole Geometry

You mark a dot on a board, put a drill bit on the dot, pull the trigger, and find that the hole emerges a centimeter away from where you expected on the other face. The entry point was correct. The hole was wrong. What failed was not necessarily the mark: it might have been the angle of the drill, the way the bit followed the wood grain, or the force used as the tool broke through. A hole has position, diameter, depth, direction, surface quality, and an exit condition. Every one of those can matter.

Before choosing a drill, ask what the hole is for. A screw pilot hole, a clearance hole, a dowel hole, a countersink, a plumbing penetration, and a threaded hole are different geometric problems. Their apparent similarity can conceal major differences in accuracy and behavior.

### Locate the axis, not merely the center

Imagine placing a long dowel through the desired hole. The dowel shows you that a circular mark is only the intersection of a three-dimensional axis with the work surface. If the axis needs to be square to the face, you must control the drill's orientation. If it must meet another hole inside a thick piece, small angular errors accumulate with depth. A one-degree tilt over a hundred millimeters produces roughly one-point-seven millimeters of lateral displacement. That may be tolerable for a loose cable passage but unacceptable for a locating pin.

A drill press constrains an axis relative to a table more consistently than freehand operation, but it does not make the setup automatically correct. The table might be tilted. The workpiece might rock on a chip. A curved surface may move when the bit first engages. A machine vise, clamping fixture, drill guide, or sacrificial support can be more important than buying a more expensive bit. A long slender bit can deflect under side force even when the spindle starts true.

On a portable drill, posture and sightlines matter. Looking from only one direction can conceal angular error in the perpendicular plane. For a critical bore, establish a guide or a reliable reference, rather than trusting that the bit appears straight from one viewpoint.

### Bits make different holes

A twist drill has helical flutes that form and remove chips. In metal, the cutting edges need appropriate feed and cutting speed, and chips must have somewhere to go. In wood, common twist drills work, but brad-point bits can locate more predictably on the fibers and produce cleaner entry geometry. Spade bits are fast for comparatively coarse holes; augers remove larger volumes of wood and tend to pull into it; Forstner bits make controlled flat-bottomed recesses in suitable setups. A hole saw removes a ring, leaving a core, and introduces different chip-clearing and torque demands.

In masonry, the material is brittle and abrasive. Rotary hammer bits break material by impact combined with rotation rather than shearing it like an ordinary metal drill. The correct bit and machine depend on brick, mortar, concrete, reinforcement, and the required anchor system. A nominal hole diameter may be only one part of an anchor's specification: depth, cleaning procedure, substrate condition and edge distance can determine whether the installed fastening works.

A drill can also make a hole larger than its own nominal diameter. Runout, chatter, motion of the workpiece, and damage at the entrance can all enlarge it. A high-quality bearing fit or dowel hole may require boring or reaming after drilling. A tap drill is intentionally smaller than the nominal screw thread so the thread has material to form into. Clearance holes intentionally permit a bolt shank to pass without engaging the material. Those choices belong to the joint design rather than a single universal drill chart.

### What happens at the far side

Breakthrough is a change in cutting conditions. The last thin web of material may tear rather than cut, especially in wood and laminates. A backer board supports it. Reduced feed near exit can help, as can drilling from a controlled pilot or from both faces when accurate references are available. In thin metal, the drill can grab when the point breaks through. A handheld workpiece may spin with substantial torque; the correct response is to restrain the work mechanically, not to grip it harder.

Deburring matters. A sharp metal burr can damage a cable sheath, cut skin, prevent parts from seating, or give a false impression of thickness when measuring. A countersink may create a recess for a fastener head, but removing too much can weaken the joint or split thin material. A cable hole through an edge may need a protective bushing, grommet, or other approved protection; smooth visual appearance is not enough if the service condition can abrade the cable later.

### Design a hole-making sequence

Suppose a bracket must be attached with two bolts. First identify the mating surfaces and their location tolerances. Mark from a consistent datum, and decide whether both parts can be held together and transfer-punched. Plan how to restrain the work and keep the bit away from hidden services. Choose a pilot, clearance, counterbore, or threaded geometry as required. Keep drilling chips from building up in the hole. Finish the opening, remove burrs, and test alignment before the whole assembly is installed.

This sequence explains why poor holes create assembly problems later. A bolt forced through offset holes bends the joint into compliance; it is not evidence that the holes were correct. A screw that wedges in an undersized clearance hole may pull one member away rather than clamp two surfaces together. A mounting hole drilled through a water-control layer introduces a new sealing obligation that should have been considered before the drill touched the surface.

The best hole is not the most precise hole possible. It is the hole whose size, axis, finish, and location suit the job, produced with a setup that controls the relevant sources of error. Good drilling begins with geometric intent and ends with an inspected result.

## Portable Workbenches, Clamping Layouts, and Repeatable Assembly

A portable workbench can be strong enough to stand on and still be frustrating for precise assembly. Every time you tighten a clamp, the top twists. Every time you saw, the bench walks across the floor. The problem is not simply load capacity. It is stiffness, stability, and the route through which tool forces reach the ground.

A useful workshop surface performs three jobs. It locates a workpiece relative to an intended reference; it resists cutting, drilling, or assembly forces; and it allows the same operation to be repeated without re-measuring the entire setup. We can evaluate a compact workbench by these functions rather than by how heavy it appears.

### Follow the force

Suppose a long board is clamped to one corner of a folding bench. A saw pushes sideways on the free end. That force creates a turning moment around the clamp and another around the bench's legs. If the board is held far from the legs, the whole assembly may oscillate. Adding a stronger clamp may only transfer a larger force into a flexible table. Moving the operation over a leg, supporting the projecting board, and keeping the clamp near the cut can improve stability more effectively.

A bench frame built as a rectangle can rack into a parallelogram. Diagonal bracing, a properly fixed shear panel, or rigid corner connections oppose that distortion. A triangular force path is not magic; it works because its geometry resists changes in shape without asking the joints to carry large bending moments. Hinges, collapsible braces, and quick-release locks introduce additional motion and should be checked in the deployed configuration, not just when the bench is folded.

We should distinguish a bench that is level from one that is stable. Four legs can stand at slightly different heights on an uneven floor. If one leg floats, the bench rocks even when the top appears horizontal. Adjustable feet, shims, or a three-point support arrangement can eliminate rocking. The appropriate choice depends on the work and required load capacity. A woodworking bench, a soldering station, and a machine stand are different engineering problems.

### The workpiece needs a reference

A clamp squeezes, but squeezing alone does not establish where the part belongs. For repeatable work, establish a reference face, a reference edge, and a stop. When several boards must be drilled at the same distance from one end, place the same sound reference edge against a fence and the same end against a stop for every part. A pair of loosely arranged clamps may hold each board firmly while placing each one differently.

Imagine assembling ten identical small frames. If you cut pieces by measuring from the previous piece, one small error can accumulate through the batch. If all pieces are cut against one set stop, variations are instead related to the stop's accuracy and repeatability. The stop itself must resist being displaced by the tool, and saw kerf and blade deflection still matter. Repeatability and absolute accuracy are separate properties: a stop can produce ten identical pieces that are all two millimeters too short.

Clamping is a load path, too. In a glue-up, the line of force should pass through the joint without bowing thin members. Distribute pressure with cauls or pads when appropriate. For drilling, restrain the workpiece against rotation as well as lift. A spinning board is evidence that the holding plan did not address torque. Small parts may require a dedicated fixture instead of fingers. Avoid fastening workpieces in ways that cause cutting tools to contact metal clamps or hidden screws.

### Design the bench for the actual sequence

Start by writing down the operations. For a portable cabinet assembly station, you may need crosscutting, hole placement, dry fitting, glue-up, and inspection. Those operations compete for surface area and access. A grid of dog holes or removable stops can help, but holes also weaken some tops and accumulate debris. Sacrificial boards preserve the main work surface when cutting through a part. A removable hard, flat panel can create a clean inspection reference after rough operations have ended.

Clearance below the top is easy to overlook. A clamp with a deep throat needs room for its screw and handle. A stop near an edge may block a drawer slide or a hinge jig. Before building an elaborate fixture, rehearse the motion of every tool and clamp through its whole travel. Convenient storage becomes an obstruction if the operator must reach across a rotating blade or place hands in a pinch point to retrieve it.

Portable equipment has a particular failure mode: the setup is forgotten between sessions. Mark the intended deployed lock positions. Inspect hinge pins, foot contact, loose fasteners, and clamps before loading the bench. Keep dust extraction, cords, and hoses routed so movement does not pull the work surface. A repeatable setup depends on both geometry and routine.

The deepest improvement is to stop treating the workbench as a passive tabletop. It is part of the measurement system. The fence defines direction, the stop defines position, the clamps define restraint, and the supports determine how much the reference moves under load. When those roles are explicit, even a small portable bench can produce precise work.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Kerf accounting

If a stock piece is \(L\) long and each full-width separation removes kerf \(k\), the sum of the kept segment lengths plus discarded segment lengths is \(L - nk\) after \(n\) cuts through the stock, neglecting trimming and measurement errors. Example: a 1000 mm piece cut once with a 3 mm kerf cannot produce two 500 mm finished pieces; at most their lengths sum to 997 mm. Nominal blade thickness and effective kerf can differ because of tooth set, runout, and deflection; test with scrap when tolerances matter.

### Drilling and Hole Geometry

**Track:** Tools, Measurement, and Workmanship. **Background:** layout, cutting, and workholding. **Purpose:** treat a hole as a designed geometry with an entry, axis, depth, and exit.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Geometry and tolerance

For an axis with angular deviation `theta`, approximate lateral displacement after depth `L` is `L tan(theta)`. At `L = 100 mm`, `theta = 1 degree`, displacement is approximately `1.75 mm`.

Distinguish nominal bit diameter, actual finished bore diameter, positional tolerance, perpendicularity, and surface finish. Consult specific hardware standards or manufacturer installation instructions for fastener clearance, tap drill sizing, anchor hole cleaning and embedment.

##### Observation exercise

Use scrap material and suitable restraint to compare clean entry, exit, and sidewall for several bit types. Record whether a backed-up exit is cleaner and whether the hole axis meets a marked target. Rotating stock must be clamped; do not hold a workpiece in the hand under a drill press. Check hidden wiring, plumbing and structural restrictions before making building penetrations.

### Portable Workbenches, Clamping Layouts, and Repeatable Assembly

### 013000 — Portable Workbenches, Clamping Layouts, and Repeatable Assembly

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Layout experiment

Make three nominally equal parts: first by serially measuring from each preceding cut, then by returning each part to one fixed stop. Compare spread and mean error separately. Record stop motion, blade kerf, reference-face defects, and clamp placement. The exercise tests a method; it does not establish a tool's certified accuracy.

##### Bench audit

- **Support:** Does the bench rack sideways or rock under lateral push? What happens over the unsupported corners?
- **Reference:** Is there a stable fence/stop and a repeatable way to register every part?
- **Constraint:** Are rotation, uplift, and sliding restrained for the actual tool force?
- **Access:** Can clamps, cutting tools, dust hoses, and hands complete the operation without conflict?
- **Inspection:** Can the surface be returned to a known, clean reference after rough work?

**Review boundary:** Load capacities and fastening prescriptions are design-specific. Tool-machine guarding and workholding instructions take precedence over generalized bench advice.
