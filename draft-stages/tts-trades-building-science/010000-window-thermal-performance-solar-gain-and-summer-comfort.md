# 010000 — Window Thermal Performance, Solar Gain, and Summer Comfort

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A west-facing room may be uncomfortable on a sunny summer afternoon even when its windows are closed, the air conditioner is running, and the room's thermometer says the temperature is reasonable. The reason is not necessarily an undersized air conditioner. Sunlight is entering through the glass, warming the floor, furniture, and occupants directly. Meanwhile, the window itself exchanges heat with the outdoors and its inner surface exchanges radiant heat with the room. A window is not just a hole filled with a transparent insulator. It is a thermal and optical component of the building.

## Three mechanisms, three different measurements

Start with conduction and convection, combined in the whole-window rating called U-factor. U-factor describes the rate of heat transfer per unit area and per degree of temperature difference. Lower is better insulation. The rating includes more than the middle of the pane: frames, spacers, edge effects, and standard test conditions matter. Do not confuse the center-of-glass performance of a premium pane with the performance of the assembled window.

Then consider solar heat gain coefficient, often shortened to S H G C. This is a fraction, between zero and one, of incident solar radiation that ends up entering the space as heat, including energy absorbed by the window and subsequently transferred inward. A low solar gain coefficient generally helps suppress summer solar heating. A higher one may be beneficial where winter solar heat is welcomed and usable. The best choice depends on orientation, climate, shading, and the building's heating and cooling priorities. A high solar gain coefficient is not simply a better or worse window.

The third mechanism is air leakage through the operable sash, weatherstripping, and installation details. A low U-factor does not guarantee a tight installation. Moving air carries both sensible heat and moisture, and a small persistent leak can produce a draft and condensation where the local surface is cold. A window can have excellent glass and a poor connection to the wall.

## Work through an example

Suppose a window is twenty square feet and has a U-factor of zero point three in inch-pound building units. With a twenty-degree Fahrenheit indoor-outdoor difference, a simplified steady heat flow is zero point three times twenty times twenty, or one hundred twenty B T U per hour. A similar-sized window with a U-factor of zero point six would double that heat flow under the same conditions. These are comparisons of an idealized rated assembly, not predictions of a household utility bill. Real conditions vary over the day, and the rating is measured at specified conditions.

Now give that window direct sunshine. The incident solar energy per area can be much larger than the conduction term at some hours. If the incoming solar radiation on its plane were two hundred B T U per square foot per hour, and its solar gain coefficient were zero point four, the simplified transmitted heat would be sixteen hundred B T U per hour. Changing the solar gain coefficient to zero point two would approximately halve that example's solar contribution. The values are deliberately illustrative; window angle, season, weather, screen, shading, and actual spectral behavior all change the result. The important comparison is between the temperature-driven term and the sun-driven term. A room can be hot because of sunlight even if the glass insulates well.

## The sun has a direction

A window facing north in the northern hemisphere generally receives less direct summer sun than one facing west, although geography and surroundings matter. East-facing glazing admits morning sun. West-facing glazing is exposed in the late afternoon, when outdoor temperatures and internal stored heat may already be high. A south-facing window can often be shaded by a properly designed horizontal overhang during high-sun seasons while admitting lower-angle winter sun. A west window is harder to protect with a horizontal overhang alone because late-afternoon sun arrives at a low angle. Exterior adjustable shades, trees, or fins can perform differently from interior curtains: stopping radiation before it crosses the glazing is generally more effective at limiting heat deposited indoors.

Low-emissivity coatings help control infrared radiation and, depending on coating formulation and glazing design, can also alter solar transmission. They are not magic films with identical properties. Two windows both described as low-e can have different U-factors, visible transmittance, and solar gain coefficients. Triple glazing may reduce conductive loss yet still admit unwanted sun unless the solar properties are appropriate. The product label is a set of distinct measurements for a reason.

## Why the window feels cold, or hot, even at the same air temperature

A person sitting by a cold pane exchanges radiant heat with it. Air in contact with the pane cools and moves downward. These two effects can make the person uncomfortable without a room thermostat recording a dramatic change. In summer, strong direct sunlight can heat a person's skin and nearby furniture while room air remains comparatively cool. Comfort depends on air temperature, mean radiant temperature, air motion, humidity, clothing, and activity. An air thermostat samples only part of the situation.

Condensation provides another clue. Interior glass can become cold enough for indoor water vapor to condense even when the rest of the room seems dry. Lower U-factors, warmer interior surface temperatures, suitable spacers, interior humidity control, and air sealing can all help, but a persistent wet corner may also indicate a flashing leak or frame failure. Condensation on the interior room-facing surface is not the same as moisture trapped between sealed panes. The latter can indicate insulating-glass seal failure. Moisture on an exterior pane in certain weather may simply indicate a well-insulating pane with a cold outside surface.

## Diagnose before replacing

Consider the west room again. Record when the discomfort happens, whether the sun reaches people or surfaces, whether the air temperature is elevated, and whether a shade changes the experience. Check the window's product label, actual orientation, visible gaps, and condition of the sash and frame. A temporary exterior shade that greatly improves the room may be more diagnostic than an expensive window quote. A room that feels cold every cloudy winter evening points to a different balance of mechanisms.

Window replacements also involve water control, structural support, and the air boundary around the opening. The highest-rated unit can be undermined by poor flashing or installation. Building science requires comparing the entire installed assembly and the room it serves. Separate conduction, solar gain, and air leakage first. Only then decide which lever to change.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Equations and definitions

- Whole-window conductive/convective heat flow approximation: `Qdot = U × A × ΔT`. Example: `0.30 Btu/(h·ft²·°F) × 20 ft² × 20 °F = 120 Btu/h`. Does not include direct solar gain or window leakage.
- Simplified solar gain: `Qdot_solar ≈ SHGC × incident irradiance × projected glazed area` using consistent units. The sample `0.40 × 200 × 20 = 1600 Btu/h` is an illustrative orientation-specific instant, not a design-day estimate.
- U-factor and SHGC are separate certified ratings; distinguish whole-unit from center-of-glass claims. Air-leakage rating and installation leakage are separate.
- Distinguish indoor surface condensation, interpane fogging, and exterior surface condensation.

### Diagnostic exercise

Compare two west-window strategies: (A) reduce U-factor substantially without changing SHGC; (B) reduce SHGC or add exterior shading without changing U-factor. For a sunny afternoon with little indoor-outdoor temperature difference, predict the dominant benefit of each. Then reconsider a cloudy cold night.

### Review and applicability

ASHRAE design methods, NFRC product ratings, climate, orientation, historic-building constraints, egress and safety glazing, and local code affect project decisions. Do not select a window on a single advertised R-value or solar coefficient alone. Technical review remains required for installed design guidance.
