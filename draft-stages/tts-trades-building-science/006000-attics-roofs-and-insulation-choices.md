# 006000 — Attics, Roofs, and Insulation Choices

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Heat, air, rain, and vapor transport. **Purpose:** identify the actual enclosure boundary at the roof and distinguish insulation strategies from ventilation strategies.

# Where does the house end?

Stand in an unfinished attic on a cold morning. Below you is a ceiling, usually pierced by wires, plumbing, light fixtures, and a hatch. Above you are rafters and roof sheathing. Between the two might be a cold, ventilated space or an insulated room. Before deciding whether the attic needs more insulation, ask a question that sounds elementary but is surprisingly consequential: which surface is supposed to separate conditioned indoor air from outdoors?

If the attic is vented and outside the conditioned enclosure, the intended thermal boundary is usually the ceiling below. If the attic is unvented and brought inside the enclosure, the boundary is at the roof plane. The difference is not a mere preference about where insulation looks tidy. It changes the temperatures of the roof deck, the air and vapor paths, the performance of ducts and equipment, and the details needed to control condensation.

## A fluffy blanket is not a lid

Fibrous insulation slows heat transfer, chiefly by immobilizing air within many small spaces. It does not automatically make an airtight surface. Warm indoor air that passes through a ceiling penetration can move through or around insulation and deliver moisture to a cold roof deck. A thick layer of insulation laid over an unsealed ceiling can therefore produce disappointing energy results while leaving an important moisture route intact.

In a conventional vented attic, the sequence matters. Find and fix active roof leaks and inappropriate exhaust outlets first. Identify the ceiling's air-control layer, then seal its discontinuities with materials suited to the location, particularly around heat-producing fixtures or vents. Only then does adding insulation give the expected benefit. The insulation should meet the ceiling air barrier without voids or compressed sections. A recessed fixture, a knee wall, and the edge over an exterior top plate are all places where that alignment commonly becomes difficult.

Near eaves, depth is often limited. The edge of a ceiling may receive much less insulation than the middle even though it has the same exposure to winter weather. Raised-heel framing creates room for more consistent insulation. With soffit-to-roof ventilation, baffles preserve the intended air passage near the underside of the roof and prevent wind from disturbing loose insulation at the edges. Blocking a soffit vent with insulation is not an improvement in the assembly's heat control.

## What attic ventilation can and cannot do

A vented attic exchanges air with outdoors. This can help manage roof-deck moisture and solar heat under appropriate conditions, but it does not repair a leaky ceiling. If humid indoor air is continuously flowing upward through a large hole, ventilation is being asked to carry away a moisture load that should have been limited at its source. Equally, ventilation is not proof that a roof has a sound rain barrier. Water entering at a flashing joint still needs its leak fixed.

Consider the classic winter ice dam. Snow on a roof can melt where the roof deck is warmed from below. Water runs toward a colder overhang and freezes. Repeated melting can build a ridge that impedes drainage. Exterior weather and roof geometry matter, but attic air leaks and uneven insulation can make the roof surface less uniformly cold. More roof ventilation may help a suitable assembly; reducing heat and air leakage from the home is the central diagnostic step when the roof is being warmed by the house.

A powered attic fan is not an automatic cure. If the attic is insufficiently separated from the home, fan suction may draw conditioned air through the ceiling. It adds a pressure difference while consuming energy. The correct question is whether the fan addresses a measured problem without creating a new airflow route.

## Bringing the attic inside

Now imagine ducts, air handlers, or finished rooms occupying the attic. Insulating at the roof line can put those systems inside the thermal enclosure. That can avoid locating conditioned-air ducts in a very hot or cold attic. However, a roof assembly that is not vented in the conventional manner needs an explicit strategy for roof-deck temperature and moisture. The type and placement of insulation, vapor permeance, exterior roof coverings, interior humidity, and local climate all matter.

Spray foam is one method, not a magic category. Open-cell and closed-cell foams have different vapor properties and thermal behavior. Foam installed over a wet roof deck can obscure the condition underneath. A product that acts as an air barrier in one thickness and installation does not establish the safety of the whole roof assembly. Mixed systems, such as exterior rigid insulation combined with fibrous insulation inside, depend on the proportion of thermal resistance on each side of a potential condensing surface.

The roof geometry may complicate everything. A low-slope roof, dormer, chimney chase, skylight shaft, or cathedral ceiling creates transitions between control layers. If you cannot draw one continuous thermal line and one continuous air line around the conditioned volume, the assembly has unresolved details. That drawing should come before material shopping.

## A diagnostic walk through an attic

An attic survey is better conducted as a search for mechanisms than a tally of insulation thickness. Look for daylight where it should not appear, staining on sheathing, frost patterns, darkened areas by penetrations, displaced insulation, bath exhaust dumped indoors, disconnected ducts, and obstructed ventilation passages. Remember that stains have histories: an old roof leak and current condensation may leave similar marks. Timing, weather, humidity, and measurement help distinguish them.

An unconditioned attic floor can be thermally adequate in the middle while leaking heat at its hatch, wall-to-ceiling junctions, and plumbing chases. A finished attic can appear comfortable while hiding a cold, moisture-sensitive roof deck. In both cases, the right unit of analysis is the whole assembly, including its joints and transitions.

The practical lesson is not that vented attics are always better or that foam is always worse. It is that insulation belongs within a deliberately chosen boundary. Once you know where that boundary is, you can ask the productive questions: is it continuous, does it control both heat and air, and what happens to moisture when the weather changes?

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Two schematic sections to draw

- **Vented attic:** interior room → sealed ceiling air barrier → attic-floor insulation → ventilated attic → roof sheathing and roofing. Mark soffit baffles and eaves where insulation is thin.
- **Unvented conditioned attic:** interior room and attic → insulated/air-sealed roof plane → roof deck and roofing. Label dew-point and drying analysis as assembly-specific.

### Diagnostic exercise

Compare two attics with identical nominal attic-floor R-value. In house A, the ceiling is airtight but insulation thins to near zero over the top plates. In house B, insulation is even but a large plumbing chase opens to the attic. Predict different thermal and moisture patterns and what observations would separate them.

**Review boundary:** These are conceptual assemblies, not prescriptive insulation ratios, ventilation dimensions, or approval of a particular roof retrofit. Assess roof condition, combustion vent clearances, indoor air quality and local climate before recommending a detail.
