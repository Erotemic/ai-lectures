# 011000 — Inspecting an Existing House: An Evidence-Based Walkthrough

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A house can look immaculate and still have a significant water problem. It can also have a stained ceiling from a leak repaired fifteen years ago. An inspection is an exercise in distinguishing current behavior from historical evidence, and an attempt to discover which mechanisms deserve closer examination. It is not a contest to identify the longest list of defects.

Imagine arriving at a two-story house after rain. Rather than start at the kitchen faucet, begin outside, across the street if possible. Observe the shape of the roof, where valleys deliver water, and whether gutters discharge beside the foundation. Look at the ground near the walls. Does it slope away, hold puddles, or send runoff from a higher neighbor's lot toward the building? We are studying the boundary conditions: what loads of rain, heat, ground moisture, and air pressure are being applied to the enclosure.

## Follow water in the direction it can actually travel

Water follows gravity when free to move, but capillarity, wind pressure, and drainage paths complicate that story. The place where a damp patch appears is not necessarily where the water entered. A stain halfway down a wall may have begun at a roof penetration, traveled along framing, then emerged where plaster offered an opening. A dark foundation patch may arise from groundwater or from warm indoor air condensing on a cold surface. The appearance alone does not settle the question.

Walk the exterior from top to bottom. At roof transitions, inspect visible flashing conditions without stepping onto a roof. Around windows and doors, ask where water that passes the outer cladding would drain. Look for an outward path above openings, at wall bottoms, and at the interface with the foundation. Peeling paint near a window can indicate repeated wetting, but it also depends on paint preparation, exposure, and sun. Evidence becomes stronger when several independent signs agree: water marks, damaged material, an uphill runoff pathway, and a moisture reading taken under suitable conditions.

An inspection needs temporal context. Ask when the symptom appears. Immediately after wind-driven rain points toward one set of paths; only in cold weather suggests another; only when an upstairs shower runs suggests plumbing. A measurement without its circumstances is merely a number. A moisture meter can help compare locations, but readings depend on material, salts, temperature, and instrument method. One displayed percentage does not automatically measure the true moisture content of every wall assembly.

## Trace heat, air, and moisture together

Inside, notice room-to-room temperature differences, cold surfaces, drafts, and persistent odors. An infrared camera may reveal surface temperature patterns, but a cold patch is not proof of missing insulation. Air washing, a structural thermal bridge, wet material, solar exposure, and changing heating operation can all alter the image. Use infrared patterns to form questions, not to issue final diagnoses.

The attic is a useful model of this discipline. A cold-climate attic with frost around a penetration might be receiving warm, moist indoor air through an unsealed chase. It might also have roof leakage. A bath exhaust duct ending in the attic creates a different localized source. Examine the relationship among the air barrier, insulation, roof ventilation design, and exhaust termination. Merely adding more insulation can conceal an air leak without stopping moisture transport.

Basements invite another important distinction. Water on the floor after storms may indicate bulk water from grading, foundation cracks, or overloaded drainage systems. A musty smell in summer with no visible intrusion may instead be linked to humid air reaching cool surfaces. Relative humidity is not a direct measure of how much water is in the air; temperature alters relative humidity. A cool basement can therefore feel damp even without a new liquid-water leak. The proper response depends on the transport mechanism, and multiple mechanisms can coexist.

## Consider the house's operating systems

The structure's enclosure is only half the story. Observe what combustion appliances and ventilation fans do to air pressure. Exhausting indoor air can draw replacement air through leaks and, in some configurations, compete with the draft of natural-draft combustion equipment. Carbon monoxide is not recognizable by smell, so working alarms and appropriate professional combustion evaluation matter more than informal reassurance. Look for documented servicing and safe, visible vent arrangements, not speculative conclusions from a single photograph.

Electrical panels, concealed plumbing, gas lines, and structural elements have inspection boundaries. You can document visible conditions and service records without opening energized enclosures, operating unknown shutoffs, climbing unsafe roofs, or treating apparent normality as certification. The aim is to identify concerns, priorities, and questions for appropriately qualified evaluators. A house inspection never reveals every concealed component.

## Build hypotheses, then try to falsify them

Suppose the northwest bedroom is cold. Hypothesis one: the window has poor thermal performance. Hypothesis two: the duct supplies too little air. Hypothesis three: the ceiling has an air leak. Begin with the inexpensive distinctions. Does the room cool when the furnace cycles off, or fail to warm while it runs? Is the air delivered at the register warm but weak? Does the temperature difference grow with wind? Are interior surfaces cold in a pattern consistent with missing insulation? Each observation changes which hypothesis deserves the next test.

Keep a record with date, weather, indoor conditions, what was observed, how it was measured, and what remains uncertain. Photograph broad context before close-ups. Record the *absence* of evidence only within the area actually examined: no water visible during a dry-day visit is not a guarantee that a wall stays dry during storms. Rank findings by potential consequence and confidence separately. A suspected structural movement or exhaust hazard may need urgent evaluation despite incomplete diagnosis; a worn finish can wait even when the defect is certain.

A careful inspection turns a house from a collection of appearances into a set of physical systems under observable loads. The useful question is not simply, "What looks wrong?" It is, "What process could produce this evidence, what else would that process predict, and how can we find out without creating a new problem?"

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Evidence matrix

| Observation | Candidate mechanisms | Distinguishing follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Wet basement after heavy rain | Surface runoff, foundation entry, sewer backup | Drainage geometry, location/timing, floor drain behavior |
| Local cold patch | Thermal bridge, missing insulation, air leak, damp material | Weather dependence, surface pattern, air-sealing details |
| Attic frost | Indoor air leakage, exhaust termination, roof water | Penetration mapping, duct terminations, event chronology |
| Stained ceiling | Historical roof leak, plumbing leak, active condensation | Date/time, moisture comparison, overhead services |

### Field protocol

Document exterior bulk-water pathways, visible flashing continuity, grading and downspout discharge, interior pressure/temperature symptoms, basement and attic evidence, and maintenance records. Include a safety boundary: visual observation does not certify structural, electrical, gas, radon, or other regulated-system compliance. Where testing is needed, define scope and conditions first.

### Sources and verification

Building America/PNNL drainage, air-barrier and basement moisture documents; DOE guidance on vapor and air transport; EPA radon, mold and combustion-air publications. Any consequential finding requires suitable site-specific assessment. An infrared image or moisture-meter number alone is not a definitive diagnosis.
