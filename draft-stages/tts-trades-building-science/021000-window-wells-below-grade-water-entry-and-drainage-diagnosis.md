# 021000 — Window Wells, Below-Grade Water Entry, and Drainage Diagnosis

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A basement window sits below the surrounding lawn. During an intense summer storm, water gathers in the well, rises above the bottom of the window frame, and appears on the basement floor. The immediate temptation is to replace the window or add a cover. Either may be useful, but neither answer is complete until we know where the water came from and where it was supposed to go.

A window well is a small outdoor excavation against a building. Its three important boundaries are the surrounding soil and ground surface, the well walls, and the foundation wall containing the opening. Rain may enter directly from above. Surface runoff may flow over the well rim. Water traveling through saturated soil may enter from the sides or below. Each source has a different remedy, and several can operate together.

## A bucket that is not supposed to be a bucket

Imagine the well as a bucket without a reliable drain. Even an ordinary rainfall can fill it if runoff from a larger area is directed toward the opening. The relevant quantity is not just rainfall depth but rainfall depth multiplied by the contributing collection area. One inch of rain falling on one hundred square feet corresponds to about sixty-two gallons of water before losses. That volume does not have to fall into the small window well itself. It may arrive from a patio, sloped lawn, roof downspout or frozen channel in the soil.

The shape of the yard matters. A small high spot or an edging strip can direct runoff; a depression near the foundation can hold it. Downspouts discharge concentrated roof runoff, so a five-minute observation of flow during a storm can be more informative than several dry-weather inspections. At the same time, a well that drains to a functioning subsurface system should not simply store every gallon that enters it. Water accumulating persistently suggests that the pathway out is blocked, overwhelmed or absent.

## Surface drainage and subsurface drainage are different systems

The first line of defense is usually control of water on the surface: roof collection and discharge, grade that leads water away, and the elevation of the window-well rim relative to adjacent ground. A cover can limit direct rainfall and debris, but it cannot solve groundwater pushing up through the bottom or surface water overtopping the well. A cover may also affect ventilation, egress and access; it cannot be chosen purely for dryness.

Below the surface, a bed of gravel may give the appearance of a drain while providing only temporary storage. Gravel has voids, but a closed gravel pocket eventually fills. A well drain must have a workable outlet, perhaps to a suitable foundation-drainage system or another designed discharge location. Whether such a connection is appropriate depends on local conditions and design. Sediment, roots and frost can compromise performance. Introducing water into an already overburdened footing drain can make basement wetting worse, rather than better.

A water stain below the window does not prove the glass or sash leaked. Water may enter at the joint between frame and foundation, follow a crack or travel behind a finish before emerging farther away. Look for staining or deposits at the lowest point of the frame, at the sill-to-foundation joint, along the well-wall connection and on nearby interior surfaces. Map evidence before choosing a sealant.

## Hydrostatic pressure and openings

Water accumulating against a below-grade wall exerts pressure that increases with depth. This is why treating a saturated wall as a simple surface-sealing problem can be unsuccessful. An opening or joint near the low point experiences a driving pressure when water rises outside it. A caulk bead can deteriorate, pull away or divert leakage to a neighboring weakness. The durable design must first reduce water accumulation and provide a drainage path. Water-resistant exterior detailing and the window assembly's own flashing or drainage provisions then become complementary barriers.

This reasoning extends to other penetrations: utility sleeves, basement doors, old vent openings and cracks. A below-grade defect is often a combined problem of water supply, storage, pressure and an imperfect boundary.

## A diagnosis in three observations

Suppose the window well floods only in short, intense storms. Start with a map of roof drains, paved areas, grades and the well rim. During an event, if safe to observe, note whether water enters over the top, runs down the well wall or appears upward from the gravel. Record how fast it rises and how long it takes to fall. Photograph a ruler or a fixed reference point; the pattern may disappear by morning.

Next compare the well to the rest of the basement. Is there a sump pump? Are foundation drains known to exist? Does the sump cycle heavily at the same time? Do other low openings leak? These observations help distinguish a local blocked well drain from a broader groundwater or drainage-system problem. A test of an individual discharge line is different from proving the whole drainage system functions during a storm.

Finally distinguish the water path from the repair surface. If runoff crosses the rim, address the contributing area and rim design. If the well drains poorly, investigate its outlet and the condition of its drainage components. If water enters at a joint while the exterior remains below the opening, examine the window, foundation and transition detail together. For egress windows, preserve the required usable clearances and means of escape when redesigning the well.

The central lesson is that a window well is not merely a hole with a window in it. It is a small drainage basin attached to a vulnerable opening in a larger foundation enclosure. Good diagnosis follows the water from the source, through the collection area, into storage, through the intended outlet and finally across any failed boundary. Once those paths are understood, the repair options become far easier to evaluate.

## Non-spoken technical companion

**Rain volume:** US gallons approximately `0.623 × rainfall(inches) × area(square feet)`. A 1-inch storm collecting from 100 square feet supplies about 62 gallons before infiltration or other losses. This is a water-budget demonstration, not a site design rule.

**Observation worksheet:** Date and storm intensity; roof downspout location; grading and hardscape direction; well rim elevation; whether entry is over the rim, through the wall, or from the bottom; maximum depth and drain-down time; interior staining locations; concurrent sump activity. Photograph water-line evidence before surfaces dry.

**Distinguishing actions:** Surface diversion versus below-grade outlet investigation versus frame/joint investigation. Do not infer that gravel alone is an outlet. Evaluate egress constraints before covers, grates or regrading; exact local requirements and existing assembly details need project-specific review.

**Sources and scope:** US DOE Building America Solution Center, *Look at Crawlspace for Moisture Issues*; NYSERDA, *Build Better Guide*, foundation drainage; DOE/PNNL Building America details on foundations and water management. Building-science guidance supports the mechanism and diagnostic framework; it does not verify a particular drainage layout or retrofit design.
