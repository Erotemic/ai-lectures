# Join foundation, wall, and ground boundaries

## Basements, Foundations, and Groundwater

A basement is not just another room with a heavier wall. On one side of that wall is indoor air. On the other is soil, which can store water, freeze, expand, settle, and conduct heat. Its conditions change after rain and snowmelt, when a nearby downspout is redirected, or when the groundwater level rises. A basement that has been dry for years can develop a leak without the concrete suddenly becoming defective. The surrounding water conditions may have changed.

To reason about foundations, separate three kinds of water. First is surface water, arriving from roofs, paving, slopes, and saturated lawns. Second is water traveling through the soil above the groundwater table. Third is groundwater, which may impose persistent pressure on below-grade surfaces. The distinctions matter because each suggests a different intervention. A coating on the inside of a wall does little to change the water arriving from a disconnected downspout. A working gutter cannot by itself lower a regional water table.

### Where water is driven

Gravity pulls water downhill. A sloped roof and gutter system should move rain away from the foundation rather than discharge it beside the footing. Surface grading should discourage puddles against the wall. But once water enters the ground, the route is determined by soil permeability, layers, and pressure differences. Water may spread sideways along a less-permeable layer instead of falling vertically. Excavated backfill often behaves differently from undisturbed surrounding soil; a foundation excavation can become a preferential water collection zone.

When the water level outside a wall rises above the level inside, pressure increases with depth. The lowest part of the wall feels the greatest hydrostatic pressure. A visible wet line high on the wall is not necessarily where water first entered. Water can travel through joints, cracks, and cavities before becoming visible indoors.

A footing drain is intended to collect water near the base of a foundation and carry it to a lower discharge point or sump. A sump pump then lifts water from the collection pit to a suitable discharge location when gravity drainage is insufficient. This is not a device that makes surrounding soil dry. It is a way to control water level at a vulnerable boundary. If the pump runs continuously, the observation calls for an explanation: sustained inflow, a failed check valve, a discharge loop that returns water, or an unexpected groundwater condition are different possibilities.

### A wall is also a material

Concrete and masonry are not impermeable by definition. They contain pores, joints, interfaces, and sometimes cracks. Capillary suction can move liquid through fine pores without a large pool of standing water. Vapor diffusion and air leakage can bring moisture through or around an assembly as well. If a cool basement wall meets humid indoor air, condensation may form even when no rainwater is crossing the foundation. Surface water, groundwater seepage, and indoor condensation can all produce damp finishes; they must not be collapsed into a single diagnosis called 'a wet basement.'

Imagine two identical-looking patches behind storage shelves. One becomes wet only after prolonged rain, especially when a downspout discharges nearby. The other appears during humid summer weather, beads on a cold pipe, and improves when indoor humidity is controlled. Painting over the patches is unlikely to resolve either mechanism. Their observation histories point toward quite different paths.

The wall also carries loads. Soil pushes laterally on a retaining basement wall. Floors, upper walls, and roofs transmit vertical loads through the foundation to the ground. Adding a drainage system, cutting a new opening, or excavating beside a footing can affect this structural role. Water-control work does not grant permission to weaken the support system.

### Layers and details

A durable below-grade assembly often relies on several complementary measures: appropriate grading, positive surface-water drainage, exterior water control, a drainage layer, and a means of collecting and discharging water at the base. The exact combination depends on climate, site, groundwater conditions, foundation type, and how the interior space will be used. Damp-proofing, waterproofing, and drainage are not interchangeable words. A membrane may resist a specified water exposure, but it needs details around seams and penetrations; a drain can reduce pressure but cannot compensate for every failed wall joint.

Interior finishes add another complication. Insulation changes wall temperatures. Vapor-impermeable finishes can prevent a damp foundation from drying toward the room. Wood studs and paper-faced materials placed against wet concrete may create a concealed reservoir of moisture. Interior comfort improvements must be designed with a plausible moisture and drying path rather than treated as decoration.

There are related hazards in the ground as well. Soil gas, including radon in some locations, can enter through foundation openings and pressure-driven pathways. A tight-looking wall is not evidence of low radon exposure; appropriate measurement is a different task from looking for water stains.

### Investigation before repair

If a basement smells damp, begin with a map and a calendar. Mark where and when moisture appears. Record rain events, snowmelt, outdoor temperature, indoor relative humidity, sump cycles, gutter discharge, and changes in nearby landscaping. Inspect the accessible exterior before assuming the source is inside the wall. Compare the wettest place with the nearest surface-water source and with the likely path through the soil. Check whether the sump actually sends water away from the structure.

A crack deserves its own assessment. Hairline shrinkage cracks, displacement across a crack, ongoing widening, and walls that bow inward do not have the same significance. A structural concern cannot be resolved by filling the visible line with sealant. Likewise, active flooding or a failed pump under rising water conditions calls for immediate control of the situation, not a prolonged experiment.

The governing lesson is to identify the pressure or moisture source and the route it takes. A basement is where the building meets the hydrology of the site. Successful work changes that relationship instead of merely hiding the evidence indoors.

## Radon, Soil Gas, and Foundation Air Boundaries

The ground boundary carries air and soil gases as well as liquid water.

Suppose two basements look identical. Both are dry, neither smells damp, and both have freshly painted concrete floors. Yet one has a low concentration of radon in its air and the other has an elevated concentration. Appearance cannot distinguish them. We need a model of the source, the route, and the quantity we want to measure.

Radon is a radioactive gas produced in the natural decay chain of uranium found in many rocks and soils. It is colorless and cannot be identified by smell. The hazard of prolonged exposure is not that the gas makes the air feel bad. Radon decays into radioactive products that may be inhaled and can damage tissue in the lungs. The relevant concern is cumulative exposure and increased lung-cancer risk. A tidy, new, well-insulated house can have a radon problem, and so can an old one with conspicuous cracks. Neither appearance nor age is a test.

### Soil is a reservoir, not a single leak

Think about the space below a slab. Even where soil looks solid, air occupies some pores between particles. Soil gas can move through connected pore spaces and enter a building wherever there is an available route. The route might follow a slab crack, the joint between the slab and foundation wall, a utility penetration, a sump opening, or a gap around a pipe. Foundation construction, soil permeability, and the pressure difference across the boundary all affect the result.

The familiar stack effect helps explain one driving force. In cold weather, warm indoor air rises and may escape through upper leaks. Replacement air is drawn inward through lower openings. If some of that replacement comes through the soil, the house is sampling a reservoir of soil gas. Exhaust fans, fireplaces, duct leakage, and wind can change pressure patterns. But pressure alone does not establish radon concentration. A pressure difference provides a possible driving force; the actual result also depends on soil, entry paths, and ventilation.

This is why the phrase 'seal the basement' is insufficient as a complete remedy. Sealing can reduce an entry route, but finding every hidden opening is difficult, and openings can reappear as materials move. Water control, airtightness, and soil-gas control overlap physically without being interchangeable. A sump pit that collects groundwater and a radon system that manages gas pressure address different flows, even if both connect to the area below a slab.

### Measure an exposure, not an impression

The appropriate way to learn whether indoor radon is elevated is to test. A short-term test is useful for initial screening, while longer-term measurement provides a better picture of average conditions when radon fluctuates with season, weather, and operation. Placement, closed-house conditions where required, test duration, and laboratory or instrument instructions matter. A reading from the wrong location or a poorly conducted test may answer the wrong question.

In the United States, EPA recommends fixing a home when radon is at or above four picocuries per liter, and recommends considering action between two and four. These are action recommendations, not a boundary between harmless and dangerous air. No level of exposure is known to be entirely risk-free. Other countries and local authorities may use different guidance; that distinction belongs with a decision to act.

Imagine a homeowner tests shortly after converting a basement to a bedroom. That is a relevant location because the room is now occupied for many hours. If an initial short-term result is elevated, the next step is not to compare it with a neighbor's result or to infer the answer from floor cracks. Follow the recommended confirmation and mitigation decision process, then verify with another measurement after any intervention. The outcome to improve is the measured indoor concentration.

### Changing the pressure beneath the slab

A common mitigation approach is active sub-slab depressurization. A fan connected to a properly designed pipe system draws soil gas from beneath the slab and discharges it outdoors at an appropriate location, establishing a pressure relationship that discourages soil gas from entering the occupied space. The details depend on slab permeability, foundation construction, drainage features, and the ability to communicate pressure through the sub-slab region. A fan that runs is not proof that the intended pressure field reaches every critical area.

Building a new home offers another opportunity: radon-resistant construction can include gas-permeable material beneath the slab, sealed entry points, and a suitable venting route that can be activated if necessary. Existing buildings require a site-specific diagnosis. In either case, post-work testing is essential. A solution is judged by the air it produces, not merely by the equipment installed.

The general lesson is larger than radon. For contaminants that are invisible, observation of surfaces is a poor substitute for measuring the relevant exposure. Follow the source, the transport mechanism, the room where people spend time, and the evidence that a change worked. Those four pieces make soil-gas reasoning a building-science problem rather than a matter of guesswork.

## Rim Joists, Cantilevers, and Concealed Air Boundaries

A floor can feel cold even when the room above it is warm and the basement beneath it seems comfortable. The cause may be concealed at the narrow strip where a floor frame meets the outside wall. That strip is called the rim joist, or sometimes the band joist. It closes the ends of the floor framing. The rim joist is a structural part, but it is also a difficult transition in the building enclosure. Heat can pass through it, air can travel around it, and water can be trapped against it. Treating only one of those flows may not solve the problem.

### Draw the boundary first

Imagine a heated first floor above an unheated basement. Draw the thermal boundary: across the floor, through the perimeter at the joist ends, and around any projecting floor bays. Now draw a second line for the air boundary. Unlike an insulation layer, an air boundary must form a continuous, reasonably airtight surface. Those lines may overlap, but they are not identical. Fibrous insulation tucked between joists can slow heat conduction while still allowing air to stream through gaps at the wood and sheathing.

The detail becomes harder where the wall sits above a masonry foundation. A sill plate rests on the foundation. Floor joists end at or cross the rim. The subfloor and wall framing meet above. Each connection is made from separate pieces, with fasteners and changing moisture content. Narrow seams can occur where the sill meets concrete, where the rim meets the subfloor, and where services penetrate the framing. Because the seams connect, an apparently small opening may communicate with an exterior wall cavity or the basement.

A cantilevered floor extends beyond the supporting wall. Picture a bay window or an upper room projecting over the outdoors. Its underside is exposed to cold air, but the joist bays often continue back into the heated floor. If the projecting cavity lacks blocking, outdoor air can reach far beyond the visible overhang. If insulation is pushed in without a sealed backstop, it may look complete while cold air washes past it. The source of a cold floor may therefore be several feet away from the spot where the occupant feels it.

### Separate heat, air, and moisture

Through a solid material, heat flows from warm to cold according to the temperature difference and the material's thermal resistance. Wood carries more heat than most cavity insulation. At the rim, repeated framing and tight geometry create thermal bridges. A thin piece of foam that stops air movement is not necessarily enough insulation; conversely, a thick but loosely fitted batt may supply thermal resistance without airtightness.

Moisture raises a different issue. Indoor air commonly contains water vapor. When that air leaks through a cold joist bay, it can cool until its moisture condenses on wood or sheathing. Bulk water from a sill leak or exterior flashing failure can wet the same surfaces. The two mechanisms leave overlapping evidence. A wet rim after rain suggests a possible exterior water path; dark staining after prolonged cold weather can suggest condensation. Neither pattern proves the source on its own.

What happens after wetting matters as much as the wetting itself. Wood that can dry in at least one direction may tolerate intermittent moisture; wood trapped between low-permeance layers can remain wet longer. A foam retrofit can reduce winter condensation by raising the temperature of a vulnerable interior surface, but the suitable foam type, thickness, vapor control, ignition or thermal protection, and existing water conditions depend on climate and assembly. There is no single rim-joist recipe for every house.

### Look for continuity rather than a product

Suppose a basement has an exposed, dry rim. Before choosing insulation, trace the intended enclosure. Where does the air barrier on the foundation wall meet the air barrier at the floor? Which joints connect to the outdoors? Do plumbing, cable, or vent openings cross that line? Is the basement actually inside the conditioned enclosure, or is the first-floor underside meant to be the thermal boundary? A floor-over-vented-crawlspace detail is different from a conditioned basement wall detail.

A durable intervention normally combines well-fitted blocking or insulation with sealed interfaces. Rigid foam panels can form both an insulating layer and an air-control surface when edges are properly sealed. Appropriately specified spray foam can also form an air barrier. But material selection must account for its intended application, substrate condition, fire protection, moisture, and manufacturer instructions. A fire stop, a chimney clearance, or a vented cavity must not be replaced by improvised foam merely because air can be felt there.

At a cantilever, the relevant questions multiply. Does the outside soffit exclude wind and rain? Is there airtight blocking at the inward end of each exposed bay? Are the joist sides and subfloor seams connected to that blocking? Can the assembly dry without holding water against its framing? Opening a concealed assembly may be necessary to know. Closing it before documenting leaks or decay can make a future problem harder to discover.

### Establish that the repair worked

A useful investigation records surface temperatures, indoor and outdoor conditions, wind, and the pattern of the complaint. An energy auditor may use a blower door and thermal imaging to identify air paths under a deliberate pressure difference. The camera reveals apparent surface-temperature patterns; it cannot directly see moving air, mold inside a cavity, or the condition of buried wood. A moisture meter gives evidence about material moisture within the instrument's limits, not a diagnosis of where the water entered.

After repair, return under comparable conditions. Is the floor less cold during similar weather? Is the leakage path closed under controlled pressure? Does the sill remain dry through rain and winter? The answer should include photographs of the boundary before it disappears and a description of the materials used. A successful detail has to keep working after seasonal expansion, contraction, and minor building movement.

Rim joists and cantilevers teach a general lesson about building work: the most important boundary is often one that nobody can see from the finished room. Finding that boundary, connecting it continuously, and protecting its ability to dry is more reliable than adding insulation wherever a hand detects cold.

## Joining the Foundation Air Barrier to the Wall Above

The details have to connect across the change from foundation to wall, not only work within each separate material.

In a winter basement, you can put your hand against a cold foundation and understand why insulation is appealing. But before adding any, imagine following a pencil line from the above-grade wall down to the basement slab without ever lifting the pencil. That line represents the enclosure. The critical question is not just whether each part of the line is insulated. It is whether the control layers connect at the joints.

The floor sits between two different worlds. Above it, a framed wall may have sheathing, a weather-resistive barrier, cavity insulation, and interior drywall. Below, the foundation might be concrete, block, or stone. Between them are a sill, fasteners, rim framing, wood end grain, and several imperfect seams. A construction drawing can make these look like one clean junction. A real house often has small voids between each component. Air finds connected voids, not the gaps that are easiest for us to see.

### Four lines, four jobs

Try drawing four lines over a vertical section through the foundation. One is the water-control layer. On the exterior wall, this is associated with the drainage plane; at the foundation it may be drainage, dampproofing, or waterproofing, depending on what water exposure the assembly faces. The second is the capillary-control layer that interrupts liquid water being drawn from masonry into wood. The third is the air-control layer. The fourth is the thermal layer. It is helpful to draw a fifth arrow showing how an assembly can dry when damp.

The lines need not be made from the same material. A polyethylene sill gasket may contribute to separating wood and concrete, but a gasket under compression is not automatically an airtight connection to all the neighboring elements. A foam panel can carry thermal and air-control functions if its edges and penetrations are appropriately sealed. The drainage plane outside the wall cannot simply terminate at an arbitrary point without a water path to the exterior. Whenever two lines appear to cross without connecting, ask what actually occupies that gap.

The slab-to-wall joint presents another transition. A concrete slab may meet the foundation wall with a shrinkage or construction joint. In a basement that is intentionally part of the conditioned enclosure, that seam may be a leakage path from soil-gas or sub-slab spaces. Yet sealing a visible seam does not substitute for radon measurement or drainage diagnosis. It is possible to reduce ordinary air leakage and still have a serious soil-gas problem, because the pressure field and hidden entry paths matter.

### Why a cold stripe is not a complete diagnosis

Suppose the upper edge of a basement wall shows a line of dark staining in February. One possibility is that warm, moist indoor air is leaking to a colder surface and condensing. Another is liquid water entering from the exterior and becoming visible at the same rim. A third is old staining from a former leak. Thermal imaging might show a cold stripe in all three cases. The image does not identify the moisture source.

The sequence of evidence matters. Record the date, outdoor temperature, indoor humidity, and whether the marks grow during rain, snowmelt, or sustained cold weather. Look for water paths downhill from siding, decks, and grade. Check the condition of the sill and rim framing. A moisture meter can help establish where materials are wetter, but readings are instrument- and material-dependent. Deteriorated structure must be evaluated before a new insulating layer hides it.

There is a second temptation: to seal everything visible with one product. That can block beneficial drying, cover termite inspection areas where those are required, or violate clearance requirements near a flue. In older stone or rubble foundations, the wall may handle moisture differently from poured concrete; interior low-permeance systems can change the drying direction. A good retrofit is therefore an assembly plan rather than a shopping list.

### Trace the pressure boundary

An air barrier is effective because it separates pressure regions. Wind, stack effect, exhaust fans, and ducts create pressure differences. Imagine a bathroom exhaust fan operating on a cold evening. If the basement and attic are connected through wiring chases and unsealed floor bays, the fan may draw replacement air through those cavities. Sealing only the visible rim seam might reduce some leakage while leaving the larger loop intact. A blower-door test can make such paths easier to detect, although the imposed test pressure is not the same as normal living conditions.

Work from a chosen boundary. Is the basement inside the heated and air-sealed volume? If so, the insulation and air layer generally follow the foundation wall and turn through its top connection to the framed wall. If the basement is outside, the first-floor ceiling or underside may be the relevant boundary; the approach changes. The goal is to avoid a design that insulates one plane but accidentally leaves the pressure boundary in another.

An example helps. Imagine a conditioned basement with an exposed concrete foundation, wooden sill, and rim joist. There may be three separate leakage paths: under the sill, between the sill and rim, and between the rim and subfloor. A continuous interior air-control detail must address all three interfaces and any services passing through. Even after closing those interfaces, exterior drainage and capillary breaks still do their own jobs. Keeping rain out is not accomplished by making the inside airtight.

### The final test is not the appearance

Photograph the dry, visible assembly before covering it. Record the intended air, water, vapor, and thermal boundaries and the product specifications. Inspect the work after installation for missed corners and penetrations, then check it again across a weather cycle. Does the pressure test show a meaningful reduction where intended? Do wood moisture readings stabilize? Do the surfaces remain free of new staining through rain and winter? A useful conclusion comes from converging evidence, not a single number.

The foundation-to-wall connection is small compared with the whole house. But it reveals the discipline of building science: trace the control layers continuously, understand the forces moving heat and moisture, and do not confuse a neat-looking patch with a functioning enclosure.

## Window Wells, Below-Grade Water Entry, and Drainage Diagnosis

A basement window sits below the surrounding lawn. During an intense summer storm, water gathers in the well, rises above the bottom of the window frame, and appears on the basement floor. The immediate temptation is to replace the window or add a cover. Either may be useful, but neither answer is complete until we know where the water came from and where it was supposed to go.

A window well is a small outdoor excavation against a building. Its three important boundaries are the surrounding soil and ground surface, the well walls, and the foundation wall containing the opening. Rain may enter directly from above. Surface runoff may flow over the well rim. Water traveling through saturated soil may enter from the sides or below. Each source has a different remedy, and several can operate together.

### A bucket that is not supposed to be a bucket

Imagine the well as a bucket without a reliable drain. Even an ordinary rainfall can fill it if runoff from a larger area is directed toward the opening. The relevant quantity is not just rainfall depth but rainfall depth multiplied by the contributing collection area. One inch of rain falling on one hundred square feet corresponds to about sixty-two gallons of water before losses. That volume does not have to fall into the small window well itself. It may arrive from a patio, sloped lawn, roof downspout or frozen channel in the soil.

The shape of the yard matters. A small high spot or an edging strip can direct runoff; a depression near the foundation can hold it. Downspouts discharge concentrated roof runoff, so a five-minute observation of flow during a storm can be more informative than several dry-weather inspections. At the same time, a well that drains to a functioning subsurface system should not simply store every gallon that enters it. Water accumulating persistently suggests that the pathway out is blocked, overwhelmed or absent.

### Surface drainage and subsurface drainage are different systems

The first line of defense is usually control of water on the surface: roof collection and discharge, grade that leads water away, and the elevation of the window-well rim relative to adjacent ground. A cover can limit direct rainfall and debris, but it cannot solve groundwater pushing up through the bottom or surface water overtopping the well. A cover may also affect ventilation, egress and access; it cannot be chosen purely for dryness.

Below the surface, a bed of gravel may give the appearance of a drain while providing only temporary storage. Gravel has voids, but a closed gravel pocket eventually fills. A well drain must have a workable outlet, perhaps to a suitable foundation-drainage system or another designed discharge location. Whether such a connection is appropriate depends on local conditions and design. Sediment, roots and frost can compromise performance. Introducing water into an already overburdened footing drain can make basement wetting worse, rather than better.

A water stain below the window does not prove the glass or sash leaked. Water may enter at the joint between frame and foundation, follow a crack or travel behind a finish before emerging farther away. Look for staining or deposits at the lowest point of the frame, at the sill-to-foundation joint, along the well-wall connection and on nearby interior surfaces. Map evidence before choosing a sealant.

### Hydrostatic pressure and openings

Water accumulating against a below-grade wall exerts pressure that increases with depth. This is why treating a saturated wall as a simple surface-sealing problem can be unsuccessful. An opening or joint near the low point experiences a driving pressure when water rises outside it. A caulk bead can deteriorate, pull away or divert leakage to a neighboring weakness. The durable design must first reduce water accumulation and provide a drainage path. Water-resistant exterior detailing and the window assembly's own flashing or drainage provisions then become complementary barriers.

This reasoning extends to other penetrations: utility sleeves, basement doors, old vent openings and cracks. A below-grade defect is often a combined problem of water supply, storage, pressure and an imperfect boundary.

### A diagnosis in three observations

Suppose the window well floods only in short, intense storms. Start with a map of roof drains, paved areas, grades and the well rim. During an event, if safe to observe, note whether water enters over the top, runs down the well wall or appears upward from the gravel. Record how fast it rises and how long it takes to fall. Photograph a ruler or a fixed reference point; the pattern may disappear by morning.

Next compare the well to the rest of the basement. Is there a sump pump? Are foundation drains known to exist? Does the sump cycle heavily at the same time? Do other low openings leak? These observations help distinguish a local blocked well drain from a broader groundwater or drainage-system problem. A test of an individual discharge line is different from proving the whole drainage system functions during a storm.

Finally distinguish the water path from the repair surface. If runoff crosses the rim, address the contributing area and rim design. If the well drains poorly, investigate its outlet and the condition of its drainage components. If water enters at a joint while the exterior remains below the opening, examine the window, foundation and transition detail together. For egress windows, preserve the required usable clearances and means of escape when redesigning the well.

The central lesson is that a window well is not merely a hole with a window in it. It is a small drainage basin attached to a vulnerable opening in a larger foundation enclosure. Good diagnosis follows the water from the source, through the collection area, into storage, through the intended outlet and finally across any failed boundary. Once those paths are understood, the repair options become far easier to evaluate.

### Basements, Foundations, and Groundwater

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Water control, vapor transport, and heat flow. **Purpose:** understand foundations as structures in contact with soil and diagnose basement water by following its driving forces.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Hydrostatic pressure sketch

For static water of density approximately 1,000 kg/m³, pressure above ambient grows as `p = ρgh`. At a depth of 0.50 m, this is approximately `1000 × 9.81 × 0.50 = 4,905 Pa`, or 4.9 kPa. Pressure is not uniform along the height of a submerged wall; a structural assessment must account for the actual water profile and loading conditions.

##### Diagnostic matrix

| Observation | Hypothesis to test | Distinguishing evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Wet immediately after rain | Surface water and wall opening | Gutters, grading, discharge, location and timing |
| Persistent seepage low on wall | Groundwater or blocked drainage | Water level, sump performance, seasonal response |
| Summer droplets on cool surfaces | Indoor-air condensation | Surface temperature, dew point, response to dehumidification |
| Dark line with displacement or bowing | Structural movement | Measurement over time, structural evaluation |

**Review boundary:** This material does not prescribe foundation excavation, structural repair, waterproofing specifications, or radon mitigation. Site investigation and qualified design are necessary where those decisions are consequential.

### Radon, Soil Gas, and Foundation Air Boundaries

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** pressure differences, air leakage, foundations, and groundwater. **Purpose:** understand the distinct problem of soil-gas entry and how measurement changes decisions.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Boundary diagram to draw

Soil pores → connected paths beneath slab → slab/utility/sump openings → occupied space. Add a second arrow from sub-slab collection point through an active fan to appropriate exterior discharge. Label **source**, **pressure-driven transport**, **measurement location**, and **verification**.

##### Diagnostic distinctions

| Observation | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Visible slab crack | A possible gas/air route | A radon concentration |
| Negative pressure at basement | Possible entry driving force | Whether the soil contains enough radon to matter |
| Fan audible at mitigation equipment | Motor operation | Effective pressure-field extension or low indoor radon |
| Valid indoor radon test | Concentration under its test conditions | Lifetime exposure without additional context |

### Joining the Foundation Air Barrier to the Wall Above

### 019000 — Joining the Foundation Air Barrier to the Wall Above

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Section-drawing exercise.** Draw a masonry or concrete foundation, sill gasket, sill plate, rim joist, subfloor, wall sheathing, and cladding. Trace separate bulk-water, capillary, air, and thermal control lines. Mark the drying directions and every transition that requires a specific detail.

**Diagnostic matrix.** Staining correlated with rain suggests bulk-water investigation; staining tied to cold weather and high interior humidity raises condensation as a hypothesis; persistent localized wetness raises plumbing, groundwater, and hidden decay possibilities. These are discriminators, not proofs.

**Boundary cautions.** Do not conceal structural decay. Preserve flue clearances, required foam thermal/ignition barriers, termite inspection access, and any intentional drainage or ventilation paths. Insulation class, thickness and vapor resistance depend on climate, assembly, adopted requirements and product documentation. Radon requires direct testing.

**Reference reading.** DOE Building America Solution Center, *Air-Sealed, Insulated Basements*; DOE BASC, *Pre-Retrofit Assessment of Crawlspaces and Basements*; DOE Building Science Education, *Air Barriers vs Vapor Barriers*.

### Window Wells, Below-Grade Water Entry, and Drainage Diagnosis

### 021000 — Window Wells, Below-Grade Water Entry, and Drainage Diagnosis

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Rain volume:** US gallons approximately `0.623 × rainfall(inches) × area(square feet)`. A 1-inch storm collecting from 100 square feet supplies about 62 gallons before infiltration or other losses. This is a water-budget demonstration, not a site design rule.

**Observation worksheet:** Date and storm intensity; roof downspout location; grading and hardscape direction; well rim elevation; whether entry is over the rim, through the wall, or from the bottom; maximum depth and drain-down time; interior staining locations; concurrent sump activity. Photograph water-line evidence before surfaces dry.

**Distinguishing actions:** Surface diversion versus below-grade outlet investigation versus frame/joint investigation. Do not infer that gravel alone is an outlet. Evaluate egress constraints before covers, grates or regrading; exact local requirements and existing assembly details need project-specific review.

**Sources and scope:** US DOE Building America Solution Center, *Look at Crawlspace for Moisture Issues*; NYSERDA, *Build Better Guide*, foundation drainage; DOE/PNNL Building America details on foundations and water management. Building-science guidance supports the mechanism and diagnostic framework; it does not verify a particular drainage layout or retrofit design.
