# Measure first and retrofit without creating new failures

## Combustion Appliances, Depressurization, and Indoor Air

Imagine a water heater burning gas in a basement. For years, its exhaust has gone up a metal vent and out of the house. Then someone installs a powerful kitchen exhaust hood. On the first cold evening after the installation, the hood is running, the clothes dryer is running, and the water heater fires. The water heater has not changed, but the direction of air movement near its vent may have changed. Why?

A combustion appliance sits at the intersection of three systems: a fuel supply, an oxygen supply, and an exhaust path. We often pay attention to the first and third while treating air as an unlimited background resource. But a building is a pressure network, not an infinite reservoir. Air that leaves the house has to be replaced. The route taken by replacement air can influence an appliance's ability to vent its combustion products.

### Combustion needs a pathway in both directions

In ideal complete combustion, a hydrocarbon fuel reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water vapor, and heat. Real flames are affected by mixing, temperature, burner geometry, and the time available for reactions to proceed. Incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide, a poisonous, odorless gas. Fuel burning can also produce nitrogen oxides, particles, and water vapor. A blue-looking flame by itself is not a reliable measurement of safe exhaust or carbon monoxide production.

A traditional naturally drafted appliance relies on buoyancy: hot combustion gases tend to rise through an appropriately configured vent. This creates a small pressure difference that can move exhaust toward the outdoors. The draft is not a magic one-way valve. Wind, chimney temperature, vent restrictions, and the indoor pressure near the appliance can all affect it. An appliance with a powered inducer or a sealed-combustion arrangement has a different set of dependencies; neither label eliminates the need for a correctly designed venting system.

Consider a mechanical exhaust fan as an air pump. A range hood rated to remove a substantial volume of air per minute takes air out of the building. If there is no adequate replacement-air path, the pressure indoors tends to fall relative to outdoors. Air may then enter through cracks, basement openings, or the vent of an appliance that was intended to carry gases in the opposite direction. That reverse movement is called backdrafting. It is a pressure and pathway failure, not simply a bad smell or a weak fan.

### A building can change the result without changing the burner

Imagine two identical water heaters in otherwise different houses. In the first, the air available to the appliance and the vent system are accounted for, and exhaust devices have suitable replacement-air provisions. In the second, weatherization has closed many incidental leaks, but a large exhaust fan was added without evaluating the combustion system. Sealing leaks can improve energy performance and comfort, yet it also changes the air-flow network. Whether a specific appliance remains safely vented is a question to measure and verify, not a conclusion to draw from how tight the house feels.

Stack effect makes the situation more variable. When warm indoor air leaks from upper portions of a building, air is pulled in at lower levels. A basement appliance already operates in a portion of the house that may be at lower pressure during cold weather. Wind can add or subtract from this effect depending on its direction and the building's openings. Exhaust fans and duct leakage can change the pressure again. A combustion problem can therefore depend on season and which equipment runs simultaneously.

### Follow the air, not the equipment labels

A useful system drawing has arrows for fuel, combustion air, exhaust gas, and other mechanical airflows. Put the kitchen hood, dryer, bathroom fan, ventilation system, and forced-air duct system on the same drawing. Note which appliances draw their combustion air from the room and which have a dedicated outside-air supply. Note where the vents terminate and where outdoor air is expected to enter.

Then distinguish three observations. The first is whether the burner operates. The second is whether exhaust moves in the intended direction under relevant operating conditions. The third is whether the resulting indoor air is safe. One does not prove the others. A flame can continue while combustion products enter the room; an exhaust fan can run while its duct is blocked. Carbon monoxide alarms provide a critical warning layer but cannot replace correct appliance installation or professional combustion-safety testing.

There is a practical diagnostic trap here. If the water heater vent looks cold or stained, it is tempting to assume that replacing the water heater will solve everything. But the driving cause could be an undersized or obstructed vent, interaction with another appliance, an altered pressure boundary, or inadequate combustion air. Conversely, making a hole in an exterior wall is not a universal remedy: the opening's size, placement, airflow, and effect on the building all matter. The correct intervention follows an assessment of the actual system.

### A worked thought experiment

Suppose an exhaust fan removes air at a rate of three hundred cubic feet per minute. For mass balance, roughly that much replacement air must ultimately enter the building when the fan is operating steadily. If exterior openings and mechanical supply cannot deliver it at small pressure differences, the pressure difference increases until other routes contribute or the actual fan flow falls. The exact pressure cannot be calculated from the fan rating alone; we would need the building leakage characteristics and operating equipment. That is why a catalog airflow number does not tell us whether backdrafting will occur.

This reasoning also explains why a draft can seem normal when checked with all the fans off and reverse during a particular operating combination. The observation is conditional. Evaluating the condition requires appropriate instruments and procedures, including assessment of the appliance, vent, and pressure interactions. Deliberately making an appliance spill exhaust as an informal experiment is not a safe substitute for a professional combustion-safety evaluation.

The general lesson reaches beyond combustion. Any device that moves air modifies a shared pressure system. An apparently successful building upgrade can expose a weakness elsewhere when the interacting systems are omitted from the design. Building science asks us to follow matter and energy across the whole building boundary. For combustion appliances, that means tracing air into the flame, exhaust out of the building, and the pressure differences that determine whether those paths remain separated.

## Inspecting an Existing House: An Evidence-Based Walkthrough

Those interactions are part of what an evidence-based house inspection must notice before a retrofit is chosen.

A house can look immaculate and still have a significant water problem. It can also have a stained ceiling from a leak repaired fifteen years ago. An inspection is an exercise in distinguishing current behavior from historical evidence, and an attempt to discover which mechanisms deserve closer examination. It is not a contest to identify the longest list of defects.

Imagine arriving at a two-story house after rain. Rather than start at the kitchen faucet, begin outside, across the street if possible. Observe the shape of the roof, where valleys deliver water, and whether gutters discharge beside the foundation. Look at the ground near the walls. Does it slope away, hold puddles, or send runoff from a higher neighbor's lot toward the building? We are studying the boundary conditions: what loads of rain, heat, ground moisture, and air pressure are being applied to the enclosure.

### Follow water in the direction it can actually travel

Water follows gravity when free to move, but capillarity, wind pressure, and drainage paths complicate that story. The place where a damp patch appears is not necessarily where the water entered. A stain halfway down a wall may have begun at a roof penetration, traveled along framing, then emerged where plaster offered an opening. A dark foundation patch may arise from groundwater or from warm indoor air condensing on a cold surface. The appearance alone does not settle the question.

Walk the exterior from top to bottom. At roof transitions, inspect visible flashing conditions without stepping onto a roof. Around windows and doors, ask where water that passes the outer cladding would drain. Look for an outward path above openings, at wall bottoms, and at the interface with the foundation. Peeling paint near a window can indicate repeated wetting, but it also depends on paint preparation, exposure, and sun. Evidence becomes stronger when several independent signs agree: water marks, damaged material, an uphill runoff pathway, and a moisture reading taken under suitable conditions.

An inspection needs temporal context. Ask when the symptom appears. Immediately after wind-driven rain points toward one set of paths; only in cold weather suggests another; only when an upstairs shower runs suggests plumbing. A measurement without its circumstances is merely a number. A moisture meter can help compare locations, but readings depend on material, salts, temperature, and instrument method. One displayed percentage does not automatically measure the true moisture content of every wall assembly.

### Trace heat, air, and moisture together

Inside, notice room-to-room temperature differences, cold surfaces, drafts, and persistent odors. An infrared camera may reveal surface temperature patterns, but a cold patch is not proof of missing insulation. Air washing, a structural thermal bridge, wet material, solar exposure, and changing heating operation can all alter the image. Use infrared patterns to form questions, not to issue final diagnoses.

The attic is a useful model of this discipline. A cold-climate attic with frost around a penetration might be receiving warm, moist indoor air through an unsealed chase. It might also have roof leakage. A bath exhaust duct ending in the attic creates a different localized source. Examine the relationship among the air barrier, insulation, roof ventilation design, and exhaust termination. Merely adding more insulation can conceal an air leak without stopping moisture transport.

Basements invite another important distinction. Water on the floor after storms may indicate bulk water from grading, foundation cracks, or overloaded drainage systems. A musty smell in summer with no visible intrusion may instead be linked to humid air reaching cool surfaces. Relative humidity is not a direct measure of how much water is in the air; temperature alters relative humidity. A cool basement can therefore feel damp even without a new liquid-water leak. The proper response depends on the transport mechanism, and multiple mechanisms can coexist.

### Consider the house's operating systems

The structure's enclosure is only half the story. Observe what combustion appliances and ventilation fans do to air pressure. Exhausting indoor air can draw replacement air through leaks and, in some configurations, compete with the draft of natural-draft combustion equipment. Carbon monoxide is not recognizable by smell, so working alarms and appropriate professional combustion evaluation matter more than informal reassurance. Look for documented servicing and safe, visible vent arrangements, not speculative conclusions from a single photograph.

Electrical panels, concealed plumbing, gas lines, and structural elements have inspection boundaries. You can document visible conditions and service records without opening energized enclosures, operating unknown shutoffs, climbing unsafe roofs, or treating apparent normality as certification. The aim is to identify concerns, priorities, and questions for appropriately qualified evaluators. A house inspection never reveals every concealed component.

### Build hypotheses, then try to falsify them

Suppose the northwest bedroom is cold. Hypothesis one: the window has poor thermal performance. Hypothesis two: the duct supplies too little air. Hypothesis three: the ceiling has an air leak. Begin with the inexpensive distinctions. Does the room cool when the furnace cycles off, or fail to warm while it runs? Is the air delivered at the register warm but weak? Does the temperature difference grow with wind? Are interior surfaces cold in a pattern consistent with missing insulation? Each observation changes which hypothesis deserves the next test.

Keep a record with date, weather, indoor conditions, what was observed, how it was measured, and what remains uncertain. Photograph broad context before close-ups. Record the *absence* of evidence only within the area actually examined: no water visible during a dry-day visit is not a guarantee that a wall stays dry during storms. Rank findings by potential consequence and confidence separately. A suspected structural movement or exhaust hazard may need urgent evaluation despite incomplete diagnosis; a worn finish can wait even when the defect is certain.

A careful inspection turns a house from a collection of appearances into a set of physical systems under observable loads. The useful question is not simply, "What looks wrong?" It is, "What process could produce this evidence, what else would that process predict, and how can we find out without creating a new problem?"

## Thermal Imaging, Blower Doors, and Diagnosing Envelope Leaks

On a cold morning, a thermal camera makes a poorly insulated corner look like a dark stain. It is tempting to call the stain a leak. But what did the camera actually measure? Not the movement of air. Not the presence of water. The camera detected infrared radiation leaving the surfaces in its field of view, and its display estimated apparent surface temperatures from that radiation. The cold patch could result from a missing piece of insulation, a framing member conducting heat, a draft cooling the surface, a wet material, or even a reflection. An instrument becomes useful when we understand both the observation and the question it can answer.

A building enclosure has several transport paths. Heat conducts through solids, moves with air, and crosses cavities by radiation. Air flows through openings when pressure differences drive it. Rain enters where water-control details fail. Vapor diffuses through materials according to vapor-pressure differences. Diagnostics should separate those processes before deciding on a repair.

### What a blower door measures

A blower door is a calibrated fan temporarily installed in an exterior doorway. It moves air until the house reaches a controlled pressure difference relative to outdoors. At a widely used reference pressure of fifty pascals, the instrument measures the airflow needed to maintain that difference. If it takes twelve hundred cubic feet per minute to keep the house depressurized by fifty pascals, the test result is twelve hundred CFM50. This is a measurement of enclosure leakage under a deliberately imposed test condition, not a statement that twelve hundred cubic feet per minute constantly enters the house in ordinary weather.

To compare houses of different size, practitioners may report air changes per hour at fifty pascals, or ACH50. Multiply CFM50 by sixty minutes and divide by the volume of the tested enclosure in cubic feet. For a twenty-thousand-cubic-foot house and twelve hundred CFM50, that is three point six ACH50. Notice the missing ingredient: the building's natural pressure history. Wind speed, indoor-outdoor temperature, height, and mechanical fans change pressures hour by hour. A measured ACH50 cannot be converted to normal ventilation by one universally valid multiplier.

The test boundary also matters. A conditioned basement, a crawlspace, and an attached garage are not interchangeable parts of the volume calculation. Open windows, intentional dampers, fireplaces, and air-handling systems must be set according to the test protocol. An apparently improved result may reflect changed test configuration rather than better workmanship.

### Use pressure to make a hidden leak visible

Consider a cold ceiling edge that shows up on a thermal camera. First observe it with the fan off. Then establish a controlled depressurization, and observe again while the indoor-outdoor temperature difference remains useful. If the cold area expands or develops a streak consistent with incoming air, that change supports an air-leak hypothesis. If the temperature pattern remains stationary along a framing member, a thermal bridge becomes more plausible. Neither conclusion is automatic. Air movement can be slow, insulation can be discontinuous, and sunlight or changing HVAC operation can distort the comparison.

A smoke pencil or other appropriate airflow indicator can supplement the image near accessible gaps. It shows local air movement in the test condition, not the hidden route through which the air entered the assembly. A draft at a baseboard may originate in an attic bypass far from the observed outlet. Sealing the visible exit without understanding the route can leave an important cavity connection unresolved.

### The limits of infrared photographs

The camera depends on surface emissivity. Most painted plaster and many building materials radiate reasonably well for ordinary comparisons. Polished metal and reflective glass are different: their apparent temperature may be dominated by reflected surroundings. Wet surfaces may cool through evaporation, but a cool region is not proof of a leak or concealed moisture. Conversely, wet materials can be close to surrounding temperature, and a camera may miss them entirely.

Thermal images also depend on timing. A sun-warmed wall retains heat after sunset. A radiator recently turned off leaves a recognizable warm patch. A heavy masonry wall reacts slowly to weather. A camera used after a stable temperature period and with the building's operating state recorded gives more interpretable evidence than a dramatic photograph taken at an arbitrary moment. Confirm suspected moisture with appropriate inspection and independent measurements, not with color alone.

### Plan the diagnostic sequence

Start with a specific question: Is this room cold because of air leakage, missing insulation, window performance, or insufficient heat delivery? Record outdoor temperature, recent weather, indoor setpoints, exhaust fans, and visible construction. Look for patterns at penetrations, top plates, rim joists, attic hatches, and transitions between assemblies. Use a blower door to quantify whole-enclosure leakage and help locate accessible paths. Use thermal imaging to compare surface patterns under known conditions. When appropriate, perform targeted pressure measurements to separate duct leakage, enclosure leakage, and room-to-room pressure imbalances.

After repair, test again using comparable settings. A reduction in measured leakage is meaningful, but it does not by itself establish adequate indoor air quality, safe combustion operation, or moisture durability. Airtightness is one part of an enclosure, and intentional ventilation is another.

The best diagnostic report contains more than a colorful thermal image. It states the hypothesis, the controlled conditions, the measurements, the competing explanations, the repair made, and the observation that would falsify the conclusion. That is how a tool becomes a method.

## Diagnosing Heat Loss with Utility Data and Weather Normalization

Suppose the heating bill rises by thirty percent. Is the house leaking more heat? Perhaps. Or the winter may have been colder, the thermostat higher, somebody may be home all day, or a furnace may have become less efficient. A bill is a measurement of purchased energy, not a direct measurement of the envelope. To learn from it, we need a model that separates weather, activity, and equipment from the building itself.

### Begin with an energy balance

On a cold day, heat leaves the conditioned space by conduction through walls, windows, floors, and roof; by exchange of indoor and outdoor air; and sometimes through uncontrolled paths into unconditioned rooms. Solar radiation, appliances, occupants, and heating equipment supply heat. When indoor temperature stays approximately constant, those gains balance the losses. A simple engineering model writes heat-loss rate as an overall heat-loss coefficient multiplied by the indoor-to-outdoor temperature difference. That coefficient, often called H or U A for a collection of assemblies, has units of power per degree of temperature difference.

This is not the same as an appliance's energy consumption. A gas furnace burns fuel to replace a portion of the lost heat, but the useful fraction depends on the furnace and distribution system. An electric resistance heater converts electrical energy to heat near where it operates. A heat pump delivers heat by moving it from outside, so electrical consumption depends on its coefficient of performance and operating conditions. Two houses with equal physical heat losses can have quite different bills.

Even the word heat loss needs care. If a heat pump supplies four kilowatt-hours of thermal energy using one kilowatt-hour of electricity, a one kilowatt-hour change in the meter does not necessarily mean the house gained or lost only one kilowatt-hour of thermal energy. A useful comparison must keep the equipment and operating mode in view.

### Degree days: putting weather on a common scale

A heating degree day measures how far, and for how long, outdoor conditions fall below a selected reference or balance-point temperature. A common published reference is sixty-five degrees Fahrenheit. If the day's average temperature is fifty-five degrees, that day contributes ten heating degree days on that basis. A twenty-degree difference sustained for two days contributes forty. Accumulating these numbers gives a compact measure of heating-weather severity for a billing period.

The sixty-five-degree convention is useful for comparison but is not the exact temperature at which every building starts heating. Internal and solar gains let some buildings coast without heat at outdoor temperatures below sixty-five degrees. A different house, or a house with unusually high ventilation, may have another balance point. Weather normalization removes one major source of variation; it does not remove every source.

Take a deliberately simple gas-heated house. Its summer bill, when space heating is off, averages twenty therms a month for water heating and cooking. In an illustrative winter billing period with eight hundred heating degree days, the meter shows one hundred therms. Subtracting the estimated twenty-therm non-heating baseline leaves eighty therms associated with heating. That is one tenth of a therm per heating degree day. If another winter has nine hundred and sixty heating degree days, the same slope predicts ninety-six heating therms, plus the baseline, or about one hundred sixteen total. A higher bill in that colder month is not evidence of a degraded enclosure by itself.

Notice how fragile the example becomes if the baseline changes. More showers, visitors, a gas fireplace, or an inaccurate billing-period alignment can spoil the estimate. We need several observations to distinguish a trend from noise.

### Fit a pattern, not a single ratio

Collect at least a season of bills, preferably several seasons if conditions were comparable. Record actual billing start and end dates rather than assigning the whole bill to a calendar month. Convert meter readings into consistent energy units. Pair each interval with heating degree days from an appropriate weather station. Plot energy use against degree days.

If the building behaves consistently, points for the heating season may form an approximately straight rising band. The intercept represents weather-independent consumption in a simple model; the slope estimates the additional purchased energy for each degree day. A sudden change in slope can signal altered equipment performance, occupancy, thermostat behavior, envelope leakage, or how heat is being distributed. A jump in intercept can suggest a base load that changed even without colder weather.

A fitted line is a diagnostic summary, not proof of a particular cause. Examine outlying points. Was a door left open during renovation? Did a vacation reduce occupancy? Was auxiliary resistance heat used during a cold spell? Were the meters estimated instead of read? Was the heat pump operated at a different setpoint? Corrections should be documented rather than silently discarded to make the graph look tidy.

### A useful boundary for inference

Electric bills often combine heating with lighting, cooking, computing, drying clothes, and cooling. When a home uses both a heat pump and resistance backup, a linear fit can hide two different operating regimes. Solar gains, wind, and humidity also matter. A more capable model may include cooling degree days, measured indoor temperature, equipment run time, or a separate cold-weather regime. But adding parameters to a small dataset can create a very persuasive fit with little predictive value.

A whole-house utility model cannot reveal whether the attic, a window, or an unsealed rim joist causes the heat loss. That is the job of additional observations and, where justified, measurements. An infrared camera shows apparent surface temperature patterns under certain conditions. A blower door measures air leakage at a prescribed pressure. Room-temperature logging, equipment service records, and duct inspections help determine whether heat is being delivered where it is needed.

### Assess the repair without rewarding a warmer winter

Imagine weather-sealing and attic work completed in October. Comparing November's bill directly with last November's confounds the retrofit with outdoor weather and changing household behavior. Instead, calculate what the pre-retrofit model predicts for this season's weather, then compare that prediction with actual consumption. Report the uncertainty, the changes in thermostat schedule and occupancy, and any changes to the heating equipment.

For a more credible comparison, keep fuel conversions consistent, examine several billing periods, and ask whether the predicted reduction is larger than ordinary month-to-month variation. If the improvement is small compared with noise, the honest conclusion is that the available bills cannot establish it. If the change is large and persistent, investigate whether an unrelated equipment or usage change could explain it before assigning all the savings to insulation.

The real power of weather normalization is not that it turns bills into a precise building audit. It teaches us to ask a better question: how much energy did the home require under comparable conditions? That question can guide an investigation and help evaluate a repair while leaving room for the uncertainty that real households introduce.

## Planning Envelope Retrofits Without Trapping Moisture

The measurements now support a retrofit decision that accounts for drying and other system boundaries, rather than heat loss alone.

Imagine an old house in winter. Heat escapes through its walls, and the owner plans to fill every cavity with insulation. The energy calculation looks favorable: less heat will move through the wall. Yet the first winter after the retrofit, the sheathing becomes wetter. The insulation did what it was asked to do. The retrofit failed because the wall was not only a thermal assembly. It was also a system that admitted, stored, and released water.

The correct design problem is not simply, “How much insulation can we add?” It is, “How will this assembly respond to rain, indoor humidity, air pressure, and changing temperatures after we modify it?” Each of those influences has its own pathway. Rain is bulk liquid water. Air leaks can carry water vapor into a cavity where it may condense. Vapor diffusion moves moisture through materials even when air is stationary, usually at a much lower rate than a significant air leak. Liquid water can also move by capillary action. A retrofit must address the dominant pathway, not just the pathway most familiar to the installer.

### First determine where water comes from

Consider a stained patch below a second-floor window. It might follow wind-driven rain through a failed flashing connection. It might result from indoor humid air leaking toward cold sheathing. It might be a plumbing leak from an adjacent room. A moisture meter reading alone does not identify the source. The location, season, wind, precipitation, temperature, and pressure conditions help distinguish the explanations. If the patch appears only after storms, inspect the exterior water-control path before deciding to install a new vapor barrier.

Existing buildings have a history. Before planning a wall retrofit, inspect visible siding, roof edges, window openings, interior staining, crawlspaces, and attic conditions. Record where water has drained, where it could accumulate, and what materials are already present. Be alert to assemblies that have performed tolerably because they could dry rapidly, not because they never became wet. The existing condition is not proof of good design, but it is evidence about how the assembly behaves.

### Insulation changes temperature and drying

A wall's temperature distribution depends on the relative thermal resistance of its layers. Add insulation to the inside, and the old sheathing may become colder during winter. Colder sheathing can make condensation from interior moisture more likely if humid air reaches it. Add suitable continuous insulation outside, and that same sheathing may become warmer in winter, but the exterior layer's vapor permeability and drainage details affect whether the wall can dry outward. Neither “inside insulation” nor “outside insulation” is categorically safe or unsafe. The correct choice depends on climate, assembly, existing water control, material permeability, and continuity.

Suppose two walls have similar nominal insulation values. One has gaps at the top plates and around outlets, allowing indoor air into cavities; the other has a continuous air-control layer. The first wall may experience moisture concentrations at leakage paths even when an average vapor-diffusion calculation looks acceptable. This is why air sealing and insulation should be considered as coordinated interventions. Sealing an assembly is not the same as putting polyethylene everywhere. Air-control and vapor-control functions are distinct, though a single material may provide both when detailed and installed correctly.

### Drying potential is an engineering resource

A wall has a wetting potential and a drying potential. If occasional wetting is unavoidable, the assembly must have a credible route to dry between events. A highly vapor-resistant layer on both sides can restrict inward and outward drying. That can be particularly consequential if the cladding leaks, construction moisture remains, or an existing wall contains damp material before the retrofit. The remedy is not necessarily to eliminate all vapor retarders: vapor control can prevent wintertime moisture accumulation in a climate where it is needed. The task is to place and select control layers so they work together, following an appropriate assembly design rather than stacking products by marketing claims.

Drainage matters separately. A weather-resistive barrier needs an exit at the bottom and flashing integrated with openings. A back-ventilated cladding cavity can improve drying and help manage wind-driven rain. If a retrofit buries a defective sill flashing beneath new exterior insulation, it may make a leak harder to find without correcting it.

### Account for the whole building

Air sealing may reduce uncontrolled ventilation. The house can then maintain different indoor humidity levels than before, and combustion appliances may respond differently to pressure changes. The effective ventilation system, exhaust fans, and any fuel-burning equipment belong in the plan. In a cold climate, controlling humidity and intentional ventilation can be as important as choosing insulation. In a hot, humid climate, air conditioning and inward vapor drives complicate the picture differently.

The retrofit sequence should be deliberate. First document existing conditions and diagnose bulk-water defects. Decide where the water, air, thermal, and vapor-control layers will lie. Draw a continuous line for each control function through windows, corners, roofs, and foundations. Specify penetrations and transitions, not only the middle of the wall. Select an assembly appropriate to the climate and materials, then decide how performance will be checked: moisture conditions, airtightness, visible drainage details, and follow-up observation over weather cycles.

Here is the important change in perspective: energy savings are one outcome of an envelope retrofit. Keeping structural materials dry is another, and a retrofit that achieves the first by sacrificing the second has not succeeded. Understand the pathways before selecting the products.

## Distinguishing Residual Moisture from New Wetting After an Enclosure Retrofit

After the retrofit, a moisture observation still has to distinguish new wetting from material that is continuing to dry.

You air-seal and insulate an old basement. The room gets warmer. Three weeks later you notice a darker patch near the bottom of a wall. Has the retrofit caused a leak? Has it concealed a leak that was already present? Or are you seeing moisture left in the old materials before the work began? The answers call for different repairs, and the appearance of a damp patch alone cannot distinguish them.

Water in a building has a history. A material may be wet today because water entered this morning, because water entered months ago and dries slowly, or because vapor from indoor air has condensed in a newly cold location. A useful investigation tries to reconstruct that history rather than merely detect a high reading on a meter.

### The water budget of a material

Imagine a small region of wall as a reservoir. It receives water from several possible sources: bulk rain or ground water, capillary transport from wet concrete, vapor diffusion, and air leakage carrying humidity. Water also leaves through evaporation toward the interior or exterior, drainage, and sometimes through mechanical drying. The material gets wetter when incoming moisture exceeds what can leave. An enclosure change can reduce a source, reduce a drying route, or both.

Adding interior insulation to a cold foundation may reduce heat that formerly warmed the foundation. If that foundation becomes colder, its drying conditions change. Adding an impermeable layer may reduce inward vapor movement but also block inward drying. These are reasons to examine the assembly, not reasons to assume every interior insulation strategy is defective. A design can include appropriate drainage, capillary separation, temperature control and deliberately chosen drying paths.

### Residual moisture and repeated wetting leave different evidence

Suppose a foundation has experienced months of intermittent rain leakage. After a drainage repair, it may remain damp for a long time. Dense concrete and masonry can store considerable water. A single elevated meter reading after the repair cannot establish a new leak. What matters is whether readings at comparable locations and under comparable conditions trend downward over time.

A second pattern is a sudden step upward after rain, snowmelt, irrigation or a plumbing event. A third is a daily pattern associated with indoor humidity and surface temperature. A fourth is a localized hot spot where air from a humid room reaches a colder concealed surface. Each pattern suggests a different test, though none proves a source on its own.

Start with the weather and building history. Record rainfall, snowmelt, groundwater observations, recent plumbing use, indoor temperature, relative humidity and visible changes. Draw the room: foundation joints, windows, service penetrations, the rim joist and the location of any insulation or vapor-control layer. Inspect outside first. Downspouts, grades, window wells and roof drainage may explain the problem more economically than opening a finished wall.

Then distinguish three measurements that are often blurred together. A humidity sensor measures moisture in air. An infrared camera measures apparent surface radiance, interpreted as temperature with important assumptions; it is not a water detector. A moisture meter responds to an electrical or electromagnetic property of its surroundings, which can be affected by material composition, salts, metal and temperature as well as moisture. A comparison at two spots is useful only if their construction and measurement conditions are comparable.

### A controlled investigation

Let's say a meter reads high at the base of two adjacent insulated bays and low on an interior partition. You might begin by choosing fixed measurement points, taking photographs and recording the instrument and its setting. Repeat at the same points after several dry days and again after a significant rainfall. If you have access to bare comparable material elsewhere, use it as a relative reference. Do not treat an arbitrary numerical setting as a universal percentage of water by mass.

If rain consistently precedes rising readings, inspect bulk-water routes and their connections before changing an entire interior wall. If readings remain elevated but slowly decline without weather correlation, investigate drying time and residual storage. If moisture is concentrated behind an interior air leak during cold weather, look at air-barrier continuity and local dew-point conditions. A change in HVAC operation, indoor humidity or a newly installed dehumidifier can confound the comparison; record those too.

We also need to understand what a successful test would look like. A repair should address a plausible entry or accumulation mechanism and then demonstrate reduced wetting, not simply hide a stain. A waterproof coating over a damp interior surface can conceal evidence and redirect water while leaving the driving force intact. A dehumidifier can make the air comfortable without solving bulk water entering from outside. Those interventions may have roles, but they answer different questions.

### When the measurement changes the building

Opening an inspection area is not a neutral act: removing trim or insulation may temporarily improve drying. Pressure tests and depressurization can change airflow and, in some houses, combustion appliance operation. Disturbing suspect mold, asbestos-containing materials or deteriorated structural components changes the task again. Decide beforehand which observations justify intrusive work and what the boundaries are.

The general method is chronological and comparative. Establish what the enclosure used to do. Identify sources and exits for water. Measure repeatedly under documented conditions. Form two or three hypotheses that predict different responses to weather or equipment operation. Repair the cause supported by the evidence, then continue monitoring. A damp wall is not a diagnosis. Its behavior over time is where the diagnosis begins.

### Combustion Appliances, Depressurization, and Indoor Air

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** pressure-driven air leakage, ventilation, and thermal boundaries. **Purpose:** explain why an appliance that burns fuel cannot be considered separately from the air pressure of the building around it.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### System model and diagnostic distinctions

- Steady building air mass balance: total supply plus infiltration approximately equals exhaust plus exfiltration, adjusted for changes in stored air mass. Fan nameplate flow is not necessarily delivered flow.
- Natural draft depends on gas-density difference, vent geometry, temperature, wind, and pressure at the appliance. A room-air appliance and a sealed-combustion appliance have different boundary conditions.
- Distinguish **source** (combustion), **transport** (pressure and vent), **entry** (spillage into occupied space), and **detection** (alarms and instruments).
- Illustrative equipment interactions to record: hood, clothes dryer, bath fan, air handler, fireplace, other vented appliances, outdoor-air systems, building tightness.

##### Field-work limitations

Never use a CO alarm as a combustion analyzer, and never disable safety interlocks for a teaching demonstration. Alarm symptoms or suspected exposure require immediate action consistent with emergency guidance. Actual commissioning and combustion-safety test sequences are appliance-, vent-, code-, and jurisdiction-dependent and require appropriate competency. EPA guidance specifically notes that exhaust hoods can induce backdrafting of naturally drafted combustion appliances.

##### Questions to test understanding

1. Explain why replacing a furnace does not necessarily solve the low-pressure condition that caused spillage.
2. Sketch how cold-weather stack effect and a dryer could combine to change basement pressure.
3. What facts would distinguish insufficient draft from an exhaust blockage or a contaminated air supply?

### Thermal Imaging, Blower Doors, and Diagnosing Envelope Leaks

### 013000 — Thermal Imaging, Blower Doors, and Diagnosing Envelope Leaks

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Quantitative example

`ACH50 = CFM50 × 60 / enclosed volume in ft³`. If `CFM50 = 1,200` and the test enclosure is `20,000 ft³`, then `ACH50 = 3.6`. This is the fan-driven 50 Pa reference result, **not** a measured natural air-change rate. Record which spaces were included, test pressure direction, and setup.

##### Diagnostic worksheet

| Observation | Candidate explanation | Discriminating check |
|---|---|---|
| Cold line over studs | Conductive thermal bridging | Compare with known framing layout and fan-off pattern |
| Cold streak enlarges under depressurization | Incoming outdoor air | Controlled pressure comparison and local airflow indicator |
| Cool area below window after rain | Bulk water, air leakage, or conductive edge | Correlate with precipitation and verify moisture independently |
| Reflective surface reads wildly different | Low emissivity / reflected radiation | Compare a suitable high-emissivity reference surface |

**Review boundary:** Follow a recognized blower-door protocol and arrange combustion safety/ventilation evaluation where relevant. No single thermal image diagnoses moisture or certifies performance. Avoid interpreting one ACH50 value as natural ventilation.

### Diagnosing Heat Loss with Utility Data and Weather Normalization

### 014000 — Diagnosing Heat Loss with Utility Data and Weather Normalization

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Model and worked calculation

- Simple billing model: `E_interval ≈ E_base + b × HDD_interval`, where `E_base` must reflect the **interval duration**; for bills of varying length, consider daily base use multiplied by days.
- On a 65°F basis: a day with mean outdoor temperature 55°F contributes `max(0, 65 − 55) = 10 HDD65`.
- Illustrative baseline: 20 therm / 30-day bill, 100 therm observed, 800 HDD65 gives `b = (100 − 20)/800 = 0.10 therm/HDD`. Under 960 HDD and the same 30-day baseline, model prediction is 116 therm. This is an *illustration*, not a home efficiency rating.
- Compare with `E_heat = H × ∫(Tin − Tout)_+ dt / effective equipment efficiency` only when the energy-input/thermal-output conventions are explicit.

##### Audit worksheet

| Recorded field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Meter read dates and whether actual/estimated | Avoid false calendar alignment |
| Fuel and unit conversions | Separate therm, kWh, delivered fuel, useful heat |
| Daily temperature / HDD source | Weather adjustment, uncertainty of station relevance |
| Thermostat, occupancy, hot-water and appliance changes | Confounding loads |
| Heating equipment mode and maintenance | Fuel-to-heat conversion not fixed across conditions |
| Notes on retrofits and dates | Establish pre/post comparison boundary |

**Review boundary:** Energy bills alone do not certify insulation, air leakage, HVAC efficiency, or predicted savings. A fixed 65°F base can be inappropriate for a particular building; weather-normalized regression, balance-point selection, and causal claims need independent review where consequential.

### Planning Envelope Retrofits Without Trapping Moisture

### 012000 — Planning Envelope Retrofits Without Trapping Moisture

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Retrofit design worksheet

1. Draw the existing wall from indoors to outdoors, including known insulation, sheathing, membranes, siding, and cavity or gap.
2. Mark **liquid water**, **air**, **heat**, and **vapor diffusion** paths separately; include window heads/sills and the roof/foundation transitions.
3. Record wetting history, observed moisture, season, rain/wind dependence, and tests not yet performed. Avoid assuming a dry reading on one date proves the assembly stays dry.
4. Draw the proposed assembly and flag any adjacent low-permeance layers. Check winter sheathing temperature and plausible seasonal drying direction for the climate.
5. Record planned ventilation and combustion-safety assessment after air sealing. Include post-retrofit monitoring, not just a one-day acceptance test.

##### Worked contrast

For identical indoor/outdoor temperatures and wall area, adding thermal resistance reduces steady-state conductive heat transfer, approximately `Q = A ΔT / R_total`. This expression says nothing by itself about liquid water entry, airflow-borne vapor, or drying. An assembly can improve its nominal `R_total` while increasing moisture risk. Quantitative moisture modeling, when warranted, requires boundary conditions and material-specific data that are not established by a simple R-value calculation.

**Editorial boundary:** The lesson is conceptual, not a climate-zone assembly prescription. Actual vapor-retarder class, insulation ratios, combustion ventilation, and code requirements need project-specific review. Historic and concealed walls may contain additional hazards requiring qualified assessment.

### Distinguishing Residual Moisture from New Wetting After an Enclosure Retrofit

### 020000 — Distinguishing Residual Moisture from New Wetting After an Enclosure Retrofit

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Conceptual balance.** For a local region, the rate of change of stored moisture is the sum of liquid, vapor and air-transport inputs minus drainage and evaporation outputs. This is an accounting model, not an invitation to assign unmeasured numerical rates.

**Logging exercise.** Create a table with date, preceding 72-hour rain or melt, indoor air temperature, indoor relative humidity, exterior temperature, fixed-point meter readings, material type, instrument setting, photographs, and drainage-system state. Graph each measurement by time rather than comparing isolated values. Include a drying-only period if available. Correlation with rain is evidence, not proof of a specific penetration point.

**Instrument limits.** Dielectric and resistance readings are often relative or material-calibrated; embedded metal and soluble salts can bias them. Infrared cameras cannot see through walls and do not measure water directly. Sampling an opened cavity changes conditions. Verify dry/wet reference spots within the same substrate.

**Sources and review.** DOE Building America Solution Center guidance on foundation water management, basement enclosure assemblies and air-sealing; EPA moisture-control guidance and radon guidance. A qualified enclosure review is required for structural damage, persistent unexplained wetting, or assemblies with concealed hazardous materials. The lecture does not establish a universal drying period, moisture threshold, or retrofit specification.
