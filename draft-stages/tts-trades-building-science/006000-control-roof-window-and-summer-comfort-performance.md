# Control roof, window, and summer comfort performance

## Attics, Roofs, and Insulation Choices

Stand in an unfinished attic on a cold morning. Below you is a ceiling, usually pierced by wires, plumbing, light fixtures, and a hatch. Above you are rafters and roof sheathing. Between the two might be a cold, ventilated space or an insulated room. Before deciding whether the attic needs more insulation, ask a question that sounds elementary but is surprisingly consequential: which surface is supposed to separate conditioned indoor air from outdoors?

If the attic is vented and outside the conditioned enclosure, the intended thermal boundary is usually the ceiling below. If the attic is unvented and brought inside the enclosure, the boundary is at the roof plane. The difference is not a mere preference about where insulation looks tidy. It changes the temperatures of the roof deck, the air and vapor paths, the performance of ducts and equipment, and the details needed to control condensation.

### A fluffy blanket is not a lid

Fibrous insulation slows heat transfer, chiefly by immobilizing air within many small spaces. It does not automatically make an airtight surface. Warm indoor air that passes through a ceiling penetration can move through or around insulation and deliver moisture to a cold roof deck. A thick layer of insulation laid over an unsealed ceiling can therefore produce disappointing energy results while leaving an important moisture route intact.

In a conventional vented attic, the sequence matters. Find and fix active roof leaks and inappropriate exhaust outlets first. Identify the ceiling's air-control layer, then seal its discontinuities with materials suited to the location, particularly around heat-producing fixtures or vents. Only then does adding insulation give the expected benefit. The insulation should meet the ceiling air barrier without voids or compressed sections. A recessed fixture, a knee wall, and the edge over an exterior top plate are all places where that alignment commonly becomes difficult.

Near eaves, depth is often limited. The edge of a ceiling may receive much less insulation than the middle even though it has the same exposure to winter weather. Raised-heel framing creates room for more consistent insulation. With soffit-to-roof ventilation, baffles preserve the intended air passage near the underside of the roof and prevent wind from disturbing loose insulation at the edges. Blocking a soffit vent with insulation is not an improvement in the assembly's heat control.

### What attic ventilation can and cannot do

A vented attic exchanges air with outdoors. This can help manage roof-deck moisture and solar heat under appropriate conditions, but it does not repair a leaky ceiling. If humid indoor air is continuously flowing upward through a large hole, ventilation is being asked to carry away a moisture load that should have been limited at its source. Equally, ventilation is not proof that a roof has a sound rain barrier. Water entering at a flashing joint still needs its leak fixed.

Consider the classic winter ice dam. Snow on a roof can melt where the roof deck is warmed from below. Water runs toward a colder overhang and freezes. Repeated melting can build a ridge that impedes drainage. Exterior weather and roof geometry matter, but attic air leaks and uneven insulation can make the roof surface less uniformly cold. More roof ventilation may help a suitable assembly; reducing heat and air leakage from the home is the central diagnostic step when the roof is being warmed by the house.

A powered attic fan is not an automatic cure. If the attic is insufficiently separated from the home, fan suction may draw conditioned air through the ceiling. It adds a pressure difference while consuming energy. The correct question is whether the fan addresses a measured problem without creating a new airflow route.

### Bringing the attic inside

Now imagine ducts, air handlers, or finished rooms occupying the attic. Insulating at the roof line can put those systems inside the thermal enclosure. That can avoid locating conditioned-air ducts in a very hot or cold attic. However, a roof assembly that is not vented in the conventional manner needs an explicit strategy for roof-deck temperature and moisture. The type and placement of insulation, vapor permeance, exterior roof coverings, interior humidity, and local climate all matter.

Spray foam is one method, not a magic category. Open-cell and closed-cell foams have different vapor properties and thermal behavior. Foam installed over a wet roof deck can obscure the condition underneath. A product that acts as an air barrier in one thickness and installation does not establish the safety of the whole roof assembly. Mixed systems, such as exterior rigid insulation combined with fibrous insulation inside, depend on the proportion of thermal resistance on each side of a potential condensing surface.

The roof geometry may complicate everything. A low-slope roof, dormer, chimney chase, skylight shaft, or cathedral ceiling creates transitions between control layers. If you cannot draw one continuous thermal line and one continuous air line around the conditioned volume, the assembly has unresolved details. That drawing should come before material shopping.

### A diagnostic walk through an attic

An attic survey is better conducted as a search for mechanisms than a tally of insulation thickness. Look for daylight where it should not appear, staining on sheathing, frost patterns, darkened areas by penetrations, displaced insulation, bath exhaust dumped indoors, disconnected ducts, and obstructed ventilation passages. Remember that stains have histories: an old roof leak and current condensation may leave similar marks. Timing, weather, humidity, and measurement help distinguish them.

An unconditioned attic floor can be thermally adequate in the middle while leaking heat at its hatch, wall-to-ceiling junctions, and plumbing chases. A finished attic can appear comfortable while hiding a cold, moisture-sensitive roof deck. In both cases, the right unit of analysis is the whole assembly, including its joints and transitions.

The practical lesson is not that vented attics are always better or that foam is always worse. It is that insulation belongs within a deliberately chosen boundary. Once you know where that boundary is, you can ask the productive questions: is it continuous, does it control both heat and air, and what happens to moisture when the weather changes?

## Window Thermal Performance, Solar Gain, and Summer Comfort

Roof and attic performance is one part of a room’s heat balance; windows create another set of losses and gains.

A west-facing room may be uncomfortable on a sunny summer afternoon even when its windows are closed, the air conditioner is running, and the room's thermometer says the temperature is reasonable. The reason is not necessarily an undersized air conditioner. Sunlight is entering through the glass, warming the floor, furniture, and occupants directly. Meanwhile, the window itself exchanges heat with the outdoors and its inner surface exchanges radiant heat with the room. A window is not just a hole filled with a transparent insulator. It is a thermal and optical component of the building.

### Three mechanisms, three different measurements

Start with conduction and convection, combined in the whole-window rating called U-factor. U-factor describes the rate of heat transfer per unit area and per degree of temperature difference. Lower is better insulation. The rating includes more than the middle of the pane: frames, spacers, edge effects, and standard test conditions matter. Do not confuse the center-of-glass performance of a premium pane with the performance of the assembled window.

Then consider solar heat gain coefficient, often shortened to S H G C. This is a fraction, between zero and one, of incident solar radiation that ends up entering the space as heat, including energy absorbed by the window and subsequently transferred inward. A low solar gain coefficient generally helps suppress summer solar heating. A higher one may be beneficial where winter solar heat is welcomed and usable. The best choice depends on orientation, climate, shading, and the building's heating and cooling priorities. A high solar gain coefficient is not simply a better or worse window.

The third mechanism is air leakage through the operable sash, weatherstripping, and installation details. A low U-factor does not guarantee a tight installation. Moving air carries both sensible heat and moisture, and a small persistent leak can produce a draft and condensation where the local surface is cold. A window can have excellent glass and a poor connection to the wall.

### Work through an example

Suppose a window is twenty square feet and has a U-factor of zero point three in inch-pound building units. With a twenty-degree Fahrenheit indoor-outdoor difference, a simplified steady heat flow is zero point three times twenty times twenty, or one hundred twenty B T U per hour. A similar-sized window with a U-factor of zero point six would double that heat flow under the same conditions. These are comparisons of an idealized rated assembly, not predictions of a household utility bill. Real conditions vary over the day, and the rating is measured at specified conditions.

Now give that window direct sunshine. The incident solar energy per area can be much larger than the conduction term at some hours. If the incoming solar radiation on its plane were two hundred B T U per square foot per hour, and its solar gain coefficient were zero point four, the simplified transmitted heat would be sixteen hundred B T U per hour. Changing the solar gain coefficient to zero point two would approximately halve that example's solar contribution. The values are deliberately illustrative; window angle, season, weather, screen, shading, and actual spectral behavior all change the result. The important comparison is between the temperature-driven term and the sun-driven term. A room can be hot because of sunlight even if the glass insulates well.

### The sun has a direction

A window facing north in the northern hemisphere generally receives less direct summer sun than one facing west, although geography and surroundings matter. East-facing glazing admits morning sun. West-facing glazing is exposed in the late afternoon, when outdoor temperatures and internal stored heat may already be high. A south-facing window can often be shaded by a properly designed horizontal overhang during high-sun seasons while admitting lower-angle winter sun. A west window is harder to protect with a horizontal overhang alone because late-afternoon sun arrives at a low angle. Exterior adjustable shades, trees, or fins can perform differently from interior curtains: stopping radiation before it crosses the glazing is generally more effective at limiting heat deposited indoors.

Low-emissivity coatings help control infrared radiation and, depending on coating formulation and glazing design, can also alter solar transmission. They are not magic films with identical properties. Two windows both described as low-e can have different U-factors, visible transmittance, and solar gain coefficients. Triple glazing may reduce conductive loss yet still admit unwanted sun unless the solar properties are appropriate. The product label is a set of distinct measurements for a reason.

### Why the window feels cold, or hot, even at the same air temperature

A person sitting by a cold pane exchanges radiant heat with it. Air in contact with the pane cools and moves downward. These two effects can make the person uncomfortable without a room thermostat recording a dramatic change. In summer, strong direct sunlight can heat a person's skin and nearby furniture while room air remains comparatively cool. Comfort depends on air temperature, mean radiant temperature, air motion, humidity, clothing, and activity. An air thermostat samples only part of the situation.

Condensation provides another clue. Interior glass can become cold enough for indoor water vapor to condense even when the rest of the room seems dry. Lower U-factors, warmer interior surface temperatures, suitable spacers, interior humidity control, and air sealing can all help, but a persistent wet corner may also indicate a flashing leak or frame failure. Condensation on the interior room-facing surface is not the same as moisture trapped between sealed panes. The latter can indicate insulating-glass seal failure. Moisture on an exterior pane in certain weather may simply indicate a well-insulating pane with a cold outside surface.

### Diagnose before replacing

Consider the west room again. Record when the discomfort happens, whether the sun reaches people or surfaces, whether the air temperature is elevated, and whether a shade changes the experience. Check the window's product label, actual orientation, visible gaps, and condition of the sash and frame. A temporary exterior shade that greatly improves the room may be more diagnostic than an expensive window quote. A room that feels cold every cloudy winter evening points to a different balance of mechanisms.

Window replacements also involve water control, structural support, and the air boundary around the opening. The highest-rated unit can be undermined by poor flashing or installation. Building science requires comparing the entire installed assembly and the room it serves. Separate conduction, solar gain, and air leakage first. Only then decide which lever to change.

## Controlling Summer Overheating with Shading and Thermal Mass

A room can be uncomfortable on a bright afternoon even when its walls are well insulated and the outdoor air is not particularly hot. A west-facing window collects direct sunlight; the sun warms the floor and furniture; the furniture warms the air and the people in the room. Closing the supply register or lowering the thermostat may change the symptom without identifying the heat source. Summer comfort is a problem of **energy entering, energy being stored, and energy leaving**, and the order in which those processes occur matters.

### Follow the incoming heat

Start with two distinct paths through a window. Heat crosses glass and frames whenever their temperatures differ. The whole-window U-factor describes that tendency; lower means less heat flow per unit area and temperature difference. Sunlight is another path. Some is reflected, some absorbed by the glazing, and some transmitted into the room. The solar heat-gain coefficient, or SHGC, describes the fraction of incident solar energy that ultimately adds heat indoors, including absorbed energy that flows inward. A low U-factor does not automatically imply low solar gain. A window may resist winter conduction well and still admit substantial summer sun.

Consider a deliberately simplified example. Two square meters of glazing receive a momentary solar irradiance of six hundred watts per square meter. With an SHGC of one half, the estimated admitted solar heat is six hundred watts at that instant. Three hours of that same exposure would add about one point eight kilowatt-hours. Actual sun angle, clouds, frame shading, curtains, and changing irradiance complicate the estimate; it is not a prediction of a particular room. But it makes the scale clear. A person might be trying to offset hundreds of watts at a single window, not a mysterious failure of the air conditioner.

### Intercept sunlight before it reaches glass

Exterior shading stops or reflects solar radiation before it enters the glazing system. An awning, appropriately placed deciduous tree, exterior blind, or projecting roof can reduce the load without forcing the cooling system to carry it away later. Interior blinds can improve glare and comfort, but radiation that has passed into the assembly or room is harder to reject outdoors. Reflective interiors may send some energy back through the window; they do not behave identically to exterior shade.

Direction and time of day matter. A fixed overhang often works well against high summer sun on a south-facing facade in the northern hemisphere, while low morning or afternoon sun from the east or west can pass beneath it. Those orientations call for different geometry: vertical fins, adjustable shade, vegetation, or solar-control glazing. A shaded south window and an unshaded west window with the same size do not impose the same afternoon cooling load. The right design follows the sun path, not a generic rule about window area.

Shading can have tradeoffs. A very low SHGC may help a cooling-dominated home but remove winter solar gains that were valuable in another climate. An exterior device that blocks summer sun should not trap bulk water at the window flashing or obstruct emergency egress. A tree must be placed with mature size, roots, and storm exposure in mind. These are design questions rather than reasons to ignore the large potential benefit of shade.

### What thermal mass can and cannot do

A heavy masonry floor, thick plaster wall, or substantial concrete surface can absorb heat while its temperature rises. Its heat capacity is energy per degree of temperature change. If an exposed mass absorbs a portion of the midday solar gain, room air may warm more slowly. This is thermal storage, not destruction of energy. Later the stored heat must go somewhere. It can return to the room in the evening, or it can leave through a deliberate path if conditions permit.

This is why a heavy room may feel tolerable at five in the afternoon but stubbornly hot at midnight. The peak was delayed, not necessarily reduced over a whole day. Thermal mass is most useful when paired with a real opportunity to discharge it: cool nights, appropriately controlled ventilation, or active cooling arranged to remove the stored heat. A thick internal wall hidden behind insulation and cabinetry will not exchange heat with the room as readily as an exposed surface. Likewise, a slab warmed continuously for days eventually reaches a new equilibrium; its ability to absorb another day's heat declines.

Insulation and thermal mass also perform different jobs. Insulation slows conduction through an envelope. Mass changes how quickly material temperatures respond. A thick concrete wall without adequate insulation may store heat and also conduct it inward over time. Calling it 'massive' does not establish energy efficiency. To decide whether a retrofit helps, trace both the heat path and the storage path, including surfaces that actually face the occupied room.

### Make a plan for releasing heat

Ventilating a home at night can cool its interior surfaces if outdoor conditions are genuinely favorable. Compare outdoor and indoor temperature and humidity, and consider air quality, security, insects, and the ability to create controlled airflow. Bringing in cool but very humid air may reduce dry-bulb temperature while adding a moisture load the cooling system must later remove. In a hot and humid climate, automatic night ventilation can be a poor bargain. In a dry climate with large day-night temperature swings, it can be powerful.

Air conditioning changes both room heat and moisture. A smaller unit of heat can move through a window in seconds, but moisture absorbed by furnishings and materials may take much longer to leave. Solar shading reduces sensible heat gain directly; it does not substitute for ventilation or dehumidification when those are needed. Ceiling fans can make occupants more comfortable through air movement, but they do not remove heat from an empty room. Fan motors actually add a little heat.

### Diagnose before retrofitting

Take notes over several representative days. Which rooms overheat? At what hour? Is the problem correlated with direct sun or with outdoor temperature after sunset? Do exposed surfaces become warm to the touch? Are window coverings open on the sunny side? Does the room recover after a cool night, or does heat accumulate day after day? Compare shaded and unshaded windows and keep thermostat behavior consistent while observing.

The most economical improvement may be a shade, not a larger compressor. But it may also be attic heat control, duct delivery, leakage, or a poorly controlled cooling system. Each produces a different pattern. By following solar entry, storage, and heat rejection separately, we can choose a repair that addresses the dominant mechanism rather than merely suppressing a hot-room complaint.

## Diagnosing Summer Heat Gain with Solar and Room Temperature Logs

Temperature and solar observations can test whether the proposed comfort explanation fits the room’s actual behavior.

One room becomes unbearably hot at four in the afternoon. Another room, supplied by the same air conditioner, stays comfortable. The thermostat in the hallway declares success. What exactly would convince us that the first room has a solar-gain problem rather than a supply-air problem? We need observations that separate the explanations. A room-temperature log is useful, but a temperature curve by itself does not reveal where the heat came from.

### A room is an energy balance, not just a thermometer

At any moment, energy enters through sunlight, warm surfaces, ventilation and leakage, occupants, lights, and equipment. The cooling system removes some of it. The room and its contents store the difference for a time. That storage matters because rooms do not respond instantaneously. Sun warms a floor, a couch, and the inside of a wall; those objects then warm the surrounding air. By the time the air thermometer reaches its maximum, the strongest solar exposure may already be over.

Sunlight arriving through a window is not the same thing as conduction through its glazing. Conductive heat transfer depends on the temperature difference, area, and whole-window U-factor. Solar gain depends on incident solar power, orientation, shading, and solar heat-gain coefficient. Picture three square meters of sunlit glass receiving six hundred watts per square meter. At an illustrative solar heat-gain coefficient of one half, about nine hundred watts of solar gain enters at that instant. That is a substantial load even if the outside air is cooler than the inside air. But it is a snapshot calculation, not an hourly or seasonal forecast.

A temperature sensor in the room also has its own environment. Place it in direct sun and you may be measuring a sun-warmed sensor body. Place it beside a supply vent and it may report a temperature no occupant experiences. A useful log needs a repeatable location away from radiators, supply jets, windows, appliances, and direct solar exposure. If possible, add a second measurement of relative humidity and record the thermostat setting and operating mode. Consistent sensor placement matters more than collecting dozens of uncalibrated numbers.

### Build a timeline of competing explanations

Suppose the room heats sharply on clear afternoons and remains closer to the rest of the house on overcast days with similar outdoor temperatures. That favors solar gain. If it becomes hot in late afternoon even on cloudy days, attic heat flow or an undersupplied room becomes more plausible. If it warms abruptly whenever the outdoor air handler turns off, controls and delivery may be involved. If it never recovers overnight, investigate stored heat, inadequate night cooling, or insufficient cooling capacity. None of those patterns proves the cause; they guide the next test.

Record time, room temperature, a nearby comparison room, outdoor shade temperature, sun or cloud conditions, window-covering position, and whether cooling is running. A ten- or fifteen-minute interval is often enough to show residential trends without creating a mountain of data. Note door positions and unusual internal loads. A family gathering, active oven, gaming computer, or closed return-air path can contaminate a comparison more than another decimal place of thermometer precision could help.

The comparison-room measurement is especially valuable. If both rooms rise together, a whole-house condition is likely. If only the west room climbs as the sun reaches it, its orientation deserves attention. A useful plot shows both temperatures on the same axes, with the periods of solar exposure and cooling operation marked. Look at the *difference* between the rooms. It can remove some common influence from outdoor weather, although it cannot eliminate all differences in ventilation, thermal mass, and internal use.

### Use controlled changes instead of guessing

Try one reversible change and keep the others as stable as practical. On a sunny day, deploy an exterior shade before the sun strikes the window. On another comparable day, do not. Compare the room-to-room temperature difference, not merely the daily maximum. If weather differs, say so; this is an observation, not a laboratory randomized trial. Interior blinds are useful to test too, but their optical and thermal behavior differs from exterior shade. A window film may have tradeoffs in winter solar gain and glazing compatibility that must be assessed separately.

An inexpensive surface-temperature measurement can be illuminating. If the sunlit floor gets markedly warmer than the room air, it is storing and reradiating heat. Infrared devices report surface radiance and depend on emissivity, reflection, and measurement geometry. A bright reflective surface can trick a thermal imager. Its apparent temperature is not automatically the surface's true temperature. Contact measurements, repeatable geometry, and cautious interpretation reduce that problem.

We can also test the air-distribution hypothesis. Record supply and return temperatures while the equipment runs steadily, register airflow observations, door status, and whether other rooms respond at the same time. A cold supply vent alone does not prove sufficient airflow. Closing registers in other rooms is not a reliable diagnostic shortcut: it changes system pressure and may create new delivery problems. The right comparison is the air temperature and flow delivered under ordinary operation, interpreted in the context of the room's heat load.

### Distinguish temperature from comfort

Two rooms at the same air temperature may not feel the same. A person facing a sun-warmed window or ceiling experiences additional radiant heat. Air movement, humidity, clothing, and activity also affect comfort. A simple room log might miss discomfort caused by a hot surface even when the thermostat reads normally. Ask occupants where they sit, when they feel hot, and whether moving away from the window changes the sensation. Their observations are evidence about operative conditions, not noise to ignore.

At the end of the investigation we should be able to write a concise hypothesis: on clear west-sun afternoons this room diverges from a shaded control room after solar exposure begins; shading reduces that divergence while cooling settings stay the same. Or perhaps: the divergence occurs regardless of clouds and tracks air-handler operation. Only then should we choose between shading, air sealing, attic improvement, duct balancing, or equipment work. We have converted a complaint into a testable physical story.

### Attics, Roofs, and Insulation Choices

**Track:** Building Science. **Background:** Heat, air, rain, and vapor transport. **Purpose:** identify the actual enclosure boundary at the roof and distinguish insulation strategies from ventilation strategies.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Two schematic sections to draw

- **Vented attic:** interior room → sealed ceiling air barrier → attic-floor insulation → ventilated attic → roof sheathing and roofing. Mark soffit baffles and eaves where insulation is thin.
- **Unvented conditioned attic:** interior room and attic → insulated/air-sealed roof plane → roof deck and roofing. Label dew-point and drying analysis as assembly-specific.

##### Diagnostic exercise

Compare two attics with identical nominal attic-floor R-value. In house A, the ceiling is airtight but insulation thins to near zero over the top plates. In house B, insulation is even but a large plumbing chase opens to the attic. Predict different thermal and moisture patterns and what observations would separate them.

**Review boundary:** These are conceptual assemblies, not prescriptive insulation ratios, ventilation dimensions, or approval of a particular roof retrofit. Assess roof condition, combustion vent clearances, indoor air quality and local climate before recommending a detail.

### Window Thermal Performance, Solar Gain, and Summer Comfort

### 010000 — Window Thermal Performance, Solar Gain, and Summer Comfort

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Equations and definitions

- Whole-window conductive/convective heat flow approximation: `Qdot = U × A × ΔT`. Example: `0.30 Btu/(h·ft²·°F) × 20 ft² × 20 °F = 120 Btu/h`. Does not include direct solar gain or window leakage.
- Simplified solar gain: `Qdot_solar ≈ SHGC × incident irradiance × projected glazed area` using consistent units. The sample `0.40 × 200 × 20 = 1600 Btu/h` is an illustrative orientation-specific instant, not a design-day estimate.
- U-factor and SHGC are separate certified ratings; distinguish whole-unit from center-of-glass claims. Air-leakage rating and installation leakage are separate.
- Distinguish indoor surface condensation, interpane fogging, and exterior surface condensation.

##### Diagnostic exercise

Compare two west-window strategies: (A) reduce U-factor substantially without changing SHGC; (B) reduce SHGC or add exterior shading without changing U-factor. For a sunny afternoon with little indoor-outdoor temperature difference, predict the dominant benefit of each. Then reconsider a cloudy cold night.

##### Review and applicability

ASHRAE design methods, NFRC product ratings, climate, orientation, historic-building constraints, egress and safety glazing, and local code affect project decisions. Do not select a window on a single advertised R-value or solar coefficient alone. Technical review remains required for installed design guidance.

### 015000 — Controlling Summer Overheating with Shading and Thermal Mass

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Simplified gain estimate

- Instantaneous solar gain estimate: `Qdot_solar ≈ A_glazing × I_incident × SHGC` with `A` in square meters and `I` in W/m². This neglects angle-dependent optical behavior and changing shade.
- For 2 m², 600 W/m², SHGC 0.5: `Qdot ≈ 600 W`. If held constant for three hours, `Q ≈ 1.8 kWh`.
- Envelope conductive transfer, separately: `Qdot_cond ≈ U × A × ΔT`. `U` and SHGC answer different questions.
- Thermal storage approximation: `Q = m × c × ΔT`; the effective mass requires heat to reach the material in the time window of interest.

##### Diagnostic worksheet

Record room orientation, glazing area, times of direct sunlight, shading position, exposed-mass temperature trend, outdoor dry-bulb and humidity, indoor humidity, air-conditioning runtime, and nighttime recovery. Ask whether the room problem follows sun exposure, outdoor heat, or equipment operation.

**Scope:** Illustrative formulas are not a replacement for climate-specific solar geometry, HVAC sizing, envelope modeling, window manufacturer data, or structural evaluation of shading attachments.

### Diagnosing Summer Heat Gain with Solar and Room Temperature Logs

### 016000 — Diagnosing Summer Heat Gain with Solar and Room Temperature Logs

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Illustrative instantaneous solar gain:** `Qdot_solar ≈ A_glass × irradiance × SHGC`. For 3 m² at 600 W/m² and SHGC 0.5, `Qdot ≈ 900 W`; actual incident angle, glazing optics, shade, and duration vary. **Conductive gain:** `Qdot_cond ≈ U × A × ΔT`, evaluated separately. **Measurement plan:** Log room A, room B, outdoor shaded air temperature, humidity, setpoint/mode, equipment runtime, occupied periods, blinds/shade, door position, and sky condition every 10–15 minutes over several representative days. Plot `T_A − T_B` and annotate sun exposure. Never place an air sensor in direct sun. A before/after shading comparison is suggestive but not controlled for changing weather.

**Interpretation boundaries:** Thermal imaging requires emissivity/reflection awareness; logger accuracy and placement may dominate small differences.
