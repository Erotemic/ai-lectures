# 012000 — Planning Envelope Retrofits Without Trapping Moisture

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Imagine an old house in winter. Heat escapes through its walls, and the owner plans to fill every cavity with insulation. The energy calculation looks favorable: less heat will move through the wall. Yet the first winter after the retrofit, the sheathing becomes wetter. The insulation did what it was asked to do. The retrofit failed because the wall was not only a thermal assembly. It was also a system that admitted, stored, and released water.

The correct design problem is not simply, “How much insulation can we add?” It is, “How will this assembly respond to rain, indoor humidity, air pressure, and changing temperatures after we modify it?” Each of those influences has its own pathway. Rain is bulk liquid water. Air leaks can carry water vapor into a cavity where it may condense. Vapor diffusion moves moisture through materials even when air is stationary, usually at a much lower rate than a significant air leak. Liquid water can also move by capillary action. A retrofit must address the dominant pathway, not just the pathway most familiar to the installer.

## First determine where water comes from

Consider a stained patch below a second-floor window. It might follow wind-driven rain through a failed flashing connection. It might result from indoor humid air leaking toward cold sheathing. It might be a plumbing leak from an adjacent room. A moisture meter reading alone does not identify the source. The location, season, wind, precipitation, temperature, and pressure conditions help distinguish the explanations. If the patch appears only after storms, inspect the exterior water-control path before deciding to install a new vapor barrier.

Existing buildings have a history. Before planning a wall retrofit, inspect visible siding, roof edges, window openings, interior staining, crawlspaces, and attic conditions. Record where water has drained, where it could accumulate, and what materials are already present. Be alert to assemblies that have performed tolerably because they could dry rapidly, not because they never became wet. The existing condition is not proof of good design, but it is evidence about how the assembly behaves.

## Insulation changes temperature and drying

A wall's temperature distribution depends on the relative thermal resistance of its layers. Add insulation to the inside, and the old sheathing may become colder during winter. Colder sheathing can make condensation from interior moisture more likely if humid air reaches it. Add suitable continuous insulation outside, and that same sheathing may become warmer in winter, but the exterior layer's vapor permeability and drainage details affect whether the wall can dry outward. Neither “inside insulation” nor “outside insulation” is categorically safe or unsafe. The correct choice depends on climate, assembly, existing water control, material permeability, and continuity.

Suppose two walls have similar nominal insulation values. One has gaps at the top plates and around outlets, allowing indoor air into cavities; the other has a continuous air-control layer. The first wall may experience moisture concentrations at leakage paths even when an average vapor-diffusion calculation looks acceptable. This is why air sealing and insulation should be considered as coordinated interventions. Sealing an assembly is not the same as putting polyethylene everywhere. Air-control and vapor-control functions are distinct, though a single material may provide both when detailed and installed correctly.

## Drying potential is an engineering resource

A wall has a wetting potential and a drying potential. If occasional wetting is unavoidable, the assembly must have a credible route to dry between events. A highly vapor-resistant layer on both sides can restrict inward and outward drying. That can be particularly consequential if the cladding leaks, construction moisture remains, or an existing wall contains damp material before the retrofit. The remedy is not necessarily to eliminate all vapor retarders: vapor control can prevent wintertime moisture accumulation in a climate where it is needed. The task is to place and select control layers so they work together, following an appropriate assembly design rather than stacking products by marketing claims.

Drainage matters separately. A weather-resistive barrier needs an exit at the bottom and flashing integrated with openings. A back-ventilated cladding cavity can improve drying and help manage wind-driven rain. If a retrofit buries a defective sill flashing beneath new exterior insulation, it may make a leak harder to find without correcting it.

## Account for the whole building

Air sealing may reduce uncontrolled ventilation. The house can then maintain different indoor humidity levels than before, and combustion appliances may respond differently to pressure changes. The effective ventilation system, exhaust fans, and any fuel-burning equipment belong in the plan. In a cold climate, controlling humidity and intentional ventilation can be as important as choosing insulation. In a hot, humid climate, air conditioning and inward vapor drives complicate the picture differently.

The retrofit sequence should be deliberate. First document existing conditions and diagnose bulk-water defects. Decide where the water, air, thermal, and vapor-control layers will lie. Draw a continuous line for each control function through windows, corners, roofs, and foundations. Specify penetrations and transitions, not only the middle of the wall. Select an assembly appropriate to the climate and materials, then decide how performance will be checked: moisture conditions, airtightness, visible drainage details, and follow-up observation over weather cycles.

Here is the important change in perspective: energy savings are one outcome of an envelope retrofit. Keeping structural materials dry is another, and a retrofit that achieves the first by sacrificing the second has not succeeded. Understand the pathways before selecting the products.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Retrofit design worksheet

1. Draw the existing wall from indoors to outdoors, including known insulation, sheathing, membranes, siding, and cavity or gap.
2. Mark **liquid water**, **air**, **heat**, and **vapor diffusion** paths separately; include window heads/sills and the roof/foundation transitions.
3. Record wetting history, observed moisture, season, rain/wind dependence, and tests not yet performed. Avoid assuming a dry reading on one date proves the assembly stays dry.
4. Draw the proposed assembly and flag any adjacent low-permeance layers. Check winter sheathing temperature and plausible seasonal drying direction for the climate.
5. Record planned ventilation and combustion-safety assessment after air sealing. Include post-retrofit monitoring, not just a one-day acceptance test.

### Worked contrast

For identical indoor/outdoor temperatures and wall area, adding thermal resistance reduces steady-state conductive heat transfer, approximately `Q = A ΔT / R_total`. This expression says nothing by itself about liquid water entry, airflow-borne vapor, or drying. An assembly can improve its nominal `R_total` while increasing moisture risk. Quantitative moisture modeling, when warranted, requires boundary conditions and material-specific data that are not established by a simple R-value calculation.

**Editorial boundary:** The lesson is conceptual, not a climate-zone assembly prescription. Actual vapor-retarder class, insulation ratios, combustion ventilation, and code requirements need project-specific review. Historic and concealed walls may contain additional hazards requiring qualified assessment.
