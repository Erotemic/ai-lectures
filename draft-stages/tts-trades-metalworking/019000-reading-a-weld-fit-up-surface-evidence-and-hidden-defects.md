# 019000 — Reading a Weld: Fit-Up, Surface Evidence, and Hidden Defects

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A weld can be smooth, shiny and weak. Another can look rough while remaining adequate for its intended service. Appearance is evidence, but its meaning depends on the joint design, loading, material, process and acceptance criteria. The first skill in weld inspection is to stop asking whether a bead is pretty and start asking whether the required joint was actually made.

Picture a steel gate hinge welded to a square-tube post. The gate carries its own weight, and every opening and closing cycle applies forces through the hinge. A weld might be large enough to look reassuring but attached to a thin wall with poor fit-up. It might have cracks at a sharp transition, lack fusion along part of the edge, or leave an unprotected pocket where water collects. If the joint fails, replacing a bead with a bigger bead may not repair the underlying load path.

## Inspection begins before the arc

Start with the drawing or the intended design. Which members carry the load? Is the joint a butt joint, lap, corner or tee? What weld size, length, location and process are specified? Does the design require continuous welding, intermittent welds or a connection that accommodates movement? If there is no clear design basis for a consequential structural assembly, an inspector cannot invent one by looking at the finished metal.

Next comes fit-up. Gaps, misalignment, dirty surfaces, incorrect bevels and inaccessible root regions affect what the welding process can achieve. Heat changes the shape of metal; tacks and fixtures control some distortion, but can also create new stresses. Inspecting the joint while it is still accessible is often more informative than examining a sealed enclosure afterward.

For a practice assembly, measure the fit before welding and after cooling. Record changes in squareness, flatness and position. The movement itself teaches why weld sequence and fixturing matter. A tiny angular change at one hinge may translate into visible misalignment at the opposite edge of a long gate.

## Surface discontinuities tell different stories

A crack is not simply an unattractive line. It may indicate shrinkage stresses, hydrogen-related cracking, fatigue, or another mechanism. A crack-like indication needs serious interpretation because crack propagation can undermine a joint under repeated loads. Porosity appears as gas-related voids; surface pores may be visible, while internal porosity may not. Undercut is a groove at the weld toe that reduces local section and can concentrate stress. Overlap is deposited metal rolling over a base surface without proper fusion. Excessive convexity can add material without proportionately improving the load path.

Lack of fusion is particularly important because the weld may appear to cover a surface while not bonding soundly to it. Some lack of fusion is visible at an accessible edge; internal lack of fusion requires other examination methods. The backside of a joint may be inaccessible after assembly. That is one reason a photograph of a smooth top bead cannot certify penetration through the joint.

A quick practical comparison: imagine a fillet weld on two plates forming a right angle. The relevant geometry includes leg sizes, effective throat and actual continuity. The face can be wide while the root is not properly joined. The shape also affects where stress concentrates at the toes. Measuring nominal bead width alone does not reveal the whole effective connection.

## Visual inspection is powerful but limited

Visual examination can find surface cracks, excessive undercut, visible porosity, poor profile, missing weld length, misalignment, arc strikes and some accessibility problems. It can also document whether the specified joint was assembled correctly. But it cannot see a hidden root or verify internal soundness. Other nondestructive examination methods—such as liquid penetrant, magnetic particle, ultrasonic or radiographic methods—address different flaw types and materials. Each has applicability limits, access requirements, trained operators and interpretation standards.

A structural job can have an acceptance procedure tied to a welding code, qualified procedure, material and service conditions. A household repair may have no formal code dossier, but that does not make all defects harmless. The more severe the consequences of failure, the stronger the need for an explicit design and appropriate inspection. A trailer hitch, pressure boundary and decorative plant stand are not the same application.

## Corrosion and fatigue finish the story

Return to the gate hinge. The weld may initially carry the load, but the tube's exposed interior can collect water. A bead may create a crevice where paint coverage is poor. Dissimilar hardware may accelerate corrosion in certain environments. Repeated gate motion can impose alternating stress at a weld toe. The repair should therefore address drainage and protection, alignment, member thickness and cyclic loading as well as the visible crack.

Suppose the hinge broke along the base metal next to the bead. That does not necessarily mean the weld was strong enough for service. It may mean the heat-affected zone, nearby section or stress concentration became the weak link. If the fracture lies in the weld, that too does not identify a unique cause without examining the joint and service history. A failed connection is a system failure, not just an appearance score.

## An evidence-based repair sequence

For a noncritical practice joint, establish the intended geometry and acceptance criteria first. Photograph the fit-up, record the material and process, then measure alignment after welding. Inspect both sides where accessible. Compare what you see with the specified criteria and, when relevant, obtain appropriate additional examination. Mark which regions were not inspectable. Record environmental protection and how moisture will drain.

For a failed service joint, preserve evidence before grinding it away. Look for fatigue marks, corrosion, repeated misalignment and hidden load paths. Repairing the immediate fracture without correcting the geometry or service environment invites recurrence. Do not use a successful-looking practice bead as proof that a load-bearing joint is ready for service.

Welding inspection is a lesson in epistemic limits. You can learn a great deal by looking carefully, measuring and following the forces. You also need to know what you cannot see. The confidence in a weld should come from its design, process control, accessible evidence and required examinations, not from how easily its surface catches the light.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Joint worksheet.** Record base material and thickness, joint geometry, fit-up, access to root, weld specification, process/consumable, tack sequence, final dimensions, surface indications, weather exposure and inspection limitations. For critical loads, require design/acceptance criteria before evaluation.

**Discontinuities versus defects.** A discontinuity is an interruption in typical structure; whether it is rejectable depends on type, size, location and the governing criteria. A smooth bead does not prove internal fusion. Note the distinct capabilities of visual, penetrant, magnetic-particle, ultrasonic and radiographic methods.

**Practical exercise.** Compare mock or documented photographs of undercut, visible porosity, overlap and a crack. Describe evidence and uncertainty without assigning pass/fail where no acceptance standard has been specified.

**References.** AWS, *Guide for the Visual Examination of Welds* (B1.11); OSHA, welding and cutting safety references; TWI, distortion and welding discontinuity guidance. Welding codes and qualifications depend on application and adopted requirements.
