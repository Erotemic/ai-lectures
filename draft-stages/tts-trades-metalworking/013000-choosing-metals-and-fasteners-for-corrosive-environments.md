# 013000 — Choosing Metals and Fasteners for Corrosive Environments

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two shiny bolts hold an exterior bracket. Five years later, the bolts still look bright, but the aluminum around one has turned powdery and the bracket is loose. The obvious question is why a corrosion-resistant bolt did not protect the entire assembly. The answer lies in the difference between choosing a metal in isolation and designing an electrochemical system.

Metals do not corrode only because they are weak or because water touches them. Many corrosion processes involve reactions at surfaces coupled by an electrolyte, such as rainwater containing dissolved salts. Some regions behave more anodically and dissolve; other regions support reduction reactions. When dissimilar metals are electrically connected and wetted by a conducting liquid, the coupling may accelerate attack on the more anodic material under those conditions. Galvanic corrosion therefore depends on the metal pair, environment, exposure geometry, and electrical continuity.

## Why the fastener matters to the entire joint

Consider a large stainless-steel plate attached to a small aluminum bracket with a thin wetted joint. The relative exposed areas of cathodic and anodic regions can affect corrosion current density at the vulnerable material. A small anodic area connected to a large cathodic area can be a poor arrangement. Reversing the area ratio may change the severity. But an area rule alone does not predict service life: films, coatings, oxygen, water retention, and alloy composition alter behavior.

Stainless steel gains much of its corrosion resistance from a thin passive surface film. It is not immune to every environment. Chloride-rich water, stagnant crevices, poor drainage, and some temperatures can cause localized pitting or crevice corrosion. A fastener labeled stainless is not automatically suitable for a dock, a coastal railing, or a chemical washdown installation. The alloy grade and the details around the joint matter.

Galvanized steel uses a zinc coating that can provide a barrier and a sacrificial electrochemical function in some conditions. The coating's protection is finite: scratches, cut edges, abrasion, and prolonged wetting can consume it. Hot-dip galvanizing and thin electroplated coatings are not interchangeable just because both products look silvery. Coating thickness, fabrication sequence, and the actual exposure must be part of selection.

## Manage water before choosing stronger metal

A horizontal joint that traps salty water can defeat an otherwise reasonable material choice. Design drainage so water does not sit in blind pockets. Avoid crevices that stay wet through repeated weather cycles. Keep dissimilar surfaces separated where appropriate with compatible insulating washers, sleeves, or coatings, but remember that a conductive path elsewhere may still complete the circuit. An insulating washer that is crushed, worn, or bypassed by bare metal may not provide the intended separation.

Imagine an outdoor steel railing attached to concrete. Water can reach anchor holes, corrosion products can expand, and local cracking of the surrounding material can occur. An anchor has to satisfy both structural loading and corrosion exposure. Selecting a rust-resistant head does not establish the condition of the embedded shank. Repairs should inspect the concealed connection and identify whether water can drain away from the mounting point.

Fastener selection is multidimensional. Tensile and shear strength, temperature, fatigue, preload, galling, expansion, tool access, and replacement requirements all matter. Two fasteners with similar diameter may have different mechanical classes and coating systems. The most corrosion-resistant available alloy may have unsuitable strength, compatibility, or installation behavior for a particular design. A specification should identify the required properties instead of substituting appearance or a broad word like marine grade.

## Coatings create maintenance obligations

Protective coatings can isolate metal from an electrolyte, but they need sound surface preparation, compatible primers, proper film thickness, and coverage at edges and welds. Edges are vulnerable because film build may be lower there. A coating that is breached at one crevice can create a concentrated deterioration site. Overpainting rust without diagnosing wetting, salt contamination, and adhesion problems may hide continued attack.

For a repair, document where corrosion is located, whether it is uniform or localized, and which material is being lost. White powder on aluminum, reddish iron oxides on steel, or a dark stained stainless crevice are observations to investigate rather than complete chemical diagnoses. Measure loss of section where capacity matters, inspect threads and bearing surfaces, and identify interacting materials. Replacing the visible fastener while leaving the supporting member compromised does not restore the assembly.

A good outdoor joint is not one made from the most impressive alloy name. It is a joint whose materials, water paths, load demands, and inspection access were chosen together. The first defense against corrosion is often geometry that keeps water from lingering. The next is an appropriate material system. The last is a maintenance plan that catches deterioration before it becomes structural.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Material-selection record

Specify: load and temperature; principal base metals; proposed fastener alloy/coating and strength class; known chloride or chemical exposure; persistent wetting/crevices; exposed cathodic/anodic area relationship; electrical separation details; coating preparation; inspection access. Do not infer compatibility from color.

### Failure comparison

- **Uniform rusting:** Consider barrier failure, standing water, and coating coverage.
- **Pitting/crevice attack:** Examine local chemistry, stagnant gaps, and alloy passivity limits.
- **Galvanic attack:** Confirm metal contact and conductive wetting; inspect both materials.
- **Galling:** Thread seizure can be an installation failure even without long-term corrosion.

**Editorial boundary:** Specific alloy grades, bolt classes, chemical resistance, and anchor capacity need published material data and project design review. Avoid broad statements such as “stainless never rusts” or “zinc works in every marine environment.”

- `nasa-barrett-fastener` — [Richard T. Barrett, Fastener Design Manual, NASA Reference Publication 1228 (1990; errata issued 2008)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19900009424). Fastener selection, torque, preload, coatings, fatigue and corrosion; publication record and summary.
