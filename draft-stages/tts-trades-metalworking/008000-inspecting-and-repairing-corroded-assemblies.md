# 008000 — Inspecting and Repairing Corroded Assemblies

**Track:** Metalworking and Fabrication. **Background:** metallurgy, welding, fatigue, galvanic corrosion, and coatings. **Purpose:** separate cosmetic corrosion from section loss and hidden joint failure.

# The rust on the surface is not the whole story

A steel bracket outdoors develops orange rust. Another bracket looks smooth but has corrosion concealed beneath its mounting plate. Which one is in worse condition? Surface color alone cannot answer. Corrosion is a chemical and electrochemical process, while the engineering consequence depends on where material has been lost, how much remains, and how forces travel through the assembly.

In a simple corrosion cell, metal oxidation occurs at anodic regions, electrons move through conductive material, and ionic reactions occur through an electrolyte such as water containing dissolved salts. Oxygen availability, surface coatings, wet-dry cycles, and contact with different metals can change the reaction. Rust on steel is a visible family of corrosion products, not a calibrated depth gauge. A thin film can look dramatic while leaving substantial sound metal. Local pitting, especially at a highly stressed edge or fastener hole, can be mechanically serious even if the total mass lost is small.

## Trace the forces before the discoloration

Imagine a steel handrail post bolted to a concrete step. Its base plate is partly hidden, and the bolts enter a damp, salt-exposed area. When someone leans on the rail, the post develops a bending moment. The base and anchors must transmit that moment to the concrete. Material loss near an anchor hole is more consequential than a similar patch of rust at an unstressed decorative surface. Corrosion at a welded root, an undersized section, or a fatigue crack also requires special attention.

Inspection therefore begins by identifying the structural job. What loads does the assembly resist? Where are forces concentrated? Which surfaces collect water, where do joints trap moisture, and which parts cannot be seen without disassembly? Sounding, visual measurement, thickness measurement, and non-destructive inspection can provide evidence when matched to the material and accessibility. A qualified assessment is required when load-bearing capacity is uncertain.

## Crevices and mixed metals

A sealed-looking overlap can be a corrosion trap. Water enters through a narrow seam, remains there, and creates an environment that differs from the open surface. Paint applied only to what is visible may improve appearance while leaving the most aggressive wet region untouched. The repair may need to change geometry or drainage, not just coating chemistry.

Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar electrically connected metals share an electrolyte. Relative electrochemical behavior can increase corrosion of the more anodic member, but the outcome also depends on area ratios, oxygen, electrolyte, and protective films. Saying that 'stainless steel never corrodes' or that one metal combination is always safe oversimplifies the problem. Stainless alloys may be vulnerable to localized attack, notably in certain chloride environments; the actual alloy and exposure matter.

The practical design question is whether a joint can stay dry, drain, be inspected, and avoid problematic material combinations. Isolating washers or barriers may help in some assemblies, but they must themselves withstand load and environment. An insulating layer that creeps under bolt preload can introduce a mechanical failure while solving an electrochemical one.

## Repair changes the structure

A repair may remove corrosion products, restore protective coating, replace a deteriorated component, reinforce a joint, or redesign the water path. Each choice changes the assembly differently. Grinding away pitting removes additional metal and can create a notch; welding introduces heat and can alter material properties and residual stress; a doubler plate can create a new moisture crevice; sealants can trap water if drainage is unresolved. Simply covering corrosion with a paint film may leave active contamination beneath the coating.

Coating performance depends on substrate preparation, environmental conditions, product compatibility, film thickness, and curing. Surface preparation needs to remove unstable corrosion and contamination to the level specified by the coating system. The new coating should protect the entire exposure path, including edges and fasteners where practical. In a load-bearing repair, a coating is not a substitute for verifying the remaining section and connections.

## Look for progression, not a single photograph

Photographs with scale and date, thickness readings at repeatable locations, notes about standing water, and records of past repairs make deterioration measurable. A fast-growing pit, loose fastener, or widening crack deserves different attention from stable superficial oxidation. A recurring rust streak below a concealed joint can be an important pointer to a source that has not been exposed yet.

A useful repair plan ends with evidence of performance: corrected drainage, sound remaining material, verified joint function, appropriate protective system, and an inspection interval matched to exposure. The goal is not to remove every orange mark at all costs. It is to preserve the required load path and service life while controlling the mechanism that caused damage.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Inspection record

Record alloy or best available material identification, coating condition, exposed environment, dissimilar contacts, fastener-hole condition, joint geometry, load role, visible corrosion morphology, and repeatable photo scale. Distinguish **uniform surface loss**, **pitting**, **crevice attack**, **galvanic attack**, and **cracking/fatigue**; different mechanisms require different inspection and repair methods.

### Decision example

A rail base plate with localized section loss adjacent to anchors must be assessed in the connection load path. An intact decorative cover with broad superficial oxidation may be a surface-preparation task. Do not infer acceptable remaining thickness or fastener capacity from the apparent amount of orange coloration.

**Review boundary:** Welding on installed load-bearing assemblies, grinding suspected cracked sections, and altering anchorage require qualified evaluation of structural capacity and work hazards. Follow the relevant coating manufacturer's substrate preparation and environmental limits; check repair compatibility and galvanic exposure.
