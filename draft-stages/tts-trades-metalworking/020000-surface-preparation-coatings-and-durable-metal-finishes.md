# 020000 — Surface Preparation, Coatings, and Durable Metal Finishes

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Two steel brackets are fabricated from the same metal and painted with the same coating. One lasts for years outdoors; the other blisters after its first winter. The difference may be hidden underneath the paint: oil, mill scale, salt, rust, incompatible coatings, sharp edges or moisture left at a joint before application.

A protective coating is a system, not a colored skin. It needs to adhere to an appropriate surface, form a continuous barrier and survive the movement, temperature, abrasion and water exposure expected in service. Some coating systems also provide sacrificial corrosion protection. Good application starts with understanding what the substrate is and what the coating is supposed to do.

## Corrosion needs a path

Rusting of carbon steel is an electrochemical process involving metal oxidation and a compatible environment that usually includes moisture and oxygen. Chlorides can make conditions more aggressive. Painting can slow access of corrosive agents to the surface, but damage at an edge, scratch, bolt hole or crevice may admit water. If that water becomes trapped under a poorly adhered film, corrosion products can lift the film and spread deterioration.

This is why edge design matters. A sharp laser-cut edge may receive a thinner coating than a rounded one, depending on material and application. A welded lap that retains moisture can corrode inside even when the external coating looks sound. A continuously sealed joint may prevent entry, while a deliberately drained detail may tolerate some water that does enter. Choosing which strategy is appropriate is a design decision before painting begins.

## Surface preparation is a sequence

First identify the actual metal. Bare carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminum and hot-dip galvanized steel have different surface chemistries and preparation requirements. Next determine what is present on the surface. Oil and grease interfere with adhesion. Rust and loose scale may prevent a durable bond. Salts can remain even after visible dirt has been removed. Surface profile affects mechanical anchoring of certain coatings.

Cleaning and abrasion are different steps. Abrading an oily surface can smear contamination into scratches rather than remove it. After cleaning, a specified abrasive treatment may establish the profile a coating requires. The surface must then stay clean and meet temperature, humidity and dew-point restrictions at application. A freshly blasted steel surface exposed to damp air can flash-rust before a primer is applied.

Imagine a simple gate repair. Someone wire-brushes a rusty patch, paints directly over it and calls it finished. A second worker cleans contaminants, assesses the remaining metal section, removes unsound material by an appropriate method, prepares the surface to the coating specification, treats edges, verifies the condition and applies a compatible primer and topcoat at the required dry-film thickness. The second process has more variables to control, but those variables directly explain the difference in durability.

## Galvanizing is not paint

Hot-dip galvanizing applies a zinc coating that protects steel partly as a physical barrier and partly through zinc's sacrificial electrochemical behavior. Painting galvanized steel can extend protection, sometimes called a duplex system. But freshly galvanized, partially weathered and fully weathered surfaces differ in their chemistry and preparation needs. Applying a generic solvent and paint without consulting the specific system may produce adhesion problems.

Stainless steel is another distinct case. Its corrosion resistance is associated with a passive surface film, but it is not immune to chloride attack or crevice corrosion. Using stainless fasteners through a coated carbon-steel panel introduces galvanic and crevice questions. Aluminum likewise demands a compatible pretreatment and coating. The label 'rustproof' does not remove the need to consider the operating environment.

## Measuring and verifying a finish

Visual appearance is not enough. An accepted coating specification may control surface cleanliness, profile, primer choice, number of coats, application window, wet-film or dry-film thickness, curing and holiday detection. The coating on a complex bracket may be thinner in deep corners or on edges; thickness needs enough sampling and suitable instruments.

Paint thickness can be measured by different techniques depending on the substrate. An instrument suitable for a nonmagnetic coating on steel may not work the same way on aluminum. Measuring wet-film thickness gives process feedback during application; measuring dry-film thickness helps verify what remains after solvent or water evaporates. Neither tells you alone whether the film adheres well to a contaminated surface.

## Coatings are maintenance decisions

A durable coating strategy anticipates access for inspection, water drainage, repairability and replacement. The most elaborate multilayer system may be unsuitable if its surface cannot be maintained. A simpler finish with better geometry and accessible touch-up points may last longer in the actual use case.

Start with the environment, the metal and the failure mode. Select a coating system rated for those conditions. Prepare the surface it requires and verify the process rather than guessing by color. Corrosion control is an exercise in chemistry, geometry and workmanship at once.

## Non-spoken technical companion

**Workflow.** Identify alloy/coating state -> assess structural loss -> remove oil/salts/loose corrosion with specified process -> prepare surface profile -> document substrate temperature, dew point and environmental conditions -> apply compatible system -> verify cure, dry-film thickness and defects according to coating specification.

**Special cases.** Newly hot-dip galvanized steel needs different cleaning/profiling from fully weathered galvanized steel. Zinc-rich primers and barrier coatings are not interchangeable. Painting does not restore metal lost to corrosion. For existing lead-based coatings, abrasive removal may create a regulated exposure hazard; test and plan accordingly.

**References.** American Galvanizers Association, *Painting Galvanized Steel—Surface Preparation*; ASTM D6386 (source issuer ASTM International, current edition and access to be checked); AMPP/SSPC surface-preparation guidance; relevant coating manufacturer application specification. Technical design and hazardous coating removal require competent review.
