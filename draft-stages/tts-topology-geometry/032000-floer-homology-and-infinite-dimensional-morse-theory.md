# 032000 — Floer Homology: Morse Theory for Periodic Trajectories

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** Hamiltonian dynamics, Morse homology, symplectic geometry and elliptic PDEs. **Central question:** how can one count Hamiltonian periodic orbits by building a chain complex from connecting trajectories?

# A Morse theory with infinitely many coordinates

On a compact smooth manifold, Morse theory studies a real-valued function by identifying its critical points and the flow lines descending between them. Those critical points form generators of a chain complex. The boundary operator counts suitable connecting trajectories, and its homology recovers topological information about the manifold.

Hamiltonian dynamics offers a related but harder question. A Hamiltonian system may have periodic orbits, and we want to prove some must exist. Instead of studying a function on the original manifold, we consider an action functional on a space of loops. A loop is itself an infinite collection of positions, one for each moment around a circle. The space of loops is therefore infinite-dimensional.

Floer homology adapts Morse theory to this setting. Its generators are periodic Hamiltonian trajectories. Its differential counts solutions of a partial differential equation connecting one periodic orbit to another. The resulting homology can force periodic orbits to exist even when direct ODE analysis does not reveal them.

# The action functional and its critical points

Let the phase space be a symplectic manifold. A Hamiltonian function changes with position and possibly time and defines a vector field through the symplectic form. Its trajectories obey Hamilton's equations.

For a contractible loop, imagine filling it by a disk. The symplectic area of that disk, combined with the time integral of the Hamiltonian, defines the Hamiltonian action. The first variation of the action vanishes exactly when the loop follows the Hamiltonian vector field. So periodic orbits are critical points of a function, but that function lives on loop space.

On a general symplectic manifold, the action may depend on which filling disk we choose. The difference between two fillings is a sphere, and its symplectic area may be nonzero. To avoid this difficulty in our first model, work on a symplectically aspherical manifold, where the symplectic area of every sphere vanishes. The ordinary two-torus is a good example.

# Gradient lines become a PDE

A descending trajectory of the action is now a path through loop space. Write the path using two variables: one variable s for progress along the descent, and another variable t around the periodic loop.

Choose a compatible almost complex structure to turn the symplectic form into a metric. The negative-gradient equation becomes a first-order partial differential equation in s and t, called the Floer equation. Its solutions look like cylinders in the phase space. As s approaches negative or positive infinity, a finite-energy cylinder approaches periodic Hamiltonian orbits.

The crucial calculation is an energy identity. The energy of a Floer trajectory equals the drop in the action between its limiting orbits, with the stated sign convention. This both controls which orbit can connect to which and supplies the compactness estimates needed to build the differential.

In finite-dimensional Morse theory, flow lines can break into two flow lines when a sequence degenerates. The same happens here. Additional bubbling of holomorphic spheres can occur on general symplectic manifolds, which is why asphericity or stronger virtual-analytic machinery is necessary. Counting trajectories without accounting for these limits would not give a reliable chain complex.

# Why the boundary operator squares to zero

Choose Hamiltonian data for which the periodic orbits are nondegenerate, meaning the return map has no eigenvalue one on the relevant linearized fixed-point problem. Under suitable transversality assumptions, the moduli spaces of connecting Floer cylinders have the expected dimensions.

We count rigid, zero-dimensional connecting trajectories, with orientations and signs or with coefficients modulo two. The resulting signed counts define a differential on the free module generated by periodic orbits.

Why does applying the differential twice give zero? Look at a one-dimensional moduli space of trajectories. Its compactification has boundary points corresponding to broken cylinders: one trajectory from orbit A to orbit B, followed by one from B to C. The algebraic boundary count of a compact oriented one-manifold vanishes. Summing those broken configurations gives exactly the coefficient of orbit C in the differential squared applied to orbit A.

This is a geometric proof that the differential squares to zero. It is not a formal rule imposed on counts after the fact. Compactification, transversality, orientation and gluing are what make the algebra valid.

# The two-torus is an instructive example

Take a two-dimensional torus with its standard area form. Because its second homotopy group vanishes, there is no sphere-area ambiguity in the action for contractible loops. Choose a sufficiently small, generic time-independent Hamiltonian corresponding to a Morse function with one minimum, two saddles, and one maximum.

For small enough data, the relevant contractible one-periodic orbits correspond to these critical points. The Floer complex recovers ordinary Morse homology, up to the grading convention. On the torus the homology ranks are one in degree zero, two in degree one, and one in degree two. Their total rank is four.

This means that a nondegenerate Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of the torus must have at least four fixed points, counted as the relevant one-periodic orbits. This is a special case of the Arnold fixed-point principle. The theorem is about Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms, not arbitrary area-preserving maps. A rigid irrational translation preserves area but need not have fixed points; it is not Hamiltonian in the relevant sense.

The difference between a symplectic map and a Hamiltonian map is therefore mathematically consequential. The Floer invariant is tied to the Hamiltonian isotopy class and the action functional, not merely to conservation of area.

# Where the hard analysis lives

There is a temptation to think we can define Floer homology by simply listing periodic orbits and counting smooth cylinders. In general this is not sufficient. One needs compactness for moduli spaces, a way to rule out or control bubbling, coherent orientations, gluing theorems, and transversality. On more general symplectic manifolds, Novikov coefficients, monotonicity conditions, or virtual perturbations may be needed.

Even the grading has content. The Conley–Zehnder index of a periodic orbit replaces the Morse index, measuring how the linearized Hamiltonian flow twists relative to a chosen trivialization or capping. It predicts which connecting moduli spaces can be rigid.

Floer homology is useful because a complicated analytic construction produces a comparatively stable algebraic invariant. Changing the Hamiltonian through appropriate continuation data induces maps between complexes, and the homology remains invariant in the relevant theory.

# Topology forces dynamics

The central mechanism parallels Morse theory but with a striking replacement. Critical points become periodic orbits. Finite-dimensional gradient lines become solutions of an elliptic-type PDE on a cylinder. Broken trajectories give the identity that the differential squares to zero. The homology then recovers topological information and forces periodic orbits to exist.

For the torus, the answer is concrete: its four units of total homology rank cannot arise from fewer than four nondegenerate periodic-orbit generators in the Hamiltonian Floer complex. That is a topological obstruction to having too few fixed points, proved by studying an infinite-dimensional action functional.

## Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let `(M,omega)` be closed and symplectically aspherical, `omega|_{pi2(M)}=0` and `c1(TM)|_{pi2(M)}=0` (the latter gives a grading without capping ambiguity). With a time-periodic Hamiltonian H and a compatible J, one common action convention is
\[
\mathcal A_H([x,u])=\int_{D^2}u^*\omega-\int_0^1 H_t(x(t))dt.
\]
Under the corresponding sign choice for `X_H`, the negative-gradient Floer equation is
\[
\partial_s v+J_t(v)(\partial_t v-X_{H_t}(v))=0.
\]
For a finite-energy trajectory from `x_-` to `x_+`, `E(v)=A_H(x_-)-A_H(x_+)` with compatible conventions. Moduli spaces of index difference one define the differential; compactified one-dimensional moduli spaces have broken-orbit boundary strata yielding `d^2=0`. On general M, bubbling and capping ambiguities require further hypotheses or virtual methods.

For a sufficiently small generic Hamiltonian on `T^2`, Hamiltonian Floer homology agrees (up to grading shift) with `H_*(T^2)`, whose ranks over a field are 1,2,1. A nondegenerate Hamiltonian diffeomorphism therefore has at least four contractible one-periodic orbits. An arbitrary symplectic, non-Hamiltonian torus translation is not covered by this conclusion.

### Exercises with answers

1. What are the generators of the Hamiltonian Floer complex? **Answer:** nondegenerate one-periodic Hamiltonian orbits in the chosen free homotopy/capping class.
2. What geometric configurations give terms in `d^2`? **Answer:** broken pairs of rigid Floer cylinders forming boundary strata of one-dimensional compactified moduli spaces.
3. What is the total Betti number of the two-torus? **Answer:** four.
4. Why is a non-Hamiltonian area-preserving translation not a counterexample? **Answer:** the fixed-point bound applies to Hamiltonian maps, not all symplectic maps.
