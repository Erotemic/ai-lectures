# Floer overview, action completion, invariance, and a complete model

## Floer Homology: Morse Theory for Periodic Trajectories

On a compact smooth manifold, Morse theory studies a real-valued function by identifying its critical points and the flow lines descending between them. Those critical points form generators of a chain complex. The boundary operator counts suitable connecting trajectories, and its homology recovers topological information about the manifold.

Hamiltonian dynamics offers a related but harder question. A Hamiltonian system may have periodic orbits, and we want to prove some must exist. Instead of studying a function on the original manifold, we consider an action functional on a space of loops. A loop is itself an infinite collection of positions, one for each moment around a circle. The space of loops is therefore infinite-dimensional.

Floer homology adapts Morse theory to this setting. Its generators are periodic Hamiltonian trajectories. Its differential counts solutions of a partial differential equation connecting one periodic orbit to another. The resulting homology can force periodic orbits to exist even when direct ODE analysis does not reveal them.

## The action functional and its critical points

Let the phase space be a symplectic manifold. A Hamiltonian function changes with position and possibly time and defines a vector field through the symplectic form. Its trajectories obey Hamilton's equations.

For a contractible loop, imagine filling it by a disk. The symplectic area of that disk, combined with the time integral of the Hamiltonian, defines the Hamiltonian action. The first variation of the action vanishes exactly when the loop follows the Hamiltonian vector field. So periodic orbits are critical points of a function, but that function lives on loop space.

On a general symplectic manifold, the action may depend on which filling disk we choose. The difference between two fillings is a sphere, and its symplectic area may be nonzero. To avoid this difficulty in our first model, work on a symplectically aspherical manifold, where the symplectic area of every sphere vanishes. The ordinary two-torus is a good example.

## Gradient lines become a PDE

A descending trajectory of the action is now a path through loop space. Write the path using two variables: one variable s for progress along the descent, and another variable t around the periodic loop.

Choose a compatible almost complex structure to turn the symplectic form into a metric. The negative-gradient equation becomes a first-order partial differential equation in s and t, called the Floer equation. Its solutions look like cylinders in the phase space. As s approaches negative or positive infinity, a finite-energy cylinder approaches periodic Hamiltonian orbits.

The crucial calculation is an energy identity. The energy of a Floer trajectory equals the drop in the action between its limiting orbits, with the stated sign convention. This both controls which orbit can connect to which and supplies the compactness estimates needed to build the differential.

In finite-dimensional Morse theory, flow lines can break into two flow lines when a sequence degenerates. The same happens here. Additional bubbling of holomorphic spheres can occur on general symplectic manifolds, which is why asphericity or stronger virtual-analytic machinery is necessary. Counting trajectories without accounting for these limits would not give a reliable chain complex.

## Why the boundary operator squares to zero

Choose Hamiltonian data for which the periodic orbits are nondegenerate, meaning the return map has no eigenvalue one on the relevant linearized fixed-point problem. Under suitable transversality assumptions, the moduli spaces of connecting Floer cylinders have the expected dimensions.

We count rigid, zero-dimensional connecting trajectories, with orientations and signs or with coefficients modulo two. The resulting signed counts define a differential on the free module generated by periodic orbits.

Why does applying the differential twice give zero? Look at a one-dimensional moduli space of trajectories. Its compactification has boundary points corresponding to broken cylinders: one trajectory from orbit A to orbit B, followed by one from B to C. The algebraic boundary count of a compact oriented one-manifold vanishes. Summing those broken configurations gives exactly the coefficient of orbit C in the differential squared applied to orbit A.

This is a geometric proof that the differential squares to zero. It is not a formal rule imposed on counts after the fact. Compactification, transversality, orientation and gluing are what make the algebra valid.

## The two-torus is an instructive example

Take a two-dimensional torus with its standard area form. Because its second homotopy group vanishes, there is no sphere-area ambiguity in the action for contractible loops. Choose a sufficiently small, generic time-independent Hamiltonian corresponding to a Morse function with one minimum, two saddles, and one maximum.

For small enough data, the relevant contractible one-periodic orbits correspond to these critical points. The Floer complex recovers ordinary Morse homology, up to the grading convention. On the torus the homology ranks are one in degree zero, two in degree one, and one in degree two. Their total rank is four.

This means that a nondegenerate Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of the torus must have at least four fixed points, counted as the relevant one-periodic orbits. This is a special case of the Arnold fixed-point principle. The theorem is about Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms, not arbitrary area-preserving maps. A rigid irrational translation preserves area but need not have fixed points; it is not Hamiltonian in the relevant sense.

The difference between a symplectic map and a Hamiltonian map is therefore mathematically consequential. The Floer invariant is tied to the Hamiltonian isotopy class and the action functional, not merely to conservation of area.

## Where the hard analysis lives

There is a temptation to think we can define Floer homology by simply listing periodic orbits and counting smooth cylinders. In general this is not sufficient. One needs compactness for moduli spaces, a way to rule out or control bubbling, coherent orientations, gluing theorems, and transversality. On more general symplectic manifolds, Novikov coefficients, monotonicity conditions, or virtual perturbations may be needed.

Even the grading has content. The Conley–Zehnder index of a periodic orbit replaces the Morse index, measuring how the linearized Hamiltonian flow twists relative to a chosen trivialization or capping. It predicts which connecting moduli spaces can be rigid.

Floer homology is useful because a complicated analytic construction produces a comparatively stable algebraic invariant. Changing the Hamiltonian through appropriate continuation data induces maps between complexes, and the homology remains invariant in the relevant theory.

## Topology forces dynamics

The central mechanism parallels Morse theory but with a striking replacement. Critical points become periodic orbits. Finite-dimensional gradient lines become solutions of an elliptic-type PDE on a cylinder. Broken trajectories give the identity that the differential squares to zero. The homology then recovers topological information and forces periodic orbits to exist.

For the torus, the answer is concrete: its four units of total homology rank cannot arise from fewer than four nondegenerate periodic-orbit generators in the Hamiltonian Floer complex. That is a topological obstruction to having too few fixed points, proved by studying an infinite-dimensional action functional.

## Action Filtrations and Novikov Series: Keeping Infinite Floer Counts Finite

A finite-dimensional Morse complex is usually easy to write down as a vector space: one generator for each critical point. On a compact manifold, a generic Morse function has finitely many critical points. The boundary of one generator is a finite sum of other generators.

Hamiltonian Floer theory can look similar when the symplectic manifold is aspherical. But on a general symplectic manifold, a periodic orbit may be equipped with many different capping disks. The same geometric loop can then represent infinitely many generators carrying different action values and gradings.

To keep the differential meaningful, we must control infinite sums. The relevant algebra is a Novikov completion, organized by symplectic area.

### One loop, several cappings

Take a contractible loop in the symplectic sphere. Fill it by one disk. Then change that filling by attaching a sphere once, twice, or many times. These fillings have different total symplectic areas even though their boundary loop is unchanged.

Under our action convention, attaching a sphere with positive area decreases the action by that area. If the sphere has normalized area one, repeating the attachment creates a sequence of actions differing by integers.

The corresponding capped orbits must be distinguished if we want real-valued action and an unambiguous integer grading. The Floer differential can connect different cappings when a cylinder carries a nontrivial relative homotopy class. Consequently, even a finite set of geometric orbits can support an infinite formal chain module.

The right question is not whether every possible coefficient is eventually zero. It is whether the series behaves well with respect to energy and action, so that any calculation at a fixed energy cutoff contains only finitely many relevant terms.

### Action decreases along Floer trajectories

Recall the energy identity. For an s-independent Hamiltonian, the energy of a Floer trajectory equals the action at its incoming capped orbit minus the action at its outgoing capped orbit.

A nonconstant trajectory consumes positive energy. Thus the differential is filtered by action: terms in the boundary move toward lower action. In a chain complex with infinitely many possible cappings, we may organize generators by their action values and complete the chain group in the direction allowed by this decrease.

Think of the chain as an infinite formal sum where there are only finitely many terms above any specified action threshold, subject to the precise Novikov finiteness rule. Then extracting all terms with action above that threshold gives a finite problem.

This is the basic reason action filtrations are useful. They remember more than the eventual homology group. They track the amount of action at which algebraic information appears or disappears.

### The simplest Novikov series

Suppose the possible sphere areas lie in the integer multiples of a positive number alpha. Introduce a formal symbol T, with exponent recording area.

A simple Novikov series can look like a zero-degree term, a term with one power of T, another with two powers, and so on without end. We allow infinitely many terms with increasing exponents, but we require only finitely many terms whose exponents lie below any fixed upper bound.

This is analogous to ordinary formal power series, except that the exponents need not be integers in more general examples, and their ordering comes from an area homomorphism. Multiplication adds the exponents. That mirrors the geometric fact that attaching two sphere classes adds their symplectic areas.

Different authors reverse the sign of the action or the Novikov exponent. We must specify which direction the completion uses instead of relying on the letter T alone. The principle is stable: formal sums are restricted so that bounded-energy portions of a count are finite.

### Recapping also changes the grading

The action ambiguity and the grading ambiguity arise from two different characteristic evaluations. Symplectic area changes the action. The first Chern class evaluated on the attached sphere changes the relevant Conley–Zehnder grading, by twice the Chern number up to the sign convention.

If symplectic area vanishes on spheres, action does not depend on the capping. If the first Chern class also vanishes on spheres, an absolute integer grading can be defined more simply. These conditions hold on a torus and allow the clean first model of Floer homology.

On a sphere, neither condition generally holds. This does not mean Floer homology ceases to exist. It means its algebra needs to remember the missing topological information.

### Why filtering does not solve analysis

A Novikov ring does not magically produce compactness of pseudoholomorphic curves. It organizes the contributions after analytic control has established what solutions exist and how their energies behave.

If sphere bubbling contributes to a boundary stratum of the moduli space, one still needs a theory that accounts for the bubbles. If transversality fails for a multiple cover, a formal variable T does not repair it. If a cylinder can escape to infinity in a noncompact target, finiteness of formal coefficients alone does not bring it back.

This separation is fundamental. The Novikov condition is an algebraic completion. Gromov compactness and virtual perturbation methods are analytic-geometric inputs. They cooperate, but neither replaces the other.

### From filtrations to spectral invariants

Once the complex is equipped with an action filtration, a homology class may be representable by cycles whose action lies below a particular level. One can ask for the smallest level, in a suitable sense, at which that class appears. Such questions lead to spectral invariants.

Those invariants can extract quantitative information about Hamiltonian dynamics, not just qualitative information about the existence of periodic orbits. Under suitable hypotheses they relate to displacement energy, Hofer geometry, and symplectic capacities.

For now we do not need the full construction. We need the reason it can work: an action-filtered chain complex retains a numerical measure of the geometric cost of the trajectories being counted.

### The conceptual boundary

Finite-dimensional Morse theory begins with a function on a finite-dimensional space. Hamiltonian Floer theory begins with a potentially multivalued action on loop space. Cappings resolve the ambiguity by enlarging the set of generators. Novikov completions then control the resulting infinite sums.

Whenever an advanced lecture speaks of a completed Floer complex, a Novikov coefficient ring, or action-filtered homology, remember those three separate operations: label the topology, calculate the area shift, and impose a finiteness condition compatible with energy. The formal algebra is answering a concrete geometric problem.

## Continuation Maps: Why Floer Homology Survives Changing the Hamiltonian

If we build Floer homology by counting periodic orbits and cylinders for a particular Hamiltonian, the answer might seem tied to that choice. Change the Hamiltonian and the periodic trajectories change. Some are born, some disappear, and their actions shift.

Yet under suitable hypotheses, the resulting Floer homology is invariant. This is what allows a calculation with a very small, almost trivial Hamiltonian to constrain a complicated Hamiltonian system.

The mathematical device is a continuation map. Instead of studying a single fixed Hamiltonian along an entire cylinder, we let the Hamiltonian gradually change as we move down the cylinder.

### Interpolate between two Hamiltonians

Choose an incoming Hamiltonian H minus and an outgoing Hamiltonian H plus. Along the s direction of a cylinder, use a smooth family that equals the incoming Hamiltonian far to the left and the outgoing one far to the right.

At each location along the cylinder, impose the Floer equation using that local Hamiltonian and a compatible almost-complex structure that may vary as well. Solutions should approach periodic orbits of the incoming system on one end and orbits of the outgoing system on the other.

Such solutions are called continuation trajectories. Counting the appropriate rigid solutions defines a linear map from the chain complex for the first Hamiltonian to the chain complex for the second.

Why should this map respect the differentials? Consider one-dimensional families of continuation trajectories. Their compactified boundaries include an ordinary Floer cylinder attached before the continuation region, or an ordinary Floer cylinder attached after it. Counting those two kinds of ends gives exactly the equation saying that continuation is a chain map.

### What happens to the energy identity?

The Hamiltonian now depends on s, so the action function itself changes as we move along the cylinder. The derivative of action has two pieces: the negative squared norm of the Floer gradient, and an additional term coming from the explicit s derivative of the Hamiltonian.

After integration, the energy is the incoming action minus the outgoing action, minus the integral of the s derivative of the interpolating Hamiltonian evaluated along the trajectory, under our sign convention.

This correction is important. We cannot blindly reuse the unchanging-Hamiltonian energy identity. But if the interpolation is controlled and the target is compact, the correction can be bounded. That restores the energy estimates needed for compactness of the continuation moduli spaces.

### Chain maps versus isomorphisms

Construct a continuation map from H minus to H plus, then another map going back. Are the composites equal to the identity on the nose? Usually not at chain level. Instead, under suitable continuation and compactness arguments, they are chain homotopic to the identity.

A chain homotopy is an explicit algebraic witness that two chain maps induce the same map on homology. A two-parameter family of continuation data provides geometric moduli spaces whose boundaries encode such a homotopy.

Consequently, the two continuation maps induce inverse isomorphisms on Floer homology. The chain generators can change substantially, but the homology remains the same.

This is the same logical pattern as in ordinary Morse theory. Different Morse functions and metrics produce different chain complexes, but continuation maps show that the homology does not depend on those auxiliary choices.

### Calculate a small example on the torus

The two-torus has one unit of ordinary homology in degree zero, two in degree one, and one in degree two, over a field. Its total Betti number is four.

Choose a very small generic autonomous Hamiltonian related to a Morse function with exactly four critical points: a minimum, two saddles, and a maximum. With the right smallness and nondegeneracy conditions, the contractible one-periodic Hamiltonian orbits correspond to those critical points, and its Floer complex identifies with a Morse-type complex up to grading convention.

The resulting Floer homology has total rank four. Now replace that small Hamiltonian by an arbitrary generic time-dependent Hamiltonian on the torus, still considering its contractible one-periodic orbits. Continuation invariance says the homology rank is still four.

The chain complex of the new Hamiltonian needs at least four generators to have homology of total rank four. Therefore the new Hamiltonian has at least four contractible one-periodic trajectories if they are nondegenerate.

These trajectories correspond to fixed points of its time-one Hamiltonian diffeomorphism. We have obtained a concrete instance of the Arnold fixed-point bound without solving the new Hamiltonian equations explicitly.

### Hamiltonian versus merely symplectic

There is a key restriction. The conclusion applies to Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms, not all symplectic diffeomorphisms. A translation of the torus by an irrational displacement preserves the area form and may have no fixed points.

But such a translation is not the time-one map of a globally defined periodic Hamiltonian isotopy in the required sense. It is not a counterexample to the bound. The distinction between preserving a symplectic form and being generated by a Hamiltonian is built into the action functional and continuation theory.

This example is a warning against overstating the theorem. The type of symmetry matters, not merely the fact that area is preserved.

### Why continuation is not purely formal

If moduli spaces were not compact or if bubbling created unaccounted boundary components, the chain-map and chain-homotopy proofs could fail. If transversality were not achieved, the relevant spaces might not be smooth of the predicted dimensions. If the Hamiltonian grew too rapidly at infinity, a continuation trajectory might escape the region where the estimate applies.

Thus continuation invariance is a theorem supported by the same analytic foundation as the differential squared zero. It is not a consequence of writing down the word 'homotopy' next to two Hamiltonians.

### The payoff for advanced lectures

When an advanced symplectic topology lecture uses Floer homology to prove that a periodic orbit exists, the hidden engine has three stages. First calculate Floer homology where the complex resembles ordinary Morse theory. Second show that changing the Hamiltonian induces an isomorphism. Third infer that the new Hamiltonian must possess enough orbit generators to support that invariant.

The torus bound makes those steps explicit. It is a model for a much broader family of existence and rigidity arguments, including ones that need Novikov coefficients, action filtrations, and more sophisticated compactness machinery.

## A Complete Lagrangian Floer Model: Two Graphs over a Circle

We have assembled many pieces of symplectic geometry: Lagrangian submanifolds, elliptic equations on strips, Maslov indices, area identities, compactness, and chain complexes. It is time to put them together in a single calculation.

Our position space will be a circle. The phase space will be its cotangent bundle, a cylinder carrying position around the circle and momentum along the cylinder's height. We will choose two Lagrangian circles and compute their Floer homology in a small-graph model.

### Choose two Lagrangian circles

The first Lagrangian is the zero section: all positions, with momentum equal to zero. The second is the graph of the derivative of a function. Choose the function cosine of two pi times position, where position is measured modulo one full turn.

The derivative vanishes at exactly two places: one maximum and one minimum. Therefore the second graph intersects the zero section at exactly two points. Both intersections are transverse because the function has a nonzero second derivative there.

If we thought only in terms of intersection counts, we might conclude that there are two special points and stop. Floer theory asks something deeper. It treats the intersections as generators of a chain complex and counts connecting holomorphic strips to define a boundary operator.

### Why these Lagrangians are exact

The cotangent bundle has a natural symplectic form, and the zero section is exact. The derivative graph is also exact. In our sign convention, the natural primitive of the symplectic form restricts to minus the derivative of the function along the second graph.

Exactness makes two later steps much more manageable. First, the action of a path between the two Lagrangians can be defined using the endpoint primitives, without ambiguity from choosing a spanning surface. Second, there can be no nonconstant compatible holomorphic disk bubble whose whole boundary lies on either exact Lagrangian, because its area would be zero.

But the ambient cotangent bundle is noncompact. We still have to choose tame almost-complex data and appropriate small-graph or confinement conditions to prevent strips from escaping to infinite momentum. We will work in that controlled standard setting.

### The action of a constant chord

Think of a path that starts on the zero section and ends on the derivative graph. When the Hamiltonian is zero, the stationary paths are constant paths at intersection points.

For such a constant path, the path integral of the ambient primitive vanishes. The endpoint correction uses the primitive on the second Lagrangian, which is minus the original function f. Thus the action at an intersection is minus the value of f at that critical point, with our conventions.

The two intersections therefore have opposite action values. This is analogous to the two critical points of the Morse function, with the direction reversed by the sign of the action.

A Floer strip connecting them follows a negative gradient trajectory of the action. In the small-graph limit, solutions of the strip equation correspond to gradient trajectories of the original function, with the expected sign and grading adjustments.

### Two routes between the critical points

Draw the circle and mark the maximum at one position and the minimum opposite it. There are exactly two arcs connecting them. For the round metric, these arcs are the two ordinary Morse gradient trajectories from maximum to minimum.

In the small-graph Floer model, the corresponding holomorphic strips give two rigid connecting trajectories between the two intersection generators, after translation is removed. Working with coefficients modulo two, two contributions add to zero.

The chain complex therefore has two generators and a zero differential. Its homology has one generator in each of two consecutive degrees, just as the ordinary homology of the circle does: one for its connected component and one for its fundamental loop.

This is the first complete example where the conclusion of Lagrangian Floer theory can be checked against a familiar topological answer.

### What happens when the graph changes?

Suppose we replace cosine by a more complicated smooth function with several maxima and minima. Its derivative graph may intersect the zero section at many points. The chain complex becomes larger. But the extra generators can be paired by nonzero differential terms, and the homology remains that of the circle under the relevant Hamiltonian isotopy and continuation results.

The graph of a derivative is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section. In our canonical coordinates, a Hamiltonian depending only on position can shift momentum vertically by the derivative of a function. That is why changing the exact graph belongs to the kind of deformation Floer theory is designed to handle.

Now compare a circle at constant nonzero momentum. It is Lagrangian and symplectically translates the zero section, but its defining closed one-form has nonzero period around the circle. It is not an exact derivative graph. It need not intersect the zero section at all. There is no contradiction: we have left the Hamiltonian-isotopic exact-graph situation.

### Why the small-graph correspondence matters

The argument that Floer strips correspond to gradient trajectories is a genuine analytic theorem in the appropriate regime, not a definition of a strip. One chooses the geometry and graph sufficiently close to the zero section, analyzes the strip equation, and controls the solutions using elliptic estimates and energy bounds.

Then a Floer strip can be viewed as a slightly thickened version of a gradient line. When the graph becomes very small, the strip's leading behavior is governed by the Morse gradient equation. This comparison gives a bridge between classical topology and Floer homology.

We have now seen what each ingredient contributes. Exactness removes disk bubbles. The small-graph setup allows analytic control. Transverse intersections give generators. The Maslov and Morse indices determine the relevant degrees. The two rigid strips cancel in the differential. And continuation explains stability under allowed deformations.

### Beyond the simple model

More complicated Lagrangian Floer theories study submanifolds that are not exact, targets with holomorphic disk bubbles, and noncompact Lagrangians extending to infinity. Wrapped Floer homology, for example, uses Hamiltonians that grow at infinity and records additional intersection or chord information. Its construction needs careful asymptotic and compactness control that our little cylinder model does not supply.

But the architecture is already visible. Floer homology is not a mysterious replacement for counting intersections. It is a way of correcting a naive intersection count with the geometry of trajectories between intersections, so that the resulting algebra remembers topology under appropriate deformations.

The circle model is a good reference point whenever the advanced theory becomes difficult. Ask what corresponds to the two intersections, what corresponds to the two connecting arcs, which bubble types have been excluded, and what theorem keeps the answer invariant.

### Floer Homology: Morse Theory for Periodic Trajectories

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** Hamiltonian dynamics, Morse homology, symplectic geometry and elliptic PDEs. **Central question:** how can one count Hamiltonian periodic orbits by building a chain complex from connecting trajectories?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let `(M,omega)` be closed and symplectically aspherical, `omega|_{pi2(M)}=0` and `c1(TM)|_{pi2(M)}=0` (the latter gives a grading without capping ambiguity). With a time-periodic Hamiltonian H and a compatible J, one common action convention is
\[
\mathcal A_H([x,u])=\int_{D^2}u^*\omega-\int_0^1 H_t(x(t))dt.
\]
Under the corresponding sign choice for `X_H`, the negative-gradient Floer equation is
\[
\partial_s v+J_t(v)(\partial_t v-X_{H_t}(v))=0.
\]
For a finite-energy trajectory from `x_-` to `x_+`, `E(v)=A_H(x_-)-A_H(x_+)` with compatible conventions. Moduli spaces of index difference one define the differential; compactified one-dimensional moduli spaces have broken-orbit boundary strata yielding `d^2=0`. On general M, bubbling and capping ambiguities require further hypotheses or virtual methods.

For a sufficiently small generic Hamiltonian on `T^2`, Hamiltonian Floer homology agrees (up to grading shift) with `H_*(T^2)`, whose ranks over a field are 1,2,1. A nondegenerate Hamiltonian diffeomorphism therefore has at least four contractible one-periodic orbits. An arbitrary symplectic, non-Hamiltonian torus translation is not covered by this conclusion.

##### Exercises with answers

1. What are the generators of the Hamiltonian Floer complex? **Answer:** nondegenerate one-periodic Hamiltonian orbits in the chosen free homotopy/capping class.
2. What geometric configurations give terms in `d^2`? **Answer:** broken pairs of rigid Floer cylinders forming boundary strata of one-dimensional compactified moduli spaces.
3. What is the total Betti number of the two-torus? **Answer:** four.
4. Why is a non-Hamiltonian area-preserving translation not a counterexample? **Answer:** the fixed-point bound applies to Hamiltonian maps, not all symplectic maps.

### Action Filtrations and Novikov Series: Keeping Infinite Floer Counts Finite

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** capped Hamiltonian action, sphere classes, chain complexes, formal power series. **Central question:** what algebra is needed when one periodic orbit admits infinitely many topologically different cappings?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

For \(A\in\pi_2(M)\), recapping \((x,u)\) by \(A\) gives
\[
\mathcal A_H([x,u\#A])=\mathcal A_H([x,u])-\omega(A).
\]
The Conley–Zehnder index changes by \(\pm2\langle c_1(TM),A\rangle\) depending on the chosen capping and index conventions. The action completion is generally taken so that for every real cutoff \(C\), only finitely many generators in a formal chain have action \(>C\) (for a bounded set of underlying periodic orbits and the usual downward action filtration). The equivalent Novikov coefficient convention can be written
\[
\Lambda_{\Gamma}=\Big\{\sum_{A\in\Gamma} a_A T^{\omega(A)}:\
\#\{A:a_A\ne0,\ \omega(A)\le C\}<\infty\text{ for every }C\in\mathbb R\Big\},
\]
for an appropriate quotient \(\Gamma\) of \(\pi_2(M)\) and coefficient field; the precise quotient and completion depend on the theory. In the one-generator area group \(\omega(\Gamma)=\alpha\mathbb Z\), \(\alpha>0\), this includes formal Laurent series \(\sum_{k\ge k_0}a_k T^{k\alpha}\). A real action filtration and a grading may require distinct group data.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Action shift.** A capped orbit has action five. After adding a sphere of area two, what is its action? **Answer:** three under the stated convention.
2. **Series.** Does \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty T^{k\alpha}\) satisfy the one-generator Novikov finiteness rule? **Answer:** yes, if \(\alpha>0\).
3. **Bad direction.** Does \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty T^{-k\alpha}\) satisfy that rule? **Answer:** no; infinitely many nonzero terms have exponent at most zero.
4. **Analysis.** Does Novikov completion alone exclude holomorphic sphere bubbling? **Answer:** no; separate geometric and analytic hypotheses are needed.

### Continuation Maps: Why Floer Homology Survives Changing the Hamiltonian

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** Floer equation, energy identity, chain homotopy, compactness, Morse homology. **Central question:** why does a chain complex built from one Hamiltonian still give information about a different Hamiltonian?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Let \(H_s\) equal \(H_-\) for \(s\ll0\) and \(H_+\) for \(s\gg0\), with compatible \(J_{s,t}\). A continuation solution satisfies \(\partial_su+J_{s,t}(u)(\partial_tu-X_{H_s,t}(u))=0\) and tends to capped periodic orbits \(x_-\), \(x_+\). With \(\mathcal A_H=-\int u^*\omega-\int Hdt\),
\[
E(u)=\mathcal A_{H_-}(x_-)-\mathcal A_{H_+}(x_+)
-\int_{\mathbb R\times S^1}\partial_sH_s(t,u(s,t))\,ds\,dt.
\]
This follows by differentiating the time-dependent action along \(s\). On a closed target the correction is bounded using \(\int\max_M|\partial_sH_s|\). Under standard regularity, compactness, and gluing hypotheses, counting rigid continuation cylinders defines a chain map. Reversed interpolations and two-parameter homotopies show the maps are inverse on homology.

On \(T^2\), \(\pi_2(T^2)=0\), so there is no sphere bubbling or capping ambiguity for contractible loops. For a sufficiently small generic Hamiltonian, \(HF_*(H)\cong H_{*+n}(T^2;\mathbb F_2)\) up to conventional grading shift (here n=1), giving total rank \(1+2+1=4\). A nondegenerate Hamiltonian time-one map therefore has at least four contractible fixed-point orbits. This does not apply to a non-Hamiltonian symplectic translation.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Corrected energy.** What extra term appears when H depends explicitly on s? **Answer:** minus the integral of \(\partial_sH_s\) along the continuation solution under the stated action convention.
2. **Chain homotopy.** Do continuation maps need to be literal inverses on the chain level? **Answer:** no; they are inverses on homology via chain homotopies.
3. **Torus bound.** What is the total Betti number of the two-torus over \(\mathbb F_2\)? **Answer:** four.
4. **Scope.** Does the four-fixed-point conclusion include arbitrary area-preserving torus translations? **Answer:** no; it concerns Hamiltonian time-one maps under nondegeneracy.

- [Arnold conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_conjecture); [Floer homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floer_homology).

### A Complete Lagrangian Floer Model: Two Graphs over a Circle

**Track:** Topology and Geometry. **Background:** cotangent bundle graphs, exact Lagrangians, Morse homology, holomorphic strips and compactness. **Central question:** how do the geometry and the algebra fit together in a model that can be calculated from start to finish?

#### Mathematical companion (not spoken)

Work on \(T^*S^1\) with \(q\in\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\), momentum \(p\in\mathbb R\), \(\omega=dq\wedge dp=d\lambda\), and \(\lambda=-p\,dq\). Put \(L_0=\{p=0\}\) and \(L_1=\Gamma_{df}\) for \(f(q)=\epsilon\cos(2\pi q)\), \(\epsilon>0\) small. Then \(\lambda|_{L_0}=df_0=0\), \(\lambda|_{L_1}=df_1=-df\), so \(f_1=-f\). The intersections are the critical points \(q=0,1/2\) of \(f\). For a path \(x:[0,1]\to T^*S^1\), \(x(0)\in L_0\), \(x(1)\in L_1\), a consistent action is
\[
\mathcal A_{H,L_0,L_1}(x)=-\int_0^1\lambda(\dot x)dt-\int_0^1H_t(x)dt+f_1(x(1))-f_0(x(0)).
\]
For zero Hamiltonian and a constant chord at critical point \(q\), this equals \(-f(q)\). Its first variation is the symplectic pairing of \(\dot x-X_H\) with a variation having endpoints tangent to the Lagrangians.

The standard small-exact-graph correspondence identifies regular rigid holomorphic strips with Morse gradient lines of \(f\) up to grading and flow-direction conventions, after confinement and transversality are established. Over \(\mathbb F_2\), the two gradient arcs cancel, so \(HF(L_0,L_1;\mathbb F_2)\cong H_*(S^1;\mathbb F_2)\) up to grading shift, with rank two. An arbitrary closed nonexact graph \(p=c\ne0\) is not covered by this exact Hamiltonian-isotopy comparison.

##### Exercises with answers

1. **Intersections.** Where do \(L_0\) and \(\Gamma_{df}\) meet for \(f(q)=\epsilon\cos(2\pi q)\)? **Answer:** at \(q=0\) and \(q=1/2\) mod one.
2. **Actions.** What are the two constant-chord actions? **Answer:** \(-\epsilon\) and \(+\epsilon\).
3. **Differential.** Why do two rigid gradient arcs contribute zero over \(\mathbb F_2\)? **Answer:** \(1+1=0\) modulo two.
4. **Counterexample.** Why can a constant-momentum Lagrangian miss the zero section without contradicting Floer intersection bounds for exact graphs? **Answer:** its underlying closed one-form is nonexact and the translation is not a Hamiltonian exact-graph deformation.
