# How a Defense Remembers

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

Consider two encounters between a vertebrate animal and the same infectious organism. In the first encounter, the animal has no experience of the pathogen's particular molecular features. In the second, weeks or months later, it may respond faster and with different protective molecules. The animal has not learned to recognize the pathogen the way it learns a landscape. It has changed the population and state of certain immune cells. Some descendants of cells activated during the first encounter remain ready to respond again. Immune memory is partly a story about what a body chooses to keep after an emergency is over.

But memory cannot come first. Before the first infection, there must be a way to recognize something the animal has never met. An animal cannot afford to place a separate inherited gene in its genome for every possible viral protein, bacterial surface, and parasite molecule. Pathogens also change. The vertebrate solution is to produce an enormous diversity of receptors during the development of immune cells, before the particular pathogen arrives. Recognition is distributed among cells whose receptors differ. Exposure then changes which of those cells multiply, what they become, and how long some descendants survive. This is the central logic of clonal selection.

Generating this repertoire is a risky kind of biological innovation. Every rearrangement takes place in one cell, not in all the cells of the animal, and it is not passed to the animal's offspring as an ordinary inherited adaptation. Daughter cells of the rearranging lymphocyte do inherit that receptor configuration as they proliferate. This creates a population-level distinction that will matter throughout immunity: the animal's genes prescribe rules for generating receptors, but the particular set of successful clones depends on random events during development and on the animal's history. Two genetically identical animals need not have identical repertoires at every point in life.

A lymphocyte is a cell belonging to a family that includes B cells and T cells. In jawed vertebrates, developing B cells build antibody receptors and developing T cells build T-cell receptors by rearranging inherited DNA segments. The segments are often labeled V, D, and J, for variable, diversity, and joining regions. Different combinations, and changes at the joins, can produce many receptor sequences from a limited inherited set of components. Not every rearrangement makes a useful protein, and many potentially dangerous receptors must be eliminated or restrained. The body is not storing a finished catalogue of enemy shapes. It is generating a repertoire of possibilities, then managing the consequences.

The experimental basis for this account was not obvious. For much of the twentieth century, researchers disputed whether an antigen acted like a mold, teaching an antibody what shape to take, or selected from antibody specificities that already existed. Niels Jerne proposed an influential selection theory in 1955. Frank Macfarlane Burnet developed clonal selection as a theory centered on populations of cells in 1957. Those theories were not final molecular demonstrations. In 1976, Nobumichi Hozumi and Susumu Tonegawa compared DNA associated with antibody genes in embryonic mouse cells and an antibody-producing tumor. The patterns differed in a way that supported rearrangement of the relevant gene sequences during the development of antibody-producing cells. Later work supplied much greater molecular detail. The experiment mattered because it showed how a lymphocyte could have an inherited genome yet build a distinctive recognition receptor during its own life.

An antibody is a protein that binds a molecular feature called an antigen, more precisely an epitope on that antigen. B-cell receptors are membrane-bound forms of related recognition molecules. Binding is not the same as destruction. An antibody may block a virus from entering a cell, bind a bacterial toxin so it cannot reach its target, agglutinate particles, mark an organism for phagocytosis, or recruit complement. The effect depends on the antigen, where it is located, the antibody's binding properties, and its other structural regions. A high concentration of antibody measured in blood is evidence of an immune response, not automatically evidence of protection in every tissue.

B cells alone do not explain how an animal chooses which clones to expand. Specialized antigen-presenting cells, especially dendritic cells, can collect material in tissues and travel to sites where lymphocytes encounter antigens. These cells carry information about the context of their encounter. Receptor engagement by itself may be insufficient to activate a naïve T cell fully; costimulatory signals and cytokines can change the outcome. This is where the early pattern-recognition system meets the antigen-specific system. The alarm helps determine whether a particular molecular encounter becomes a call to action.

T cells recognize antigen in a different fashion from most antibodies. Many T-cell receptors see a short peptide held in the groove of a major histocompatibility complex molecule, usually abbreviated M H C. Class one M H C molecules commonly display peptides derived from proteins within a cell, making it possible for certain cytotoxic T cells to recognize infected or otherwise altered cells. Class two M H C molecules are found especially on professional antigen-presenting cells and display peptides derived largely from material brought into cellular compartments from outside. Helper T cells responding to class two presentations can organize other immune cells, including B cells. The rule is a starting map, not a complete catalogue: antigen-processing and presentation pathways have important exceptions, including cross-presentation.

Why not let a T cell recognize any viral protein anywhere? Because a T cell has to decide which of the body's cells to act on. The pairing between antigen-derived peptide and an animal's own M H C molecule supplies information about the target cell. In 1974, Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty reported experiments on virus-specific killing by mouse T cells showing that recognition was restricted by the histocompatibility type of the target cells. Their finding became fundamental to understanding T-cell recognition. The result was not that a T cell could see a pathogen floating freely and then decide which tissue it happened to be in. The target cell's molecular presentation mattered.

When an appropriately activated B cell receives the necessary help, some descendants become plasma cells that secrete antibodies in large quantities. Other B-cell responses enter specialized structures called germinal centers, where the immune system can further alter and select antibody receptors. An enzyme called activation-induced cytidine deaminase participates in the process that produces somatic hypermutation in antibody variable regions and can also contribute to changes in antibody class. Some variants bind their target more effectively and obtain survival and help signals; others do not. This is an evolutionary process within the life of an individual animal, involving variation, competition, and selection under strong biological constraints.

Affinity maturation needs careful interpretation. It does not imply that a whole immune system automatically develops the single best imaginable antibody. The available starting clones, the antigen's shape, the timing of exposure, and the rules of cell selection limit the result. A pathogen that changes its surface may escape the antibodies that were most useful against an earlier form. Different parts of the response can also emphasize different antigens. Selection continues within a body, but the landscape being selected against may change while the process is happening.

Some of the expanded cells die when the immediate response subsides. Others persist as memory lymphocytes or long-lived antibody-secreting plasma cells. These are not identical forms of memory. A resting memory B cell can later proliferate and produce new descendants when it sees antigen in an appropriate context. A long-lived plasma cell can continue secreting antibody without being reactivated by the same sequence. Memory T cells can occupy different locations, including tissues that have previously faced a threat. Their survival and maintenance depend on distinct signals and conditions. The body's record of a past encounter is therefore spread across cells, molecular products, locations, and developmental states.

This makes the time course of protection more interesting than a single rising line on a graph. A first encounter often requires activation, expansion and differentiation before a strong antigen-specific response is available. A later encounter may meet circulating antibodies immediately, encounter memory cells already positioned in a tissue, and prompt quicker expansion of responsive clones. But immunity need not be sterilizing. A protected animal might still allow some replication while preventing the pathogen from causing its most serious effects. How much protection is achieved depends on exposure, pathogen variation, immune state, tissue, and time since the earlier encounter.

Vaccination is one application of this capacity, but it is not the subject of the mechanism. The relevant physiological idea is that presenting a suitable antigen in a suitable immunological context can alter the repertoire and generate protective memory without the full course of natural disease. Not every vaccine produces the same type, durability or location of protection, just as natural infections do not all do so. The useful comparison is between what the immune system has prepared to do and what a later infection actually demands.

The variety of receptor genes is only one source of difference among vertebrates. Genes of the major histocompatibility complex also vary markedly between individuals and populations. That variation changes which peptide fragments can be displayed to T cells. A pathogen's protein sequence is therefore not enough by itself to predict the exact T-cell response of every host. At the same time, a broad and flexible recognition system does not spare an animal the need for well-maintained barriers, phagocytes, complement, and other early defenses. Adaptive responses take cellular time: a B cell must receive appropriate signals, proliferate, differentiate, and, in some circumstances, undergo further selection. A microbe can multiply while that is happening. The first hours of protection and the later memory response are parts of the same physiology.

There is a cost to preparing a repertoire so broad. Some receptors will recognize molecules belonging to the animal itself. Developmental selection removes many such clones, but it is not perfect. The animal also encounters harmless environmental molecules and living organisms that should not trigger destructive attacks. The next problem is not how to expand a matching clone but how to keep it from attacking in the wrong circumstances. Immune memory is valuable because it can preserve a useful response. The same persistence makes errors in recognition or regulation especially consequential.

We should also avoid assuming that every animal makes memory through V, D, and J rearrangement. The antibody-and-T-cell-receptor system just described is characteristic of jawed vertebrates, from sharks and rays through bony fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals, with differences among lineages. Jawless vertebrates have other ways to generate diverse antigen receptors. Some invertebrates show enhanced defense after an earlier exposure without possessing these vertebrate receptor systems. The ability to respond differently on a second encounter is broader than any one molecular invention. What is distinctive here is the particular machinery by which a jawed vertebrate combines diversity, selection, and long-lived cellular populations.

Return to the two encounters with a pathogen. The second does not start from the same immune landscape as the first. The body has selected some cells, changed the abundance of others, and sometimes left antibodies in circulation or at vulnerable surfaces. Yet this memory emerged only because early recognition and communication brought antigen to the right cells, and because developmental and regulatory processes permitted some responses and rejected others. Immunity is not a stored photograph of a germ. It is a reconfigured living system, whose earlier encounter has changed what it can do next.

## Scientific companion (not narrated)

### Which stage produces which kind of diversity?

| Stage | Main change | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lymphocyte development | Somatic V(D)J recombination and junctional diversity | Creates antigen-receptor repertoire before specific encounter |
| Initial antigen encounter | Activation and clonal expansion | Selects responsive clones, not new germline genes |
| B-cell germinal-center reaction | Somatic hypermutation, affinity-dependent selection, often class switching | Can refine antibody properties after activation |
| Memory formation | Differentiated memory B/T cells and long-lived plasma cells | Different mechanisms preserve faster responses or ongoing antibodies |

**Antigen presentation.** MHC I is typically associated with intracellular peptide presentation to CD8 T cells, and MHC II with extracellularly acquired peptide presentation to CD4 T cells. Cross-presentation, antigen origin, tissue context, and other lymphocyte populations complicate the simple teaching diagram. Natural killer cells can use MHC I expression as part of inhibitory signaling but do not detect peptide–MHC with the same clonally rearranged receptors as conventional T cells.

**Immunological memory versus immunity.** A memory response can reduce severity without preventing all pathogen entry or replication. Persistence of antibodies is not interchangeable with persistence of memory lymphocytes. Avoid equating concentration of an antibody in blood with protection at every tissue surface.
