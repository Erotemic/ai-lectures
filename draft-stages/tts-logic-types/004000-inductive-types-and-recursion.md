# Inductive Types, Recursion, and Structural Induction: Building Proofs from Constructors

Imagine a function that counts the entries in a list. Its program seems almost embarrassingly simple. An empty list has length zero. A list formed by putting a new item in front of an existing list has length one greater than the length of the existing list. Yet that two-line description contains a complete account of both computation and proof. It tells us what lists can be, how to take them apart, and why a statement about every finite list can be established by checking just two cases.

There is a question hiding in that description. Why are those two cases enough? We have seen only the empty list and the list made by adding one item. How do we know there is no third kind of finite list that escapes our reasoning? To answer, we need to define the data before we begin manipulating it. An inductive definition gives a finite collection of ways to make objects, and declares that every object of the type is generated from those ways. This is what licenses the recursive algorithm and the induction proof.

Our running examples will be natural numbers and lists. We will derive a recursion rule, prove that adding zero on the left leaves a natural number unchanged, and prove an associativity law for list concatenation. We will also examine where the beautiful symmetry between recursion and induction stops: a program has to terminate, and an induction principle proves only what its constructors permit.

## Constructing numbers rather than presupposing them

Begin with a deliberately small account of natural numbers. We have zero. And whenever we have a natural number, we can form its successor. One is the successor of zero, two is the successor of one, and so on. There is no constructor for negative one, no constructor for infinity, and no additional mysterious number that cannot be reached through finitely many successors.

That last clause is essential. If we merely said that zero is a natural number and the successor of any natural number is a natural number, we might be describing a much larger collection containing objects unrelated to zero. An inductive definition specifies the collection freely generated by its constructors, subject to its type's rules. Every ordinary natural number has a finite constructor history: start at zero, then apply successor a finite number of times.

Now we can ask how to define a function on natural numbers. Suppose its output is always another natural number. To specify it, we give a starting value for zero and a rule that turns the result at a number into the result at its successor. These two pieces of information are enough to calculate a value at any natural-number input. The calculation begins at zero and follows the constructor history outward.

Consider doubling. The doubled value at zero is zero. At the successor of n, the doubled value is two more than the doubled value at n. At three, the computation unfolds as three successor steps and reaches six. Nothing about this function calls for an infinite search. Every recursive call moves to the predecessor stored inside the constructor that we just examined.

This is structural recursion. A function is defined by considering each constructor of its input, and any recursive computation proceeds on an immediate piece of the input that is structurally smaller. The program follows the way the data was built, in reverse.

## Why a proof follows the same skeleton

Replace the output number with a proposition about the input. For every natural number n, let P of n mean a property we hope to establish. There is again a zero case and a successor case. In the zero case we must prove P of zero. In the successor case we are allowed to assume P of n and must establish P of the successor of n. If we can supply both pieces, induction gives P of every natural number.

Why should an assumption about n be available in the successor case? Because every successor number has been formed from some n, and the inductive proof is constructed by following that formation. For zero there is nothing smaller to prove. For successor of n, a proof built for n can be extended by the successor rule. Each natural number has a finite history, so every proof built by this process eventually reaches its base case.

This resembles the recursive function, but one detail changes. When we compute, the smaller result might be a number, a list, or some other data. When we prove, the smaller result is evidence of the proposition at the smaller input. In dependent type theory these can be described by a single general mechanism: the output family may depend on the input. Taking a constant output family gives ordinary recursion. Taking the output family to be propositions gives induction.

The general mechanism is often called an eliminator or recursor. Its name points to what it allows us to do: given a value assembled by constructors, use its structure to produce a result of another specified type. Constructor rules introduce values; eliminator rules specify how to use all possible values. For natural numbers, the eliminator requires a base result and a successor-step function.

The constructor history is finite, but do not mistake that for a claim that every mathematical function can be implemented by structural recursion. Some algorithms use different decreasing measures, and some total computable functions require more elaborate recursion principles. The present rule is powerful because its termination argument is visible in the shape of the data.

## Proving a small law that computation alone will not settle

Let addition be defined by recursion on its second argument. For any natural m, adding zero to m gives m. Adding the successor of n to m gives the successor of m plus n. This is a legitimate definition: in the second clause the recursive call takes n, which is structurally smaller than its successor.

Now ask whether zero plus n equals n for every n. You may expect the answer because addition is familiar. But look carefully at the recursive equations. They tell us directly that m plus zero equals m. They do not directly tell us how zero plus an arbitrary n simplifies. The second argument of addition is the recursive argument, and in zero plus n that second argument is an unknown variable. A computer cannot unfold it until it knows whether n is zero or a successor.

Induction supplies exactly the missing information. For n equal to zero, zero plus zero is zero by the defining equation. That establishes the base case. Suppose zero plus n equals n. We must show zero plus the successor of n equals the successor of n. The definition of addition turns the left-hand side into the successor of zero plus n. The induction hypothesis replaces zero plus n with n. The two sides are now the same successor. This finishes the step.

Notice the order of the reasoning. We chose the variable on which the function recurses. We used its constructors to identify the two cases. We simplified using a defining equation, then applied the induction hypothesis to a smaller argument, and finally compared the expressions. The proof does not say merely that the result is obvious from arithmetic. It explains how the proposition is preserved each time another successor is added.

This example also distinguishes two notions of equality that become important in formal mathematics. The equation m plus zero equals m may simplify directly because it is built into the definition. The equation zero plus n equals n needs a proof by induction in the formulation we chose. Both are true for natural numbers, but one is available by computation and the other by a theorem. Definitional computation depends partly on how a function was defined.

## Lists expose the recursive structure even more clearly

Natural numbers have one nonrecursive constructor and one recursive constructor. Lists have the same pattern. An empty list contains no entries. A nonempty list consists of a first entry, called its head, and another list, called its tail. A list with three entries is therefore a head followed by a head followed by a head followed by the empty list.

We need to say what sort of entries the list holds. A list of integers and a list of strings are different types. But the recursion pattern is uniform: once we fix an element type, every list is either empty or a head of that type attached to a smaller list of the same type.

Length is defined by the same rule described at the opening. The empty list has length zero. A head attached to a tail has length one plus the tail's length. To calculate the length of a particular list, peel off a head, ask for the length of the tail, and repeat until reaching empty. The result accumulates as we return from the recursive calls.

Now consider concatenation. If we join the empty list to a second list, we get the second list. If the first list begins with an entry a and has tail xs, then concatenating it with ys gives a at the front of the concatenation of xs with ys. The recursion proceeds on the first list. The second list is held fixed as the first list is traversed.

This definition preserves order. Joining the list consisting of red then blue with the list consisting of green gives red, blue, green. Swapping the inputs would change that order. Concatenation is generally not commutative. It is associative, however: grouping three lists in either order produces the same list. Rather than appealing to a picture of objects lined up on a table, we can prove the law for all finite lists.

## A complete associativity proof

Fix two lists ys and zs. We will prove that joining xs to ys, then joining the result to zs, gives the same list as joining xs to the result of joining ys to zs. The induction variable is xs. That choice follows from the definition of concatenation: its recursion happens on the first argument.

When xs is empty, the left side first joins empty to ys, leaving ys, and then joins ys to zs. The right side joins empty to the already concatenated list, also leaving ys joined to zs. Both sides simplify to the same expression. The base case holds for every choice of ys and zs.

For the step, suppose xs begins with a head a and has tail t. Assume the associativity statement is already known for t, with the same arbitrary ys and zs. On the left, joining the head-and-tail list to ys keeps a at the front and joins t to ys behind it. Joining that result to zs again keeps a at the front. What remains after that head is the list obtained by joining t to ys and then joining the result to zs.

On the right, joining the head-and-tail list to the concatenation of ys and zs also keeps a at the front. What remains is t joined to the result of joining ys and zs. The induction hypothesis says exactly that these two tails are equal. Attaching the same head a to equal tails produces equal lists. The step is complete.

That proof contains a common and useful pattern. A proposition about a composite object reduces to a proposition about a smaller component. The constructor on the outside is the same on both sides, so we only need equality of the recursive pieces. If a theorem does not reduce in that way, we may have chosen the wrong induction variable, or we may need a stronger induction hypothesis.

For example, a proof about reversing lists often becomes easier if we first prove a lemma describing what reversal does to a concatenation. Reversal is recursive, but its recursive equation changes where the head ends up. The corresponding theorem does not automatically simplify merely because it is about lists. Discovering the right intermediate statement is part of mathematical proof design.

## Inductive propositions are built in the same manner

Constructors need not build data that we intend to inspect at runtime. They can build evidence. Define a proposition that says a natural number is even. We can give one constructor establishing that zero is even, and a second constructor saying that if n is even, then the number two greater than n is even. There are no further constructors for the proposition.

A proof that six is even can be assembled by applying the second constructor three times to the base proof. Conversely, if someone gives evidence that a number is even, the structure of that evidence reveals which constructor was used last. We can prove properties of even numbers by induction on the evidence itself, rather than by induction on the number alone.

This distinction is valuable when the proposition admits only certain forms of evidence. Suppose we want to show that every even natural number can be written as two times another natural number. Induction on the evenness derivation supplies the needed two cases: zero corresponds to two times zero, and adding two increases the multiplier by one. Induction on every natural number would demand a statement about odd numbers as well, and the desired proposition would not even hold there.

Here the induction target is a derivation of a proposition. The smaller object is a smaller derivation. The mathematics follows the logical structure of the evidence, which may be more informative than the syntax of the number it concerns.

## The subtlety of indexed families

Some inductive types come in families indexed by another value. A vector of length three and a vector of length five contain the same kind of elements, but the required lengths differ. We can define a family of vectors indexed by natural numbers. An empty constructor produces a vector of length zero. A constructor that attaches a head to a vector of length n produces a vector of length the successor of n.

This arrangement makes impossible lengths hard to express. A constructor cannot directly attach an element while keeping the same claimed length. Appending two vectors should produce a vector whose length is the sum of their lengths. Its output type records that relation. A proof about vector append then has to track arithmetic on the indices as well as recursion on the vectors.

The resulting eliminator is more precise than an ordinary case distinction. When we examine an empty vector, we learn that its index is zero. When we examine a nonempty vector, we learn that its index has successor form. Those index constraints are useful information for a dependently typed program or theorem.

There is a constraint behind the convenience. We must choose the constructors carefully so that they define a coherent positive inductive family. A type cannot be declared with arbitrary negative recursive occurrences and still automatically receive a sound induction principle. Positivity conditions prevent a class of circular definitions that would undermine the logical interpretation. The precise technical restrictions differ among foundational systems, so the general slogan that every recursive equation defines a type is false.

## What makes the recursion terminate?

Return to the length function. Every call removes one head. No finite list can supply an endless descending chain of tails. Termination follows from the inductive structure itself. An implementation can compile this recursion to a loop, but its justification does not depend on the machine-level technique.

Other functions take a larger recursive leap. The Euclidean algorithm for the greatest common divisor replaces a pair of positive integers by a pair with a smaller remainder. Its recursive argument is not simply a direct constructor field of the previous argument. To justify it, we use a decreasing numerical measure, or more generally a well-founded relation: one that admits no infinite descending sequence.

Structural recursion is a particularly transparent special case of a broader idea. Every recursive call must make progress toward a base case under a justified notion of decrease. The decrease can be built into the input's syntax, or it can come from a mathematical proof about a measure. If we neglect that obligation, a definition like f of n equals f of n plus one could be accepted as an equation by a naive evaluator and never produce an answer.

This explains why a system designed to treat programs as mathematical proofs regulates general recursion. A nonterminating program cannot automatically serve as a witness of every proposition. The foundational discipline demands evidence that recursive definitions are legitimate, while practical languages may allow unrestricted programs with partial or diverging behavior.

## Reading the general elimination rule

There is a compact way to assemble the pieces. To define a result depending on a natural number n, specify what result is needed at zero. Then specify how, for arbitrary n, a result at n can be turned into a result at successor of n. The eliminator supplies a result at every natural number.

When the required result is a fixed type, we read this as recursion. When it is a family of propositions, we read it as induction. When it is a family of data types depending on n, we read it as dependent recursion. In all three cases, the constructors of the input type govern the cases and the available smaller results.

This does not eliminate mathematical creativity. An induction principle tells us what obligations are sufficient; it does not find the right proposition for us. For a difficult theorem, strengthening the induction hypothesis can be decisive. For a difficult program, selecting an appropriate representation or a well-founded measure can be the main work. The logical framework ensures that a completed argument covers every constructed object, but the proof writer still has to discover an argument worth checking.

There is also a practical distinction between recognizing the structure of a datatype and choosing an implementation representation. A tree may be stored using pointers, an array, or compact indexing. Its inductive mathematical definition can remain the same. A proof about all finite trees usually proceeds by considering a leaf or a node with its smaller subtrees, even when the machine never uses recursive function calls.

## Returning to the list

The length function, the concatenation function, and the associativity proof all came from the same two constructors. Empty supplied the base behavior. Cons, the constructor that attaches a head, supplied the step behavior. The reason the proof reached every finite list was the inductive definition of what a list is.

Natural numbers displayed the same principle through zero and successor. But we also discovered a distinction within equalities: some equations unfold directly from a chosen recursive definition; others require induction to establish. And we saw the limit of purely structural recursion: algorithms whose decrease is indirect need a more general termination argument.

An inductive type therefore gives us three things at once. It specifies how values can be built, permits functions to be computed by taking those values apart, and supplies an induction principle for proving properties of all such values. The corresponding proof succeeds when its base cases establish the desired property at the starting constructors, and its step cases preserve that property under every constructor. For a finite list, nothing else is left to check.

## Mathematical companion (display only)

Inductive presentation of the natural numbers:

```lean
inductive MyNat where
  | zero : MyNat
  | succ : MyNat → MyNat
```

Recursion on the *second* argument of addition, schematically:

\[
 m+0=m,\qquad m+S(n)=S(m+n).
\]

Induction proving the less direct equation:

\[
 P(n):\ 0+n=n,\quad P(0):\ 0+0=0,\quad
 P(n)\Longrightarrow 0+S(n)=S(0+n)=S(n).
\]

Lists over a type \(A\) are freely generated by \(\mathrm{nil}\) and \(\mathrm{cons}:A\times\mathrm{List}(A)\to\mathrm{List}(A)\). The recursion for concatenation is

\[
 \mathrm{nil}\mathbin{++}ys=ys,\qquad
 (a::xs)\mathbin{++}ys=a::(xs\mathbin{++}ys).
\]

The induction invariant for associativity is

\[
 P(xs):\ \forall ys,zs,\ (xs\mathbin{++}ys)\mathbin{++}zs
 =xs\mathbin{++}(ys\mathbin{++}zs).
\]

For \(xs=a::t\), rewrite both sides to `a :: ...`; then apply \(P(t)\) to the tails. The universal quantifiers over \(ys,zs\) belong inside the invariant; this preserves the required generality of the induction hypothesis.

Example indexed family (schematic Lean):

```lean
inductive Vec (α : Type u) : Nat → Type u where
  | nil : Vec α 0
  | cons : α → Vec α n → Vec α (n + 1)
```

General eliminator for natural numbers, suppressing universe details:

\[
 \mathrm{Nat.rec} : P(0)\to
 (\forall n, P(n)\to P(n+1))\to\forall n,P(n).
\]

### Exercises with answers

1. **Structural recursion.** Give the two equations for counting list elements. **Answer:** \(\ell([])=0\) and \(\ell(a::xs)=1+\ell(xs)\).
2. **Induction variable.** Why induct on the first argument of list concatenation? **Answer:** its recursive defining equations unfold on the first argument.
3. **Proof from evidence.** How would you prove every derivably even natural number has the form \(2k\)? **Answer:** induction on its evenness derivation; constructors correspond to \(k=0\) and \(k\mapsto k+1\).
4. **Definitional versus proved.** Which equation for recursively defined addition is immediately simplified by its second-argument recursion: \(m+0=m\) or \(0+n=n\)? **Answer:** the first; the second needs induction on \(n\).
5. **Termination.** What does a recursive call on a list tail certify? **Answer:** structural descent toward the empty constructor for every finite list.
