# 021000 — Roof Sheathing, Uplift Connections, and Wind Load Transfer

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A roof can lose shingles in a storm and still protect the rooms below. A roof that loses its wood sheathing has a much more serious problem. The distinction is structural: roofing materials shed weather, while sheathing and its connections help make a roof into a stiff, load-transferring assembly. Wind does not attack only the shingles. It creates pressures on the whole roof, including upward forces that have to find a continuous path into the building.

We tend to picture roof loading as weight pressing downward: shingles, snow, workers and the roof itself. Wind adds a different direction. As air flows around the building, the pressure above parts of the roof can be lower than the pressure below. At corners, edges and other regions, local suction can become especially strong. The direction of the net force on roof sheathing may then be outward and upward. In a severe event, the capacity of connections can matter as much as the strength of the sheathing panel.

## The roof is a layered structural system

Start at the outer layer. Roofing underlayment and shingles provide weather protection, but they are not intended to substitute for the roof deck's structural attachment. Beneath them lie sheathing panels, often plywood or oriented strand board, fastened to rafters or trusses. The framing transfers loads toward bearing walls and beams; the walls transfer them into the foundation. The exact path depends on the building, but the basic question is always the same: which connection carries each force to the next element?

Sheathing also acts as part of a diaphragm that transfers forces within the roof plane. This matters under lateral wind loads as well as suction. A diaphragm is not simply a thick sheet of wood. It is a coordinated system of panel orientation, edge support, fastening patterns, collectors and connections to supporting elements. A detail that improves local panel retention does not automatically complete the load path at the roof-to-wall connection.

Wind design standards recognize that roof corners and edges experience different uplift demands from many interior areas. That is why a fastening schedule cannot be inferred from the center of one roof alone. Exposure, geometry, roof slope, construction date and governing design assumptions all matter. A coastal home designed for hurricanes may require detailing different from a low-wind inland building.

## What makes an attachment resist uplift?

A nail transfers force through the wood and the panel. Resistance depends on its diameter and shank, head size, penetration, the material and condition of the framing, the panel, and whether the fastener was placed correctly. Under uplift, withdrawal of a fastener and pull-through of its head through the panel are distinct failure modes. A nail with inadequate penetration may withdraw even if the panel is sound. A fastener driven too deeply can crush or cut the surface fibers and reduce the resistance to head pull-through. Missed framing contributes almost nothing to attachment at that point.

Fastener spacing changes how force is shared along an edge or framing member. Closer spacing can increase connection capacity, but only within a coherent design and when workmanship preserves edge distances and wood integrity. Overdriving, splitting, corroded fasteners and previously damaged sheathing can defeat the intention of a close pattern. A nail gun's speed is not a substitute for checking whether nails actually entered the intended member.

Panel edges matter, too. A supported edge behaves differently from an unsupported one. Some roof assemblies use clips or blocking for panel-edge support. The specified gaps between panels accommodate dimensional change; forcing panels tightly together can encourage buckling when moisture expands them. Sheathing thickness, span rating and framing spacing must agree with the intended loading and roof assembly.

## Inspection with the load path in mind

Suppose an older attic has scattered nail tips visible between rafters. That may be evidence of missed framing during original installation. Elsewhere, sheathing may appear softened around a former roof leak. A purely visual count of fasteners is not enough to declare an attachment adequate. The inspector wants to know where the panel is actually attached, whether the wood substrate can resist the connection forces and whether roof-to-wall ties transfer uplift beyond the framing.

During a planned reroof, the sheathing surface becomes accessible for inspection. That is a chance to document rotten or delaminated areas, verify panel type and thickness, inspect edge support, identify inadequate fasteners and compare the proposed reattachment to a recognized design. A reroofing specification should name the fastening and inspection method, not merely state that the roof deck will be secure.

Consider a roof panel that remains attached to every truss, but the trusses are poorly connected to the supporting walls. The panel may survive while a larger assembly separates. Conversely, strong roof-to-wall connectors cannot keep weakly nailed panels from being pulled away. Engineering the roof means considering both levels of connection, plus the paths through walls, floors and foundations.

## A useful documentation exercise

Sketch one typical roof bay. Mark panel edges and spans, rafters or trusses, observed fastener locations, any blocking and the supporting wall. Add arrows for downward gravity loads, upward wind suction and in-plane forces. Now ask where each arrow goes next and which connection is expected to carry it. The exercise often reveals that an apparently isolated hardware detail depends on two or three unseen connections.

The durable principle is that a roof deck does two jobs: it supports the weather covering and contributes to the building's structure. Its strength cannot be separated from the pattern and quality of its attachments or from the continuous load path below it. A competent inspection and a competent construction specification follow that entire chain rather than treating a better nail as a complete answer.

## Non-spoken technical companion

**Structural layers to draw:** Roof covering, underlayment, panel sheathing, sheathing fastener, rafter or truss, roof-to-wall connector, supporting wall, wall anchorage and foundation. Show separate uplift and in-plane paths.

**Inspection record:** Roof geometry/exposure assumptions; panel thickness/type and condition; edge support; framing spacing; fastener type/spacing and actual framing engagement; overdriven/missing fasteners; signs of rot/corrosion; roof-to-wall and wall-to-foundation connections. Photograph before concealment.

**Design boundary:** Exact sheathing-nailing patterns, fastener schedule and high-wind upgrades vary by adopted building standards, location and engineering assumptions. FORTIFIED and FEMA guidance illustrate robust practice but do not automatically govern every jurisdiction. Do not invent a universal fastener spacing from one example. Review roof modifications with a qualified designer when structural loads change.

**Sources:** Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, FORTIFIED Roof guidance; FEMA, home-builder wind resistance and continuous load path guidance; American Wood Council, wood construction design resources.
