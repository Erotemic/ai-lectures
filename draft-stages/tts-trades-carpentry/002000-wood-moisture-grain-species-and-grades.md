# A Board Is a History of Growth

Take two pieces of lumber that look alike. One stays straight through a season of indoor heating. The other cups, twists, and opens a seam. The difference is not necessarily that one board was cut accurately and the other badly. Wood carries its history into the building: the way a tree grew, how the log was sawn, which fibers were included, how it was dried, and what moisture conditions it encounters after installation.

Wood is an engineered material only in the sense that we select, grade, dry and shape a biological one. Its fundamental organization comes from the tree. Cells are mostly aligned along the stem. That alignment makes properties strongly directional. In many conditions a piece of wood behaves differently along the grain than across it. A species name tells us something useful, but not everything: grade, moisture condition, defects, treatment, dimensions and orientation matter too.

## What a growth ring tells us

Imagine a log as concentric growth rings around a central pith. Boards can be cut roughly tangential to those rings or so that rings meet the face more nearly at right angles. We often call their appearances flat grain and edge grain. These patterns influence how the board changes shape during drying.

Wood usually shrinks much more across the grain than along it when bound water leaves the cell walls. Shrinkage is also commonly greater in the tangential direction, around the growth rings, than in the radial direction, from pith to bark. Because the directions do not shrink equally, a flat-sawn board can cup. A board containing the pith or reaction wood may warp in additional ways. Grain direction changes local strength and splitting behavior, especially near knots or sloping grain.

Think of a shelf made from a broad solid-wood panel. In a humid season it can expand across its width. In a dry season it can contract. If the panel is rigidly fastened across its full width without allowing movement, it may crack, bow or damage its fasteners. A plywood panel often behaves differently because adjacent veneers are oriented in different directions. That cross-lamination is not magic, but it modifies dimensional response and structural properties.

## Moisture content uses a surprising denominator

Carpenters often say a board is at twelve percent moisture content. They do not mean twelve percent of its current weight is water. In the conventional oven-dry-basis definition, moisture content is the mass of water divided by the oven-dry mass of the wood, multiplied by one hundred percent.

Suppose a sample weighs one hundred and twelve grams and, after careful drying to the relevant test endpoint, its wood substance weighs one hundred grams. Its moisture content is twelve percent. If it weighs one hundred and thirty grams with the same dry mass, its moisture content is thirty percent. That last number is around the often-used conceptual fiber-saturation range for many woods, but the actual transition varies by wood and even within a piece. It is not a universal exact threshold.

Above that region, additional water can occupy cell cavities without proportionally causing further cell-wall swelling. Below it, changes in bound water within the cell walls tend to produce dimensional changes. A board drying from twenty percent toward eight percent can therefore shrink materially even though it may already look dry and feel dry. A surface finish slows moisture exchange; it generally does not abolish equilibrium with surrounding humidity.

## Equilibrium is a moving target

Leave a piece of wood in air at a particular temperature and relative humidity for long enough, and it tends toward an equilibrium moisture content. Change the indoor humidity, and that target changes. The wood responds over time, with the surface commonly changing before the core. The speed depends on dimensions, orientation, coating and conditions.

This is why millwork and flooring are often conditioned in the environment where they will be installed, and why storage matters. A stack of boards left on damp concrete or exposed to rain can develop moisture gradients even when originally kiln-dried. The goal is not to make all wood completely dry. It is to use wood at a suitable moisture state for the service it will experience, with room for expected movement.

## Species is not a grade

A species group describes botanical and material tendencies. A grade characterizes a particular piece of lumber under specified rules. Knots, checks, slope of grain, wane and other characteristics can reduce the capacity of a member or alter how it must be used. Structural grades are not cosmetic grades. A board with attractive clear faces could be the wrong structural product; a rough-looking but properly graded member may perform its intended task.

A structural stamp can include a grade, species or species group, grading agency identifier, mill information and moisture or treatment designations. Interpret a stamp according to the governing grading system, not by treating every letter as an intuitive adjective. Stamped dimensions and actual dimensions also differ: a nominal two-by-four is not literally two inches by four inches after surfacing. Plans and fittings must use actual sizes and specified tolerances.

## Direction of load changes the problem

Picture a beam that must carry a floor load. Its bending capacity depends on cross-section, span, wood properties, support conditions and defects. Turning a rectangular member on edge can greatly change its bending stiffness. Driving a screw parallel to grain may create a different splitting risk than fastening across grain. A connection has its own design limits that cannot be inferred from the grade of the surrounding wood alone.

In a structure, design values come from recognized grading and engineering methods with appropriate adjustment factors. A lecture on moisture and grade cannot supply a safe span chart for an arbitrary room. But it can teach us which properties and labels to gather before a structural decision.

## Read the board before the cut

When choosing stock, examine end grain and faces. Find knots and checks, sight along the length for bow and twist, look for cupping, and consider whether the board has reached a suitable moisture condition. Ask which face will be the reference and which faces will remain visible. If a plank will span a gap, the grade and structural use matter; if it will become decorative trim, movement and finish compatibility may dominate.

The biggest change in thinking is that a board is not a uniform plastic bar. It is a directional material whose water content and internal structure evolve. Wood's usefulness comes partly from accepting those realities and designing around them. The carpenter does not force wood to stop being wood; the carpenter gives its strength the right direction and its movement an acceptable place to go.

## Technical companion: moisture and selection

**Dry-basis moisture content:** `MC (%) = 100*(m_wet - m_oven_dry)/m_oven_dry`. The example uses `112 g` and `100 g`, yielding `12%`. The fiber-saturation point is a variable concept, roughly 30% for many species; it is not a universal cut-off. Tangential, radial and longitudinal shrinkage differ and cannot be replaced with one coefficient for every board.

**Evidence:** USDA Forest Service *Wood Handbook* (2021), especially chapters 3, 4, 6, 7 and 13. Richard Bergman's chapter 13 describes equilibrium moisture and dimensional control. Structural grade and design should be traced to the relevant grading rules and engineering references, not assumed from appearance. Appropriate species, treatment and moisture specification are application-dependent.

**Observation:** Compare end-grain ring orientation on three boards and predict which face is more likely to cup under a drying change. Record grain, visible defects and stamp markings without claiming a structural grade where no stamp or documentation exists.
