# 010000 — Finish Carpentry, Trim Joints, and Seasonal Movement

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A narrow gap appears at the top of a door casing in January. Someone fills it, sands it flush, and paints it. In July, the joint closes and a new paint ridge appears. The repair made the surface look continuous, but it did not change the way the joined materials move. Finish carpentry is often described as decorative work. In reality, it is applied geometry and material science performed at a scale where the eye can detect tiny errors.

## Wood does not move equally in every direction

Wood is anisotropic: its properties depend on direction relative to the grain. In a typical piece of solid wood, dimensional change across the grain as moisture content changes is much larger than change along the grain. Tangential and radial shrinkage differ as well. Temperature matters, but for many interior solid-wood trim problems, moisture exchange with indoor air is the dominant seasonal driver.

A long piece of baseboard barely changes length compared with how much a wide solid-wood panel may change across its width. This matters when building paneled doors, fitting solid-wood shelves into rigid frames, or fastening broad boards tightly at both edges. A movement joint or an allowance for sliding is not a defect; it can be a planned escape route for a dimension that will change.

Manufactured sheet goods and engineered trims have different movement characteristics. Medium-density fiberboard, plywood, laminated products, and PVC trim are not interchangeable just because they come in similar profiles. Their responses to moisture, temperature, fastening, cutting, and paint differ. One expansion gap copied from a solid-wood detail may be wrong for a polymer trim installed outdoors.

## The eye reads lines before dimensions

A casing around a door has at least two geometries: the shape of the door opening and the visible reveals between the casing and the jamb. The wall may not be flat, plumb, or square. A perfectly square casing placed against an imperfect opening can look crooked because the reveal changes. The craft decision is which relationships must remain visually consistent and where variation can be absorbed without compromising the door's operation.

A reveal is a deliberate small offset. Keeping it even around an opening often matters more visually than whether the surrounding plaster is exactly straight. The casing should not obstruct hinges, latches, weather seals, or the door's necessary clearance. A thin flexible trim piece can conceal slight wall irregularity, but forcing trim to follow a severe hollow may produce a wavy shadow line. Scribing a piece to meet an irregular surface is often more effective than demanding the wall conform to a factory-straight board.

## Why some corners open

A miter joint is made by cutting two pieces so their angled faces meet to form a corner. For an ideal ninety-degree inside or outside corner, forty-five-degree cuts on matching profiles are a familiar starting point. But the actual corner may be eighty-eight degrees, and the two profiles may differ slightly in thickness or shape. An idealized miter angle is therefore a geometric assumption. Measuring the real corner and testing the profile alignment matters more than blindly trusting a saw stop.

The joint can also open because of differential movement. A miter joins members whose grain directions follow different wall axes, while a cope joint allows one profiled piece to overlap another at an inside corner. A coped joint can be more forgiving of modest seasonal movement and imperfect wall angles, especially for painted baseboards and crown molding. Coping does not make wood stop moving; it changes where the movement appears and how much of it the eye sees.

Long scarf joints create a sloping joint along the length of trim where one piece must meet another. Their quality depends on support, profile continuity, grain and moisture, fastening locations, and whether the joint is likely to be stressed. A poorly located scarf can telegraph through paint, especially when one side is attached to moving framing and the other is not.

## Fasteners make constraints

The purpose of a finish nail, brad, or screw is to hold the trim to the intended support while keeping it aligned. More fasteners do not automatically produce a better result. Nailing a wide board rigidly across a moisture-sensitive direction can create a restraint that encourages splitting or cupping. Conversely, placing a nail into nothing but soft wallboard may create a joint that gradually pulls away. Locate the substrate and choose attachment points with the actual load path in mind.

Adhesives and caulk have separate jobs. An adhesive transfers load when bond preparation and application are suitable. Paintable caulk can bridge small visual gaps but is not an engineered structural connector. Heavy caulking cannot permanently eliminate a repeated dimensional mismatch. Surface preparation, wetting, movement capacity, and compatibility with primers and finishes determine whether a joint remains sound.

## Build a process that catches errors before they multiply

Consider fitting baseboards in an old room. Start with a sketch that identifies long runs, interior corners, outside corners, transitions, and door casings. Check the floor for high and low points. Install or dry-fit a long reference piece, then work through the corners with trial cuts instead of cutting every piece to a nominal wall length. Preserve sufficient material for adjustment. Compare the reveal and shadow line from the room's normal viewing locations.

A useful shop habit is to make a short test joint from scraps of the actual profile. This reveals not just the corner angle but whether two nominally identical pieces truly align. Prepaint and finish choices change what errors remain visible; lighting from a nearby window can make small surface waviness obvious. Good finish carpentry is not invisible because the materials are perfect. It is convincing because the worker understands how the materials, surfaces, geometry, and viewing conditions interact.

The gap that appeared in January was a clue. It might indicate moisture movement, a poorly supported joint, or a shifting opening. The durable repair begins by deciding which kind of gap it is. A clean joint is the result of an explained movement allowance and repeatable layout, not merely a bead of filler.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Geometry and movement

- Across-grain wood movement differs from along-grain change; actual shrinkage depends on species, grain orientation, moisture-content change, and product construction.
- For an included corner angle `θ`, a symmetric ideal miter starts at `θ/2`; out-of-square conditions and trim profile alignment can require adjustment.
- Distinguish scribing, coping, scarfing, butt joining, mitering, caulking, and structural adhesion.
- In panel construction, fastener/slot arrangements must permit anticipated movement where required.

### Shop exercise

Mock up a ninety-degree interior corner and a slightly out-of-square corner using actual baseboard profile. Compare two miters and a coped joint after a small separation is introduced. Record which joint reveals a dark gap and why.

### Limits

Wood movement tables in USDA Forest Service publications are material-specific. Exterior composite/PVC trim and fire-rated door assemblies have different requirements. Local code and manufacturer instructions control critical doors, egress, stairs, and flame-spread details.
