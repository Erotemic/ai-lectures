# Inspect and repair exterior load paths

## Decks, Porches, and Exterior Load Paths

A deck looks simpler than a house. There may be no enclosed rooms and no roof, only boards, posts, and a guardrail. Yet its structural system deserves careful reasoning because many of its critical joints are exposed to water, seasonal movement, and loads from groups of people. A deck that feels stiff under one person is not thereby proven safe for the combinations of loads it may experience.

Begin with a simple deck attached to a house on one side and supported by a beam and posts on the other. Someone standing on a decking board loads the boards, which transfer force to joists. The joists send force to their supports: perhaps a ledger at the house and a beam toward the yard. The beam delivers load through posts to footings and then to the ground. The whole path must remain continuous. The weakest connection, not the prettiest lumber, can govern failure.

### The ledger is not a decorative board

The ledger is the board connecting a deck frame to an existing structure. For a ledger-supported deck, it carries reactions from joists into appropriate structural framing of the house. That requires a suitable connection to actual load-bearing material, compatible fasteners, correct spacing, and attention to the house's construction. A ledger screwed into siding or a masonry veneer is not the same as a ledger properly attached to the structural frame behind it. Where a suitable ledger attachment is unavailable, an independently supported design can separate vertical load support from the house, subject to a proper lateral-resistance design.

Exterior connections face a second problem: water. If rain collects behind a ledger, the house rim framing may remain damp long enough to decay. A deck can therefore have fasteners installed in the right pattern yet slowly lose the wood that gives those fasteners purchase. Flashing must route water outward, integrated with the wall's drainage plane rather than treated as a bead of caulk alone. A correct structural detail and a correct water-management detail are two sides of the same joint.

### Gravity is not the only force

Now imagine several people leaning on the outer guardrail. They apply a horizontal force and a moment, not merely vertical weight on the deck surface. The guard posts, their connections, and the deck framing must transfer that loading to a stable support. A guard that feels solid when grasped lightly may fail under a substantial lateral load if its posts are simply screwed into the ends of decking boards or inadequately attached to the framing.

Wind, movement, and the crowd's motion also generate forces that attempt to rack the frame or pull the platform away from its support. Blocking, bracing, hold-down devices, and lateral connections have particular jobs. The visible diagonal brace beneath a deck is not evidence that every required load path exists. Each member and connection must be considered as part of the system.

### Posts, footings, and soil complete the path

Follow the force downward. A beam needs a deliberate bearing or connector arrangement where it meets a post; resting a beam on a slender connection that is poorly detailed can create a failure at the joint. Posts may require bracing against lateral movement. Footings distribute loads into soil of adequate bearing capacity and must be located and sized for site conditions, including frost and proximity to existing foundations. Large concentrated loads such as a hot tub are not equivalent to the uniformly distributed loads used by some prescriptive deck guides.

Outdoor wood differs from dry indoor framing. Moisture content can vary considerably. Wood can swell, shrink, split, or decay; metal hardware can corrode. Preservative-treated lumber and metal connectors must be compatible, and material ratings matter in wet or salt-exposed environments. A connection designed with incompatible metal can lose capacity even when the deck looks superficially intact. Not all stains or dark marks prove decay, but softening, fungal deterioration, or loss of fastener grip are significant observations.

### Inspect through structural questions

Rather than searching for a single 'bad deck' symptom, walk the load path in a non-destructive inspection. Where do the joists bear? Can each support be identified? What do the ledger and its fasteners engage? Can water escape from the wall connection? Are the posts connected to beams and footings in a way that can resist the expected forces? How are guard posts tied into the structure? Is there visible deterioration around the most highly stressed joints?

Consider a deck with new surface boards over old framing. Fresh decking can hide aging joists, a rotting ledger, or inadequate guard connections. Replacing the top boards may improve slip resistance and appearance while doing nothing for the structure. Similarly, adding extra screws to a visibly loose guard may conceal a missing load path. The inspection should identify the force path before prescribing a patch.

The American Wood Council's prescriptive deck guides and the International Code Council's technical materials illustrate legitimate design methods and critical limitations, but their provisions depend on the chosen edition, occupancy, geometry, loading assumptions, and local adoption. An older drawing can teach the mechanism without authorizing its dimensions for a new project. A structural assessment is necessary when a deck falls outside an accepted prescriptive design or shows signs of instability.

The central lesson is not a shopping list of brackets. A safe exterior platform is a network of members, connections, waterproofing details, and soil supports that must all work together. If you can trace gravity loads down to the ground, lateral loads back to stable supports, and rainwater out of the building, you can ask far better questions about both a new design and an aging deck.

## Repairing Rot and Evaluating Structural Wood Damage

An exterior load path must be assessed in the condition that moisture and weather have actually left it.

A screwdriver passes through the edge of a wooden porch post. The temptation is to scrape out the soft part, fill the cavity, paint it, and declare the problem solved. But the screwdriver has found damaged material, not measured the remaining structural capacity. A porch post is not decorative trim. It transfers loads from above into a foundation. The question is whether enough sound material remains, whether the connections still work, and why decay occurred in the first place.

Wood is a biological composite: fibers aligned predominantly along the tree's length, bound together by a matrix that gives the material different strengths in different directions. Its ability to carry tension along the grain differs greatly from its resistance to splitting across the grain. It also gains and loses moisture as surrounding conditions change. When wood stays sufficiently wet for fungi to grow, biological decay can progressively destroy the material responsible for strength. A surface that looks acceptable may hide a softened core; a stained surface may have sound structure below. Color is evidence to investigate, not a capacity rating.

### Map the load path before picking a tool

Consider a floor joist whose end is damaged near a masonry pocket. The middle of the joist may be solid, yet the end carries the reaction from the span into the support. That reaction creates bearing stress near the end and shear demand through the wood. Removing the decayed end and filling it with a nonstructural compound does not restore those forces automatically. A repair must supply an alternate load path and adequate bearing, fasteners, and connection geometry.

A wall stud beneath a window carries different demands from a deck ledger, a sill plate, or a rafter. A sill may be crushed at an anchor or degraded along its concealed bottom face. A joist may be compromised by an old notch or hole near a support. A post can be apparently straight while rotting where it contacts a damp concrete footing. The location and function of the damaged portion matter as much as the visible area.

Before removing structural wood, identify supported loads and temporary support needs. A replacement member cannot simply be slipped into a loaded assembly if that operation removes the support it relies on. Shoring, load transfer, and evaluation of connections are structural design issues. The educational lesson is to recognize those issues, not to guess a universal sistering length or screw pattern.

### Diagnose the moisture pathway

Decay is a symptom of sustained or repeated wetting. A porch post may draw liquid through an end grain cut sitting in standing water. A window sill may remain damp because flashing directs rain behind trim. A joist in a basement may be exposed to humid air, condensation, or water entering through masonry. Eliminating visible decay without correcting water entry creates conditions for the next failure.

Inspection begins with environmental history: weather, roof drainage, splashback, soil contact, indoor humidity, and plumbing. Probe suspicious accessible areas carefully and compare with known sound wood of similar species and orientation. A moisture meter can help map relative conditions, but readings depend on species, depth, temperature, treatments, and instrument type. Record its setting and limitations. Photographs and measured dimensions are more useful than an undocumented statement that the wood is “a little soft.”

Look for insect channels, fungal growth, separated laminations, cracked paint, and failed joints. Not every hole indicates active biological damage. Treatment history and current activity require separate evaluation. Concealed damage may extend beyond what a small exploratory opening reveals, particularly where adjacent materials trap moisture.

### Repair categories are not interchangeable

A surface finish repair can protect otherwise sound wood once moisture has been controlled. A small nonstructural trim replacement primarily restores weather protection or appearance. Structural replacement, reinforcement, or engineered splicing must restore the needed strength, stiffness, connection behavior, and durability. Epoxy consolidants and fillers have legitimate uses in some conservation practices, but their presence does not certify a damaged beam, post, or connection as safe.

Sistering adds a member alongside an existing one. It works only if the new member receives the load and transfers it to adequate supports. A sister that stops short of a bearing point and connects through a handful of arbitrary fasteners may do little for a decayed joist end. Splice repairs similarly depend on the forces being transmitted and on tested or engineered details. Structural repair proposals should identify the load path in a drawing, not just the product name in an estimate.

Exterior durability often depends on details that look small: an end cut that sheds water, a gap permitting drainage, a compatible corrosion-resistant fastener, and a separation between wood and wet masonry. Paint can slow moisture exchange but cannot rescue a joint that traps bulk water behind it. Repair design should let the assembly dry and should leave enough access to inspect it later.

### Verify the outcome

Document the conditions before work, including photographs at supports, connections, and any concealed areas that will be closed. Define a clear acceptance check: moisture source corrected, damaged material extent addressed, load path understood, connection details recorded, and water-shedding finish complete. Inspect after meaningful weather events, not only on the day the new wood is installed.

The point is not that every rotten board requires an engineer. Many nonstructural repairs are straightforward. The important distinction is knowing when wood is only a surface, when it is part of a load-bearing assembly, and when the remedy must change from cosmetic repair to structural restoration. The screwdriver found a problem. The job of diagnosis is to determine which problem it found.

## Deck Anchorage and Long-Term Inspection

An outdoor deck may look solid on a sunny afternoon. The boards are straight, the railing feels reasonably firm, and the paint is fresh. Yet the place where the deck meets the house may have been wetted for years. A connection can lose capacity behind trim long before a person notices movement. The key inspection question is not merely whether the deck looks maintained, but whether every load still has a reliable path into sound structure and stable ground.

### Name the connections carrying the loads

A typical attached deck has decking supported by joists, joists on beams and possibly a house-side ledger, beams on posts, and posts on footings. That description traces gravity loads. People and furniture press downward; each joint must pass that force to the next member and ultimately to the ground or the house structure. Lateral loads require another path. Walking, crowd movement, and guards being pushed can produce sideways forces that the gravity connections alone may not adequately resist.

The ledger deserves special scrutiny because it may carry a large part of the deck's vertical support and can be difficult to inspect. The ledger is not simply a board screwed to siding. A reliable attachment must engage an appropriate structural member of the house through a design matched to the actual wall and floor construction. Decorative cladding, thin sheathing, certain floor-truss details, and deteriorated rim boards cannot be treated as equivalent to a sound solid-sawn band joist. A ledger connection also needs water-control detailing that prevents moisture reaching concealed wood.

The International Code Council and American Wood Council publish guidance on deck ledger connections, with conditions and limits. Their tables are not universally interchangeable. Fastener size, spacing, substrate, joist span, corrosion exposure, and the adopted code matter. Existing decks often predate current provisions or were built from a mixture of product systems. Where the attachment condition cannot be verified, a freestanding or specially engineered support system may be a more defensible solution than guessing new fastener spacing.

### Water can quietly rewrite the load path

Rain enters around unflashed ledgers, fastener holes, doors, or wall penetrations. The exposed decking dries readily, while a trapped pocket behind the ledger may stay wet. Fungal decay weakens wood and often begins where it is least visible. In addition, metal hangers, bolts, and connectors can corrode, especially with treated lumber, salt exposure, or incompatible materials. A connection may simultaneously have decayed wood and corroded steel.

Probe observations can help identify suspect material, but a screwdriver poke is not a structural capacity test. Surface softness, fungal growth, discoloration, water stains, separation, missing hardware, and a persistently wet joint all warrant attention. Conversely, clean paint does not prove the underlying member is sound. A deck's age and exposure history should influence inspection frequency and depth, but age alone does not determine condition.

Consider a ledger that appears straight but has dark stains below its flashing. The stain might reflect historical wetting already repaired, or an active drainage problem. Inspect the flashing continuity, the joint above, and any access from the basement or floor framing. If the concealed support is suspect, evaluation may require controlled opening by a competent professional. Adding caulk to the visible edge without restoring drainage can hide the evidence while trapping water behind it.

### Guardrails and stairs have separate failure modes

A guard post acts like a lever when someone leans against the top rail. The connection at its base must resist a bending moment, not simply vertical weight. A few nails holding decorative trim are not a structural guard connection. Shake-testing a visibly deteriorated rail aggressively can precipitate failure; do not use people as proof loads. Record movement and obtain a suitable inspection.

Stairs add repeated load cycles and slipping or tripping risks. Check stringer connections, tread support, footing settlement, handrail continuity, and whether rise and run remain consistent. Ice and water concentrate around the ground end of exterior stairs. Differential movement between the deck and stair landing can create a subtle but dangerous change in step height. Neither a new coat of stain nor replacing loose treads corrects a displaced support.

An inspection should also separate the deck's built-in elements from later additions. Hot tubs, planters full of saturated soil, roof systems, and privacy walls can impose much larger or differently directed loads than the original design anticipated. A deck that performs adequately under ordinary occupancy may not safely carry a concentrated water-filled vessel. The correct response is load-path evaluation, not relying on the fact that it has stood for years.

### Inspect and record, then decide

A practical inspection starts with a diagram: ledger or freestanding support, joist direction, beams, posts, footings, braces, guards, stairs, and connection points. Photograph each support and any area of water exposure. From safe access, look for split or decayed members, looseness, missing hangers, unsupported joists, corroded fasteners, poor drainage, settlement, and unusual deflection. Determine which parts cannot actually be seen, because unknown does not mean satisfactory.

Make findings actionable. 'Ledger concealed and attachment type cannot be verified' is more useful than 'Deck seems okay.' A significant movement, separated ledger, decayed support, unstable guard, or uncertain critical attachment calls for restricting access and competent structural assessment rather than continued use while debating a cosmetic fix. A sound repair addresses the cause of wetting, restores an engineered connection, and allows the new work to be inspected later.

The lesson of a deck is broader than carpentry: long-term safety depends on invisible connections and on controlling the environment around them. Follow gravity and lateral forces, identify every joint, ask where water goes, and record what is known and what remains concealed. Maintenance succeeds when it preserves the load path, not just the surface finish.

## Repairing Deck Connections Without Concealing Decay

A repair must expose and address the damaged connection, rather than hide the evidence under a stronger-looking surface.

An aging deck can look freshly painted and still be unsafe. The visible boards are only one part of the structure. Beneath them, joists connect to beams; beams bear on posts and foundations; and an attached deck may connect to the house through a ledger. A repair is reliable only if it restores the load path and prevents the source of damage from returning. Screwing another board over a soft connection can hide a failure without solving it.

### Draw every path the forces take

Imagine several people standing along the outside railing. Their weight pushes downward on the decking, then through joists, beams, posts, and footings into the ground. The deck-to-house connection may carry some gravity load through a ledger. Meanwhile, a person leaning on the guard creates a horizontal force and overturning moment. Wind and shifting occupants can add other lateral effects. Connections that transfer downward load are not automatically designed to resist pull-away, racking, or guard forces.

This distinction matters when replacing hardware. A metal joist hanger carries loads through specified nails or screws into a supporting member. Substituting a random short screw because it fits the hole may drastically change its load behavior. A ledger fastener must penetrate the appropriate supporting structure, not merely siding or nonstructural sheathing. Hardware for corrosion exposure must be compatible with the treated wood and environment. The correct part is a combination of geometry, substrate, loads, fasteners, and installation method.

### Decay hides near the hardest connections to see

Water tends to linger where surfaces are horizontal, joints are tight, and drying is slow. On an attached deck, the ledger can be particularly vulnerable. Rainwater may run behind trim or siding, follow a fastener, collect above a flashing defect, or enter damaged sheathing. Joist ends may also stay wet where boards and hangers hold moisture. Paint can hide the surface while the wood beneath has lost mechanical capacity.

Surface color alone is a poor test of strength. Gray weathered wood may be sound; brown wood under intact paint may be deteriorated. Probe suspicious material with an appropriate tool, but remember that a small probing hole cannot certify the entire connection. Look for softness, crumbling fibers, persistent dampness, fungal damage, warped connections, corroded hardware, separated joints, and movement under ordinary use. If signs suggest loss of structural capacity, keep the area out of service until a qualified evaluation determines what support remains.

Examine what is supporting the ledger. A connection to a solid rim joist is not the same as a connection through brick veneer or to a cantilevered floor. Many prescriptive ledger details explicitly exclude certain supporting conditions. Simply copying a bolt spacing pattern from a familiar diagram can be wrong if the house framing is different. An independent freestanding deck structure may be appropriate where attachment cannot be justified, but that alternative also needs a complete load path and foundation design.

### A repair sequence is an evidence sequence

Suppose a deck feels springy and the ledger flashing is stained. Before purchasing lumber, document dimensions, framing orientation, member spans, beam and footing locations, guard connections, ledger conditions, and where water flows. Look inside or remove localized cladding where justified to see the rim or supporting member. Determine which parts are structurally damaged and which are only wet or discolored. The investigation must cover the cause of wetting and the condition of the house as well as the deck.

Temporary support can be needed when a major connection is opened. A deck that appears stable while attached may shift once fasteners are removed. Support and sequencing are structural design questions, not merely convenient carpentry choices. Avoid using a pry bar to learn whether a heavily loaded assembly will stay up. A repair plan identifies what can be safely unloaded, what has to be shored, and which connections must be restored before support is removed.

When replacement is appropriate, aim to remove damaged material back to sound structure rather than cover it. Use the designed members and rated connectors with specified corrosion resistance, fastener type, size, and placement. Restore the water-management layer so water sheds out and away rather than behind the new framing. A water-tight caulk bead alone is rarely the entire flashing strategy. Sealed joints move, and concealed water needs a path to exit.

Now consider the guardrail. It is not an afterthought to attach once the decking is new. Its posts and attachments see large bending demands. A guard post fastened only to rim decking with a few screws may look rigid on a quiet day while lacking a reliable path for horizontal load. The guard connection needs independent attention and inspection according to the applicable deck design.

### Test under the conditions the structure will face

A finished repair should be inspected before connectors and flashing are hidden. Confirm the specified fasteners and placement, wood condition, member bearings, water shed, spacing, and post connections. Record photos, drawings, and material information for future maintenance. Observe whether water drains after rain and whether vulnerable connections can still be inspected. A deck should not pass because it looks level under no load.

A deck is exposed to seasonal wetting and drying. Even a correctly repaired connection may deteriorate if mulch, soil, leaves, or stored objects hold water against the framing. A maintenance plan that clears drainage paths, monitors corrosion, and reassesses attachment conditions preserves the repair's value.

The larger lesson is about repair depth. A good repair reaches the actual structure, restores the forces' route into safe supporting material, and corrects the conditions that caused the damage. Cosmetic concealment is not the same as reconstruction. If the supporting conditions are unknown, that uncertainty is a reason to investigate, not permission to improvise.

## Deck Footings, Post Bases, and the Long-Term Load Path

A deck post can look perfectly straight while the connection at its base has been failing for years. The wood may be soft just above the concrete, the footing may be slowly tilting, or a connection may resist vertical weight while doing little to resist uplift or sideways movement. A deck is not simply a wooden platform. It is a series of load transfers, and the ground is part of that series.

Trace the loads downward. A person stands on deck boards. The boards transfer weight to joists, the joists to beams, the beams to posts or another support, and the posts to footings. The footings distribute force into soil. A lateral force, such as a person leaning into a guardrail, takes a different route. Wind and certain geometric conditions can introduce uplift. Every route needs appropriate connections. A gravity load path that looks solid does not prove that the lateral or uplift paths are complete.

### Footings are interfaces with soil, not just concrete shapes

Concrete resists substantial compression, but that does not tell us whether the soil underneath is competent. A footing spreads load over an area so the ground can support it without unacceptable settlement. If the bearing material varies from one support to another, even similar posts may settle differently. A shallow footing may also move in freezing soils as moisture freezes and expands, depending on local conditions. Frost depth, allowable bearing, drainage, and footing geometry are therefore site-and-code questions, not numbers to guess from the size of a post.

Imagine two supports under a beam. One rests on firm, undisturbed soil. The other sits in loose backfill that was never properly compacted. Under the same load, the second may move farther. The resulting rotation can stress the ledger, joists, guard posts, and beam connections. Shimming a sagging deck at the top conceals the displacement; it does not restore the original support conditions.

Look for symptoms that tell you about movement: posts no longer plumb, beam seats with fresh gaps, cracked piers, tilted bases, deck boards pulling away from the house, and rails that have developed new racking. A level or plumb bob can document geometry, but a single snapshot cannot reveal whether movement is continuing. Dated measurements and photographs are more useful.

### The post base has multiple jobs

Wood in direct contact with standing water, wet soil or poorly drained concrete can stay damp. Even preservative-treated wood is not invulnerable: durability depends on treatment category, exposed cut surfaces, species, moisture, fastener compatibility, and maintenance. A properly selected metal standoff connector can separate the end grain from a persistently wet surface while transferring forces into the footing. But not every standoff is rated for the same uplift, shear, and moment demands.

Do not infer the connector's capacity from its appearance. A thin plate and a handful of screws might be intended only to locate the post, not anchor the structure against all required forces. The post-to-beam joint also has a defined load path. Where beam weight is carried by a bolt passing through the side of a post rather than by adequate direct bearing or an engineered connector, the joint may depend on bolt behavior in a way that is not acceptable for the applicable design. The American Wood Council's residential deck guidance illustrates the distinction between bearing and attachment.

The base of a post is an inspection location. Look for corrosion, movement, split wood, loose anchor nuts, missing washers, and trapped debris. Probe suspect wood only by methods appropriate to the material; a screwdriver gouge is not an engineering strength test. Corrosion of metal connectors may be accelerated by local moisture and by material incompatibility. Match fastener coatings and connector materials to the actual preservative treatment and exposure specifications.

### Water management is structural maintenance

Picture a deck beside a house where the downspout empties beside one post. Each storm saturates the surrounding soil. Winter may subject the region to repeated freeze-thaw movement, while summer leaves damp material at the base. Over several seasons the post can show degradation and the footing can change position. A repair that installs a new post without fixing drainage returns the new member to the old environment.

The ledger is another critical water transition. Flashing has to steer water over the appropriate exterior surfaces rather than behind the ledger into the wall. But flashing alone does not supply structural attachment, and a row of bolts alone does not supply drainage. The two systems overlap in physical space while doing different jobs. When a deck is freestanding, the framing, footing and lateral bracing requirements differ; the absence of a ledger removes one problem and creates other load paths to design.

### A careful inspection has boundaries

Start with visual documentation from above and below, if accessible. Sketch the beams, posts, footings, ledger, braces, guard posts and visible connectors. Record which components bear directly on supports. Photograph every suspected water trap, split and corroded metal contact. Check posts for visible displacement relative to a fixed reference. Investigate drainage around the foundations. Do not put people on a deck with signs of severe deterioration or inadequate support in order to conduct a loaded test.

Now distinguish maintenance from redesign. Clearing debris and improving exterior drainage may be maintenance. Replacing a connector, reinforcing a guard post, altering a footing, or restoring a deteriorated ledger can be structural work. Existing code provisions, soil conditions, proprietary connector tables, and the deck's actual geometry determine the required detail. Simply adding screws until the assembly feels stiff is not a substitute for a complete load-path design.

A good deck inspection ends with a map of how gravity, lateral loads, and moisture travel through the structure. The deck can look fine at its surface while the critical weak point sits near the dirt. Learn to follow the forces all the way into the ground, and the inspection becomes much more than a search for rotten boards.

## Companions, sources, and study material (not spoken)

### Decks, Porches, and Exterior Load Paths

**Track:** Carpentry and Construction. **Background:** framing load paths, connectors, wood movement, and water control. **Purpose:** understand exterior platforms as structures that must carry gravity and lateral loads while remaining durable in wet service.

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

##### Two load-path sketches to prepare

1. **Gravity:** decking → joists → ledger/beam → house framing or posts → footings → soil.
2. **Lateral/guard:** concentrated push at rail → guard post → engineered post connection and blocking → deck frame → stable resisting structure. Include house separation and deck racking where applicable.

##### Inspection worksheet

- Record deck dimensions and construction type; count supports; identify ledger versus non-ledger vertical support.
- Note water-entry details at ledger, penetrations, and openings; inspect from below where safely accessible.
- Identify compatible fasteners/hardware; look for corrosion, decay, split ends, looseness, displacement, and inadequate bearing.
- Distinguish a post anchored to a footing from a post simply bearing on a surface; do not infer capacity from appearance alone.

##### Scope warning

The AWC DCA 6 guide is a prescriptive system with limited scope (commonly single-level residential wood decks under specified loads and construction). It is **not** a universal structural calculator. Local codes, editions, snow loads, lateral-force requirements, materials, and existing framing conditions require separate verification. An unstable deck should not be used for an informal load test.

### Deck Anchorage and Long-Term Inspection

### 017000 — Deck Anchorage and Long-Term Inspection

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Load path:** decking → joists → beams and/or approved ledger → posts/footings and verified house framing. Separate lateral loads (bracing, hold-downs, guard posts, attachment) from gravity loads. A decorative screw or railing nail does not establish structural resistance.

**Inspection checklist:** ledger accessibility and substrate; continuous flashing and water exit; hangers/fasteners and compatible corrosion protection; beam/post joints; footing settlement and uplift; guard attachment; stair stringers/landings; altered loads; evidence of rot or insect damage; uninspectable areas.

**Do not infer compliance:** Generic prescriptive connection tables have assumptions about wood species, joist span, rim-board construction, design load, code edition, and protection. Local adoption and the actual construction control what details apply.

**Escalation:** Displaced ledger, unstable guard, major decay, missing primary supports, severe corrosion, and undocumented hot-tub or roof loads warrant restricting access and structural evaluation. Photograph and document before remedial covering.

### Repairing Deck Connections Without Concealing Decay

### 018000 — Repairing Deck Connections Without Concealing Decay

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Load-path drawing.** Use three different arrows for gravity loads, lateral deck loads, and guard-post forces. Trace each arrow into a specifically identified supporting element, not an abstract 'house.'

**Condition inventory.** Photos of deck underside, ledger, flashing, joist hangers, posts, guard connections and footings. Record soft wood, fastener corrosion, staining, water exit path, and evidence of previous repairs.

**Repair gate.** Do not substitute paint, surface filler or additional deck screws for structurally unsound wood. Repairs that remove load-bearing material require evaluated temporary support and an engineered/prescriptive design applicable to actual substrate. Do not assume published ledger tables apply to brick veneer, hollow masonry, cantilevers, or unknown rim conditions.

**Acceptance.** Photograph flashing and connectors before concealment. Compare fastener schedules, corrosion compatibility, structural bearings, and guard connection with the approved design. Record any unresolved concealed conditions.

### 019000 — Deck Footings, Post Bases, and the Long-Term Load Path

#### Technical companion (non-spoken)
