# 006000 — Nails, Screws, Connectors, and Load Transfer

**Track:** Carpentry. **Background:** Wall, floor and roof load paths. **Purpose:** understand how framed structures transmit loads across joints.

# A load path is made from joints

Imagine a roof holding snow. The rafters or trusses carry the weight toward supports; walls carry it toward floors or foundations; foundations pass it into the ground. We call this a load path, but the phrase can conceal the real engineering question. At every boundary between pieces of material, how does the force cross the joint?

A beam can be strong enough while its connection is too weak. A sheathed wall can resist a large lateral force while the floor-to-wall connection is poorly detailed. A roof truss can be carefully engineered while a missing hanger fastener leaves the intended support incomplete. So this lecture is about joints: not only what holds wood in place during construction, but what carries force after the structure is loaded.

## Different loads ask different things of a connection

Gravity tends to load framing downward. Wind can push walls inward or outward, shear a wall sideways, and create uplift that tries to detach the roof from its supports. Earthquake loading reverses directions during a sequence of movements. Connections intended for one of these cases are not automatically suitable for the others.

A simple joist bearing on a beam transfers downward load through contact: wood presses on wood. A joist hanger creates a different path, usually from the joist into its fasteners, through the hanger metal, into more fasteners, and then into the support. Every part of that chain matters. If the specified nails are absent from the hanger's side holes, the installed connection may be a different device from the one whose capacity was published.

Likewise, a roof-to-wall tie intended to resist uplift must actually connect the structural members on both sides. Fastening a strap to sheathing alone may not provide the expected connection to the underlying framing. A connector can be mechanically sound but attached to a member that is itself inadequately supported. The load-path sketch should show where the force comes from, the connection, and where it goes next.

## Why nails remain important

People often assume screws are stronger because they are threaded and hard to pull out. Withdrawal resistance is one property; lateral load resistance and ductility are others. Certain structural nails can bend and redistribute force across a connection rather than fracture abruptly. Some common brittle screws may fail when forced to carry cyclic shear. Structural screws exist, but their approved uses depend on the actual product and installation—not merely on having threads.

Nail dimensions are not a minor naming detail. Diameter, length, head, shank, coating, and whether the fastener is fully driven influence the result. A clipped-head fastener may not substitute for a full-head nail where the head contributes to resistance. A pneumatic nailer can make work fast, yet an underdriven or overdriven nail can damage the intended connection. The number and placement of nails also determine how load is shared and whether the wood splits.

Think of a plywood wall panel used to resist sideways load. Its edges are attached to framing by many fasteners. Each fastener transmits force between panel and frame, and the collective pattern helps the assembly act as a shear wall. If some fasteners miss the framing or their heads tear through damaged panel edges, the total behavior changes. Extra nails placed too tightly together can split the framing or weaken the panel edge. Quantity without spacing discipline is not quality.

## Metal connectors are engineered systems

A metal connector can redirect force around a geometrically difficult joint, but its strength is not a property of the steel bracket alone. The manufacturer's capacity is tied to the connection as installed: substrate, specified fasteners, fastening pattern, orientation, corrosion environment, and the load direction. A galvanized hanger may have particular corrosion limitations in treated wood or wet exposure, so durability is also part of the design.

Consider a deck ledger. A line of bolts or approved screws is visible, but the joint connects an exterior structure to a house that may contain sheathing, flashing, rim joists, and finishes. If those materials are not what the fastener assumes, the load transfer may be wrong. The same detail needs control of water intrusion: a structurally adequate joint can still rot the load-bearing member through poor flashing. Structural design and moisture management do not occur in separate universes.

Reinforcement should be understood through the same model. Adding a metal plate to the face of a cracked member may not restore a lost load path unless the plate is sized and its fasteners are designed to transfer the relevant force. An obvious bolt pattern may be under-strength for a concentrated load while making the wood more vulnerable to splitting.

## Reading the marks of a stressed joint

A sound joint may show slight dimensional change as wood moisture varies. But gaps that widen under load, crushed fibers at bearing points, elongated holes, torn-out fasteners, and diagonal cracks can indicate a connection problem. A sloping floor can arise from member deflection, support settlement, or connection movement. Looking at the far end of a member may be as important as looking at the middle.

A useful inspection proceeds from force to evidence. Identify the member carrying the load. Trace its route to a support. At every change of material or direction, locate the joint and ask which component transfers the force. Look for evidence of the intended fastener and any movement or deterioration. Draw the result before deciding what replacement hardware might be appropriate.

This is why a carpentry education should not reduce to memorizing cuts and nail patterns. Those patterns are important, but their meaning comes from mechanics. A building frame is a network of members whose connections allow loads to move continuously into stable supports. The more clearly you see that network, the better you can understand both sound construction and a developing failure.

## Selected reading and evidence records (non-spoken)

- `rammer-fastenings` — [Douglas R. Rammer, Fastenings, Wood Handbook ch. 8, USDA Forest Service Forest Products Laboratory (2010)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/37424). Wood connections, nails, screws, lateral and withdrawal capacity, joist hangers.
- `pnnl-loadpath` — [PNNL Building America Solution Center, Continuous Load Path from Roof Through Wall to Foundation](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/continuous-load-path-provided-connections-roof-through-wall-foundation). Gravity and lateral loads, openings, headers and connectors; cites FEMA P-804.
- `awc-wfcm` — [American Wood Council, 2024 Wood Frame Construction Manual (WFCM)](https://awc.org/resources/2024-wfcm/). Committee-developed ANSI standard covers connections, floors, walls, and roofs in limited one- and two-family dwelling designs; scope and local adoption must be checked.
