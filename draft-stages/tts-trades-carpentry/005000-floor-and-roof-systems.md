# Why floors feel solid and some floors bounce

Walk across two wooden floors. One feels rigid. The other moves slightly with each step. Neither has necessarily reached a breaking load, but the second may already be unsuitable for its intended finish or use. To understand this distinction, imagine a joist as a beam supported at two ends. The beam has to resist bending stress, but it also has to limit deflection, the distance it bends under load. Strength and stiffness are related, yet they are not the same requirement.

A residential floor is not just a row of pieces of wood. It is a load-sharing assembly made of joists or engineered members, subfloor sheathing, connections, beams, walls, columns, and foundations. A roof has a related but different set of load cases: its own weight, snow, wind pressure and suction, plus the geometry that carries these forces into the building below.

## Load goes somewhere

Imagine a heavy bookcase standing midway across a room. Its weight first bears on the finished floor, then the subfloor, then the framing members that support it. A joist carries load toward its bearings. A beam below it collects reactions from several joists and sends them toward posts or walls. Eventually the foundation distributes the load to soil. If any connection in that path is absent, weak, or incorrectly configured, the problem may occur far from the furniture.

The subfloor helps distribute concentrated loads and braces framing members, but it does not make joists interchangeable. A floor framed with close-spaced modest joists may behave differently from one with wider-spaced deeper members. The member's span, stiffness, species or engineered design, grade, spacing, and how loads are shared all matter. A joist bearing on a plate must have adequate support. A hanger has to transfer the reaction through appropriate fasteners and compatible hardware. Decorative trim does not create a structural load path.

We can gain intuition from a simplified beam relationship. For a simply supported beam with a central point load, bending deflection grows roughly with the cube of span when all other properties are held fixed. Increase span substantially and the beam gets noticeably more flexible. Member depth is powerful because bending stiffness depends strongly on cross-sectional shape. This is why a deeper joist can outperform a wider but shallower member of similar material. However, adding depth alone does not validate a real floor; shear, bending strength, bearing, connections, lateral stability, vibration, and code-prescribed loads still need checking.

## Why span tables have so many columns

A span table is not just a list of how far each piece of lumber can reach. It embodies assumptions about grade, species, spacing, load, and deflection limit. The American Wood Council explains these assumptions in its span-table tutorials. A living-room floor might be checked under one live-load case and a bedroom under another; a ceiling with no storage is a different case again. Model-code examples illustrate common assumptions, but adopted requirements and actual building uses determine the design inputs.

Deflection limits are often expressed as a fraction of span. An L over 360 criterion, for example, would give a one-third-inch deflection limit on a ten-foot span under the load case to which the criterion applies. That is a serviceability criterion, not a declaration that the member becomes unsafe at the next tiny increment. A floor can meet a specified static limit and still feel lively because vibration depends on frequency, mass, damping, and how occupants excite the structure.

## Roofs have gravity and uplift

At roof level, snow and self-weight push downward. Wind can push inward on one face while lifting away from another. Rafters carry distributed load to their bearings, but also need restraint against lateral movement and connections that resist uplift. A collar tie, rafter tie, ceiling joist, ridge board, and structural ridge beam do different jobs. Treating these pieces as interchangeable can change whether the assembly resists outward wall thrust.

Think about an ordinary pair of rafters leaning together, with their feet on opposite exterior walls. Under downward roof load, the geometry can create outward forces at those feet unless the framing provides an appropriate tie or a ridge support system that carries the loads differently. A ridge board used for alignment is not automatically a structural beam capable of carrying the roof reaction. The correct configuration follows from the whole roof system.

Trusses are another engineered solution. Their slender webs, chords and connection plates work together to form a system designed for specific loads. Cutting a web to create storage space can destroy its intended force path. The fact that a piece is thin does not mean it is expendable.

## Openings, holes, and surprises

Floors often need ducts, plumbing, stair openings, and mechanical penetrations. Framing around an opening introduces headers, trimmers, hangers, and new reaction paths. Boring holes and making notches in solid sawn joists have constraints because they remove material from regions where forces differ. Engineered I-joists and trusses have manufacturer-specific rules; a practice acceptable in solid wood may be destructive to an engineered member.

When investigating a bouncy floor, look beyond the one joist you can see. Ask whether the span has changed because a support was removed, whether bearings have shifted, whether a connection has loosened, or whether wetting and decay have reduced material performance. A cracked finish might reveal differential movement rather than a defective finish product.

The point of structural reasoning is to read the building as a network of reactions. Every load needs a path to the ground. Every path needs adequate strength, stiffness, connections and stability. Carpentry becomes more systematic when each board is understood not only as material to cut, but as part of that network.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Beam reasoning, not a sizing prescription

For a simply supported, constant-section beam with center point load `P`, span `L`, modulus `E`, and second moment `I`, the idealized midspan deflection is `delta = P L^3/(48 E I)`. For a rectangle with width `b` and depth `h`, `I = b h^3/12`. Assumptions exclude composite action, bearing deformation, creep, vibration, and distributed/multiple loading. Actual design requires strength, shear, serviceability, stability, bearing, connections, and applicable load combinations.

A 120-inch span with `L/360` has a corresponding displacement criterion of `120/360 = 0.333 inch`; this criterion is load-case-specific and not a universal pass/fail test for safety.

### Diagnostic exercise

On a floor plan, mark joist direction, spacing, clear span, bearings, beams, columns, and assumed dead/live load. Follow one reaction through the foundation. On a roof sketch, distinguish a ridge board with ties from a load-carrying ridge beam, and trace wind uplift connections. Verify adopted code, engineered design and manufacturer restrictions before cutting, notching, drilling or modifying load-bearing framing.
