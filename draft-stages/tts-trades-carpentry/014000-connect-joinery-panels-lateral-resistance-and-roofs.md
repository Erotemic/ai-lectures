# Connect joinery, panels, lateral resistance, and roofs

## Wood Joinery, Glue, and Mechanical Load Transfer

A joint can look as if two pieces of wood have become one, yet break apart when someone leans on it. Another joint, with exposed screws and an obvious seam, may carry load for years. The difference is not elegance. It is how force travels from one member through the connection into another, and whether that force stays within the capacities of wood, adhesive, and fastener over time.

Consider a shelf joined to the side of a cabinet. The shelf bends under books. At the joint, bending creates a tendency for the upper and lower portions of the connection to pull and push in opposite directions. A screw alone may resist this through its threads and bearing against the wood. A dado, a groove that supports the shelf edge, transfers part of the vertical load directly through wood bearing. Glue may spread forces across a much larger area. The same nominal joint can behave differently depending on its geometry.

### Wood is directional

Wood is not a homogeneous plastic block. Its fibers run primarily along the grain, and its strength and dimensional movement vary greatly by direction. Pulling parallel to straight grain differs from pulling perpendicular to the grain. End grain, side grain, and face grain give an adhesive different structures to wet and penetrate. A narrow screw near an edge can split wood along the grain even if the metal screw itself is far from failing.

Water content matters. Wood expands and contracts across the grain as moisture changes; lengthwise movement is much smaller for ordinary lumber. A wide solid-wood tabletop fastened rigidly across its width to a stationary base can split or distort seasonally. Good joinery can restrain the part where needed and allow controlled movement elsewhere. A floating panel, slotted fastener hole, or suitable tabletop connector has a purpose beyond convenience.

### Bearing, shear, pullout, and splitting

When evaluating a joint, trace the path of force. A vertical load may press one piece onto another, producing compression or bearing. It can slide two faces relative to each other, creating shear. A prying force may pull screws out of the wood; the same screws may carry much more effectively when loaded in a direction that avoids withdrawal. At the edge of a board, load can create a split because little intact material remains around the connection.

Imagine fastening a heavy wall shelf to a cabinet side. Screws driven through the side into the shelf's end may provide initial alignment, but thread holding and splitting resistance depend on species, density, pilot holes, screw dimensions, spacing, and grain direction. A cleat beneath the shelf adds a direct bearing path. A dado helps with location and load distribution. An installed bracket can transfer load with a different moment arm. None of these details can be judged solely by how tightly the screw feels when driven.

A load path continues beyond the visible joint. A wall cabinet may have strong shelf joints yet fail because the mounting screws miss the structural backing or the cabinet back cannot transfer weight to the mount. Test the entire assembly conceptually: books to shelf, shelf to case, case to mount, mount to framing, framing to building.

### What glue really does

Woodworking adhesive creates a bond when it wets compatible surfaces and develops its designed structure during cure. Strength depends on joint fit, surface preparation, moisture, temperature, adequate assembly pressure, and the adhesive's intended service environment. More squeeze-out is not proof of a stronger bond. Too little adhesive leaves discontinuities; excessive force can deprive a joint of an appropriate bond line, depending on the formulation and wood.

Adhesives also have different properties. Some are best suited to interior, normally dry furniture. Others are formulated for more moisture exposure. Manufacturers specify open time, assembly time, clamp time, temperature ranges, and full cure. A label saying 'water resistant' does not mean a joint can be left underwater indefinitely. An adhesive that bonds wood satisfactorily may be wrong for an oily tropical hardwood or a porous, contaminated repair surface.

End-grain joints deserve special attention. A plain butt joint connecting the ends of two boards offers a small or mechanically unfavorable bonding surface under bending. A scarf, lap, finger, or other geometry can provide more effective area and a different force path. Joint geometry often improves reliability more than selecting a more expensive bottle of glue.

### Accuracy and tolerance

A tight-looking joint is not automatically a well-designed joint. A mortise-and-tenon fit that must be hammered together may split the mortised member or prevent the adhesive from distributing properly. A very loose fit may leave an uncontrolled bond line or poor mechanical support. The correct fit depends on the joint and adhesive. Measure the actual tooling and material; do not assume nominal lumber dimensions or a bit marked with a number will produce precisely that slot.

Assembly order matters too. Dry-fit before applying glue. Check diagonals for square, confirm the reference faces, and arrange clamps so pressure closes the intended joint rather than twisting the whole carcass. Clamps are temporary machines that apply forces, not magic instruments that make inaccurate parts fit. A cabinet can cure permanently out of square while every clamp is tight.

### When screws, nails, and joints cooperate

Mechanical fasteners can locate parts while adhesive cures, provide a direct load path, or permit disassembly. They can also inhibit seasonal movement if applied without thought. A nail often tolerates some joint movement differently from a brittle adhesive line; a screw offers serviceability but can concentrate stress around its hole. In structural applications, approved connectors and specified fastener schedules are design elements, not suggestions replaceable by an attractive piece of furniture joinery.

For a small cabinet, test fit, measure diagonals, verify shelf support, and check whether the piece can be repaired in the future. For an occupied deck, stair, beam, or other load-bearing construction, the stakes are different: the connection must be evaluated under the loads and code provisions applicable to that system. A joinery demonstration is not a structural capacity certification.

The lasting lesson is to trace forces rather than merely recognize joint names. A mortise-and-tenon, dado, lap, dovetail, biscuit, screw, or adhesive all change the available path for load and movement. The best joint is the one whose geometry and materials match the forces, environment, accuracy, and service life of the actual object.

## Engineered Wood Panels and Moisture-Sensitive Connections

A connection’s behavior also depends on the panel or engineered material into which it transfers load.

A sheet of plywood, a sheet of oriented strand board, and a solid wood plank can all look like flat pieces of wood. But their internal structure is different enough to change how they bend, hold fasteners, and respond to water. Engineered wood solves some of the weaknesses of natural lumber and introduces new design requirements. The practical question is not simply which sheet is stronger. It is **what load the product carries, in what direction, through which connections, and under what moisture conditions?**

### Why arrange wood instead of using one board?

Solid wood is anisotropic: its stiffness, strength, and movement depend strongly on grain direction. A wide board shrinks substantially across its grain as it dries, while its length changes far less. Knots and natural growth features add local variability. Engineers exploit those properties by selecting, orienting, and bonding wood pieces into products with controlled dimensions and more predictable behavior.

Plywood contains thin veneers, often with adjacent layers' grain directions approximately perpendicular. That cross-lamination reduces the difference between lengthwise and crosswise dimensional behavior compared with a single board. It also allows a large, relatively stable sheet to distribute load over framing members. But plywood is not a perfectly isotropic metal plate. Face-grain direction, ply count, thickness, grade, defects, and support spacing still affect bending stiffness and capacity.

Oriented strand board, or OSB, contains wood strands arranged and bonded in layers, with a majority oriented along preferred axes. Its performance depends on grade, adhesive system, density, and exposure classification. A sheet marketed for sheathing is not automatically interchangeable with another sheet simply because both are three-quarters of an inch thick. The performance rating, span designation, edge support, fastener schedule, and installation instructions are part of the structural system.

Engineered structural members carry the idea further. Laminated veneer lumber builds thicker members from bonded veneers; glued-laminated timber builds beams from selected laminations; an I-joist combines thin flanges with a web to put more material where bending stresses are greatest. Each product has design data and restrictions. An I-joist's flange is not spare wood that can be shaved down to clear a pipe. Cutting or notching it can interrupt a primary load path, while allowed web openings depend on exact manufacturer rules.

### Strength is not stiffness

Suppose a panel spans between supports and holds a person walking on it. It may have ample ultimate bending strength but still flex enough to crack tile or create a springy floor. Bending capacity asks when a material or connection would fail. Deflection asks how far it moves under service load. For a simple supported beam, deflection is strongly affected by span length, and often rises much faster than load when the span increases. Two panels that both satisfy a load rating under one support spacing may feel quite different under another.

Panel thickness and orientation matter, but so do continuity and support. A tongue-and-groove edge or a blocked panel edge helps adjacent sheets transfer or share forces. Fastener spacing and type influence whether a panel can act as intended on the framing. Missing support, a misdriven fastener, or a gap at the wrong location may change the observed behavior more than a small nominal thickness difference.

Imagine replacing a small subfloor patch. The visible defect is only a hole, but the repair needs to find solid framing or add engineered support where approved, maintain edge continuity, and restore the connection pattern. A patch screwed only into the damaged sheet edges may appear flush while failing to transmit forces to joists. The repair must reconstruct the load path, not merely hide the opening.

### Water does not affect every wood product in the same way

Moisture moves into wood and changes its dimensions. With enough prolonged wetting, vulnerable products can swell, especially at exposed edges. Some composites have more pronounced thickness swelling than comparable plywood in certain exposure conditions. Repeated wetting and drying can damage finishes, adhesive bonds, or adjacent assemblies. Moisture also promotes biological decay where the conditions support it. The fact that a panel survives a rainstorm during construction is not the same as saying it is designed for continuous wet service.

This is where labels cause confusion. A panel's 'exposure' or bond classification describes a defined glue-bond or application context, not complete waterproofing. Exterior-rated products still require correct detailing, drainage, drying opportunities, and protection of vulnerable edges. Similarly, a coat of paint can slow wetting but will not fix a leaking window or a trapped-water joint.

Connections are especially sensitive. A damp panel may swell around a fastener while the framing shrinks or moves differently; the joint can loosen, squeak, or split. Corrosion can reduce fastener capacity in persistent moisture. Adhesive bonding may depend on surface preparation, wood moisture, cure conditions, and compatible product selection. Treat the sheet, framing, adhesive, and fasteners as one assembly.

### Follow the installation rules, not the silhouette

Look at the stamp and product data before assigning a panel a structural job. Record thickness, grade, intended application, span rating, exposure classification, and the required fastener type and pattern. Determine the intended strength axis relative to the support layout. For beam or I-joist products, keep the manufacturer's hole, bearing, blocking, and fastening details with the project notes. A part made of engineered wood is still a designed structural component.

A quality inspection should ask more than whether the floor is flat. Are panel edges appropriately supported? Are the sheets installed with the specified gaps for expansion? Are damaged edges or wet panels concealed by finish? Were fasteners placed in sound material without crushing, overdriving, or missing the supporting member? Does the assembly have a route to dry if incidental moisture reaches it?

The larger lesson is that engineered wood exchanges the natural variability of a tree for manufactured geometry and documented performance. That is valuable, but only if the installation respects the assumptions behind that performance. The right product in the wrong orientation or a wet connection can undermine an otherwise careful design.

## Shear Walls, Diaphragms, and Lateral Resistance

A house must support its own weight, the people and furniture inside, and the snow that lands on its roof. But it must also survive forces that try to push it sideways. A long wall with studs and drywall looks solid, yet its ability to resist wind or earthquake loads depends on connections that may be almost invisible after construction. Understanding a lateral load path changes how we think about windows, bracing, sheathing, and even apparently small remodeling cuts.

### Gravity takes one path; side loads take another

A gravity load usually travels downward: roof framing to supporting walls or beams, through floors, and ultimately to the foundation and soil. Lateral forces can enter across the roof and exterior walls. A roof or floor diaphragm collects those forces and transfers them to designated walls or frames. A shear wall resists in its own plane and carries the load to the foundation. Connections transfer forces across each interface. If any link is weak or missing, the strength of the remaining components may be of little help.

Think of a cardboard box with its flaps open. It can rack into a parallelogram under a sideways push. Close and secure the faces, and the box becomes much harder to distort. Structural sheathing can perform a similar bracing role, but the actual mechanism is not merely that the panel is stiff. Sheathing, fasteners, framing, boundary members, and anchorage must act as one assembly. A strong plywood sheet poorly fastened to the studs does not become a strong shear wall by itself.

The distinction between a *diaphragm* and a *shear wall* is largely about orientation and load collection. Roof and floor planes act like horizontal collectors and distributors; wall planes often resist the transferred load vertically. Their edges need continuity through blocking, framing, and connections. Where forces enter and where they exit matter as much as panel thickness. A floor can be well built for vertical loads and still have a weak lateral connection to the story below.

### Shear and overturning happen together

Push the top of a tall, narrow panel sideways while holding its base. It wants not only to slide but also to rotate. The edge being pushed down develops compression; the opposite edge tends to lift. Real shear walls therefore need connections that resist sliding along the base and uplift at boundaries where design calls for it. These may include sill anchorage and engineered hold-down devices. The precise required details follow the design loads, wall length, height, geometry, and governing standards; their capacities cannot be inferred from the presence of a few visible bolts.

Openings interrupt potential wall length. A door or window changes which segments can act as shear walls and how forces travel around those openings. That is one reason a proposed broad patio-door opening is not merely a matter of finding a header large enough to hold the roof. A header solves a gravity-load problem. It may not replace the lateral resistance lost by removing an adequately braced wall segment. New posts, shear-wall segments, frames, collectors, and foundations can all enter the design.

Lateral loading is also direction-dependent. A wall that is effective against force parallel to its face may offer much less resistance in the perpendicular direction without support from intersecting walls or diaphragms. A home must resist forces from different directions, and the load path varies with the direction. A long open-plan wall line may need a different system from a plan with many continuous interior partitions.

### Connections are part of the material

Fastener type, length, spacing, edge distance, penetration, and whether the sheathing edge is supported can change a panel assembly's performance. Substituting convenient screws for specified nails, omitting blocking, or moving an anchor because it interferes with plumbing can alter the intended load path. A nail that transfers cyclic lateral shear is not interchangeable with a brittle fastener chosen for high withdrawal strength. Follow the structural detail rather than a general intuition that more metal means more strength.

Construction sequencing matters. Before permanent sheathing and bracing are complete, a wall may need temporary support. And a cut made decades later to route a duct or create an opening can interrupt a collector, chord, boundary stud, or anchorage even if the wall still appears visually intact. The fact that a house has stood for many years does not prove that every available load path is adequate for its current configuration.

### Inspect the path rather than the surface

When reviewing a framed building, start with a sketch of roof and floor planes. Mark which walls appear continuous and which have large openings. Follow the expected horizontal transfer from those planes into wall segments, then down to the foundation. At each transition ask: what carries shear, what resists uplift or overturning, and how is the connection made? Photos taken before walls are closed are far more useful than later guesses about hidden clips and nails.

A structural drawing may designate braced wall lines, wall segments, hold-downs, collectors, and panel nailing schedules. The job of the inspector or builder is to verify the installed details against those plans, not to make up a substitute on site. Building codes and engineered designs have specific limits on prescriptive wall layouts, opening ratios, and connection schedules. Those are design inputs, not generic rules that should be recited without context.

This principle extends beyond wood houses. Steel braced frames, masonry shear walls, and concrete cores provide other ways to resist horizontal loads, but each still depends on continuity and connections. The deeper lesson is simple: lateral strength is a property of a *system*. You cannot understand it by inspecting a single stud, panel, or bolt in isolation. Trace the full route from the force entering the building to the ground receiving it.

## Roof Trusses, Load Paths, and the Limits of Field Modifications

Roof systems provide a clear case of why continuity cannot be inferred from the apparent strength of one member.

You want to store holiday boxes in an attic, or run a new duct across it. A diagonal piece of lumber lies in the way. It is tempting to imagine it as a brace that can be shifted. But in a prefabricated roof truss, that diagonal may be carrying a precisely calculated part of the roof load. Moving it can change the forces throughout the entire assembly.

A truss is a structural arrangement of connected members, often approximated in analysis as a set of triangles. Its members carry combinations of tension and compression; its joints transfer the forces. A truss is not an ordinary rafter system with extra scrap lumber inside. Many residential roof trusses use metal connector plates embedded into the wood at joints, engineered for a particular design, span, load and bracing scheme.

### Why a triangle changes the problem

Consider a simple triangular frame supported at two ends. The roof pushes downward, and the members channel that force through the joints to the supports. The lower chord may be in tension, resisting the horizontal separation of the supports, while top chords and web members carry other combinations of forces depending on the truss geometry and loading. The exact force in any member depends on the full arrangement; it cannot reliably be read from the visual thickness of one stick of wood.

A rectangular frame can change shape without its members changing length if joints are free to rotate. A triangle cannot change shape in the same way without a member changing length. That geometric constraint explains much of a truss's efficiency. It also explains the hazard of treating a web as removable. With one member absent, the original triangular force path may disappear and other members may see bending that they were not designed to carry.

Imagine two roof trusses next to each other. One has a small platform laid across the bottom chords for storage. The other has an HVAC unit suspended from a chord. Both changes add load, but in different locations. The total roof might appear strong, yet the original design may not have included either the extra permanent load or the concentrated point load. The truss manufacturer's design drawings specify assumed loading, bearing locations, member forces, connector details and required bracing. Without them, visual inspection does not establish spare capacity.

### Temporary and permanent stability

Trusses are especially vulnerable during handling and installation. Before they are sheathed and braced, they can be unstable out of plane. A long compression member may buckle sideways even when the member is strong in axial compression. Temporary restraint keeps an assembly upright during erection; permanent lateral restraint and bracing ensure the finished system resists out-of-plane instability during service.

The roof sheathing often participates as a diaphragm distributing lateral forces, but the actual connections determine whether that load path exists. Bracing that looks adequate because it touches a truss is not automatically an engineered restraint. A brace must reach a stable anchorage, and its connection must transfer the design forces.

This leads to a useful distinction between a member's cross-sectional strength and the stability of the complete structure. A slender compression member can fail by buckling at a force below the compressive capacity of its material. A plate connection may fail before the wood member. A bottom chord can deflect excessively even when it does not break. The governing failure mode can be a joint, a member, a brace or the bearing itself.

### The remodeling question

Let's say a new ventilation duct needs to pass through a space blocked by a web. There are at least three design choices: route the duct around the obstruction, select a different duct configuration, or obtain an engineered modification to the truss. Cutting or drilling the web first, then asking how to reinforce it, reverses the process. An engineered repair may involve supplemental members, plates, bolts, fasteners and a specified installation sequence; it is not adequately replaced by a piece of plywood nailed across the cut.

What if someone already cut a web? The absence of visible roof sag does not prove the assembly is sound. A truss can retain enough apparent stiffness under today's loads while lacking a required margin for snow, wind or long-term service. Document the damage, avoid adding loads, obtain the original truss information if possible, and have an appropriate design professional or truss engineer specify repair details before altering it further.

The same applies to an attic floor. Some bottom chords serve as ceiling joists for light interior finishes, not storage-floor members. The word 'floor' on a piece of plywood does not confer the design capacity of a habitable floor.

### Read the structure as a network

For inspection, follow forces from the roof covering into sheathing, into truss members, through the heel or other bearings, down walls or beams, and finally to the foundation. Look for displaced plates, cracked lumber, unapproved cuts, missing bracing, altered bearings, water exposure and connections that no longer match their design intent. These observations justify further examination; they are not a substitute for structural calculations.

A roof truss succeeds because its members and joints work together. The correct question about a proposed field change is not, 'Will this piece still look strong afterward?' It is, 'Where will the load travel after the change, and has the complete new path been designed and checked?'

## Roof Sheathing, Uplift Connections, and Wind Load Transfer

A roof can lose shingles in a storm and still protect the rooms below. A roof that loses its wood sheathing has a much more serious problem. The distinction is structural: roofing materials shed weather, while sheathing and its connections help make a roof into a stiff, load-transferring assembly. Wind does not attack only the shingles. It creates pressures on the whole roof, including upward forces that have to find a continuous path into the building.

We tend to picture roof loading as weight pressing downward: shingles, snow, workers and the roof itself. Wind adds a different direction. As air flows around the building, the pressure above parts of the roof can be lower than the pressure below. At corners, edges and other regions, local suction can become especially strong. The direction of the net force on roof sheathing may then be outward and upward. In a severe event, the capacity of connections can matter as much as the strength of the sheathing panel.

### The roof is a layered structural system

Start at the outer layer. Roofing underlayment and shingles provide weather protection, but they are not intended to substitute for the roof deck's structural attachment. Beneath them lie sheathing panels, often plywood or oriented strand board, fastened to rafters or trusses. The framing transfers loads toward bearing walls and beams; the walls transfer them into the foundation. The exact path depends on the building, but the basic question is always the same: which connection carries each force to the next element?

Sheathing also acts as part of a diaphragm that transfers forces within the roof plane. This matters under lateral wind loads as well as suction. A diaphragm is not simply a thick sheet of wood. It is a coordinated system of panel orientation, edge support, fastening patterns, collectors and connections to supporting elements. A detail that improves local panel retention does not automatically complete the load path at the roof-to-wall connection.

Wind design standards recognize that roof corners and edges experience different uplift demands from many interior areas. That is why a fastening schedule cannot be inferred from the center of one roof alone. Exposure, geometry, roof slope, construction date and governing design assumptions all matter. A coastal home designed for hurricanes may require detailing different from a low-wind inland building.

### What makes an attachment resist uplift?

A nail transfers force through the wood and the panel. Resistance depends on its diameter and shank, head size, penetration, the material and condition of the framing, the panel, and whether the fastener was placed correctly. Under uplift, withdrawal of a fastener and pull-through of its head through the panel are distinct failure modes. A nail with inadequate penetration may withdraw even if the panel is sound. A fastener driven too deeply can crush or cut the surface fibers and reduce the resistance to head pull-through. Missed framing contributes almost nothing to attachment at that point.

Fastener spacing changes how force is shared along an edge or framing member. Closer spacing can increase connection capacity, but only within a coherent design and when workmanship preserves edge distances and wood integrity. Overdriving, splitting, corroded fasteners and previously damaged sheathing can defeat the intention of a close pattern. A nail gun's speed is not a substitute for checking whether nails actually entered the intended member.

Panel edges matter, too. A supported edge behaves differently from an unsupported one. Some roof assemblies use clips or blocking for panel-edge support. The specified gaps between panels accommodate dimensional change; forcing panels tightly together can encourage buckling when moisture expands them. Sheathing thickness, span rating and framing spacing must agree with the intended loading and roof assembly.

### Inspection with the load path in mind

Suppose an older attic has scattered nail tips visible between rafters. That may be evidence of missed framing during original installation. Elsewhere, sheathing may appear softened around a former roof leak. A purely visual count of fasteners is not enough to declare an attachment adequate. The inspector wants to know where the panel is actually attached, whether the wood substrate can resist the connection forces and whether roof-to-wall ties transfer uplift beyond the framing.

During a planned reroof, the sheathing surface becomes accessible for inspection. That is a chance to document rotten or delaminated areas, verify panel type and thickness, inspect edge support, identify inadequate fasteners and compare the proposed reattachment to a recognized design. A reroofing specification should name the fastening and inspection method, not merely state that the roof deck will be secure.

Consider a roof panel that remains attached to every truss, but the trusses are poorly connected to the supporting walls. The panel may survive while a larger assembly separates. Conversely, strong roof-to-wall connectors cannot keep weakly nailed panels from being pulled away. Engineering the roof means considering both levels of connection, plus the paths through walls, floors and foundations.

### A useful documentation exercise

Sketch one typical roof bay. Mark panel edges and spans, rafters or trusses, observed fastener locations, any blocking and the supporting wall. Add arrows for downward gravity loads, upward wind suction and in-plane forces. Now ask where each arrow goes next and which connection is expected to carry it. The exercise often reveals that an apparently isolated hardware detail depends on two or three unseen connections.

The durable principle is that a roof deck does two jobs: it supports the weather covering and contributes to the building's structure. Its strength cannot be separated from the pattern and quality of its attachments or from the continuous load path below it. A competent inspection and a competent construction specification follow that entire chain rather than treating a better nail as a complete answer.

### Roof Trusses, Load Paths, and the Limits of Field Modifications

### 020000 — Roof Trusses, Load Paths, and the Limits of Field Modifications

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Concept example.** For a simply supported system carrying symmetric gravity loads, equilibrium requires upward reactions balancing total downward load. Individual chord and web forces require truss geometry and joint analysis; axial force can reverse sign under uplift or different load combinations.

**Document checklist.** Identify truss marks; locate truss design drawings; verify span, bearing locations, design loads, original bracing notes, engineered connector plates, and any later concentrated equipment or storage loads. Inspect installed damage and wetting. Record the specific member and joint, not merely the roof bay.

**Authority and limits.** ANSI/TPI 1 is issued through the Truss Plate Institute; locally adopted codes determine formal approvals. ICC code material includes provisions against altering truss members or changing loads without appropriate design concurrence. The AWC provides framing context; manufacturer truss drawings are specific to the assembled roof. Never assume an allowable cut, nail schedule, repair plate or storage load from a general lecture.

### Roof Sheathing, Uplift Connections, and Wind Load Transfer

### 021000 — Roof Sheathing, Uplift Connections, and Wind Load Transfer

#### Non-spoken technical companion

**Structural layers to draw:** Roof covering, underlayment, panel sheathing, sheathing fastener, rafter or truss, roof-to-wall connector, supporting wall, wall anchorage and foundation. Show separate uplift and in-plane paths.

**Inspection record:** Roof geometry/exposure assumptions; panel thickness/type and condition; edge support; framing spacing; fastener type/spacing and actual framing engagement; overdriven/missing fasteners; signs of rot/corrosion; roof-to-wall and wall-to-foundation connections. Photograph before concealment.

**Design boundary:** Exact sheathing-nailing patterns, fastener schedule and high-wind upgrades vary by adopted building standards, location and engineering assumptions. FORTIFIED and FEMA guidance illustrate robust practice but do not automatically govern every jurisdiction. Do not invent a universal fastener spacing from one example. Review roof modifications with a qualified designer when structural loads change.

**Sources:** Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, FORTIFIED Roof guidance; FEMA, home-builder wind resistance and continuous load path guidance; American Wood Council, wood construction design resources.
