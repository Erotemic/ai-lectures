# 019000 — Deck Footings, Post Bases, and the Long-Term Load Path

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A deck post can look perfectly straight while the connection at its base has been failing for years. The wood may be soft just above the concrete, the footing may be slowly tilting, or a connection may resist vertical weight while doing little to resist uplift or sideways movement. A deck is not simply a wooden platform. It is a series of load transfers, and the ground is part of that series.

Trace the loads downward. A person stands on deck boards. The boards transfer weight to joists, the joists to beams, the beams to posts or another support, and the posts to footings. The footings distribute force into soil. A lateral force, such as a person leaning into a guardrail, takes a different route. Wind and certain geometric conditions can introduce uplift. Every route needs appropriate connections. A gravity load path that looks solid does not prove that the lateral or uplift paths are complete.

## Footings are interfaces with soil, not just concrete shapes

Concrete resists substantial compression, but that does not tell us whether the soil underneath is competent. A footing spreads load over an area so the ground can support it without unacceptable settlement. If the bearing material varies from one support to another, even similar posts may settle differently. A shallow footing may also move in freezing soils as moisture freezes and expands, depending on local conditions. Frost depth, allowable bearing, drainage, and footing geometry are therefore site-and-code questions, not numbers to guess from the size of a post.

Imagine two supports under a beam. One rests on firm, undisturbed soil. The other sits in loose backfill that was never properly compacted. Under the same load, the second may move farther. The resulting rotation can stress the ledger, joists, guard posts, and beam connections. Shimming a sagging deck at the top conceals the displacement; it does not restore the original support conditions.

Look for symptoms that tell you about movement: posts no longer plumb, beam seats with fresh gaps, cracked piers, tilted bases, deck boards pulling away from the house, and rails that have developed new racking. A level or plumb bob can document geometry, but a single snapshot cannot reveal whether movement is continuing. Dated measurements and photographs are more useful.

## The post base has multiple jobs

Wood in direct contact with standing water, wet soil or poorly drained concrete can stay damp. Even preservative-treated wood is not invulnerable: durability depends on treatment category, exposed cut surfaces, species, moisture, fastener compatibility, and maintenance. A properly selected metal standoff connector can separate the end grain from a persistently wet surface while transferring forces into the footing. But not every standoff is rated for the same uplift, shear, and moment demands.

Do not infer the connector's capacity from its appearance. A thin plate and a handful of screws might be intended only to locate the post, not anchor the structure against all required forces. The post-to-beam joint also has a defined load path. Where beam weight is carried by a bolt passing through the side of a post rather than by adequate direct bearing or an engineered connector, the joint may depend on bolt behavior in a way that is not acceptable for the applicable design. The American Wood Council's residential deck guidance illustrates the distinction between bearing and attachment.

The base of a post is an inspection location. Look for corrosion, movement, split wood, loose anchor nuts, missing washers, and trapped debris. Probe suspect wood only by methods appropriate to the material; a screwdriver gouge is not an engineering strength test. Corrosion of metal connectors may be accelerated by local moisture and by material incompatibility. Match fastener coatings and connector materials to the actual preservative treatment and exposure specifications.

## Water management is structural maintenance

Picture a deck beside a house where the downspout empties beside one post. Each storm saturates the surrounding soil. Winter may subject the region to repeated freeze-thaw movement, while summer leaves damp material at the base. Over several seasons the post can show degradation and the footing can change position. A repair that installs a new post without fixing drainage returns the new member to the old environment.

The ledger is another critical water transition. Flashing has to steer water over the appropriate exterior surfaces rather than behind the ledger into the wall. But flashing alone does not supply structural attachment, and a row of bolts alone does not supply drainage. The two systems overlap in physical space while doing different jobs. When a deck is freestanding, the framing, footing and lateral bracing requirements differ; the absence of a ledger removes one problem and creates other load paths to design.

## A careful inspection has boundaries

Start with visual documentation from above and below, if accessible. Sketch the beams, posts, footings, ledger, braces, guard posts and visible connectors. Record which components bear directly on supports. Photograph every suspected water trap, split and corroded metal contact. Check posts for visible displacement relative to a fixed reference. Investigate drainage around the foundations. Do not put people on a deck with signs of severe deterioration or inadequate support in order to conduct a loaded test.

Now distinguish maintenance from redesign. Clearing debris and improving exterior drainage may be maintenance. Replacing a connector, reinforcing a guard post, altering a footing, or restoring a deteriorated ledger can be structural work. Existing code provisions, soil conditions, proprietary connector tables, and the deck's actual geometry determine the required detail. Simply adding screws until the assembly feels stiff is not a substitute for a complete load-path design.

A good deck inspection ends with a map of how gravity, lateral loads, and moisture travel through the structure. The deck can look fine at its surface while the critical weak point sits near the dirt. Learn to follow the forces all the way into the ground, and the inspection becomes much more than a search for rotten boards.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

**Load-path drawing.** Show deck board → joist → beam → post → rated post base → footing → competent soil for gravity. Separately trace guard loads, lateral forces, and possible uplift to their designed resistance. Identify the role of bracing or other lateral systems in a freestanding layout.

**Inspection record.** Sketch support grid, footing exposure, post/base condition, corrosion, wood-to-ground clearances, drainage patterns, reference elevations, ledger flashing and accessible attachment. Record dates to distinguish ongoing movement from old geometry.

**Design boundaries.** The AWC DCA-6 prescriptive guide illustrates common deck details, but applicability depends on the edition of adopted code, design assumptions, frost depth, ground bearing and product-specific hardware. Do not infer a footing size or connector capacity from the examples in this lecture. Structural modifications require verified design and appropriate inspection.

**References.** American Wood Council, *Prescriptive Residential Wood Deck Construction Guide (DCA-6)* and deck beam-to-post FAQ; USDA Forest Service, *Wood Handbook*; ICC ledger and deck guidance.
