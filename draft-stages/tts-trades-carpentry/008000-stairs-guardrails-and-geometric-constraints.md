# The arithmetic of a safe change in height

A staircase is a machine made of fixed geometry. It converts a large vertical change in elevation into a sequence of smaller changes that a walking person can manage. It has no motor, yet slight errors in one part can matter every time someone uses it. Unlike a decorative shelf, a stair is not successful merely because the boards fit together.

There are three questions to ask at once. Is the walking geometry consistent and usable? Can the supporting structure safely carry loads into the building? And does the arrangement protect people at exposed edges? The questions interact. An elegant flight can fail because of a weak connection; a strong set of stringers can fail because the steps have poor proportions; and an otherwise serviceable stair can become hazardous when a guard is not anchored correctly.

## Rise, run, and the total height

The rise is the vertical change from one tread to the next. The run is the horizontal progress between successive risers; tread depth is the available foot-bearing surface and may include nosing details, so it should not always be treated as identical to run. Begin with the **finished-floor-to-finished-floor height**, not merely the height between rough framing members. Tile, hardwood, underlayment, and finished landings can alter the effective first and last riser.

Imagine a vertical difference of 104 inches and a design with fourteen equal risers. Each nominal rise would be about 7.43 inches. There are often thirteen intermediate horizontal runs if the upper floor itself serves as the final landing, but configuration and local rules decide the actual count. The arithmetic is straightforward. The demanding part is deciding what constitutes a riser, where the finished surfaces will be, and whether there is sufficient horizontal room and head clearance.

Small systematic errors are more consequential than isolated rounding. If you mark each rise by repeatedly stepping a tape from the previous mark, an error can accumulate. A more reliable layout references a common datum and calculates the position of every riser from the total height. Then check the first and last rises against the intended finished surfaces. A stair with one markedly different step can surprise someone whose gait has adapted to the preceding steps.

## The stringer is a structure

In a common wood stair, stringers support the treads and transfer load into the upper and lower bearing points. A cut stringer gains its stepped shape by notching the lumber, but notches remove section and create stress concentrations. Choosing lumber solely by its nominal size without considering remaining depth and support conditions can produce a weak member. Connections at the top and bottom must carry the intended forces; the visual fact that the stringer touches a framing member does not establish adequate attachment.

Treads bend between supports. Risers, blocking, and finish materials affect stiffness and noise but cannot be assumed to replace structural framing. A squeak may be movement at a joint; a visible crack across a stringer or a shifting landing points to a different level of concern. Exterior stairs add water, frost movement, corrosion, and slippery surfaces to the structural problem.

## Guards and handrails have different jobs

A handrail is something a person grasps for support while ascending or descending. A guard is intended to reduce the risk of falling from an exposed edge. A single assembly may serve both purposes, but each has requirements involving height, grip, continuity, opening sizes, strength, and attachment. These requirements depend on the adopted building code, occupancy, and the details of the installation. A thin decorative rail secured to trim might look finished while lacking the load path expected of a protective guard.

Think about someone leaning against a railing. The force goes from the person into the rail, then into posts or brackets, through fasteners and connectors, and finally into the supporting structure. A weak link anywhere in that chain defeats the apparent strength of the visible timber or metal. Fastening a guard post through a deck or stair finish without addressing the framing beneath can create exactly this weak link.

## The entire route matters

Stair design is constrained by the available floor opening, landing dimensions, turning space, headroom, doors, lighting, and the surfaces encountered at either end. Changing the run to save floor space may make the steps too steep. Moving the upper landing may introduce headroom conflicts. Widening a stair may alter framing loads. A door that swings onto a small landing changes how people approach the first step. These are layout decisions that should be tested before cutting lumber.

When assessing an existing stair, observe rather than immediately 'fixing' one uncomfortable step. Measure all risers from finished walking surfaces; look for variations and movement; trace stringer bearing and guard connections; distinguish surface damage from structural failure. A handrail that moves under load deserves investigation of its attachment, not just new paint.

A staircase teaches an important lesson in construction: geometry is a performance requirement, and dimensions cannot be separated from load paths. Accurate cutting is only one part of the work. Successful construction makes the series of steps predictable for a moving human body and makes the forces travel into a sound structure.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Example geometry — illustrative only

- Finished elevation change: `104 in`
- Proposed number of equal rises: `14`
- Computed nominal rise: `104 / 14 = 7.428571... in`
- For a straight flight terminating at an upper floor, there may be `13` intermediate runs; confirm the configured landing geometry.
- Establish tread and landing locations from **one datum**. Finished surface build-up changes the first and last effective rise.

### Load-path checklist

Trace tread → stringer or carriage → top/bottom connection → structural support. For guards, trace rail → post/bracket → fastener assembly → framing. Check geometry, attachment, headroom, and opening constraints against the adopted local code and professional design; do not interpret the numerical example as a permitted stair dimension or approval.

**Review boundary:** Stair, guard, and handrail requirements vary by occupancy, jurisdiction, and adopted code edition. Structural work, particularly modifying openings or guards on occupied buildings, requires appropriate code and engineering review.
