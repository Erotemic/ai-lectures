# 014000 — Wood Joinery, Glue, and Mechanical Load Transfer

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A joint can look as if two pieces of wood have become one, yet break apart when someone leans on it. Another joint, with exposed screws and an obvious seam, may carry load for years. The difference is not elegance. It is how force travels from one member through the connection into another, and whether that force stays within the capacities of wood, adhesive, and fastener over time.

Consider a shelf joined to the side of a cabinet. The shelf bends under books. At the joint, bending creates a tendency for the upper and lower portions of the connection to pull and push in opposite directions. A screw alone may resist this through its threads and bearing against the wood. A dado, a groove that supports the shelf edge, transfers part of the vertical load directly through wood bearing. Glue may spread forces across a much larger area. The same nominal joint can behave differently depending on its geometry.

## Wood is directional

Wood is not a homogeneous plastic block. Its fibers run primarily along the grain, and its strength and dimensional movement vary greatly by direction. Pulling parallel to straight grain differs from pulling perpendicular to the grain. End grain, side grain, and face grain give an adhesive different structures to wet and penetrate. A narrow screw near an edge can split wood along the grain even if the metal screw itself is far from failing.

Water content matters. Wood expands and contracts across the grain as moisture changes; lengthwise movement is much smaller for ordinary lumber. A wide solid-wood tabletop fastened rigidly across its width to a stationary base can split or distort seasonally. Good joinery can restrain the part where needed and allow controlled movement elsewhere. A floating panel, slotted fastener hole, or suitable tabletop connector has a purpose beyond convenience.

## Bearing, shear, pullout, and splitting

When evaluating a joint, trace the path of force. A vertical load may press one piece onto another, producing compression or bearing. It can slide two faces relative to each other, creating shear. A prying force may pull screws out of the wood; the same screws may carry much more effectively when loaded in a direction that avoids withdrawal. At the edge of a board, load can create a split because little intact material remains around the connection.

Imagine fastening a heavy wall shelf to a cabinet side. Screws driven through the side into the shelf's end may provide initial alignment, but thread holding and splitting resistance depend on species, density, pilot holes, screw dimensions, spacing, and grain direction. A cleat beneath the shelf adds a direct bearing path. A dado helps with location and load distribution. An installed bracket can transfer load with a different moment arm. None of these details can be judged solely by how tightly the screw feels when driven.

A load path continues beyond the visible joint. A wall cabinet may have strong shelf joints yet fail because the mounting screws miss the structural backing or the cabinet back cannot transfer weight to the mount. Test the entire assembly conceptually: books to shelf, shelf to case, case to mount, mount to framing, framing to building.

## What glue really does

Woodworking adhesive creates a bond when it wets compatible surfaces and develops its designed structure during cure. Strength depends on joint fit, surface preparation, moisture, temperature, adequate assembly pressure, and the adhesive's intended service environment. More squeeze-out is not proof of a stronger bond. Too little adhesive leaves discontinuities; excessive force can deprive a joint of an appropriate bond line, depending on the formulation and wood.

Adhesives also have different properties. Some are best suited to interior, normally dry furniture. Others are formulated for more moisture exposure. Manufacturers specify open time, assembly time, clamp time, temperature ranges, and full cure. A label saying 'water resistant' does not mean a joint can be left underwater indefinitely. An adhesive that bonds wood satisfactorily may be wrong for an oily tropical hardwood or a porous, contaminated repair surface.

End-grain joints deserve special attention. A plain butt joint connecting the ends of two boards offers a small or mechanically unfavorable bonding surface under bending. A scarf, lap, finger, or other geometry can provide more effective area and a different force path. Joint geometry often improves reliability more than selecting a more expensive bottle of glue.

## Accuracy and tolerance

A tight-looking joint is not automatically a well-designed joint. A mortise-and-tenon fit that must be hammered together may split the mortised member or prevent the adhesive from distributing properly. A very loose fit may leave an uncontrolled bond line or poor mechanical support. The correct fit depends on the joint and adhesive. Measure the actual tooling and material; do not assume nominal lumber dimensions or a bit marked with a number will produce precisely that slot.

Assembly order matters too. Dry-fit before applying glue. Check diagonals for square, confirm the reference faces, and arrange clamps so pressure closes the intended joint rather than twisting the whole carcass. Clamps are temporary machines that apply forces, not magic instruments that make inaccurate parts fit. A cabinet can cure permanently out of square while every clamp is tight.

## When screws, nails, and joints cooperate

Mechanical fasteners can locate parts while adhesive cures, provide a direct load path, or permit disassembly. They can also inhibit seasonal movement if applied without thought. A nail often tolerates some joint movement differently from a brittle adhesive line; a screw offers serviceability but can concentrate stress around its hole. In structural applications, approved connectors and specified fastener schedules are design elements, not suggestions replaceable by an attractive piece of furniture joinery.

For a small cabinet, test fit, measure diagonals, verify shelf support, and check whether the piece can be repaired in the future. For an occupied deck, stair, beam, or other load-bearing construction, the stakes are different: the connection must be evaluated under the loads and code provisions applicable to that system. A joinery demonstration is not a structural capacity certification.

The lasting lesson is to trace forces rather than merely recognize joint names. A mortise-and-tenon, dado, lap, dovetail, biscuit, screw, or adhesive all change the available path for load and movement. The best joint is the one whose geometry and materials match the forces, environment, accuracy, and service life of the actual object.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Joint inspection matrix

| Force or condition | Design question | Common failure observation |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical bearing | Does load pass through supported wood? | Crushed fibers or ledge failure |
| Shear | What face and fasteners transmit the sliding load? | Joint slip or fastener shear |
| Pullout | Is the force acting along screw withdrawal? | Threads strip in wood |
| Bending / prying | What is the lever arm at the connection? | Opening gap at one side |
| Humidity cycling | Where can cross-grain movement occur? | Splits, bowed panel, loosened bond |
| Wet service | Is adhesive and species suitable for exposure? | Delamination, decay, staining |

### Design exercise

Draw a bookshelf full of books. Trace the force from each shelf into side panels, back, mounts, and wall. Compare an end-screwed shelf, supported cleat, dadoed shelf, and bracket. Identify which failure mode each change helps, and which failure modes remain.

**Review boundary:** Structural fastener sizing, allowable loads, and approved joint specifications require applicable engineering references and local adopted code. Furniture adhesives must be selected and cured according to their product data; dry-fit and verify before assembly.

- `usda-wood` — [USDA Forest Service, Wood Handbook: Wood as an Engineering Material (2021)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/fpl/wood-handbook). Chapters 4–9, 13, 17: moisture, strength, fastening, beam equations and framing.
- `usda-drying` — [Richard Bergman (2021), Drying and Control of Moisture Content and Dimensional Changes, USDA Wood Handbook Chapter 13](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62261). Moisture content, equilibrium, shrinkage and storage.
- `pnnl-loadpath` — [PNNL Building America Solution Center, Continuous Load Path from Roof Through Wall to Foundation](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/continuous-load-path-provided-connections-roof-through-wall-foundation). Gravity and lateral loads, openings, headers and connectors; cites FEMA P-804.
- `usfs-hunt-finishing-2021` — [Christopher G. Hunt, 2021, Finishing Wood, USDA Forest Service Wood Handbook Chapter 16](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62269). Water, ultraviolet exposure, exterior finishes, preparation and expected performance.
- `awc-wfcm` — [American Wood Council, 2024 Wood Frame Construction Manual (WFCM)](https://awc.org/resources/2024-wfcm/). Committee-developed ANSI standard covers connections, floors, walls, and roofs in limited one- and two-family dwelling designs; scope and local adoption must be checked.
