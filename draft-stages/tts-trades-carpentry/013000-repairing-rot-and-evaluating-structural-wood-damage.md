# 013000 — Repairing Rot and Evaluating Structural Wood Damage

<!-- BEGIN SPOKEN LECTURE -->

A screwdriver passes through the edge of a wooden porch post. The temptation is to scrape out the soft part, fill the cavity, paint it, and declare the problem solved. But the screwdriver has found damaged material, not measured the remaining structural capacity. A porch post is not decorative trim. It transfers loads from above into a foundation. The question is whether enough sound material remains, whether the connections still work, and why decay occurred in the first place.

Wood is a biological composite: fibers aligned predominantly along the tree's length, bound together by a matrix that gives the material different strengths in different directions. Its ability to carry tension along the grain differs greatly from its resistance to splitting across the grain. It also gains and loses moisture as surrounding conditions change. When wood stays sufficiently wet for fungi to grow, biological decay can progressively destroy the material responsible for strength. A surface that looks acceptable may hide a softened core; a stained surface may have sound structure below. Color is evidence to investigate, not a capacity rating.

## Map the load path before picking a tool

Consider a floor joist whose end is damaged near a masonry pocket. The middle of the joist may be solid, yet the end carries the reaction from the span into the support. That reaction creates bearing stress near the end and shear demand through the wood. Removing the decayed end and filling it with a nonstructural compound does not restore those forces automatically. A repair must supply an alternate load path and adequate bearing, fasteners, and connection geometry.

A wall stud beneath a window carries different demands from a deck ledger, a sill plate, or a rafter. A sill may be crushed at an anchor or degraded along its concealed bottom face. A joist may be compromised by an old notch or hole near a support. A post can be apparently straight while rotting where it contacts a damp concrete footing. The location and function of the damaged portion matter as much as the visible area.

Before removing structural wood, identify supported loads and temporary support needs. A replacement member cannot simply be slipped into a loaded assembly if that operation removes the support it relies on. Shoring, load transfer, and evaluation of connections are structural design issues. The educational lesson is to recognize those issues, not to guess a universal sistering length or screw pattern.

## Diagnose the moisture pathway

Decay is a symptom of sustained or repeated wetting. A porch post may draw liquid through an end grain cut sitting in standing water. A window sill may remain damp because flashing directs rain behind trim. A joist in a basement may be exposed to humid air, condensation, or water entering through masonry. Eliminating visible decay without correcting water entry creates conditions for the next failure.

Inspection begins with environmental history: weather, roof drainage, splashback, soil contact, indoor humidity, and plumbing. Probe suspicious accessible areas carefully and compare with known sound wood of similar species and orientation. A moisture meter can help map relative conditions, but readings depend on species, depth, temperature, treatments, and instrument type. Record its setting and limitations. Photographs and measured dimensions are more useful than an undocumented statement that the wood is “a little soft.”

Look for insect channels, fungal growth, separated laminations, cracked paint, and failed joints. Not every hole indicates active biological damage. Treatment history and current activity require separate evaluation. Concealed damage may extend beyond what a small exploratory opening reveals, particularly where adjacent materials trap moisture.

## Repair categories are not interchangeable

A surface finish repair can protect otherwise sound wood once moisture has been controlled. A small nonstructural trim replacement primarily restores weather protection or appearance. Structural replacement, reinforcement, or engineered splicing must restore the needed strength, stiffness, connection behavior, and durability. Epoxy consolidants and fillers have legitimate uses in some conservation practices, but their presence does not certify a damaged beam, post, or connection as safe.

Sistering adds a member alongside an existing one. It works only if the new member receives the load and transfers it to adequate supports. A sister that stops short of a bearing point and connects through a handful of arbitrary fasteners may do little for a decayed joist end. Splice repairs similarly depend on the forces being transmitted and on tested or engineered details. Structural repair proposals should identify the load path in a drawing, not just the product name in an estimate.

Exterior durability often depends on details that look small: an end cut that sheds water, a gap permitting drainage, a compatible corrosion-resistant fastener, and a separation between wood and wet masonry. Paint can slow moisture exchange but cannot rescue a joint that traps bulk water behind it. Repair design should let the assembly dry and should leave enough access to inspect it later.

## Verify the outcome

Document the conditions before work, including photographs at supports, connections, and any concealed areas that will be closed. Define a clear acceptance check: moisture source corrected, damaged material extent addressed, load path understood, connection details recorded, and water-shedding finish complete. Inspect after meaningful weather events, not only on the day the new wood is installed.

The point is not that every rotten board requires an engineer. Many nonstructural repairs are straightforward. The important distinction is knowing when wood is only a surface, when it is part of a load-bearing assembly, and when the remedy must change from cosmetic repair to structural restoration. The screwdriver found a problem. The job of diagnosis is to determine which problem it found.

## Technical companion (non-spoken)

### Evaluation grid

| Location | Required function | Risk when damaged | Documentation before repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decorative casing | Weather finish, appearance | Local water penetration | Flashing and drainage path |
| Sill plate | Distributes vertical/shear loads; anchors wall | Loss of bearing or anchor continuity | Support layout, fasteners, moisture history |
| Joist end | Transfers span reaction into bearing | Shear or bearing failure | Span, support, end condition, adjacent joists |
| Deck ledger/post | Carries exterior live/dead load | Collapse potential | Full connection and foundation details |

**Editorial boundary:** No general-purpose sister length, connector schedule, filler product, or probe depth establishes structural adequacy. Load-bearing repairs and shoring warrant project-specific structural review; moisture remediation is part of the repair, not a cosmetic afterthought.

## Selected reading and evidence records (non-spoken)

- `usda-wood` — [USDA Forest Service, Wood Handbook: Wood as an Engineering Material (2021)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/fpl/wood-handbook). Chapters 4–9, 13, 17: moisture, strength, fastening, beam equations and framing.
- `usda-drying` — [Richard Bergman (2021), Drying and Control of Moisture Content and Dimensional Changes, USDA Wood Handbook Chapter 13](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62261). Moisture content, equilibrium, shrinkage and storage.
- `pnnl-loadpath` — [PNNL Building America Solution Center, Continuous Load Path from Roof Through Wall to Foundation](https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/continuous-load-path-provided-connections-roof-through-wall-foundation). Gravity and lateral loads, openings, headers and connectors; cites FEMA P-804.
- `awc-wfcm` — [American Wood Council, 2024 Wood Frame Construction Manual (WFCM)](https://awc.org/resources/2024-wfcm/). Committee-developed ANSI standard covers connections, floors, walls, and roofs in limited one- and two-family dwelling designs; scope and local adoption must be checked.
