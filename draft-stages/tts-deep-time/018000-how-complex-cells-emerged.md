# How Complex Cells Emerged

Take a sample of pond water, look through a microscope, and watch a single-celled organism steer around a bit of debris. It may be swimming with beating cilia, changing its shape to engulf food, routing material through membranes, and managing hundreds of chemical reactions. This is an entire working life enclosed in one cell. We call it a eukaryote, a member of the broad group that also includes animals, plants and fungi.

Now place an ordinary bacterial cell beside it. Many bacteria possess intricate internal organization and sophisticated behavior, so the contrast must not become a caricature. Nevertheless, eukaryotic cells tend to possess a distinctive set of membrane-bound compartments and complex molecular systems: a nucleus enclosing most of the DNA, a dynamic internal skeleton, machinery for transporting membranes, and an organelle called the mitochondrion. How could these features emerge from earlier microbial life?

The best-supported part of the answer begins with an evolutionary merger. Mitochondria descend from bacteria related to the alphaproteobacterial lineage. The host lineage was related to archaea, especially the group now called Asgard archaea. That conclusion is supported by multiple independent features of genomes and cell structures. The detailed sequence of events is much harder to recover. Did the host acquire complex internal architecture before the bacterial partnership, or did that partnership enable the complexity to develop? How much of the modern cell was already in place when the partners joined? Those are questions we can investigate, while recognizing that no intermediate cell from the ancient transition survives in a museum jar.

## The evidence hidden inside mitochondria

Look inside mitochondria and the case for bacterial ancestry becomes concrete. Most mitochondria retain a small genome. Their genes and ribosomal components show evolutionary relationships to bacterial systems. They divide from preexisting mitochondria, and their membranes and biochemical pathways contain features that fit a history of endosymbiosis. When scientists compare homologous DNA and protein sequences, mitochondrial genes fall within a broader bacterial history, although the precise closest living relatives of the original partner remain uncertain.

This combination is stronger than simply saying mitochondria look a bit like bacteria. It resembles finding a working machine inside a factory, discovering that the machine carries its own reduced instruction manual, and then realizing that its manual shares distinctive errors and components with a lineage of free-living machines. Common descent explains an interconnected pattern. An independent invention would need to reproduce many of those particulars.

There is a further historical clue: mitochondria have lost or transferred much of the genome of their bacterial ancestor. Many proteins used inside modern mitochondria are encoded by nuclear DNA and imported after they are manufactured in the surrounding cell. That makes the present organelle a dependent partner, neither a free bacterium nor simply a compartment the host invented from scratch. Some living eukaryotes inhabit oxygen-poor environments and have highly reduced mitochondrial derivatives, including mitosomes and hydrogenosomes. The discovery of such organelles changed interpretations of species once thought to represent a primitively mitochondrion-free stage. Their reduction is evidence about secondary loss and adaptation, not direct preservation of the earliest eukaryote.

A similar argument applies to chloroplasts, the photosynthetic organelles of plants and many algae. Chloroplast genomes and cellular machinery tie them to cyanobacteria. Photosynthetic eukaryotes therefore contain another major symbiotic inheritance. In some algae, later rounds of endosymbiosis produced even more complicated organelles when one eukaryote acquired another photosynthetic eukaryote. The broader evolutionary principle has been tested repeatedly: a long-lived association can turn formerly separate organisms into parts of a new kind of organism.

## The archaeal side of the ancestry

For many years, scientists compared the genes that handle information in eukaryotic cells with those in bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotic machinery for transcribing and translating genetic information has deep affinities to archaeal systems. More recently, sequencing of sediments uncovered a diverse group of archaea known as Asgard archaea, named after figures and places in Norse mythology. Their genomes contain homologues of proteins involved in cytoskeletal dynamics, membrane traffic and other functions once regarded as particularly eukaryotic.

Genome sequences are observations: individual pieces of DNA recovered from environmental samples and assembled computationally. Placing their organisms on an evolutionary tree is an inference that depends on which genes are compared, how sequences are aligned, and which models account for their histories. Cultured representatives provide complementary evidence about actual cell structure, but the few organisms we have cultivated cannot be treated as living ancestors. They are modern relatives with their own evolutionary histories.

One cultivated Asgard archaeon, described in 2020 after prolonged laboratory effort, grew extremely slowly and lived in association with other microbes. Its cellular extensions and microbial partnerships made researchers take syntrophy seriously. In syntrophy, two organisms exchange products so that a set of chemical reactions becomes energetically favorable for the partners together. Such partnerships suggest plausible ecological settings for an ancient merger. They do not demonstrate that we have observed the merger that produced eukaryotes.

Two major studies published around the turn of 2026 brought this argument into sharper focus. A phylogenomic analysis led by Victor Tobiasson and colleagues compared the origins of many conserved eukaryotic gene families. It concluded that Asgard-related ancestry contributed much of the core cellular machinery, while the alphaproteobacterial contribution was concentrated in energy metabolism and mitochondrial functions. A separate study used the relative ages of ancient gene duplications to infer that several eukaryote-like systems evolved before mitochondrial acquisition. Those results make a host that was already becoming complex a serious contender. But both are reconstructions from living genes. Their answers depend on gene-tree inference, duplicate dating, lineage sampling and assumptions about losses and transfers.

## The ordering problem

A useful way to test the story is to write down the ordering claims. In one family of hypotheses, a relatively simple archaeal host acquired a bacterial partner early. The energetic benefits of that partnership then facilitated the growth of large genomes, elaborate compartments and novel cellular processes. In another family, the archaeal host acquired membrane remodeling, a cytoskeleton, perhaps even phagocytosis-like behavior, before the bacterium became its internal partner. Additional proposals involve close metabolic partnerships, membrane contacts, or multiple transfers of bacterial genes before the definitive mitochondrion.

Each sequence solves one problem and creates another. A host that engulfed a bacterium must have possessed a way to enclose or internalize it; familiar phagocytosis in modern cells requires complex machinery. A model in which phagocytosis emerged after acquisition must supply a different entry mechanism, such as an external partnership that became progressively enclosed. Conversely, a fully complex host before mitochondria must explain how it maintained that complexity under the energetic conditions of ancient environments.

Sometimes a proposed solution is advertised as though the mitochondrion supplied an almost magical energy surplus. Mitochondrial respiration can support demanding cell functions, but the energetic argument requires explicit comparisons of cell size, membrane area, metabolic rate and evolutionary constraints. A universal law stating that prokaryotes cannot become complex would be stronger than the evidence warrants; present archaea and bacteria display a surprising range of cellular organization.

The 2026 gene-duplication study reports a sequence favoring several host-complexity features before the mitochondrion. Treat this as a testable, model-dependent result. The genes associated with a component might have arisen before that component assumed its modern cellular role. Dating a duplication does not directly date a visible nucleus. A molecular function could be repurposed in stages. Conversely, the universal presence of mitochondria or their derivatives among sampled eukaryotic lineages supports their presence by the last eukaryotic common ancestor, without resolving every earlier step.

## When did eukaryotes appear?

The timing is difficult because several different events are being dated. One is the divergence between the lineage leading to eukaryotes and its closest archaeal relatives. A later event is the merger that produced mitochondria. A third is the last common ancestor of living eukaryotes, often abbreviated LECA. A fourth is the first fossil that we are confident belongs to a eukaryotic lineage. These dates need not agree. A lineage can exist long before a recognizable fossil is deposited, and LECA can postdate the beginning of eukaryogenesis by a very long interval.

Rocks provide snapshots of structures and chemical molecules, not a continuous film. Some ancient microfossils display large size, elaborate walls, ornamentation or internal organization consistent with eukaryotes. Classification still needs care: size alone is weak evidence, and microbial structures can converge. Steroid-derived hydrocarbons, often called steranes, can support eukaryotic interpretations, but geological alteration, different biosynthetic pathways and contamination must be considered. Their presence or absence is not a direct record of when the first nucleus formed.

A study published in May 2026 examined the sedimentary environments of fossils interpreted as eukaryotes from roughly one point seven five to one point four billion years ago. It argued that these early organisms occupied oxygenated seafloor settings, using a combination of fossil occurrence and geochemical reconstruction. That helps locate ecological opportunities for complex cells. It does not prove that oxygen first permitted their origin at that exact time. Biological evolution and environmental expansion are separate chronological questions.

Molecular clocks reach farther back by combining differences among present gene sequences with geological calibration points. But deeply diverged sequences are difficult to compare; rates vary, substitutions can obscure earlier substitutions, and horizontal gene transfers complicate the tree. A recent duplication-based study inferred a long span of eukaryogenesis reaching substantially deeper into the Precambrian than the clearest recognizable eukaryotic fossils. Other methods give different timings. It is appropriate to express dates as results conditional on particular models and calibrations.

## A merger is also a problem of inheritance

One difficulty with endosymbiosis is that the partnership must persist from one generation to another. A host and its symbiont both reproduce. If the symbiont divides too quickly, it can become harmful; if it divides too slowly or fails to enter daughter cells, the host's descendants may lose a useful partner. Long-term integration therefore favors mechanisms for coordinating replication, exchanging useful substances and eliminating conflicts between the partners.

We can observe analogous conflicts in living endosymbioses, including associations in which insects carry bacteria supplying nutrients missing from their diet. These modern associations do not reenact the ancient origin of mitochondria, but they show that an obligate partnership can evolve through gene loss, metabolic dependence and host control. Some endosymbionts have very small genomes precisely because the host or its surroundings supply functions the symbiont no longer performs independently.

An especially informative question is which genes moved into the nucleus and when. The mere presence of bacterial genes in a eukaryotic genome cannot tell us that every such gene entered through the mitochondrial ancestor. Microbes exchange genes, and additional bacterial donors can contribute to the host lineage. The 2026 source-attribution analysis attempts to separate those contributions by comparing many trees. The signal is stronger when independent gene families agree, and weaker when sequences are short, duplicated or subject to frequent transfers.

This exposes a genuine observational limit. We can sequence the descendants of a successful partnership and recover the dependencies that partnership produced. Earlier failed partnerships leave no readily identifiable genomic lineage. A full history must therefore allow for experiments in symbiosis that never became ancestors of extant eukaryotes. Their possible existence is an inference about the space of evolutionary paths, not a claim supported by a particular fossil or sequence.

## The nucleus and the missing intermediate

We have explained the mitochondrion through bacterial ancestry and the host through archaeal ancestry. What about the nucleus? Modern eukaryotic DNA is separated from much of the cell by a double membrane pierced by intricate transport pores. The nuclear envelope connects to an elaborate internal membrane system. Proteins from varied evolutionary sources perform these functions. No single fossil records the appearance of the nuclear envelope, and no universally accepted sequence derives every part of it from a particular ancestral membrane.

There are plausible scenarios involving the growth and reshaping of internal membranes, or folds and contacts between partners. Proteins controlling membranes and cytoskeletal filaments have archaeal antecedents; much of the chemistry of contemporary membranes also bears bacterial contributions. Phylogenetic relationships constrain which components could have been available. They do not provide a direct movie of which structure formed first.

This is where the question of explanation becomes especially precise. We have high confidence that all known modern eukaryotes share a deep history, that mitochondrial ancestry is bacterial, and that the eukaryotic host is closely tied to archaeal lineages. We have substantial evidence about the ancestry of many molecular systems. We have lower confidence in the exact physical mechanism of the merger, the order of internal innovations, the age of the first eukaryotic cell and the initial ecological setting. There may have been intermediate forms that left no surviving descendants. Their absence limits how sharply we can distinguish several plausible histories.

The lesson is neither that the entire story is settled nor that all histories are equally plausible. Different hypotheses must explain the same genes, organelles, membranes, observed physiology and geological constraints. Some have gained support from recent molecular reconstructions; others must be revised. The origin of complex cells was a biological transition whose surviving molecular fingerprints are remarkably informative. The precise steps of that transition remain a research problem.

## Companion: What a cell merger can prove

| Evidence | What is directly examined | Historical inference | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mitochondrial genomes, ribosomes and proteins | Organelle DNA, sequence homology, cell division, targeting pathways | Mitochondria descend from a bacterial endosymbiont | Exact closest living bacterial relative and timing uncertain |
| Asgard-related proteins and phylogenies | Sequenced archaeal genomes, cultured cell morphology | Eukaryotic host lineage shares deep ancestry with Asgard archaea | Gene trees, horizontal transfers, limited cultured lineages |
| 2025–2026 gene-origin and duplication models | Modern gene-family distributions and calibrated trees | Host complexity plausibly preceded mitochondrial integration | Gene date need not equal structure date; clock and reconciliation assumptions |
| Proterozoic fossils and steranes | Fossil cells, sedimentary chemistry, geological dates | Eukaryotes had appeared by certain rock ages | Taxonomic ambiguity, preservation, contamination, older unsampled lineages |

**Dependency graph:** Molecular homologs → candidate gene family history → ancestral function reconstruction → proposed chronological ordering. Every arrow introduces additional assumptions.

**Dating caveat:** If a gene duplication predates mitochondrial integration in a model, that is evidence about the history of the gene family. Inferring a functioning modern organelle at the same date needs independent cell-biological premises.

### Questions for study (five-second pause each)

1. What observation strongly supports a bacterial origin of mitochondria? (A) Their modern shape alone (B) Their own DNA and bacterial homologs (C) Their location near the nucleus. **Answer: B.**
2. Why does the most recent common ancestor of extant eukaryotes fail to date the first eukaryote? **Answer:** The lineage and its innovations may predate that ancestor, with intermediate branches extinct.
3. What does dating a gene duplication establish directly? **Answer:** An evolutionary relationship and a model-calibrated age for the duplicated gene family, rather than a fossil observation of a whole cellular structure.
4. Name one observation that could discriminate early- versus late-mitochondrion models. **Answer:** Better-resolved gene family histories combined with reliable relative dating of cytoskeletal or membrane-system innovations, checked against comparative cell biology.
