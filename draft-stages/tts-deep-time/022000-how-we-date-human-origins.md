# How We Date Human Origins

Imagine excavating a fragment of a jaw from a cliff. It carries a few teeth, and the shape of the teeth looks strikingly human. The question that arrives first is deceptively simple. How old is it? Answering requires more than putting the fossil into a machine. The jaw might have lain under a volcanic ash fall. Its teeth might contain traces of radioactive elements acquired after burial. It might have been washed from one sediment bed into another. And even if we learn its age, there is a second question. Does its anatomy place it inside the lineage we call Homo sapiens, or near that lineage, or on a neighboring branch?

The two questions are related, but they can disagree. A fossil can be dated with substantial confidence while its species assignment is disputed. A specimen may have an apparently diagnostic face but a differently shaped braincase. A scientific date can be revised without changing the fossil, because a volcanic layer previously associated with it has been correlated with a different eruption. The deep-time evidence ledger must therefore distinguish the physical object, its geological context, the age calculation, and the evolutionary claim.

## Time lives in the surrounding sediments

Picture three layers of sediment stacked above one another, laid down undisturbed. The lower layer generally formed before the upper layer. That is relative dating, and in many excavations it is the first thing that can be established. Yet it needs geological checks. A burrowing animal can move material downward. A stream can cut into an old deposit and refill the channel with younger sediment. Cracks can introduce a later object into an earlier horizon. An excavation map must show where each object occurred in three dimensions, not merely which objects came out of the same trench.

An archaeologist watches the boundary between layers, the grain sizes, the direction of water transport, and evidence of disturbance. A fossil found inside a coherent deposit is evidence of a different quality from an isolated tooth eroded onto the surface. The difference is not about how impressive the tooth looks; it is about whether the chain from object to date can be reconstructed.

Now suppose the fossil-bearing layer lies below a volcanic ash. If the ash fell after the fossil was buried, the fossil must be at least as old as the ash. A date of two hundred thousand years for the ash therefore establishes a minimum age for the fossil, not an exact fossil age of two hundred thousand years. Reversing that inequality would invert the history. If an older ash lies below the fossil, it may supply an upper age bound, if the sequence is intact. The combination can bracket the fossil between two events.

An ash layer can be recognized by its mineral grains and its characteristic chemical fingerprint. But similar ash can fall from different eruptions, or the same volcanic material can be redeposited long afterward. That is why geochemical correlation, mineral dating, sedimentary context, and independent checks must agree before a single number is attached to a specimen.

## Radiometric clocks date events, not labels

Many volcanic minerals contain unstable isotopes that decay at known rates. A potassium-bearing mineral may allow potassium–argon or argon–argon dating. The laboratory measures isotope ratios and uses a calibrated decay process to estimate how long the mineral has behaved as a reasonably closed system. It does not directly read a calendar date printed inside the crystal. The calculation requires assumptions about initial composition, loss or gain of isotopes, and the event that reset the clock.

Think of an hourglass that started running when a lava flow cooled enough to trap its daughter isotope. If reheating later let gas escape, the hourglass may have been partly reset. A mineral in a volcanic ash can date the eruption or crystallization history, provided the grains are truly associated with the event being investigated. A mineral inherited from an older rock can yield an older age than the ash deposit that contains it. This is one reason laboratories date multiple grains and examine the spread of results.

Other methods date different events. Thermoluminescence can estimate the time since a piece of flint was sufficiently heated to empty trapped charge. Optically stimulated luminescence can estimate the time since certain mineral grains were last exposed to light before burial. Electron spin resonance can use radiation-induced signals in tooth enamel, but translating that signal into age requires a model of radiation dose through time and uranium uptake. Uranium-series dating can provide bounds in suitable carbonates or bones, subject to open-system behavior. None of these clocks is interchangeable without examining the physical event it dates.

The first useful mathematical idea is exponential decay. If a parent isotope has a half-life of one million years, after one half-life half the original parent atoms remain; after two, one quarter remain. The general calculation uses a decay constant and the ratio of parent to daughter isotopes, with corrections for any original daughter and later exchange. In an ideal closed system the age follows from this ratio. In a real rock a laboratory also reports uncertainty from analytical precision, standards, decay constants, and the geological model.

An error bar is meaningful only once we know what it represents. A quoted uncertainty may cover repeated laboratory measurement while excluding the possibility that the sediment layer was wrongly correlated. Statistical precision is not a guarantee against a mistaken geological interpretation. If the supposed clock started at the wrong event, reporting another decimal place does not solve the problem.

## Morocco and the problem of a species beginning

Consider the fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco. A 2017 study dated heated flint artifacts associated with the human remains using thermoluminescence, finding a weighted age of about three hundred and fifteen thousand years, with an uncertainty of thirty-four thousand years. A complementary uranium-series and electron-spin-resonance estimate from a tooth was consistent within its uncertainties. The associated anatomy combined faces resembling later Homo sapiens with more elongated braincase shapes. The authors placed these remains in an early stage of the Homo sapiens lineage.

That last step is classification, not an isotope measurement. A later researcher could accept the estimated age and argue for a different boundary of the species. Species names classify populations with gradual, branching evolutionary histories; the natural world need not have supplied one clearly marked individual who was the first human. The fossil record samples scattered individuals separated by tens of thousands of years, and researchers disagree about how much variation belongs inside a species.

A different example comes from Omo Kibish in Ethiopia. In a 2022 study, scientists compared the chemical fingerprint of volcanic material above the layer containing the Omo I fossils with deposits from a particular eruption. The correlation led to a minimum age of approximately two hundred and thirty-three thousand years, with its own uncertainty. Notice the logic. The study dated a volcanic event, established that the relevant ash lay above the fossil-bearing unit, and inferred that the fossil was older. It did not directly measure the time of death of Omo I.

These examples also demonstrate why the phrase 'oldest human fossil' should invite questions. Oldest within which taxonomic definition? Oldest dated by which method? Oldest recovered so far, or first member of a lineage? The oldest identified specimen is a minimum age for the lineage's existence; its origin could be older, and most early members may never fossilize or be discovered.

## A new ancestor candidate is still a hypothesis

In January 2026 researchers described hominin fossils from Thomas Quarry I, Casablanca, Morocco, in deposits dated to roughly seven hundred and seventy-three thousand years ago. Their interpretation placed the Moroccan fossils close to the ancestral stock from which later Homo sapiens and related lineages diverged. The age estimate and observed anatomy are concrete evidence. Calling the fossils close to a particular branching point adds a comparative evolutionary model that can be revised as new fossils appear.

A proposed last common ancestor between our lineage and the lineages represented by Neanderthals and Denisovans is not usually excavated as a known individual. Its time is reconstructed from fossils, the distribution of anatomical features, and genetic divergence estimates that themselves require mutation-rate and demographic assumptions. An ancestor can have living descendants in several branches; an ancient specimen resembling that ancestor is not automatically the ancestor itself.

Consider a family photograph of cousins. Some facial features suggest relationships, but a resemblance does not uniquely identify a grandparent. The fossil problem is more difficult because genes may be absent, bone preservation is incomplete, and some traits evolve repeatedly. Inferences should be expressed as competing tree placements with varying support, rather than a parade of named species standing in a straight line.

## Calibration, correlations, and moving targets

Radiocarbon dating enters later parts of this story, but it cannot directly date million-year-old hominins. Carbon fourteen decays too quickly; beyond roughly fifty thousand years, contamination and the scarcity of remaining carbon make direct radiocarbon ages difficult or unreliable. For much older fossils, archaeologists use other chronometers and relative constraints. Even for younger remains, radiocarbon years must be calibrated because atmospheric carbon fourteen concentrations varied. A calendar age is inferred by comparison with independently dated records, such as tree rings and other archives.

Different dates can be concordant without being independent. Two laboratories might use the same mistaken ash correlation. A carbon date from a bone and a luminescence date from its enclosing sediment rely on different physical mechanisms, which is valuable, though the excavation context still links them. Scientists strengthen a chronology by asking what would have to go wrong simultaneously for the inference to fail.

There is also the relationship between time and preservation. A species may have existed for a hundred thousand years before its earliest surviving fossil. Sites erode, submerge, or remain inaccessible. A humid forest may dissolve bone where an arid basin preserves it. Our sampled history reflects geography, fieldwork, politics, geology, and funding as well as the organisms that once lived. We cannot safely equate the place with the oldest fossil to the unique place where the species originated.

The same caution applies to stone tools. A tool-producing action can be dated through sedimentary context. But a technological category is not a species, and tools can outlast their inventors and pass among different populations. Finding fossils and tools in the same broad layer does not prove that those specific individuals made those objects. A stronger attribution may come from repeated spatial associations, very fine stratigraphy, and other ecological evidence, though maker identity often remains open.

## Reconstructing the question we can answer

Let us return to the jaw in the cliff. First we document exactly where it was found and whether the deposit was disturbed. Then we compare fossils and sediments above and below it. We seek directly dateable material and ask which event each clock records. We check laboratory uncertainties separately from assumptions about the deposit. Only then do we compare anatomy and, where possible, molecules to infer the branch of the human family tree to which it may belong.

Suppose the evidence gives us a secure deposit older than two hundred thousand years and a jaw with features shared with several hominin populations. We can report the minimum age and observed features firmly, yet leave its precise species designation unsettled. This is a useful result. Scientific accuracy does not require every question to have the same answer quality.

The central claim is modest but powerful. We can reconstruct much of human deep history because multiple independent clocks, geological relationships, comparative anatomy and later genetic evidence constrain one another. We cannot reconstruct a single precise birthday for our species, and the oldest specimen currently known is only the oldest we have found. ## Companion: from measured ages to evolutionary chronology

| Observable | Directly established | Additional inference | Important failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fossil beneath undisturbed ash | Superposition relationship | Fossil older than dated ash (minimum fossil age) | Reworked ash, erroneous correlation or intrusion |
| Radiometric isotope ratios | Parent/daughter ratio and controls | Cooling/crystallization age | Isotope loss, inheritance or open-system chemistry |
| Heated flint charge signal | Thermoluminescence signal | Time since adequate heating | Partial heating, dose-rate model |
| Buried sediment luminescence | Trapped charge and measured dose rate | Time since sufficiently exposed to sunlight | Incomplete bleaching and sediment mixing |
| Tooth ESR/uranium series | Radiation signal and uranium isotopes | Burial-age model | Changing uranium uptake and environmental dose |
| Fossil comparative characters | Measured dental and cranial anatomy | Taxonomic assignment, ancestral relationship | Convergence, small samples and arbitrary species boundaries |

**Companion equation:** In a simple closed system with no initial daughter product, \(N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda t}\) and \(t=\lambda^{-1}\ln(1+D/P)\). Here \(D\) is daughter product generated by decay and \(P\) is the remaining parent. Real protocols include corrections, calibration and tests of the closed-system assumption. When an overlying tuff is dated to \(T\) years before present, an undisturbed underlying fossil has \(t_{\mathrm{fossil}}>T\); this is a minimum age in years-before-present language.

**Worked logical example:** Ash below the fossil formed at 260 ka; ash above formed at 220 ka. In an intact depositional sequence the fossil's age is between 220 and 260 ka. The two ash estimates must each carry uncertainties; the fossil is not automatically 240 ka.

### Questions for study

1. Why did the Omo I volcanic ash correlation produce a minimum age rather than an exact death date? **Answer:** The correlated ash overlies the fossil-bearing unit; the fossil must predate it if the stratigraphy is intact.
2. What event does luminescence dating of sediment aim to date? **Answer:** The time since mineral grains were last sufficiently exposed to light before deposition, with bleaching and dose-rate assumptions.
3. Can a precise date determine a fossil's species? **Answer:** No. Age and taxonomic assignment are different inferences.
4. Why is the oldest known fossil a minimum bound for the lineage's existence? **Answer:** An earlier origin may have left no fossil yet recovered.
