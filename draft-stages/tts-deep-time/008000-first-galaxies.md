# When the First Galaxies Appeared

A faint point in an infrared image may be a nearby cool star, a dusty galaxy much closer than expected, or a galaxy whose light has traveled for more than thirteen billion years. The image alone does not tell us which. Astronomers begin with an observation as ordinary as it is difficult: a few detected photons, spread across a small patch of a detector. To turn those photons into an account of the first galaxies, they must establish that a source is real, determine its redshift, estimate how much light it emits, and infer what kind of stars or gas produced that light.

Each step changes the kind of conclusion we are entitled to make. This becomes particularly important when we talk about galaxies that seem to have formed only a few hundred million years after the beginning of the standard hot-universe timeline. Were those early galaxies really bigger, older, or brighter than expected? Did they demand a replacement for cosmology, or were the expectations for galaxy formation too conservative? We can answer more by reconstructing one object carefully than by relying on an image caption that says the universe looked impossible.

## How to identify a remote galaxy

Hydrogen absorbs ultraviolet photons at characteristic wavelengths. In a very distant galaxy, the intervening neutral hydrogen also removes much of the light at short rest-frame wavelengths. Because cosmic expansion stretches all wavelengths, the position of this sharp spectral break moves into the infrared at very high redshift. Astronomers exploit that shift by imaging the same sky field through several filters. A source may appear in long-wavelength filters but nearly vanish in a shorter one; such a dropout is a candidate high-redshift galaxy.

A dropout is an efficient screening method, not a secure historical date. A dusty or old nearby galaxy can have a color pattern that mimics a distant one. Emission lines can brighten one filter and distort a photometric estimate. Measurement noise near the detection threshold can also make colors look more extreme. Photometric redshift algorithms compare the observed color sequence to candidate spectral energy distributions, which encode assumptions about stellar populations, gas, dust, and sometimes active black holes.

Spectroscopy is a stronger test. Instead of a few broad filters, a spectrograph disperses light by wavelength. A distinct break, perhaps combined with identified emission lines, gives much stronger evidence of redshift. Multiple features whose wavelengths agree under one redshift are stronger still. Spectroscopy is expensive for faint galaxies, so the largest candidate catalogs are not the same as the smaller confirmed samples.

Take the redshift convention. If a line emitted at one hundred units of wavelength is observed at one thousand five hundred forty-four units, the ratio is fifteen point four four, so one plus redshift is fifteen point four four and redshift is fourteen point four four. That ratio is measured from spectral features; translating it to an elapsed cosmic time needs the cosmological expansion model. It also involves the source's own velocity and local environment, though the cosmological contribution dominates at these extreme redshifts.

## A galaxy called MoM-z14

In January 2026, researchers reported the spectroscopic confirmation of MoM-z14, a luminous source at redshift fourteen point four four. Its designation is merely a catalog name. Its photons were collected by the James Webb Space Telescope, first in broad infrared images and then with the near-infrared spectrograph. The spectrum includes a sharp Lyman-alpha break and approximately three-sigma indications of several ultraviolet emission lines. Taken together, they support the high-redshift identification.

Within the standard cosmological expansion history, redshift fourteen point four four places the observed galaxy at roughly two hundred eighty million years after the start of the hot-universe timeline. That figure is a fitted cosmic age, not an instrument recording two hundred eighty million on a clock. The physical source existed when the photons left it. We see a particular phase of its history, selected by its brightness and the telescope's sensitivity.

MoM-z14 is also luminous in the ultraviolet, with a reported absolute magnitude around minus twenty point two. A negative astronomical magnitude sounds backward because brighter sources have smaller magnitude values. The number is derived by converting apparent brightness into intrinsic brightness with the inferred distance, calibration, and spectral corrections. Its high luminosity suggests active star formation or another energetic source, but the exact stellar mass, star-formation rate, and age require more extensive modeling.

The survey authors estimated that bright sources around redshift fourteen to fifteen may be far more common than many pre-JWST forecasts expected. Their inference involved a relatively small surveyed area and a small number of detections, so it carries substantial uncertainty from cosmic variance, candidate selection, contamination, and population modeling. Nevertheless, even one convincing object at this epoch gives a firm constraint: some galaxies were already producing appreciable light remarkably early in the standard cosmic history.

This is a story about improving predictions as much as finding old objects. The existence of a galaxy this early does not automatically falsify the expanding-universe model. The expansion model supplies its redshift-time conversion. The surprising part is the efficiency and speed with which baryonic gas may have assembled and formed luminous stars in small, growing gravitational halos.

## What does a bright galaxy weigh?

Brightness is not mass. Imagine two stellar populations. One contains many low-mass, long-lived stars; the other has recently formed a smaller number of massive, hot, short-lived stars. The second can dominate ultraviolet luminosity while containing less total stellar mass. Dust can dim and redden the light. Nebular gas can contribute a great deal of emission in particular filters. A growing black hole may add another continuum or line component.

Astronomers use stellar-population synthesis: models of stars across mass and age distributions, combined to predict a spectrum. The models assume an initial mass function, a star-formation history, chemical composition, attenuation by dust, and sometimes bursts or nebular emission. They fit these choices to observed photometry and spectroscopy, then report possible stellar masses and ages. When someone says a galaxy formed half a billion solar masses of stars by a certain epoch, that is a model-dependent result of this fitting process. It should be accompanied by its assumptions and errors.

An especially difficult degeneracy is between age and dust. An old population can be redder because its hot, blue stars have died. A young population can be reddened by dust. At very large redshift, only a restricted part of the rest-frame spectrum may be available, so different histories can explain the same colors. Spectral lines and longer-wavelength measurements help break the degeneracy, but not perfectly.

There is another step before comparing those stellar masses to cosmological predictions. In cold-dark-matter simulations, gas falls into gravitationally assembled halos. The halo mass is not the same thing as the stellar mass, and only a fraction of a halo's ordinary matter becomes stars. Stellar feedback, ionizing radiation, supernova explosions, gas cooling, and the inflow of fresh matter alter that fraction. A bright early galaxy can imply efficient star formation, a burst, a particular dust history, or a difference in the halo population. The same observed brightness may be consistent with several combinations.

## Oxygen where little time has passed

One early galaxy illustrates a different and more direct constraint on stellar history. JADES-GS-z14-0 was identified with James Webb near-infrared observations and subsequently observed by the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, ALMA. In 2025 researchers detected the eighty-eight-micrometer fine-structure emission line of doubly ionized oxygen at an observed frequency that fixed the redshift very precisely, near fourteen point one eight. That strengthens the identification far beyond a broadband-color estimate.

Oxygen is the important part of the story. The standard hot-universe nucleosynthesis produced predominantly hydrogen and helium, with traces of light nuclei. Substantial oxygen requires earlier stellar nuclear reactions, and then a way to return oxygen-rich material to surrounding gas. The observed line shows that chemically processed material existed in that galaxy when its light was emitted. Models of the line and other measured features suggest notable enrichment, perhaps a few to twenty percent of the Sun's overall metallicity, even though the standard cosmic clock allows only about three hundred million years by that epoch.

Be careful with the final percentage. A line identifies oxygen-bearing gas more directly than it fixes the entire metal inventory. Converting line luminosity into an abundance also requires assumptions about ionization, density, temperature, geometry, and radiation field. The inference that stellar processing occurred is strong; the precise enrichment fraction is more model-sensitive.

But the implication is already striking. At least one generation of massive stars had time to form, fuse heavier nuclei, and redistribute them before the photons we observe were emitted. Depending on stellar lifetimes and feedback, that processing could happen relatively fast. The evidence is a boundary condition on the earliest star-formation histories, rather than a complete biography of the first star that formed.

## Where were the first stars?

A galaxy is a structured ensemble of stars, gas, dust, and gravitational mass. The first stars probably formed in smaller progenitors that later merged or grew into the luminous objects we can currently detect. The relevant gas was initially dominated by hydrogen and helium. Without metals and dust, cooling proceeds differently, often through molecular hydrogen. This affects how efficiently clouds fragment and what stellar masses are favored.

The hypothetical first generation, often called Population Three, has not been securely identified as a class by direct observation in a way that fixes its initial mass distribution. Simulations often predict relatively massive stars under some initial conditions, but radiative feedback, gas dynamics, turbulence, and magnetic fields matter. A massive star can live only a few million years, making rapid enrichment possible. The first galaxy we detect need not be the first halo or the first site of star formation.

We have independent evidence for a broader transition called reionization. The universe became mostly neutral after photon decoupling and then was ionized again over a long interval by energetic sources. Absorption in quasar spectra, the visibility of Lyman-alpha emission, and the microwave background's scattering optical depth constrain the timing and duration. Reionization is a population-scale process. No single bright galaxy tells us how many faint galaxies existed, how easily ionizing photons escaped, or what fraction of reionization came from active black holes.

Many reconstructions assign a major role to numerous faint star-forming galaxies that remain difficult to observe individually. The high-redshift bright objects tell us about the luminous tail of that population, while reionization measurements constrain their integrated influence on intergalactic hydrogen. The two archives must be analyzed together if we want to understand the transformation of the cosmic gas.

## What about the early black holes?

Some very distant galaxies show evidence of accreting black holes. Their spectra can include broadened emission lines, compact high-energy continua, or other signatures of active galactic nuclei. If large black holes existed early, how did their seeds form and grow so quickly? Possibilities include remnants of massive early stars, the collapse of dense gas under special conditions, and rapid or episodic accretion. Such possibilities are models to be tested against spectra, luminosities, host-galaxy data, and theoretical accretion constraints.

A bright compact source need not be dominated by a black hole, and an observed ultraviolet excess cannot automatically be converted to a black-hole mass. Conversely, overlooking an active nucleus can make inferred stellar masses too high. Spectral shapes, spatial extension, variability, and line ratios help distinguish mechanisms, although at these distances several diagnostics are faint or inaccessible.

This is part of the reason the first-galaxies story keeps changing. New instruments can improve resolution, add longer wavelengths, or obtain spectra of candidates previously represented by two or three faint points. Some formerly startling objects turn out to have lower redshifts or smaller masses. Others survive scrutiny and demand faster assembly or different astrophysical prescriptions. Scientific revision at this frontier is an expected consequence of progressively measuring more than color.

## What can we conclude?

The evidence supports luminous galaxies within the first few hundred million years of the standard cosmological reconstruction, and at least some already contained gas enriched by stellar processes. Redshifts confirmed by multiple spectral features can be secure; their conversion to ages depends on cosmology. Brightness is well measured once calibrated, but stellar masses, formation times, star-formation efficiencies, and the contribution of black holes are more sensitive to models. A galaxy census from a small observed field must include selection completeness and cosmic variance before becoming a claim about the universe as a whole.

No telescope has yet shown a movie of a galaxy assembling from its first gas cloud. We have snapshots at different redshifts, through filters and spectra, combined with physical models for the intervening history. That is enough to test theories of cooling, feedback, star formation, and structure growth. It is not enough to say that every route to the first galaxies has been uniquely reconstructed.

An instructive final thought is the oxygen line. One measured feature, at a known observed frequency, can constrain a complicated sequence of events: hydrogen collected; stars formed; nuclear reactions proceeded; material escaped or mixed; ionized oxygen emitted light; light crossed the expanding universe. Each part has its own theory and uncertainty. Deep history advances by connecting such traces while keeping the strength of every link visible.

### Equations and reasoning checks

1. Spectroscopic redshift \(1+z=\lambda_{obs}/\lambda_{rest}\), identifying the same spectral feature in both frames.
2. A line emitted at \(121.6\,\mathrm{nm}\) is shifted near \(1.88\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) at \(z=14.44\); observationally the intergalactic Lyman-alpha break is broadened and affected by absorption.
3. Cosmic time \(t(z)=\int_z^\infty [(1+z')H(z')]^{-1}\,dz'\) within a specified homogeneous expansion model; a galaxy's formation time is earlier than its observation epoch.
4. Flux-luminosity relation \(F=L/(4\pi D_L^2)\) for bolometric luminosity distance. Band-limited UV magnitudes additionally require a spectral correction and calibration.
5. A UV luminosity function describes number density per luminosity or magnitude bin. Counts in a finite survey depend on survey volume, redshift completeness, selection function, and cosmic variance.
6. Oxygen-line detection does not uniquely fix \(Z/Z_\odot\): ionization correction, electron density, temperature, and enrichment model enter the inference.

### Study questions and answers

1. **Why is a broad-filter dropout only a candidate?** Dust, line contamination, and low-redshift spectral breaks can mimic a distant source; spectroscopy tests the identification.
2. **What is directly established by the ALMA oxygen line?** Oxygen-bearing gas emitting at the measured transition and a precise redshift; total metallicity is inferred with additional assumptions.
3. **Why can a luminous galaxy have a low stellar mass?** A young burst of massive stars is efficient at producing ultraviolet light, so luminosity alone does not specify total stellar material.
4. **Does a redshift of fourteen mean the stars are fourteen billion years old?** No. It is a wavelength ratio, converted to a cosmic emission time within the expansion model.
5. **Why does reionization require a population argument?** The transition of intergalactic hydrogen depends on the integrated output and escape of ionizing photons from many sources.
6. **What would most reduce model ambiguity?** Deeper spectroscopy across rest-frame features, larger surveyed areas, resolved morphology, and independent long-wavelength data.
