# The Changing Web of Life: Plants, Animals, and Deep Ecology

Stand on a coast in southwestern England and look at the sandstone cliffs near Minehead. Inside the rock are impressions of trees that grew about three hundred and ninety million years ago. The trees were small by the standards of a modern forest. They had forms unlike most familiar trees, and their branches and trunks have been preserved in sediment that also records the surfaces on which they grew. Researchers described this assemblage in 2024 as the oldest known fossil forest, older than a previously recognized site in what is now New York State.

The words 'oldest known' are important. The cliffs establish a dated example of a forest-like environment. They do not give the precise day on which forests first originated anywhere on Earth. New discoveries may extend that record. Even so, the find allows us to reconstruct something more than the anatomy of an isolated fossil: an area of land where multiple plants grew, shed material and influenced sediment together.

An ecosystem is made of organisms interacting with one another and with the physical environment. It includes flows of energy, cycling of matter, food and habitat relationships, and the engineering of surroundings by living things. Deep history asks how those relationships changed as life colonized land, organisms altered the oceans, and new kinds of plants and animals appeared. Reconstructing a whole food web from stone is difficult. Nevertheless, fossils, sediments, pollen, isotopes and living comparative biology together reveal major transformations.

## Land was inhabited before it became green

A familiar simplified picture says that life began in the oceans and later moved onto empty continents. Continental surfaces, however, had been exposed to weathering, microbes and chemical processes long before large terrestrial plants spread. Some ancient soils and sedimentary structures contain evidence consistent with microbial activity. The challenge is distinguishing biological effects from physical and chemical processes that can leave similar patterns.

The early history of land plants has its own clock problem. Small fossil spores appear in ancient rocks, and their walls contain materials that resist decay. Spores may be transported far from the organisms that produced them. Identifying whether they belonged to early land plants requires interpretation of shape, wall structure and the evolutionary histories of spore production. Body fossils of early plants provide different, often younger, information about stems, branching and reproductive structures.

For decades an often-cited picture placed the origin of land plants around the Ordovician, sometimes near four hundred and seventy million years ago, based in part on early spores. In October 2026 a study reconstructing the evolution of spore characters argued that the interpretation of certain fossil spores as direct calibrations needed revision. Its model placed land-plant origins closer to four hundred and fifty-seven to four hundred and fifty million years ago. The paper does not erase older fossil grains; it changes which ancestral characteristics and evolutionary relationships are inferred from them. Different calibrations and molecular-clock methods may still disagree. This is an excellent example of how a fossil date and a lineage-origin estimate are related but distinct measurements and models.

Plants living on land needed ways to resist drying, exchange gases, reproduce and acquire minerals. Cuticles, protected reproductive stages, stomata, transport tissues and roots emerged in different combinations over time. Fungi and microbes also played important roles in the movement of nutrients through soils. Symbiotic relationships between roots and fungi have modern analogues, though direct claims about the first such partnership require fossil or phylogenetic evidence.

When plants acquired more effective vascular systems, they could move water and dissolved substances through taller bodies. Wood and extensive roots changed the mechanical possibilities for growth. Over geological time, vegetation helped create soils, trap sediments, alter river banks, and affect the weathering of rocks. Because silicate weathering can consume atmospheric carbon dioxide over long periods, plant-driven changes in weathering may also influence climate. The magnitude of those effects is reconstructed from geochemical records and models, not read directly from one preserved leaf.

## A Devonian forest is a system

The Minehead forest gives us a scene we can investigate. Its trees, commonly interpreted as Calamophyton or closely related cladoxylopsids, had relatively narrow trunks with branching crowns. Their geometry and preserved distribution indicate groups growing on a landscape affected by sediment movement. Related Middle Devonian forest sites in New York preserve roots, trunks and other remains showing that plants were beginning to reshape ground surfaces on a scale larger than a patch of moss.

A fossil forest tells us about density, size and the association of specimens. Sedimentary layers record the setting. Root traces indicate how plants penetrated and stabilized the ground. But reconstructing canopy cover, rainfall, total biomass or every animal visitor takes additional assumptions. A particular plant's existence can be high-confidence; a detailed painted landscape may remain partly speculative.

Animals also colonized land in stages. The record includes arthropods in ancient terrestrial or near-shore settings, and later evidence of terrestrial vertebrates derived from fish lineages. Adaptations to life outside water involved locomotion, breathing, reproduction and water balance, not one universal transition. Some lineages returned to water, while others became increasingly dependent on terrestrial environments.

Consider a footprint. It proves that something with a suitable moving appendage contacted the surface when the trace formed, if the attribution and depositional interpretation are sound. It does not always identify the exact species or tell us whether it spent its entire life on land. Likewise, a fossilized jaw or limb can constrain feeding and movement but cannot on its own establish a complete habitat preference. The strength of a historical reconstruction grows when trackways, body fossils, sedimentary setting and anatomy reinforce one another.

## Oceans changed too

Much of complex life's evolutionary history unfolded in the sea, and the marine record lets us observe ecological transformations that land fossils alone would miss. Shell-building animals modify carbonate budgets and create habitat. Reef-building organisms change the physical landscape of shallow water. Burrowing animals disturb seafloor sediments and affect oxygen and nutrient exchange. Plankton transport organic material through the water column. The rise and decline of particular groups therefore changes the environment experienced by many others.

Marine fossils also show turnover in ecological roles. After major extinction events, surviving organisms expanded into vacant settings, formed new communities, and sometimes took on feeding or habitat-building functions performed earlier by unrelated lineages. An ecological role can persist across the disappearance of particular species, although the replacement communities differ in their details. This makes it important to distinguish taxonomic history, which follows named lineages, from functional history, which follows feeding, movement and environmental effects.

Chemical sediments provide a parallel archive. Carbon and oxygen isotope ratios, together with specific elemental and mineral proxies, can indicate changes in carbon cycling, temperature or redox conditions. Such interpretations need calibration and checks for alteration after burial. A shift in a carbon isotope curve does not map uniquely to a change in plankton productivity. It might reflect carbon sources, burial, circulation or several processes working together.

Food-web reconstructions use different types of evidence. Gut contents can directly demonstrate that one organism consumed another, but they are rarely preserved. Bite marks, coprolites, tooth shapes and functional anatomy constrain diets. Stable isotopes in suitable materials can sometimes help estimate trophic relationships. Modern relatives offer hypotheses about ecology, though ancient groups may have behaved differently. Combining these observations can support a network of interactions, but the links vary in certainty.

## The world of flowers arrived in stages

In a modern meadow, flowering plants support pollinators, herbivores, seed dispersers, soil organisms and predators. It is tempting to imagine that one revolutionary new plant group instantly created this familiar world. The fossil record indicates a more extended transformation.

Flowering plants, or angiosperms, diversified substantially through the Cretaceous. Pollen, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds give different forms of evidence. Their evolutionary origin may precede the earliest unambiguous fossil flowers. A 2026 study integrating fossil occurrences with molecular dating inferred a latest-Jurassic origin for angiosperms, whereas their major documented expansion appears later. That inference depends on calibration and evolutionary models. A 2025 review of terrestrial vegetation concluded that the rise to angiosperm dominance occurred in stages across the Cretaceous and beyond, rather than all at the moment of origin.

Pollen is especially useful because its walls can survive long after flowers and leaves decay. Large sediment samples allow researchers to count and classify pollen forms through time. Even here, abundance has traps: some plants produce vastly more wind-dispersed pollen than others, and transport and burial affect what reaches the sample. Changes in the proportion of fossil pollen are evidence about vegetation, but they are not a perfectly calibrated census of the plants growing above the basin.

Interactions with insects also have a deeper history than flowers. Many insects visited, fed on, or pollinated non-flowering seed plants before angiosperms spread. A 2024 study examining insect-family fossil histories found that flowering plants had effects on both extinction and origination of insect groups, but the timing was not one simultaneous mutual diversification. A 2025 review and later work similarly emphasize that complex plant–insect reproductive relationships existed with gymnosperms well before modern flower-rich ecosystems.

A fossil insect with pollen attached can support a close association. Specialized mouthparts may imply a type of feeding. To infer coevolution, however, scientists need to distinguish correlated dates from reciprocal evolutionary effects. Flowering plants might diversify because of pollinators, pollinators because of plants, both because of changing climate, or each through several interacting causes. Phylogenetic trees and comparative models help test these possibilities without resolving every link uniquely.

## Evolution has no single upward direction

The words 'more complex ecosystem' can be useful but can also hide the question of what exactly increased. Species richness, network connectivity, total energy use, structural habitat diversity and number of feeding levels are different quantities. A rainforest and a microbial mat can both have intricate interactions, while differing enormously in structure. Losing one large-bodied species may change the function of an ecosystem more than losing several species whose roles overlap.

The history of life is full of evolutionary convergence: unrelated lineages develop similar functional solutions under similar physical demands. Streamlined swimmers, hard protective structures, burrowing strategies, and plant-like forms have arisen in different lineages. Convergence can make it easier to reconstruct the function of an extinct organism from its anatomy, but it can mislead ancestry if we assume every similar feature was inherited from a recent shared ancestor.

Ecologists and paleontologists use networks to organize these claims. Nodes might represent species or functional groups; connections might represent feeding, competition or symbiosis. The fossil record is incomplete, so most reconstructed ancient networks contain hypothetical links. That is acceptable when we explain which links come from direct evidence, which come from anatomy or chemistry, and which are imported from comparative models. A beautiful diagram with many arrows is still a model if few of those arrows are observed.

## What the ancient landscape can tell us

Return to the Devonian coastline. We can inspect preserved trunks and sediment layers. We can measure their geologic context and compare the plants' anatomy with related fossils. From these observations we infer a landscape occupied by stands of early trees, and we can model how vegetation affected sediment and soil. We have much less direct knowledge of the light beneath every crown or the sound of animals moving among the trunks.

The same ordering applies to marine food webs and flower–insect associations. A dated fossil supplies a minimum age and a physical trait. A repeated association of fossils suggests ecological relationships. Genetic data and phylogenetic methods can reconstruct older divergences. An Earth-system model explores how organisms altered climate and sedimentary cycles. The chain grows more powerful when independent evidence converges, but each added step also creates new assumptions.

Across hundreds of millions of years, life transformed planetary surfaces and the conditions for later life. Plants contributed to soils and atmospheric cycling; animals reshaped sediments and habitats; marine organisms built reefs and recycled nutrients; flowering plants and insects formed new networks. We can recover the broad timing and many mechanisms with considerable confidence. The exact origin of some lineages, the strength of individual environmental feedbacks, and the detailed interactions in any ancient habitat remain partly unresolved. That is enough evidence for a rich history, provided the reconstruction keeps its sources and its limits visible.

## Companion: Deep ecology requires several archives

| Evidence | Direct measurement | Reconstruction | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ancient spores and pollen | Preserved grains in dated sediments | Land-plant presence, vegetation changes, lineage calibrations | Transport, morphology and evolving spore characters |
| 390 Ma Devonian forest | Trunks, roots, branch impressions and sedimentary context | Forest structure and terrestrial sediment engineering | Missing canopy, climate and food-web details |
| Marine body and trace fossils | Shells, skeletons, burrows, reefs, gut contents where preserved | Habitat engineering, feeding and ecosystem turnover | Strong sampling and preservation biases |
| Geochemical time series | Isotopic ratios, mineral and elemental concentrations | Climate, weathering and biogeochemical changes | Multiple processes can share proxy signatures |
| Cretaceous pollen and angiosperm fossils | Pollen morphotypes, flowers, leaves and seeds | Timing of flowering-plant diversification and ecological spread | First occurrence differs from origination and abundance |
| Fossil insects and flowers | Anatomy, pollen associations and time-calibrated lineages | Ancient pollination networks and coevolution candidates | Functional analogy and correlated causes |

**Conceptual network:** An ecological reconstruction can be represented as a directed graph \(G=(V,E)\). A feeding edge \((u,v)\) means organism or functional group \(u\) consumes \(v\). Fossils constrain only some nodes and edges; adding unobserved edges requires explicit assumptions. The graph is a model, not a direct field census.

**Date distinction:** An ancient spore at a measured horizon constrains the age of the organism producing it if its identity is defensible. The origin of the land-plant crown group is a phylogenetic inference that can move when ancestral spore characters or calibration rules change.

### Questions for study (five-second pause each)

1. What does the Minehead fossil forest directly show? **Answer:** Dated trees and related traces in a sedimentary setting that supports interpretation as a forest; not a complete census of its ecosystem.
2. Why might fossil pollen abundance misrepresent plant abundance? **Answer:** Different pollen production, transport, decay and burial probabilities.
3. Does insect pollination begin with flowering plants? **Answer:** No. The fossil record supports insect interactions with non-flowering seed plants earlier.
4. What is the difference between an ecological network edge observed as gut contents and one inferred from modern analogues? **Answer:** The former is direct evidence of feeding; the latter is a hypothesis that needs to be labeled and tested.
