# A Light Cone Is Not a Time Machine: Reading Redshift and Cosmic Distance

Suppose a telescope records a faint smudge with two dark lines in its spectrum. The lines have the same spacing pattern that hydrogen produces in a laboratory, but the whole pattern lies farther toward the red. A second telescope confirms the positions. We have learned something quite precise about the light that arrived. Have we learned how far away the source is? Have we learned how old it was when it emitted that light? And have we learned how old the universe was at the time? Those questions sound interchangeable until we try to answer them.

There is no cosmic ruler marked off in light-years along the line of sight. Light reaches us only from events whose signals have had time to arrive. In a changing universe, the distance between the source and us changes while a photon travels. The signal carries a wavelength, a direction, an arrival time, and some information about its source. We ask geometry and physics to recover the rest. That recovery is an astonishing achievement, but the different quantities must be kept separate.

## One line, two measurements, and a hypothesis

Every atom permits particular transitions between its quantum energy levels. When radiation of a corresponding wavelength passes through gas, the gas can absorb that light and leave a dark line in a spectrum. In practice, astronomers identify several lines together, correct for the telescope's wavelength scale, and ask whether one consistent displacement explains the pattern. A single ambiguous notch is rarely enough.

Imagine a laboratory feature at five hundred nanometers. The corresponding feature in a distant galaxy appears at seven hundred fifty nanometers. The observed wavelength is one and a half times the laboratory wavelength. Astronomers call the fractional shift redshift, and in this case it is one half. We can express the observational calculation in a sentence: take the ratio of the observed wavelength to the reference wavelength, then subtract one. A ratio of one and a half produces redshift zero point five.

This calculation is almost embarrassingly simple compared with the machinery that gives it historical meaning. It depends on identifying the atomic feature correctly, calibrating the spectrograph, and correcting for the relative motion of the observer. It does not depend on selecting an age for the universe. Nor, by itself, does it distinguish recession motion, gravitational effects, and the stretching of light during cosmic expansion.

A nearby moving source produces a Doppler shift. A photon climbing out of a gravitational potential can also be redshifted. In the standard large-scale description of an expanding universe, however, the scale of spatial separations changes while the light travels. The wavelength stretches in step with the cosmological scale factor, so the observed-to-emitted wavelength ratio equals the scale factor today divided by its value at emission. In that description, redshift zero point five means the scale factor at emission was two thirds of today's value. It does not mean that the galaxy was two thirds of its present physical size.

The model adds a global statement to a local spectral comparison. We should neither forget the model nor dismiss the independently measured spectral line just because an interpretation is required.

## Our view is a light cone

Imagine standing beside a clock and taking a photograph of the sky at noon. The clock reads noon only at the observer. The nearest star contributes light that has traveled years. A distant galaxy contributes light that may have traveled billions of years. The image puts photons arriving together on one screen, although the events that produced them happened at very different times.

The set of past events capable of sending light to an observer now is called a past light cone. A light cone is a geometric limit on what could have been observed, given the speed of light and the intervening geometry. It is not a magical video archive with every epoch available. Some events lie beyond our observable past. Other events sent radiation that was absorbed or scattered before reaching us. The early universe was opaque to ordinary light long before galaxies appeared.

Consider a signal that took five billion years to reach us. Its lookback time is five billion years. But the proper separation from the source at emission, the separation today, and the distance inferred from its brightness are generally three different quantities. During the flight, the universe may have continued expanding. The galaxy can now be much farther away than five billion light-years, even though the journey lasted five billion years. There is no contradiction with light moving at its local speed. The distance between remote comoving locations is a property of the changing geometry; it is not the measured speed of a spacecraft passing a nearby observer.

This is also why a phrase like 'thirteen billion light-years away' needs a definition. Does it refer to light-travel distance, present-day proper distance, or a luminosity distance? Popular descriptions sometimes switch between them without warning. Astronomers do not have to switch. They calculate distinct quantities, each useful for a different task.

## How to calculate a date from redshift

What additional information is needed to turn the observed redshift into a lookback time? We need the expansion rate at every stage of the photon's trip. It is usually written as a function of redshift, the Hubble parameter of that epoch. The lookback-time calculation adds up the travel intervals across a succession of small redshift intervals, accounting for the changing expansion rate. To obtain the distance between comoving locations, a related calculation adds up the corresponding increments of light travel.

The important difference is easy to hear. Both calculations depend on the history of expansion, but the time calculation carries an extra correction for how the clock rate and scale factor relate along the trajectory. If two cosmological models predict different expansion histories, they can assign different ages and distances to the same observed spectral shift.

At low redshift, a useful approximation says that recession speed is proportional to distance. We call the constant of proportionality the local Hubble constant. If a galaxy is sufficiently near that cosmic expansion dominates over its local motion, measuring distance and redshift helps estimate that constant. But a galaxy can also move relative to its neighbors because gravity draws structures together. These peculiar motions introduce scatter and bias in nearby samples. One does not recover the expansion history by measuring a single galaxy's redshift and drawing a straight line to the origin.

We can now understand a critical limitation of astronomical timelines. A spectacularly precise redshift may correspond to a less precise date. The spectrograph can be well calibrated while the conversion from redshift to time remains conditional on cosmology. A table of galaxy ages needs to say what background model supplied the conversion.

## Building a distance ladder from nearer objects

Our strongest local distances begin with geometry. As Earth moves around the Sun, a nearby star appears to shift against more distant objects. The tiny angular shift is parallax. The baseline is known from orbital geometry. To leading order, distance is inversely proportional to the parallax angle. A star with half the measured parallax of another, observed with comparable geometry, is roughly twice as distant. More distant stars have smaller parallaxes, so the result becomes sensitive to small instrumental biases.

Missions such as Gaia measure stellar positions with extraordinary precision, but their catalogs still require careful treatment of parallax offsets, source quality, and correlations. A star's catalog distance is not simply a number printed by a camera. It is an estimate from measured angles, uncertainties, and often a statistical prior. When a parallax is noisy, directly taking its reciprocal can itself produce biased distances.

Farther out, geometry alone becomes difficult. Astronomers use objects whose intrinsic luminosity can be inferred from other observables. Henrietta Leavitt's work on Cepheid variable stars helped reveal a relation between pulsation period and luminosity. A star that pulses with a measured period can be assigned an estimated intrinsic brightness after calibration. Compare that intrinsic brightness to the light received at Earth, correcting for dust and other effects, and one can calculate a luminosity distance.

Here is a small audible calculation. If two otherwise identical lamps have known luminosity, and one looks one hundred times fainter, the inverse-square law puts it ten times farther away. A factor of one hundred in brightness is the square of ten in distance. This reasoning is clean for a nearby static situation. In cosmology, expansion changes photon energies and the arrival rate, so the relevant quantity is specifically luminosity distance. The inverse-square concept still operates, but the distance has a cosmological definition.

Type Ia supernovae extend this process. These explosions have broadly related light-curve shapes and peak luminosities. They are standardized using empirical correlations and calibrated in galaxies where Cepheids or other anchors can be studied. The supernova can then act as a bright indicator in a far more distant galaxy. Each rung adds information, and each rung can add systematic error: crowding in a Cepheid image, dust extinction, differences among stellar populations, supernova selection, photometric calibration, or assumptions about the distribution of light-curve properties.

A disagreement between local-ladder and early-universe estimates of the Hubble constant does not identify which physical assumption failed. It is a challenge to locate that failure, potentially in measurement, calibration, modeling, or cosmological theory. In 2026 the Hubble tension remains an open problem. Its significance depends on which samples and analysis assumptions are compared.

## An independent ruler made of ancient sound

Standard candles are one kind of measurement. Another approach looks for a preferred separation between galaxies rather than a known luminosity. In the early hot plasma, radiation pressure pushed against gravitational compression, creating acoustic waves. The waves could travel only a finite distance before baryons ceased to be tightly coupled to photons. That history leaves a preferred comoving scale, often called the sound horizon.

Later gravitational evolution turns the imprint into a slight statistical preference for galaxies to be found a characteristic distance apart. There is no visible ring around each galaxy. We must analyze very large samples and count pairs of galaxies at different separations. A small bump in the correlation statistics reveals the scale. It is called a baryon acoustic oscillation, or B A O, feature.

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, DESI, has used millions of galaxy and quasar spectra to map that feature over cosmic time. Its second data release yielded measurements based on more than fourteen million galaxies and quasars. Those observations constrain transverse and line-of-sight distances relative to the sound horizon. This is an important qualification: the survey measures angles, redshifts, and statistical clustering. A distance in megaparsecs emerges once the physical ruler is calibrated by early-universe physics or by another inference chain.

That calibration is the bridge connecting late-time galaxy surveys to early-universe microwave observations. It also creates a place where new physics could imitate a shift in the inferred Hubble constant. Changing the predicted early sound horizon can change the inferred late expansion parameters while leaving the observed galaxy positions the same. Some proposed resolutions of the Hubble tension work in precisely this part of the inference chain, but they must also preserve the acoustic structure of the microwave sky and the success of primordial nuclear calculations.

The contrast is useful. A Cepheid or supernova ladder asks how bright a source would be at a known distance. A B A O measurement asks how large an ancient acoustic ruler appears at a given redshift. The dominant assumptions differ, so agreement between them is valuable. If they disagree, the difference can reveal where the model needs refinement; it does not automatically mean every distance estimate is unreliable.

## A test that tired light cannot easily evade

In the early twentieth century, some cosmologists proposed that light might gradually lose energy as it crossed enormous distances. That mechanism could make remote spectra redder even without an expanding universe. It is a serious historical reminder that one kind of observation can admit multiple explanations. But an explanation is judged by all the observations it predicts.

Suppose a distant supernova goes through its recognizable rise and fall. In an expanding universe, the observed duration should be stretched by the same factor that stretches its wavelengths: one plus redshift. A supernova at redshift one should, after accounting for intrinsic light-curve differences, appear to evolve over roughly twice the observer's time compared with an otherwise similar nearby event.

A simple tired-light mechanism that removes photon energy while leaving the source's clock unchanged does not produce that time stretching. The Dark Energy Survey analyzed the light curves of more than fifteen hundred Type Ia supernovae and found a dilation exponent very close to one, the expansion prediction. This does not make every imaginable static-universe model logically impossible. A complicated alternative could try to imitate the timing as well as the wavelengths. It must then explain the thermal microwave background, galaxy populations, and the B A O scale too. A successful hypothesis cannot merely fit the one datum that first motivated it.

Other proposed tests use surface brightness and the aging of astronomical populations. Their interpretation requires careful corrections for how galaxies evolve, their internal structures, and observational selection. We should avoid claiming that one oversimplified historical test, considered in isolation, settled all of cosmology.

## Selection turns a telescope into a biased historian

Look at a deep-field image and notice the tiny galaxies visible only because they are exceptionally bright or happened to fall in a sensitive band. If we move the same population farther away, many members drop below the detector threshold. This is a selection function: the probability that an object with particular properties enters our catalog.

A flux-limited survey preferentially finds luminous objects at large distance. Dust hides some sources. Redshift moves an emission line out of one detector's wavelength range and into another. Strong gravitational lensing can magnify a source, but lensing itself changes which sources are discovered. Spectroscopic redshifts tend to be more secure when strong features can be identified. Photometric redshifts use broad-band colors and statistical templates instead, which can give ambiguous solutions.

If we observe that the most distant galaxies in a catalog are very bright, we cannot immediately infer that all early galaxies were extraordinarily bright. The survey may be seeing only the rare, detectable tail of their population. Astronomers model survey completeness, simulate mock catalogs, and obtain deeper or spectroscopic follow-up observations. The discipline here is the same as in archaeology: what survives, or what is detected, is not necessarily representative of what once existed.

In cosmic history there is another source of selection. We occupy only one location in the universe and receive information only from our past light cone. Large-scale statistical inferences assume our observable region is sufficiently representative, a principle supported by many observations but not directly checked outside the observable universe. We can use a model to discuss regions beyond our horizon, yet we cannot claim to have observed them.

## What our distances justify

We can now return to the faint galaxy with its displaced hydrogen lines. We can say exactly how the observed wavelengths compare with laboratory wavelengths. We can interpret their displacement as expansion within a physical model that passes a great many independent tests. We can estimate a lookback time and a present-day distance after specifying the expansion history. We can compare the result against candles, rulers, and other observations. We can also identify the assumptions most likely to shift the final numerical answer.

None of those steps requires pretending that the inference is a direct measurement. Nor is a distance estimate just a guess because it requires theory. It is a constrained reconstruction. Its reliability depends on calibration, multiple independent routes, and the survival of predictions that could have failed.

The sky gives us a layered archive: wavelengths, shapes, brightness, timing, angles, and correlations. The past light cone tells us which events we could possibly receive. Cosmological dynamics tells us how those signals relate to a changing geometry. The next mystery is already visible inside this architecture: the oldest light we can measure directly is not a picture of the earliest instant. It is a radiation field carrying the marks of an earlier plasma, and its apparent simplicity hides an enormous amount of structured information.

### Mathematics and distances

1. Observed redshift: \(z=(\lambda_{\rm obs}-\lambda_{\rm em})/\lambda_{\rm em}\). Under FLRW expansion, \(1+z=a_0/a_{\rm em}\).
2. Flat FLRW line-of-sight comoving distance: \(D_C(z)=c\int_0^z dz'/H(z')\); lookback time: \(t_L(z)=\int_0^z dz'/[(1+z')H(z')]\). Units and assumed \(H(z)\) must be specified.
3. Flat FLRW relation \(D_L=(1+z)D_C\), while \(D_A=D_C/(1+z)\); more generally use transverse comoving distance \(D_M\). Hence \(D_L=(1+z)^2 D_A\) if photon number is conserved and light follows metric null geodesics.
4. For a luminosity \(L\) and observed bolometric flux \(F\), \(D_L=\sqrt{L/(4\pi F)}\). 'Standard candle' means calibrating or standardizing \(L\), not directly measuring distance.
5. B A O primarily yields \(D_M(z)/r_d\) and \(D_H(z)/r_d\) where \(D_H=c/H(z)\). An independent determination of \(r_d\) is needed to express absolute distance in length units.
6. A simple redshift test: rest-frame duration \(\Delta t\) is observed as \((1+z)\Delta t\) for cosmological expansion; redshift 1 gives a factor 2.

### Study questions with answers

1. **Can redshift 1 tell you the photon's travel time without a cosmological model?** No. It fixes an observed wavelength ratio; the time comes from the integrated expansion history.
2. **How can a present-day distance exceed light-travel distance?** Because the proper separation between comoving locations grows while light travels; neither implies locally superluminal motion of the photon.
3. **Why does a survey not directly measure the BAO ruler in megaparsecs?** Its primary observables are angular separations and redshift differences; converting to a physical length uses distance assumptions and a ruler calibration.
4. **What would falsify simple tired light?** Time dilation of distant transients proportional to 1+z, in addition to CMB and other evidence.
5. **Why do some catalogs overrepresent luminous early galaxies?** A flux threshold selects intrinsically bright objects at large distance, plus wavelength and lensing selection.
