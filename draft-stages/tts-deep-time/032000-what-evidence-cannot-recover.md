# What the Evidence Cannot Currently Recover

Picture a person walking on a shoreline twenty thousand years ago. The sand briefly takes the shape of a foot. Most such tracks vanish under wind, water or other footsteps. On one shore, at one extraordinary moment, sediment covers them and begins a long process of preservation. The person keeps walking. They speak to someone outside the frame of what survived, perhaps about food, the weather, a child or a private worry. We might discover the track and date its geological setting. The conversation is gone.

It would be easy to say that there is an infinite amount we do not know about the past. That is true but not very useful. A more interesting question is whether different kinds of missing information can be distinguished. Some evidence may still be buried. Some may have been destroyed. Some could not have reached us because of physical laws. Some questions are underspecified: multiple historical explanations make the same predictions for every observation presently available. And some questions are formulated in ways that no surviving object could ever answer.

These distinctions determine whether a research program can make progress. Excavating deeper might find a previously unknown artifact. A better detector might reveal a faint signal. More samples might distinguish two population models. But no improved telescope can recover an ordinary visible-light photograph from before the universe became transparent, and no DNA sequence can directly tell us what language a particular prehistoric person spoke. Different limits call for different forms of restraint.

## The horizon of a telescope

Astronomy offers a clear example of a limit imposed partly by physics. A distant galaxy appears to us by light that has traveled for a very long time. Telescopes receive photons, measure their energies and directions, and interpret those observations with models of space, expansion and astrophysical sources. Looking far away lets us observe earlier stages of cosmic history, though not every stage.

In the hot early universe, free electrons scattered photons so frequently that radiation could not travel unimpeded across large distances. As expansion cooled the plasma and neutral atoms formed, the universe became effectively transparent to the radiation that now appears as the cosmic microwave background. Its measured spectrum and structure contain evidence about the hot universe and its earlier fluctuations. But ordinary electromagnetic observation does not give us an image of what lay behind that scattering surface.

This is an observational boundary, not a proof that nothing occurred earlier. Models can extrapolate to higher temperatures, and processes before transparency can leave effects on later observables. In principle, relic neutrinos or gravitational waves could provide other channels, although extracting early-universe history from them presents major practical and model-dependent challenges. We must distinguish 'not directly visible in this messenger' from 'logically unknowable'.

There is also a cosmological horizon. We cannot currently receive information from events outside our past light cone. The observable universe is a region defined by what signals could have reached us under cosmic history, not necessarily the totality of all space. Statements about the universe as a whole, especially beyond the observable region, therefore involve additional assumptions about large-scale structure. Even perfect measurements within one observable patch cannot uniquely map every possible arrangement outside it.

A cosmological model may estimate the universe's age to a fraction of a percent under its assumptions. That precision cannot be transferred automatically to a claim about what caused cosmic expansion, why the constants take their observed values, or whether a larger reality existed before the hot phase. These questions are open to different degrees. We should name which observation could separate proposals, and which proposed answers lack an observable distinction at present.

## The disappearing rocks

The Earth has a different kind of archive: minerals, sedimentary layers, deformation patterns and fossils. But the planet itself continually edits that archive. Erosion removes layers. Metamorphism changes mineral structures. Subduction carries crust into the mantle. Sediments can be reworked into younger deposits, preserving old grains in contexts that no longer record their original environment.

A zircon grain can retain information about its crystallization age even after the rock containing it has been transported and redeposited. That makes it a valuable clock, but the old age of the crystal need not be the age of the present sediment or of an environmental event we wish to date. A claim about early oceans inferred from zircon chemistry requires assumptions linking a chemical signal to the conditions in which the grain formed. As rocks vanish, the range of viable reconstructions may remain wider than the instrumental error of the remaining crystals.

The oldest surviving crust is a sparse and geographically selective sample. Most of Earth's first several hundred million years does not survive in an undisturbed, continuous terrestrial record. Researchers use planetary isotope systems, meteorites and ancient minerals to piece together parts of that era. These are meaningful constraints, but the absence of a complete early landscape means that some questions, such as the detailed arrangement of the earliest continents, might admit many histories consistent with all existing samples.

The limits also differ by material. A volcanic ash layer can provide a numerical anchor for surrounding sedimentary strata. Fossils may date a minimum age for a species or lineage. A fossil's oldest known appearance cannot establish that the lineage originated at that moment. We are looking at a surviving and discovered sample from a once-continuous biological history.

## The fossil record and the missing body

Imagine a species that lived for a hundred thousand years, mostly in forests where acidic soils dissolved bone. It might have been common and ecologically important and still leave no fossils that researchers will ever find. Another species may be represented by hundreds of shells deposited in an environment ideal for preservation. Counting preserved specimens is not equivalent to counting ancient individuals, species or ecological importance.

This selection affects evolutionary trees. A tree based on surviving DNA includes only sampled descendants. Extinct lineages may have branched between the surviving ones without leaving sequenced material. Genetic methods can infer ancestral relationships, gene transfers and episodes of mixture, but their reconstructions depend on mutation models, taxon sampling and the assumption that the analyzed segments carry informative historical signals.

A common ancestor inferred from living organisms is a node in a reconstruction of shared descent. It need not have been the first living thing, the only organism alive at its time, or a single organism that would satisfy every modern category we attach to life. An estimated date for a deep ancestor is conditional on the clock and calibration scheme. The oldest fossil and the inferred ancestor answer different questions; neither reveals a full day-to-day account of the organisms involved.

Consider a tempting but invalid inference: 'We found no fossils of this animal in a layer, so it was absent from the region.' To evaluate it, we need to know how well its body fossilizes, whether it inhabited the depositional environment, how much of the layer has survived, and the extent of the search. If an animal rarely leaves preservable remains, searching ten more exposures might not help much. If a distinctive hard shell is almost always preserved in that sediment, a systematic absence may be stronger evidence. Absence becomes informative only with a model of detection.

## A simple calculation about absence

Suppose a hypothetical archaeological site contains an artifact class that would be detectable in one well-examined sample with probability one in five, if the activity of interest actually occurred. Assume for the moment that each sample is independent and that the detection probability is the same. The probability of missing the artifact in all five samples is four fifths multiplied by itself five times, roughly one third. Five failures to find it therefore would not decisively rule out the activity.

Now imagine an artifact that would be detected with probability nine in ten in each independent sample. Missing it five times under the same assumptions would be extremely improbable. The negative evidence would be much stronger. Notice what changed: not the number of empty samples, but the expected detectability of the trace if the proposed activity happened.

Real archaeological samples are not independent draws from a jar. Entire sites can be destroyed, one excavation trench can miss a specialized activity area, and some materials are preferentially recycled. The point of the calculation is to expose the assumptions behind a confident claim of absence. It does not license assigning invented detection probabilities to a poorly understood ancient practice.

The same reasoning applies beyond archaeology. If a particle detector is sensitive only above a certain interaction strength, a null result constrains models predicting signals in that region. It does not rule out all possible particles. If a telescope surveyed only bright galaxies, a failure to find faint ones may reveal a selection limit rather than a cosmic absence. The quality of negative evidence depends on the experiment's coverage and failure modes.

## Different pasts, the same surviving evidence

There is a deeper difficulty than a poor sample. Sometimes two causal histories lead to the same observations. In statistics, this is a problem of identifiability. Imagine an imported stone found in a village. One traveler may have carried it from the source, or it may have passed through five exchanges. If both routes produce the same final stone, the observation by itself cannot distinguish them, no matter how accurately we measure the stone's chemistry.

The way forward is to find evidence on which the routes differ: intermediate workshops, distribution gradients, associated objects, wear patterns or repeated movements over time. Sometimes such traces have survived; sometimes they have not. When no discriminating observation exists, adding decimals to a chemical fingerprint cannot supply the missing social history.

Similarly, a genetic pattern may be consistent with several sequences of migration and mixture. Additional ancient samples from a missing region or century can separate models, but a model can also remain non-identifiable if multiple demographic histories produce indistinguishable sampled genomes. Researchers can compare likelihoods conditional on assumptions, report sensitivity to unsampled populations, and avoid mistaking the best-fitting simple model for the uniquely true genealogy.

Historical interpretation presents a related but distinct challenge. A burial with valuable goods might reflect inherited social rank, the affection of survivors, an exceptional ceremony or other meanings. Archaeology can test related predictions about inheritance, diets and household inequality. It cannot guarantee access to what the mourners privately believed. Human intentions sometimes leave statements and actions that constrain interpretation; many intentions leave no recoverable trace.

There is a difference between 'we do not yet know' and 'the existing record underdetermines the answer.' The first is an honest status. The second identifies an information problem. Some underdetermination can be reduced by new data; some may persist because the relevant alternatives produced the same surviving traces. A strong research plan specifies which category a question currently occupies.

## Archives that have not been read

Knowledge can also be blocked by access. A manuscript may be buried, hidden in a dispersed collection, damaged, unreadable, written in a script nobody has convincingly deciphered, or restricted because it contains sensitive community knowledge. Different obstacles require different responses. Recovering an archive may involve new excavation, conservation, digitization, a bilingual inscription or collaboration with those who transmit its meaning.

The Herculaneum papyri show how one boundary can move. The carbonized scrolls were physically known long before their texts could be recovered computationally. In 2026, researchers described substantial new text detected through tomography and virtual unwrapping. It would have been mistaken to conclude a century earlier that all those words were permanently destroyed. But the same success should not lead us to assume that every lost manuscript survives somewhere to be scanned.

Nor does access always mean that publication is appropriate. Historical inquiry can intersect with sacred places, remains, genetic materials and information held within living communities. Ethical limits on destructive sampling and disclosure are real considerations, separate from questions of what could technically be extracted. The existence of a possible measurement does not create an unrestricted right to make it.

## How to assign confidence without pretending to know a probability

It is helpful to keep three labels separate: the observation, the reconstruction, and the open alternative. We directly measure a spectrum, isotope ratio or DNA fragment, subject to calibration. We reconstruct the past by asking what physical and historical processes produce those traces. And we compare other processes that might do likewise.

A conclusion has stronger support when multiple lines of evidence converge for different reasons. The hot early universe is supported by expansion observations, microwave radiation and light-element abundances. A radiometric age becomes more persuasive when stratigraphic relationships and another clock agree. A migration scenario becomes stronger when ancient DNA, archaeological dates and geographical patterns fit together. These are constrained inferences, not arbitrary guesses.

Yet confidence does not come in one number for an entire narrative. We can be confident that the early universe was once denser and hotter while remaining uncertain about inflation's physical cause. We can be confident that life existed billions of years ago while lacking a verified account of its chemical origin. We can be confident that people crossed a particular Ice Age shoreline while knowing almost nothing about the words they spoke.

The key danger is false precision about a conditional inference. A model might assign an age of 13.8 billion years with a narrow interval. That is not the same as a comparably precise date of an absolute beginning under every conceivable cosmology. Another danger is false humility: saying 'we know nothing' because a deeper question remains open. Evidence has excluded entire classes of explanation and constrained many others. Those achievements survive the admission that some questions are beyond present methods.

## What questions should we ask tomorrow?

Consider four kinds of future work. First, better calibration: improving the relation between a measured signal and an inferred time, environment or event. Second, new observations: finding a sedimentary layer, fossil, ancient genome, archived letter or faint signal that existing surveys missed. Third, competing mechanisms: designing tests that differ between models fitting current data. Fourth, clearer historical questions: asking whether the evidence addresses migration, ancestry, language, belief, technology or political authority rather than sliding between them.

A defensible claim should be accompanied by a plausible route to checking it or a clear account of why checking is presently impossible. A non-mainstream model can earn attention by predicting something testable, while an established model should be open to experiments that might reveal its shortcomings. Proposals that cannot distinguish themselves from known explanations by any observation currently imagined belong in a different epistemic category from a mechanism with successful independent tests.

There will also be historical details that no future laboratory can retrieve. Most people who ever lived left no preserved written words. Much of Earth's surface has been recycled. Many extinct organisms left no identifiable descendants or fossils. And light from some cosmic events has never reached us. These losses should shape the questions we ask without erasing the immense amount that can still be learned.

Return to the footprint. We may establish the age of its sediment, the environmental conditions of the shore, the direction of a trackway and the presence of other animals nearby. New surveys might reveal more footprints or a neighboring camp. But a person once walked away from that impression into a life of which only scattered traces could endure. The most accurate history gives those traces their full evidentiary force and leaves the rest unfilled. It is rich enough to tell a genuine story, and honest enough to say where the story ends.

## Companion: a taxonomy of limits

| Limit | Example | What could help? | What the result cannot justify |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Instrumental sensitivity | Undetected faint galaxy or particle interaction | Better detector, new wavelength or exposure | That the entity is absent in every regime |
| Messenger / causal horizon | Electromagnetic last-scattering surface and finite past light cone | Other messengers or model-dependent relic constraints | Direct visible-light observation from an opaque earlier plasma |
| Destruction / recycling | Missing early terrestrial crust | Surviving zircon, meteorites, other planetary samples | A unique reconstruction of all vanished landscapes |
| Preservation / sampling bias | Missing fossils or archives | Targeted search, preservation model, independent traces | Equating absence of discoveries with absence of activity |
| Non-identifiability | One obsidian object and multiple possible trade routes | Intermediate sites, wear, temporal distributions | A unique social pathway from one chemical fingerprint |
| Human intention / missing voices | Political meaning of a burial | Contextual records, comparative archaeology, descendant testimony | Certainty about every private belief |

**An illustrative detection model.** If an event produces an independently detectable trace with probability \(p\) in each of \(n\) sampling opportunities, then under that *simplified* model

\[P(\text{no detections}\mid\text{event})=(1-p)^n.\]

For \(p=0.2,n=5\), the no-detection probability is \(0.8^5\approx0.328\). For \(p=0.9,n=5\), it is \(10^{-5}\). The dramatic difference comes from specified detection conditions. Archaeological exposures commonly violate the independence and uniform-detection assumptions; the expression is a teaching model, not a field estimate.

**Identifiability.** If two historical models \(H_1,H_2\) induce the same distribution for every accessible observation \(D\), so \(P(D\mid H_1)=P(D\mid H_2)\), then no test using only those observations can choose between them. New types of data or assumptions are required. This formalizes the difference between more precise measurement and greater inferential leverage.

### Questions for study

1. Why can the CMB constrain earlier history while failing to photograph the earlier plasma? **Answer:** Earlier processes affect later radiation, but photons could not freely propagate through the opaque plasma to form a direct image.
2. Why is the oldest known fossil only a minimum age for a lineage? **Answer:** The lineage existed when the fossil was deposited; earlier members may not have fossilized or been discovered.
3. What makes an absence scientifically informative? **Answer:** A well-specified observation process in which the proposed event would probably have left detectable evidence.
4. Give an example of two histories consistent with one artifact. **Answer:** Obsidian can arrive by one long journey or several exchanges; both yield the same geological source at the destination.
5. Why isn't a small model-fit error bar a complete measure of historical confidence? **Answer:** It may omit uncertainty from model choice, sampling, context, calibration and alternative mechanisms.
