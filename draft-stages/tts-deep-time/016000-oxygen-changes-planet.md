# Oxygen Changes the Planet

Stand on a modern hill, take a breath, and look around. Oxygen surrounds us in a concentration that supports large, energy-hungry animals and allows fires to burn. Now imagine a time when Earth's atmosphere contained hardly any free oxygen, even though the planet already hosted living organisms. That is the setting for much of early biological history.

A familiar story says that photosynthetic microbes invented oxygen, oxygen poured into the air, and an oxygen-rich world began. The broad direction is sound. The timing and mechanism are less straightforward. Oxygen can be produced and immediately consumed. Ancient rocks give us indirect chemical records, with measurements that respond to different oxygen concentrations and sometimes to different environments. There is no preserved sample of air from two and a half billion years ago that can be placed in a modern gas analyzer.

Our problem is to reconstruct the difference between the origin of an oxygen-producing process and the long-delayed accumulation of its product in the atmosphere and oceans. We need a ledger of sources and sinks, a set of geological observations, and the discipline to refrain from turning a threshold crossing into an exact percentage.

## A gas that can be made and consumed

Modern oxygenic photosynthesis uses light to move electrons, ultimately extracting them from water and producing molecular oxygen. The carbon stored in organic matter represents a chemically reduced product. If that carbon later reacts with oxygen, through respiration or other oxidation, much of the oxygen production can be effectively canceled over the complete cycle. Persistent oxygen accumulation requires that reducing power be removed from contact with oxygen or that other changes decrease the capacity of oxygen sinks.

Imagine a shallow microbial sea. Each sunny day an organism releases oxygen. Nearby dissolved iron reacts with it. Volcanic gases and reduced minerals consume more. Organisms also respire, and organic debris decomposes. At sunset, a sensor placed near the microbial community might have recorded some oxygen, while an imaginary sensor in the global atmosphere would detect very little. Oxygen production and atmospheric oxygenation are different observables.

Over geological time, burying some organic carbon prevents it from recombining promptly with oxygen. Burial of reduced sulfur can similarly change the redox accounting. Conversely, volcanic release of hydrogen-rich or sulfur-rich gases, oxidative weathering of old organic rocks, and reactions with reduced iron can consume oxygen. Plate tectonics, the emergence of continents, nutrient supply, erosion and volcanic activity all influence the terms.

The simplest bookkeeping equation says that atmospheric oxygen increases when effective sources exceed effective sinks. It does not say why the balance changes. Small shifts in a large flux can accumulate over millions of years, and the response can be nonlinear. This is the first reason it would be a mistake to ask only, 'When was photosynthesis invented?'

Some forms of photosynthesis can use chemicals such as sulfide instead of water and do not release oxygen. We therefore cannot assign oxygenic photosynthesis to every ancient microbial mat merely because it was phototrophic. Genetic clocks and biological comparisons suggest that oxygen-producing organisms may predate the main atmospheric oxygenation interval, but early fossil organisms rarely preserve diagnostic metabolic machinery. The exact innovation date is contested.

## A strange signature in sulfur

Sulfur has several stable isotopes, and chemical reactions separate them by slightly different amounts. Most familiar chemical fractionations depend approximately on isotope mass. Under certain atmospheric photochemical conditions, however, ultraviolet radiation can produce sulfur isotope patterns that do not follow that simple mass-dependent relationship. Researchers call this mass-independent fractionation, often shortened to sulfur MIF.

Ancient sedimentary sulfide and sulfate minerals preserve such patterns. Before the major oxygenation transition, large sulfur MIF signals are common. Later they largely disappear from the sedimentary record. This is one of the most powerful clues we possess because the photochemical pathways and the survival of their products depend on atmospheric composition, including oxygen and its effects on ultraviolet chemistry.

Think of the signature as a tracer of a class of atmospheric states. If the atmosphere were oxygen poor, particular ultraviolet-driven reactions could create and preserve sulfur isotope anomalies. When oxygen increased past certain model-dependent thresholds and atmospheric chemistry changed, that record became hard to maintain. The disappearance is strong evidence of a change in the atmosphere. It is not a direct measurement showing, for example, that oxygen was precisely two percent at an exact date.

The lower threshold often discussed for the disappearance of sulfur MIF is around one millionth of the present atmospheric oxygen level, though modeling choices can shift the estimate. That sounds vanishingly small compared with modern air, and it is. A chemical record can signal an important regime change even while the absolute oxygen concentration remains far below what most of today's animals could tolerate.

Geochronological calibration of the layers containing and lacking this signal places the onset of the Great Oxidation Episode around two point four three billion years ago in current syntheses. Rather than a single instant, studies describe a complicated interval extending toward about two point two billion years ago. Some records may reflect crossings back and forth near the relevant chemical threshold. A sharp isotope boundary in one formation need not mean every part of the atmosphere and ocean changed instantly or permanently.

A 2026 study exploring nutrient control of oxygenation used improved sulfur-isotope chronologies to assess the onset and dynamics of this transition. Like earlier work, it treats the sulfur record as a calibrated constraint on atmospheric chemistry, then uses a model to examine which environmental feedbacks could generate the observed changes. The isotope measurements and the proposed nutrient mechanism have different evidentiary status.

## Iron, uranium, and a patchwork ocean

Imagine rain falling on fresh rock. If atmospheric oxygen becomes more abundant, iron, sulfur and other elements are oxidized and their solubility or transport behavior changes. Some iron-rich sediments preserve large deposits of iron oxides. Such banded iron formations are important archives of water chemistry and microbial or inorganic redox reactions. But their existence alone cannot tell us how oxygen rich the whole atmosphere was. Iron can be oxidized by processes other than globally abundant oxygen, and deposition depends on local basins and nutrient flows.

Another clue comes from minerals that are unstable under strongly oxidizing conditions. Grains of pyrite or uraninite can survive transport in sufficiently oxygen-poor waters and air, but tend to alter under some oxygen-rich surface conditions. Their presence or disappearance in sedimentary deposits, when interpreted with sediment transport and weathering context, supports changing environmental redox conditions.

Red beds, sedimentary rocks stained by oxidized iron minerals, give related evidence. But a red color is not an oxygen meter, and red coloration could develop after original deposition. Geologists compare rock fabric, timing of alteration, trace elements, multiple isotope systems and neighboring environments before proposing a global atmospheric transition.

Oceans also had an uneven history. Oxygen can exist in a shallow sunlit patch while deep water remains anoxic. Ferrous iron can be available in one basin while sulfide dominates another. A 2025 study of persistent surface-ocean oxygenation across the Great Oxidation Event examined how the marine response differed from the atmospheric one. It emphasized that the ocean's timing and degree of oxygenation cannot be read simply from a global atmospheric label.

These archives overlap. Sulfur isotope fractionation restricts atmospheric photochemistry. Oxidation-sensitive detrital minerals constrain weathering and transport. Iron and trace metals indicate marine redox processes. Their strengths and blind spots differ, which makes their combined interpretation more powerful than any one test.

## How large was the event?

The name Great Oxidation Event evokes a large jump. Yet a 2026 review called *Revisiting the greatness of Earth's great oxidation* pointed out that defensible estimates of atmospheric oxygen during parts of this interval span orders of magnitude. Sulfur MIF can impose a lower bound after a transition without determining a precise upper bound. Other tracers and ozone modeling support further inferences, but their answers depend on chemistry, climate and environmental assumptions.

This is a case where very strong qualitative evidence coexists with a wide quantitative uncertainty range. The atmosphere underwent a major and persistent shift relative to the Archean state. Exactly how much oxygen it contained at each point, whether there were transient excursions, and how quickly the oceans responded remain the subjects of active work.

Nor did oxygenation simply climb forever afterward. A 2025 reconstruction based on mass-independent oxygen isotopes and sulfur records argued for a very long transition with generally low and fluctuating atmospheric oxygen over much of the next two billion years. Higher oxygen conditions more like the modern atmosphere were established much later in Earth's history. The human-readable graph of oxygen versus time should therefore be a set of broad uncertain bands, not a clean staircase whose steps are fixed by a few fossil dates.

## Why did oxygen persist at last?

There are several broad proposals. One family emphasizes biological production: increasing oxygenic photosynthesis and changing nutrient availability, especially phosphorus, might have raised effective oxygen production or the burial of organic matter. Another emphasizes falling oxygen sinks, through changes in volcanism, mantle chemistry, hydrogen escape or the supply of reduced material. A third emphasizes feedbacks among oxygen, methane, temperature, erosion, continental emergence and biological productivity. Most realistic models combine some of these ingredients.

To see why the distinction is difficult, consider two hypothetical worlds. In the first, photosynthetic oxygen production stays constant while volcanic reducing-gas emissions fall. Atmospheric oxygen accumulates. In the second, volcanic input remains constant while more organic carbon is buried. Oxygen also accumulates. The same qualitative sulfur-isotope transition could result from very different causal histories. Measurements of volcanic history, carbon and sulfur cycles, nutrient supply and climate might help discriminate, but those records have their own gaps and uncertainties.

Oxygenation could also alter its own sources and sinks. Oxygen in air contributes to ozone chemistry. Methane can be removed through atmospheric oxidation, changing the greenhouse effect and potentially cooling the climate. Weathering can alter phosphorus delivery and ocean productivity. The rock record records glaciations around the broader oxygenation episode, and some scenarios connect oxygen and methane changes with cooling. That is an interesting feedback hypothesis, but matching the timing of two events does not by itself identify which caused the other.

Several 2025 and 2026 reviews now describe the transition as an episode rather than a single switch. Some changes may have started earlier than the conventional boundary; some were geographically local; and some came and went before oxygen accumulated more durably. There may be no unique 'trigger' if the transition was a response to an evolving network of biological and geological fluxes.

## Evolutionary consequences and their limits

Oxygen offers a powerful way to obtain energy from some organic molecules. Yet free oxygen also damages many chemical components, especially organisms not equipped to control reactive oxygen species. Oxygenation could create ecological opportunities for some lineages while imposing severe pressure on others. It changed mineral availability and chemical gradients; it altered habitats even where free oxygen never reached modern levels.

A common simplified narrative says that oxygen caused complex life to appear. Oxygen availability was eventually important to many large organisms and ecosystems. But the rise of complex cells and multicellular organisms occurred in later stages, with their own long and poorly resolved evolutionary histories. The first Great Oxidation Episode should not be treated as a direct fossil record of the first animals, nor as a single necessary and sufficient explanation for the diversity we observe now.

The source of atmospheric oxygen illustrates how biology and geology become inseparable in deep-time reconstruction. Life changes its environment. The environment changes which biological strategies can succeed. Sediments preserve traces of both processes, often superimposed on one another. If we wish to reconstruct the causal sequence, we need an Earth-system model that can generate the archived signals, plus independent checks that distinguish it from competing models.

So what can we state confidently? The early Earth was predominantly oxygen poor compared with today. There were oxygen-producing processes and local oxygenated settings before the atmosphere became persistently more oxidizing. Across the period centered roughly on two point four billion years ago, the sulfur isotope record and other geochemical observations support a profound change in atmospheric and surface conditions. The quantitative trajectory, exact onset of oxygenic photosynthesis and balance of biological and geological triggers remain uncertain.

In our next lecture we will turn from rocks to living genomes. The astonishing persistence of molecular machinery across bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes allows us to infer a shared deep ancestry. But the farther back we use that evidence, the more difficult it becomes to separate the properties of an ancestral population from assumptions built into the evolutionary clock.

**Scale check:** If modern oxygen abundance is one present atmospheric level (PAL), \(10^{-6}\) PAL is one millionth of that level. A tracer changing across a threshold near this scale cannot by itself determine a modern-like atmosphere.
