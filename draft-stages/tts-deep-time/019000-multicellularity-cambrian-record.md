# Multicellularity, the Ediacaran Seafloor, and the Cambrian Record

Imagine walking across a seabed about five hundred and sixty million years ago. There are no forests and no birds overhead. The bottom is covered in microbial films, and some of the large organisms lying on it resemble quilted disks, fronds or branching shapes. One leaves an oval impression with repeated ribs. Another stands above the sediment like a leaf fixed to a stalk. A wormlike animal may move below the surface, pushing sediment aside and leaving a narrow record of its activity.

We can imagine that scene because ancient seafloors occasionally became preserved as rock. We have found body impressions, mineralized parts, microscopic skeletons and tracks left in soft sediment. But no single exposure records the whole living community. A fossil is the result of life, death, burial, chemistry, geological survival, erosion and eventual discovery. The shapes that reach us have already passed through several filters.

The broad story is that animal body plans and ecological interactions diversified dramatically from the late Precambrian into the Cambrian. The phrase 'Cambrian explosion' has been used to describe this change. To understand it scientifically, we must separate biological innovations from their first appearance in the surviving record, and separate the origin of multicellularity from the later expansion of large, mobile animals.

## Multicellularity arose many times

A multicellular organism contains cells that remain associated, divide labor and coordinate development. The simplest cases may be little more than clonal chains or groups. More integrated forms develop tissues and specialized structures. Multicellularity is therefore a set of evolutionary possibilities rather than one indivisible invention.

We know from living lineages that multicellular forms emerged independently in different branches of life, including animals, plants, fungi and several algal groups. Some bacteria also grow as filaments or organized collectives. A change that appears once in a school diagram may have arisen multiple times under different ecological circumstances. Simple multicellularity can evolve without nervous systems, large bodies or elaborate organ formation.

A 2024 study described fossils called Qingshania magnifica from approximately one point six three five billion-year-old rocks in northern China. The specimens are cellularly preserved filaments made of repeated large compartments, with some internal structures interpreted as possible spores. Their size, shape and chemistry support the authors' interpretation that they are multicellular eukaryotes, perhaps related to photosynthetic organisms. The cells and their arrangement are fossil observations; the evolutionary placement is an argument drawn from comparisons. The specimens are strong evidence that fairly organized multicellular forms existed long before familiar Cambrian animals.

Another crucial distinction concerns the animal lineage. Even if a fossil is clearly multicellular, it may be an alga, fungus or member of some other group. Calling something the earliest animal requires additional anatomical, chemical or phylogenetic grounds. Molecular comparisons can estimate when major animal lineages diverged, but those estimates inherit assumptions about mutation rates and fossil calibration. The first date inferred by a clock need not correspond to a preserved animal that we can recognize.

## The Ediacaran archive

The Ediacaran Period runs from about six hundred and thirty-five million years ago to the beginning of the Cambrian, around five hundred and thirty-nine million years ago. Especially in its later portion, some rock formations preserve large organisms as impressions on bedding planes. A well-known example is Dickinsonia, an oval, repeatedly ribbed form. For decades, researchers argued about whether such fossils were animals, giant single cells, lichens or forms with no close living equivalent.

In 2018 a team led by Ilya Bobrovskiy measured ancient steroid molecules associated with organically preserved Dickinsonia fossils. They reported a predominance of cholesterol-related compounds, which supported an animal affinity. That is an elegant bridge between morphology and chemistry. At the same time, assigning a biomarker to the original organism requires handling contamination, burial alteration and possible contributions from neighboring microbes. Subsequent research continues to examine what the lipid signals say about physiology and feeding. The 2018 evidence strengthened an animal interpretation; it did not tell us exactly how Dickinsonia moved, reproduced or acquired food.

Some of the best-preserved Ediacaran communities have few obvious equivalents in modern ecosystems. Their body plans may represent extinct lineages, early branches within familiar groups, or organisms whose anatomy has been flattened beyond easy recognition. We should be wary of drawing an entire classification from a two-dimensional imprint. An organism buried under sediment can be stretched, folded, decayed or pressed into patterns that disguise its original three-dimensional form.

Then there are trace fossils. A burrow, feeding track or series of impressions records an action. A body fossil can indicate what an organism was shaped like; a trace can show where it moved, disturbed sediment or interacted with the environment. Those records answer different questions. An animal that left a trail may never have left a recognizable body. Conversely, a body impression cannot tell us all the behaviors its owner performed.

In 2026 researchers reported the Jiangchuan Biota from rocks of terminal Ediacaran age in southwestern China. The assemblage contains Ediacaran-like forms together with fossils interpreted as recognizable animal groups, including wormlike bilateral animals and possible early deuterostome relatives. This finding narrows some of the apparent gap between the earlier Ediacaran biota and the Cambrian record. The precise affiliations of early animals remain subjects for detailed anatomical comparison, and the term 'oldest' always applies to currently known and correctly dated specimens.

## What changed in the Cambrian?

Near the beginning of the Cambrian, small skeletal remains become more conspicuous in many sediments. Over the succeeding tens of millions of years, deposits reveal a striking diversity of shells, spines, mineral plates, articulated bodies and evidence of animals digging more deeply into the seafloor. Exceptionally preserved fossil sites, including Chengjiang in China and the Burgess Shale in Canada, allow us to see soft tissues that ordinary fossil deposits would rarely retain.

This creates two simultaneous signals. Organisms evolved new ways of feeding, moving, defending themselves and sensing their surroundings. And more of their anatomy became likely to fossilize, especially when hard parts appeared. The observed increase in fossils is partly a biological change and partly a change in the probability that organisms leave evidence. Those causes can act together.

Think about a marine ecosystem before large-scale sediment mixing. Microbial mats may stabilize the seafloor. If more animals begin crawling, feeding and burrowing through those mats, they transform the sediment itself. More niches appear for some organisms while others lose habitat. Predation can favor shells, spines, rapid motion and improved sensing. Those changes can generate feedback: a new predator alters prey populations, their defenses influence predator success, and physical disturbance changes the environment experienced by both.

There is no need to choose one magic trigger. The availability of oxygen matters for some demanding tissues and behaviors, but evidence for seawater oxygenation varies by place and time. Developmental regulatory systems make new arrangements of tissues possible, yet their genetic building blocks can predate the anatomical forms they help build. Ecological feedback, climate, nutrient supply, plate configuration and preservation all contribute to competing explanations. Researchers attempt to distinguish them by asking whether their predicted changes line up in time and space with the fossils and geochemistry.

## A counting problem disguised as a historical event

Suppose a lineage existed in a coastal habitat for ten million years but its members had soft bodies and were rarely buried under suitable conditions. Its first known fossil might occur late in that interval. If a mineralized skeleton later became common, the lineage could suddenly become abundant in the fossil record with little change in the underlying time of origin. This is the difference between a first appearance datum and an origination date.

Paleontologists therefore pay attention to how much rock of the relevant age is accessible, which environments those rocks represent, how intensively they have been sampled, and whether the organism possessed preservable parts. A count of fossil genera can be corrected or modeled for the amount and character of the available record, although no correction perfectly reconstructs lost organisms.

There is an analogous problem in molecular phylogeny. When two living lineages share an ancestor, their divergence must predate the lineages' distinctive later fossils. A molecular clock might estimate an earlier split than the first appearance of either lineage in stone. Such a discrepancy is a prompt to inspect rates, calibrations, fossil assignments and preservation, rather than a reason to declare one entire method invalid.

A particularly revealing test compares several data streams. Are new trace types appearing before their makers' mineralized bodies? Do the same strata show changing sediment textures? Are oxygen-sensitive minerals changing at a comparable time? Do independent regions show a similar sequence, or is the apparent pattern produced by one exceptionally preserved locality? The more those observations align under a causal scenario, the better that scenario performs. The remaining gaps must still be stated.

## Oxygen, development, and ecology as competing tests

One proposed explanation emphasizes oxygen. Active animals with high metabolic demand may require more oxygen than small, slow organisms. If oxygen availability expanded in shallow seas, additional habitats could have supported larger bodies and more active feeding strategies. Researchers test this hypothesis using iron chemistry, sulfur isotopes and other indicators of past marine oxygen conditions. Yet these measurements sample particular depositional environments. An oxygen-rich coastal shelf does not imply an equally oxygenated deep ocean, and global averages can conceal the ecological conditions in which a fossil organism actually lived.

Another proposal emphasizes developmental genetic programs. Related living animals share genes that help establish body axes, specify tissues, and pattern appendages. The existence of conserved systems is evidence of common evolutionary ancestry and developmental capacity. But a present-day gene does not carry a label giving the date when it first generated a particular anatomical structure. Evolution can modify where and when an old gene is expressed. To move from comparisons among living genomes to an ancient event, we need phylogenetic assumptions, molecular-clock calibrations and links to anatomy that may be preserved only incompletely.

A third family of explanations considers ecological escalation. Predation could favor better detection, harder coverings and faster movement. Burrowing could aerate sediment and alter which microbes survived at the surface. Specialized feeding could open opportunities for yet more specialization. A 2026 study tried to estimate how far early motile animals could respond to environmental cues by combining quantitative trace-fossil patterns with simulations of foraging behavior. Its estimates depend on the assumed behavioral models; the fossil itself records a path, not a nervous system. Still, the work demonstrates that a trace fossil can constrain a mechanism beyond mere presence or absence.

These proposals can reinforce one another. Oxygen may permit a demanding behavior, developmental changes may make it physically possible, and ecological competition may favor its spread. To tell which sequence occurred, researchers need fossil and environmental time series with enough chronological resolution to distinguish cause from response. The evidence is improving, but a single fossil bed cannot identify every environmental and evolutionary interaction.

## Was the explosion really an explosion?

Geologically, a change spread over millions or tens of millions of years can be relatively rapid. Biologically, that span allows countless generations. 'Explosion' is a name for an apparent evolutionary radiation, not a claim that fully formed animals materialized in a single day. It also need not mean the animal lineages originated entirely within the Cambrian. The Ediacaran record, dated traces and molecular clocks supply reasons to expect deeper roots for at least some of them.

The deeper evolutionary question is how much of the Cambrian diversification represents the arrival of new genetic developmental capacities, the assembly of older capacities into new bodies, ecological release, or changing preservation. Different groups may have different answers. The geological record constrains this debate through dated fossils and environments, while the genomic record contributes models of lineage divergence and functional evolution.

Now return to the imagined Ediacaran seafloor. The impression of a quilted organism is real stone. The claim that it belonged to a particular lineage depends on anatomy and chemistry. The idea that changing oxygen conditions helped animals spread is a causal hypothesis tested against chemical and ecological evidence. And the complete landscape, with every organism present and every interaction, remains unrecoverable.

This is the scale at which the history of life must be told. We can reconstruct broad changes in body form, behavior and ecosystems with substantial confidence, yet disagree over their exact order and drivers. The record has made major evolutionary transitions visible while leaving us the job of explaining how the record itself was made.

## Companion: A first fossil is a constraint, not an origination clock

| Archive | Direct observation | Historical inference | Principal confounder |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.635 Ga Qingshania | Large multicellular filaments, cellular structures | Early organized eukaryotic multicellularity | Affinity and function inferred from morphology and chemistry |
| Ediacaran Dickinsonia | Dated impressions and associated steroid signatures | Supports an animal placement for at least some Ediacaran organisms | Alteration, contamination, taxonomic inference |
| Trace fossils | Burrows, tracks, sediment deformation | Behavior and ecological engineering | Uncertain maker identity; similar traces can be produced by multiple animals |
| Cambrian shelly and soft-bodied deposits | More anatomical diversity, hard parts, rare soft-tissue preservation | Animal diversification and ecological transformation | Rock availability, sampling, preservational change |
| 2026 Jiangchuan assemblage | Co-occurrence of late-Ediacaran forms and animal-like fossils | A more continuous record across the boundary | Difficult early-animal phylogenetic placements |

**Minimum-age logic:** A dated fossil attributable to lineage L establishes that L existed by that time, subject to dating and identification. It does not establish that L originated on the day that fossil was deposited.

**Sampling illustration:** If a fossil is preserved with independent probability \(p\) at each of \(n\) suitable opportunities, the probability of at least one record is \(1-(1-p)^n\). Even an old lineage may have zero observed fossils when preservation is rare; the independence assumption is only illustrative.

### Questions for study (five-second pause each)

1. Does multicellularity refer to a single evolutionary invention? **Answer:** No; independent multicellular lineages evolved repeatedly and with different degrees of integration.
2. What extra evidence helped evaluate whether Dickinsonia was an animal? **Answer:** Steroid molecules associated with the fossils, analyzed with contamination and alteration in mind.
3. Why can the first fossil of an animal group appear later than that group's actual origin? **Answer:** Preservation and sampling are incomplete, and early members may lack recognizable hard parts.
4. How could a burrow provide ecological information unavailable from a body fossil? **Answer:** It records movement, feeding or sediment disturbance and thus a behavior in a particular environment.
