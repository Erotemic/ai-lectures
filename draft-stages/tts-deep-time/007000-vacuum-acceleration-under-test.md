# Does the Vacuum Accelerate? The Expansion History Under Test

A distant exploding star brightens and fades. Its spectrum indicates what sort of explosion we have found. The wavelengths of its spectral features indicate the redshift of its host galaxy. After the light curve has been measured, standardized, and corrected, its apparent brightness helps estimate how far away it is. Put many such explosions at many redshifts, and they reveal the geometry of cosmic expansion.

In the late nineteen nineties, two teams studying Type Ia supernovae found that the distances did not match the simplest expectation for a matter-dominated universe whose expansion continually slows under gravity. The pattern favored an accelerating expansion. A term already present in Einstein's equations, the cosmological constant, could produce that behavior. It was a compact explanation, and subsequent microwave, galaxy-clustering, and gravitational-lensing results made a cosmological model containing matter and a cosmological constant remarkably effective.

But is the underlying cause literally the energy of empty space? And is that energy density constant in time? These are different questions from whether the expansion has accelerated. The observations inform the history of distances and growth. A physical explanation requires assumptions about gravity, matter, and the relation between measured light and cosmic geometry.

## A distance ladder built from fading light

Type Ia supernovae are thermonuclear stellar explosions. Their peak luminosities are related to the shape and color of their light curves. That relation lets astronomers standardize them, though not treat every supernova as an identical bulb. First they measure flux in calibrated bands across time. Then they fit the light curve, account for the filter through which rest-frame light has been shifted, correct for dust and population effects, and use the host-galaxy redshift to locate the event on a distance-redshift diagram.

The raw facts are thousands of measured fluxes, spectra, dates, and calibration observations. The distance modulus on the final diagram is already a processed estimate. It depends on the supernova luminosity model, selection effects, photometric calibration, and a reference luminosity. To infer cosmic acceleration from the diagram we also specify a metric description of cosmic expansion, commonly a homogeneous and isotropic spacetime at large scales.

Picture a universe filled only with ordinary gravitating matter. Expansion begins fast and slows. For a fixed redshift, its predicted distance-redshift relation differs from that of an accelerating universe. The supernovae tend to be fainter, and therefore farther in the standard interpretation, than the simple decelerating model predicts. Large samples now establish this acceleration signal strongly within the usual metric framework. The Dark Energy Survey's five-year supernova analysis, for example, found evidence for present acceleration above five standard deviations under its adopted flat cosmological model.

Yet distance is an integral record of expansion. Light accumulates its travel through a changing universe. One distance measurement cannot tell us the instantaneous acceleration at every past epoch. A good model must fit an entire redshift sequence; many flexible expansion histories can reproduce similar integrated distances over limited ranges. The argument for acceleration draws strength from the consistency of independent probes and the coherence of the fitted shape, rather than from a single faint supernova.

## What does an accelerating scale factor require?

In a homogeneous expanding universe, the scale factor is a bookkeeping device for the changing distance between observers carried with the cosmic flow. If the scale factor doubles, wavelengths carried along that flow double. Acceleration means the second derivative of this scale factor with respect to cosmic proper time is positive. It does not mean that every galaxy is being pushed through space by a force, or that a galaxy at a fixed distance necessarily speeds up at a fixed rate.

General relativity connects acceleration to both energy density and pressure. Matter with negligible pressure makes expansion decelerate, as does ordinary radiation. An ingredient with sufficiently negative pressure can make the scale factor accelerate. For a cosmological constant, pressure is the negative of its energy density, in units where the speed of light is one. That relation lets its energy density stay constant as space expands.

We often call the corresponding component dark energy, but that label combines things with different meanings. A cosmological constant is a particular term in the gravitational field equations. A slowly varying scalar field is a dynamical physical model. An effective expansion term arising from changing gravity is another possibility. Measurements of the expansion history can constrain all three, but a numerical fit to the scale factor does not by itself identify a field, vacuum mechanism, or modification of gravity.

There is also a theoretical problem. Quantum field theory includes contributions to vacuum energy, but a naive extrapolation of those contributions to gravitational energy density gives a scale badly mismatched with the observed acceleration. Explaining why a cosmological constant has its measured small value is an open theoretical question. It would be a mistake to treat a successful parameter in a phenomenological model as a solved microscopic derivation.

## A ruler left by the early plasma

Supernovae offer relative distances after standardization. Baryon acoustic oscillations provide a different tool. Before neutral atoms formed, gravity and pressure produced sound waves in the photon-baryon plasma. As that plasma changed, the waves left a characteristic comoving separation imprinted in the later distribution of galaxies and gas.

Observers do not see a line painted across the sky. They measure redshifts and angular positions of many galaxies or absorption features in quasar spectra. They count pairs at different separations and find a slight excess around a characteristic scale. Across the sky, its apparent angle constrains a transverse distance divided by the physical ruler length. Along the line of sight, its separation in redshift constrains an expansion-rate-related distance divided by the same ruler. Calibration of that ruler requires early-universe physics and assumptions about the baryon and radiation contents. That dependence is useful because the early microwave and primordial-element evidence can cross-check it.

DESI, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, has collected enormous samples of galaxy and quasar spectra. Its March 2025 second-release cosmology analyses used nearly fifteen million objects from three years of observations. Combined with microwave data and supernova compilations, some fits favored a dark-energy equation of state that changes with redshift over a constant value. Depending on which supernova sample and model parameters were selected, reported nominal preferences ranged from around three to a little above four standard deviations.

That phrase, standard deviations, needs care. A reported preference is a comparison of fit quality under a specified parameterization, covariance treatment, and combination of data sets. It does not directly measure a new field. The same set of observations can give different preference strengths under different combinations and calibrations, and a more flexible model can fit noise or unrecognized systematics. Bayesian comparisons also penalize added parameter freedom and can be less decisive than a change in best-fit chi squared suggests.

## A 2026 correction to the narrative

The expansion story is actively changing. On July thirtieth, 2026, DESI published a further analysis of the Lyman-alpha forest: absorption features made by intergalactic hydrogen along the lines of sight to remote quasars. Rather than extracting only the sound-wave peak, that work analyzed the shape of correlations and their anisotropy, called the Alcock-Paczynski effect.

Its measurement at redshift about two point three three gave a more precise independent geometric constraint. The new central result moved toward the standard cosmological-constant prediction relative to the earlier Lyman-alpha BAO-only analysis. In that paper, the preference for an evolving equation of state was about two point seven sigma with DESI and microwave data, and about three point two sigma after adding supernovae. The specific value pertains to those data and assumptions, and is not a universal current verdict on dark energy.

Here is what makes the July result important for historical reconstruction. Two analyses of related cosmic tracers use different information and make overlapping but nonidentical assumptions. Their outcomes can shift the apparent strength of evidence for a new physical component. The 2025 hints have not vanished, but neither can we say dark energy's evolution has been discovered. A 2026 review explicitly emphasized calibration, supernova selection, and data-combination effects. Such concerns demand quantitative tests, rather than a choice between either dismissing all tensions or declaring a revolution.

The next decisive step is to ask whether the pattern survives alternative standardizations, independent sky regions, new supernova samples, and growth-of-structure measurements. A genuine new physical effect should make successful predictions outside the observations used to fit it.

## The Hubble tension is a related but distinct problem

The Hubble constant is the present rate of expansion, expressed in kilometers per second per megaparsec. One method infers it from the microwave sky, using a fitted early-universe model to evolve forward to today. A widely cited Planck result in the six-parameter cosmological-constant model is about sixty-seven point four kilometers per second per megaparsec.

A second approach builds a local distance ladder. Geometric calibrators set the scale of nearby Cepheid variable stars; Cepheids help calibrate Type Ia supernovae; a larger sample of supernovae then relates distances to redshifts in the cosmic expansion flow. A 2025 analysis from the SH0ES team incorporating further Hubble and James Webb telescope work reported a value around seventy-three point two with uncertainty below one in the same units. James Webb's sharper infrared images have been used to test whether blended stars in Hubble images biased the Cepheid distances, without finding a bias large enough to remove that team's tension.

There are additional local calibration routes. The Chicago-Carnegie Hubble Program uses, among other methods, the brightness of the tip of the red giant branch. Its 2025 status report gave an estimate around seventy point four with combined uncertainties of several kilometers per second per megaparsec for a broader tip-of-the-red-giant-branch calibration, while some JWST-only subsamples gave lower central values. The details of sample, zero point, and correlated errors affect the significance of comparisons.

Neither side is a direct readout of a cosmic dashboard. The microwave value is precise conditional on the fitted model. The local values depend on standardization, geometry, and the structure of the distance ladder. A difference between two methods can indicate a missing piece of physics, an observational bias, or an inadequate accounting of uncertainties. Its existence does not uniquely identify time-dependent dark energy. In many cosmological extensions, repairing the Hubble constant discrepancy introduces new problems with other measurements.

As a small illustration, imagine a survey that reports a distance of one hundred units with an uncertainty of one unit under a calibration. Another survey reports one hundred and five with an uncertainty of two units. You cannot decide their consistency by comparing the printed error bars alone if both used the same calibration star: an error in that shared calibration shifts both together. Nor can you combine samples as independent if they reuse the same supernovae. Covariances, correlated systematics, and model assumptions are as important as nominal precision.

## Competing accounts of acceleration

A constant cosmological term remains the simplest widely used account. It predicts a specific relation between density and pressure and a corresponding expansion history when combined with matter and radiation. Its predictions have survived many tests, so it serves as the baseline.

One alternative is quintessence, a slowly evolving scalar field whose effective pressure may vary. Another class allows the equation of state to cross the value negative one, though not every simple scalar-field model can do this consistently. Modified-gravity theories change the relation between cosmic expansion, the gravitational potentials governing lensing, and the rate at which structure grows. A model that adjusts expansion distances while predicting the wrong clustering of matter fails an important cross-check.

These are live theoretical directions, each with many realizations. One can also reconstruct the cosmic expansion using smooth functions without claiming to know the microscopic cause. Such reconstructions are useful for detecting departure from a baseline, but they must state their priors and flexibility. Far less viable are proposals that ignore measured supernova time dilation, the CMB acoustic pattern, or the consistency of multiple distance probes. A fair comparison demands agreement with the entire evidence base, not just one plot.

We can now state what is established at different levels. The light curves and redshifts are measured after explicit calibration and processing. Their pattern, together with independent rulers and the microwave sky, strongly supports accelerated cosmic expansion within a well-tested large-scale relativistic description. A cosmological constant provides a compact fit. The physical origin of that term is unknown. Several 2025 and 2026 combinations show a preference for evolving dark energy, but the preference is sensitive to data selection and inference methodology, and a later high-redshift cross-check has softened it.

It is possible to know the history of expansion fairly well while remaining unsure what supplies the acceleration. Improving that knowledge means designing measurements whose dependence on calibration, geometry, and gravitational dynamics differs. The universe has left several kinds of records; the strength of an explanation is how many of them it can predict together.

### Mathematical companion

1. Distance modulus \(\mu=m-M=5\log_{10}(D_L/10\,\mathrm{pc})\), using corrected magnitudes. Absolute magnitude calibration enters any absolute distance scale.
2. Redshift and scale factor: \(1+z=a_0/a(t)\). Instantaneous Hubble rate: \(H(t)=\dot a/a\). Acceleration concerns \(\ddot a\), which is a different quantity.
3. In FLRW general relativity, \(\ddot a/a=-(4\pi G/3)(\rho+3p/c^2)+\Lambda c^2/3\) if \(\rho,p\) denote non-Lambda fluids. Negative enough pressure can make acceleration positive.
4. For an ideal cosmic component, \(w=p/(\rho c^2)\), with \(w=-1\) for a constant-vacuum-energy term. A widely used phenomenological extension is \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a/a_0)\); parameter choice affects fits.
5. The BAO angular and radial observables constrain \(D_M(z)/r_d\) and \(D_H(z)/r_d\), with \(D_H(z)=c/H(z)\). Ruler calibration uses early-universe sound-horizon physics.
6. Representative published constraints have distinct provenance: Planck-base-Lambda-CDM \(H_0\approx67.4\); 2025 SH0ES local estimate \(\approx73.2\); 2025 CCHP TRGB-inclusive estimate \(\approx70.4\), with different uncertainty decompositions and correlations.

### Study questions and answers

1. **Why does supernova brightness tell us about expansion?** Calibrated explosions give luminosity distances at measured redshifts, constraining how light propagated through expanding spacetime.
2. **Why is the sound horizon called a standard ruler?** Early plasma physics predicts a preferred comoving separation, whose transverse and line-of-sight apparent sizes constrain distance ratios.
3. **What did the July 2026 Lyman-alpha result add?** More precise geometry from full correlation shapes; its central value shifted toward Lambda-CDM while combined evidence for evolution remained dataset-dependent.
4. **Does the Hubble tension prove dynamical dark energy?** No. The two involve overlapping cosmological assumptions but neither uniquely determines the other's physical explanation.
5. **What does a cosmological constant specify?** A constant term in the field equations, equivalent at background level to an energy density with pressure equal to minus energy density.
6. **What independent result could discriminate modified gravity from evolving fluid energy?** An accurately calibrated comparison of the expansion history with lensing and growth of density perturbations.
