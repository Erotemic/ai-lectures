# How People Peopled the World

Imagine standing on a beach sixty thousand years ago, somewhere in the island chains between mainland Southeast Asia and the great landmass that joined Australia with New Guinea. The islands are not arranged as they are on a modern map. Sea levels are lower because much water is locked in ice sheets. Some coastlines extend far beyond today's shores. Yet deep channels still separate islands, and reaching the distant landmass requires crossing open water. How did people get there, when did they arrive, and what evidence can survive after the coastlines are flooded again?

That question captures the challenge of reconstructing human migrations. Archaeologists find stone objects, hearths, burials and footprints at particular places. Geneticists infer relationships among populations from surviving genomes. Geologists reconstruct the landscape and ocean barriers. Each archive measures something different. A map with one sweeping arrow may be a convenient teaching picture, but it can conceal many possible routes, repeated movements, extinctions of local populations and gaps where the evidence has disappeared.

Human dispersal was not a single journey with an obvious starting gun. Different hominin lineages moved at different times. Early Homo reached regions outside Africa well before the emergence of our species. Later Homo sapiens populations spread across Africa and beyond it, sometimes encountering Neanderthals and Denisovans. The ancestors of living people are only part of that history. Some groups left archaeological traces but little detectable ancestry in later populations.

## The first constraint is the place itself

An excavated layer containing unambiguous tools demonstrates human or hominin activity at that place, provided the context is secure. Its age supplies a latest possible date by which a population had reached the area. If the site is forty-five thousand years old, we can say that people were there by forty-five thousand years ago. We cannot infer from that one site that they arrived exactly then; the earliest encampment may have eroded or remains undiscovered.

To identify a route, we need more than two points on a map. We need the dated order of intermediate sites, which landscapes were passable, how coastlines changed, and whether populations might have been replaced. A mountain pass that exists today may have been covered by an ice sheet at the relevant time. A coastal route may have been viable but left most of its camps underwater after sea level rose. The missing sites can be caused by preservation rather than absence of travelers.

Imagine two models for a journey. Model A places a migration through a river corridor. Model B follows the coast. The same earliest inland site could be produced by either model. To discriminate them we need dated coastal or corridor sites, resource reconstructions, ancient DNA or other evidence that would differ between the routes. Without that, two arrows on a map represent competing hypotheses, not an observation of footsteps along either line.

The word 'dispersal' can also refer to several processes. A few families might extend their territory over generations. A population might move in a rapid episode because of environmental change. Trade, marriage and neighboring groups can move genes and tools without movement of an entire community. Genetics may detect ancestry exchange even when archaeology shows continuity of local material culture. The scale and mechanism of a movement therefore need to be specified.

## Out of Africa was neither one departure nor the whole story

Fossils and archaeological sites establish that Homo sapiens evolved in Africa, but the exact structure of the ancestral African populations remains actively studied. A pan-African origin model emphasizes separated but interacting groups across the continent. Other models give more weight to specific regions or population histories. Evidence from the oldest fossils constrains these models but cannot yet reconstruct every movement among ancient African populations.

Some evidence indicates modern-human presence beyond Africa earlier than the principal ancestry shared among many living non-African populations. There were multiple opportunities for movement into Southwest Asia and beyond. Not all early groups necessarily survived as a substantial genetic source for later populations. This distinction resolves an apparent contradiction between relatively early archaeological presences and younger genetic estimates for particular ancestry histories.

The Ranis and Zlatý kůň genomes published in 2024 and 2025 illustrate the problem. Their sequence data supported a shared Neanderthal-admixture event for sampled non-African ancestry around forty-five to forty-nine thousand years ago. That genetic event is one historical constraint; it is not the departure date of every modern-human population that ever left Africa. Earlier movements may have left few or no descendants in the currently sequenced populations. Different episodes of admixture can also complicate the story.

Europe offers direct archaeological context. Fossil fragments and artifacts associated with modern humans from Ranis and Bacho Kiro indicate presence there by roughly forty-five thousand years ago, alongside a landscape previously occupied by Neanderthals. The timing, identity and interaction patterns differ among localities. A stone assemblage alone cannot certify the ancestry of its producers; dated fossils and molecules help when available. Even with them, a population's movement is reconstructed from scattered events rather than witnessed as a continuous migration.

## Sahul requires both a clock and a boat problem

During periods of low sea level, Australia, New Guinea and neighboring islands formed a connected landmass called Sahul. It remained separated from the Asian mainland by waters in Wallacea. Reaching Sahul therefore involved sea crossings at some point, although the exact islands, stages and abilities involved remain debated. The relevant geography must be reconstructed for the particular sea level and time, not borrowed from a present-day atlas.

A 2017 study reported human occupation at Madjedbebe, in northern Australia, at approximately sixty-five thousand years ago. Excavations documented stone artifacts, pigments and other material in deposits evaluated with optically stimulated luminescence and additional stratigraphic checks. The argument combines identification of human activity, the integrity of the sedimentary layers and an age model. Researchers have debated the oldest dates and the mechanisms of sediment disturbance; the wider chronology has therefore been investigated through independent archaeology and genetics.

A study published in late 2025 analyzed extensive mitochondrial DNA variation and argued that a longer chronology for settlement of Sahul, around sixty to sixty-five thousand years ago, was consistent with its genetic modeling. The study also recognized the competing shorter chronology of roughly forty-seven to fifty-one thousand years. Genetic mutation-rate assumptions, population structure and uncertainty in the branching history affect these estimates. Agreement between some archaeological and genetic results is encouraging, but their agreement does not make the models assumption-free.

This is a good place to ask what would make a settlement claim secure. We need artifacts or other signs of humans in an undisturbed context, chronological measurements tied to that context, and a careful treatment of potential mixing. A genetic signal indicating an older lineage divergence is different: it may constrain when ancestors separated, but divergence need not equal the arrival date on a particular shore. The founding population may have remained elsewhere for many generations after the genetic split.

Migration is often described as a triumph of a single ingenious journey. The archaeological evidence more directly supports populations developing practical ways of exploiting new habitats across long intervals. People arriving in Sahul encountered plants, animals and seasonal patterns that were different from those of the islands they passed. The artifacts they left indicate varied technologies and food-processing practices. Their exact languages, oral traditions and social institutions are not retrievable from the surviving stones, though living descendant communities preserve histories and relationships to these places that are important to responsible research.

## The Americas and the changing meaning of earliest

For much of the twentieth century, a common textbook picture began the peopling of the Americas with the Clovis archaeological tradition, about thirteen thousand years ago. Discoveries older than Clovis made that simple chronology untenable. The live questions concern how early people arrived, which routes they used, how populations spread, and the quality of evidence for individual sites.

Human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, have become an important test case. A 2021 report proposed that footprints formed around twenty-three to twenty-one thousand years ago, during the Last Glacial Maximum. The initial dating involved aquatic plant seeds, raising questions about possible reservoir effects and reworked material. Later work used independent measurements, including terrestrial pollen and luminescence. A 2025 study reported additional stratigraphic and radiocarbon evidence. In October 2026 a further study documented many footprint-bearing horizons and refined the sequence to approximately twenty-three-point-five to twenty-one-point-seven thousand years ago.

That cumulative evidence substantially strengthens the interpretation that people occupied the White Sands area during the ice age. It does not identify the first migrants to the continent, the exact path they used, or the ancestry of every person whose tracks are preserved. Footprint morphology and sedimentary context support a human interpretation; several dating systems and stratigraphy support an age range. A regional population history still needs additional observations.

The word 'first' appears in at least three different claims: earliest human presence anywhere in North America, earliest well-established site, and ancestral population of most later Indigenous peoples. These need not point to the same moment. A population may leave footprints but have no sampled genetic descendants. New dated sites could shift the earliest physical evidence again. And the ancestry of present-day Indigenous peoples cannot be collapsed into a single ancient camp or migration arrow.

## Could people have followed the Pacific coast?

One proposed route into the Americas runs along the Pacific margin of Beringia and the northwestern coast, using marine and shoreline resources. Another emphasizes an interior ice-free corridor, which opened and became ecologically usable at particular stages. Both require careful dating of ice retreat, food availability and archaeological presence. An inland route that was physically open but unable to sustain traveling populations is a different proposition from a fully usable migration corridor.

Coastal models face a preservation problem. Many shorelines people might have followed are now submerged because sea level rose after the last glacial period. The absence of ancient shoreline sites in today's exposed landscape has limited power to reject an ancient coastal migration. But it would be equally wrong to treat every missing site as proof that it existed underwater. Researchers need submerged landscape surveys, suitable coastal deposits, dated sites and explicit predictions about where traces should survive.

A 2026 research review discussed a Pacific coastal dispersal framework emphasizing roughly twenty-one to sixteen thousand years ago, while calling for stronger evidence and broader comparisons among routes. This proposal is relevant to the White Sands chronology, but it does not prove an exact coastal journey. The geographical feasibility of a route, the presence of people on a continent and the genetic ancestry of later populations are different levels of inference.

One particularly instructive alternative question asks whether people moved in several phases, with some leaving little genetic trace. Such a scenario might help reconcile early archaeological evidence with some later population-genetic reconstructions. It also adds parameters: how many groups, which dates, what happened to their descendants? Without independent evidence, a multiple-wave account can become a flexible explanation for any disagreement. A good model must risk predictions that new dated sites or ancient genomes could contradict.

## The danger of projecting modern boundaries backward

A modern country is a political boundary. An ancient archaeological industry is a pattern of material practices. A genetic population in a model is a statistical summary of ancestry. A language family is a historical linguistic hypothesis. These four classifications do not map one-to-one. A region occupied by different communities over millennia might show genetic continuity with changing tools, or new ancestry with continuity of local practices.

Maps of ancient migration can exaggerate certainty because arrows look like routes observed by travelers. The responsible way to read such maps is to ask whether each segment is constrained by dated archaeology, reconstructed landscape, genomic ancestry, or simply by a plausible connection between points. Arrows should carry dates, uncertainty and alternative routes where justified. An interval such as forty-five to forty-nine thousand years for an admixture event does not describe the timing of a walk across a continent.

We should also resist turning the movement of ancestry into a story of one group replacing another everywhere. Many ancient contacts involved mixture. Genetic evidence of replacement at one sampled cemetery does not mean every person in the surrounding region disappeared. Changes in allele frequencies can arise through migration, unequal population growth, drift and selection. Demographic simulations compare these possibilities, but sparse ancient samples often leave several explanations viable.

Climate is similarly important but not deterministic. Ice ages change coastal geometry, deserts and ecological corridors. They make some journeys more feasible and others less so. They do not compel a particular population to move on a schedule. People make choices, learn environments and maintain networks. Our models capture constraints on those choices much better than the individual decisions themselves.

## A world assembled from incomplete routes

We can now return to the ancient beach near Wallacea. Geological evidence constrains the islands and sea passages at the time. Archaeological evidence places humans at Madjedbebe by a dated occupation horizon, conditional on its site model. Genetic evidence provides a separate history of population branching that may favor one settlement chronology over another. None of these observations independently draws the exact route across the water.

The Americas supply a complementary lesson. Footprints at White Sands and their increasingly well-tested chronology establish presence at a particular lake margin, at a particular interval. Coastal and inland models offer explanations for how ancestors might have arrived. More dated sites, underwater archaeology and responsibly collected ancient genomic evidence can test those hypotheses. The resulting history is substantial even while its first journeys remain debated.

The strongest conclusions are that human lineages dispersed repeatedly, that Homo sapiens reached many parts of the world before the end of the last ice age, and that encounters and admixture make simple one-way diagrams inadequate. We know some places where people stood, worked and left traces. We cannot yet narrate every route or reconstruct all populations whose descendants are absent from the present genetic sample. The next lecture will look at behavior itself: fire, food preparation, and language, where evidence of an action becomes still harder to translate into a statement about mind and society.

## Companion: how to audit a migration arrow

| Archive | Directly observed | Inferred quantity | Why the inference is limited |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Excavated artifact layer | Objects, stratigraphy, dating samples | Presence at site by an age bound | Earliest known site need not equal first arrival |
| Genome from dated person | Sequenced alleles and damage characteristics | Kinship, ancestry exchange, demographic model | Sampling, mutation/recombination assumptions |
| Sea-level reconstruction | Shoreline indicators, ocean/sediment records | Land bridges, water crossings and route feasibility | Local bathymetry, date, shoreline loss |
| White Sands footprints | Human-shaped tracks in dated sediment sequence | Presence during Last Glacial Maximum | No direct proof of a particular entry route |
| Madjedbebe artifacts | Human material culture in optically dated deposits | Occupation of northern Sahul by proposed date | Stratigraphy, luminescence model, chronology debates |

**Arrival bound:** If the oldest securely dated occupation of site \(S\) is \(T\) years before present, then first occupation occurred at or before the corresponding calendar event, so in years-before-present form \(t_{\mathrm{arrival}}\ge T\). The unknown interval between arrival and the oldest preserved traces may be very large. Reconstructing a route requires constraints at more than one site.

**Migration models:** A path on a paleogeographic graph \(G_t=(V_t,E_t)\) is feasible only if every traversed edge is available at time \(t\) and its cost is compatible with assumed technologies and resources. Feasibility is a necessary condition for a proposed journey, not a direct observation that the journey occurred.

### Questions for study

1. Why does a secure 65 ka occupation at one shelter not prove people first reached Sahul at exactly 65 ka? **Answer:** The earliest preserved and excavated site may postdate arrival.
2. What do the White Sands footprints date? **Answer:** Human presence on particular ancient lake-margin surfaces, not a unique route into the Americas.
3. How can archaeological and genetic chronologies disagree without either measurement being fraudulent? **Answer:** They may date different events, have different preservation biases, and depend on different model assumptions.
4. What is the evidence problem for ancient coastal migration? **Answer:** Relevant shorelines are often submerged or eroded, reducing the number of testable sites.
